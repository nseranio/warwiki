# History of Prosthetics & Erectile Dysfunction Treatment

The history of erectile dysfunction treatment and penile prosthetics traces an arc from ancient aphrodisiacs and spiritual remedies through the landmark mechanical inventions of the 1970s to the pharmacological revolution of the 1990s. The inflatable penile prosthesis — first implanted in 1973 — remains one of the most elegantly engineered devices in all of surgery, and its story is inextricably linked to the concurrent development of the artificial urinary sphincter by the same inventor.

---

## Ancient and Pre-Modern Treatments

Erectile dysfunction has been recognized and treated since antiquity, though treatments ranged from the genuinely empirical to the ritualistic.

**Ancient Egypt** (*Ebers Papyrus*, c. 1550 BC): Prescriptions for impotence included mixtures of baby crocodile hearts dissolved in fat, rubbed onto the penis. Internal remedies employed dried beetle preparations and sesame oils.

**Ancient Greece and Rome**: Galen described impotence as arising from excess "cold, moist humors." Pliny the Elder (*Naturalis Historia*, 77 AD) catalogued dozens of botanical aphrodisiacs including rocket (*arugula*), hippocampus fat, hyena genitalia, and wolf liver. **Dioscorides** and **Theophrastus** described orchid tubers (*Orchis*) as simulacra of testes, to be taken as erectile remedies — one of the earliest examples of sympathetic magic in medicine.

**Medieval Islamic Medicine**: **Avicenna** (Ibn Sina, 1025 AD) in *The Canon of Medicine* described impotence from psychological, vascular, and neurological perspectives — remarkably close to modern classification. He prescribed compound preparations including theriac, nutmeg, and clove.

**Renaissance Europe**: Surgeon **Ambroise Paré** (1575) described erection physiology and recommended ligatures around the base of the penis in cases of arteriovenous insufficiency — a concept ancestral to modern constrictor ring therapy.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup>

## 19th and Early 20th Century: Physical Devices

### Early Rigid Implants

The concept of a surgically implanted penile support predates the modern prosthesis by decades. **Bogoras** (1936), Russian surgeon, is credited with the first reported penile reconstruction using a rib cartilage graft implanted into a phallic construct — an early application of rigid autologous material to restore penile rigidity, published in *Zentralblatt für Chirurgie*.<sup>[[2]](#ref2)</sup>

**Frumkin** (1944) and **Goodwin and Scott** (1952) subsequently described acrylic and other synthetic materials implanted alongside or within the corpus cavernosum to maintain penile rigidity after trauma or surgery. These early implants were plagued by erosion, infection, and extrusion — but established the principle that internal mechanical support was surgically achievable.

### Vacuum Erection Devices

The vacuum erection device (VED) has origins in the 19th century — American patent records document a "Surgical Appliance" for erectile purposes as early as **1874**. However, the modern VED traces its commercial origins to **Geddings Osbon** (Augusta, Georgia), a businessman who, following a prostatectomy in the 1960s, experimented with vacuum-based erection induction and patented the "ErecAid" device. Osbon donated the patent to the University of Rochester's Department of Urology; its utility was formally described in the urological literature by **Nadig, Ware, and Blumoff** (1986) and **Witherington** (1989).<sup>[[3]](#ref3)</sup>

The VED is now a first-line non-surgical treatment for ED and an important component of penile rehabilitation protocols after radical prostatectomy.

## The Modern Era: Penile Prosthesis Surgery

### Brantley Scott and the Inflatable Penile Prosthesis (AMS 700) (1973)

The transformative advance in ED treatment — and arguably one of the most significant developments in all of reconstructive urology — was the inflatable penile prosthesis designed by **F. Brantley Scott**, **William E. Bradley**, and **Gerald W. Timm** at Baylor College of Medicine.

Inspired by Scott's concurrent work on the **artificial urinary sphincter** (AUS), the team recognized that a hydraulic mechanism could also restore erectile function. The key concept: two inflatable cylinders placed within the corpora cavernosa, connected via tubing to a reservoir of saline fluid and a scrotal pump — mimicking tumescence by fluid transfer and allowing detumescence by valve deflation.

**1973**: Scott, Bradley, and Timm performed the first human implantation of the inflatable penile prosthesis (IPP). The results were published:
> Scott FB, Bradley WE, Timm GW. "Management of erectile impotence. Use of implantable inflatable prosthesis." *Urology.* 1973;2(1):80–82. [PMID: 4714416]

The device commercialized as the **AMS 700** (American Medical Systems) became the dominant three-piece IPP for decades and remains the basis of all modern inflatable prostheses.<sup>[[4]](#ref4)</sup>

### Small-Carrion Semi-Rigid Prosthesis (1973)

Almost simultaneously, **Michael Small** and **Hernan Carrion** (University of Miami) described a simpler, semi-rigid malleable rod prosthesis — implanted bilaterally into the corpora cavernosa and providing constant rigidity without inflation mechanisms.

> Small MP, Carrion HM, Gordon JA. "Small-Carrion penile prosthesis: new implant for management of impotence." *Urology.* 1975;5(4):479–486. [PMID: 1091875]

The semi-rigid prosthesis offered mechanical simplicity and reliability — features that remain relevant today, particularly in patients with dexterity limitations or limited surgical access to scrotal/reservoir space.<sup>[[5]](#ref5)</sup>

### Jonas and Brantley Scott — Subsequent Generations

Early AMS 700 devices were plagued by mechanical failure rates approaching 20% at 5 years. Progressive engineering refinements addressed these problems:

- **Rear-tip extenders**: Improved cylinder sizing
- **Kink-resistant tubing**: Reduced connection failures  
- **Controlled expansion cylinders**: Prevented aneurysmal dilation
- **Parylene coating**: Reduced friction
- **Inhibizone antibiotic coating** (minocycline/rifampin, AMS): Significantly reduced infection rates
- **Titan hydrophilic coating** (Coloplast): Absorbed local antibiotic solution at time of implant

Modern three-piece IPP devices (AMS LGX, AMS 700 CX/CXR; Coloplast Titan, Titan Zero Degree) demonstrate mechanical survival rates exceeding 90% at 5 years and patient satisfaction rates of 90–95%.<sup>[[6]](#ref6)</sup>

## Artificial Urinary Sphincter: The Companion Device

### Scott, Bradley, and Timm — AMS 800 (1972–1973)

The **artificial urinary sphincter (AUS)** was developed in parallel with the IPP by the same Baylor College of Medicine group. F. Brantley Scott's work on hydraulic devices was motivated by the problem of post-prostatectomy urinary incontinence — a problem of sphincteric incompetence rather than erectile failure, but requiring the same mechanical principle.

**1972**: Scott described the prototype hydraulic AUS; first human implantation was performed.

> Scott FB, Bradley WE, Timm GW. "Treatment of urinary incontinence by an implantable prosthetic urinary sphincter." *J Urol.* 1974;112(1):75–80. [PMID: 4833750]

The device consisted of an inflatable cuff placed around the bulbar urethra (or bladder neck), a pressure-regulating balloon reservoir, and a control pump in the scrotum (or labia). Activation inflated the cuff to occlude the urethra; voiding was initiated by squeezing the pump to deflate the cuff and transfer fluid to the reservoir, allowing a timed refill.

The **AMS 800** (1983 design refinement) became and remains the gold standard for post-prostatectomy incontinence treatment — the only AUS device approved for use in the United States. Over 150,000 devices have been implanted worldwide.<sup>[[7]](#ref7)</sup>

## Intracavernosal Injection Therapy (1982–1983)

### Vasoactive Agents and Giles Brindley

One of the more dramatic presentations in medical history occurred at the **1983 annual meeting of the American Urological Association** in Las Vegas. British neurophysiologist and experimental urologist **Giles Brindley** (Medical Research Council, London) presented his data on intracavernosal injection of vasoactive agents — and, to demonstrate efficacy in real time, injected himself with phenoxybenzamine before the lecture and lowered his trousers before the audience to display the resulting erection.

Brindley had begun experimenting with self-injection of adrenergic blockers in 1982 and published:
> Brindley GS. "Cavernosal alpha-blockade: a new technique for investigating and treating erectile impotence." *Br J Psychiatry.* 1983;143:332–337. [PMID: 6414479]

**Ronald Virag** (Paris) independently reported the vasoactive effects of papaverine during intraoperative cavernosal injection in 1982:
> Virag R. "Intracavernous injection of papaverine for erectile failure." *Lancet.* 1982;2(8304):938. [PMID: 6127524]

Together, the Virag and Brindley reports established intracavernosal injection (ICI) as a practical treatment for ED — the first effective pharmacological approach and a major alternative to prosthesis surgery.<sup>[[8]](#ref8)</sup>

**Alprostadil (prostaglandin E1)** was subsequently demonstrated to be the most effective and best-tolerated vasoactive agent for ICI, and was FDA-approved for this indication (Caverject) in 1995. Alprostadil in an intraurethral suppository (MUSE — Medicated Urethral System for Erection) was approved in 1996.

## The Sildenafil (Viagra) Revolution (1998)

### Discovery and FDA Approval

The discovery of sildenafil's erectile effects was accidental. **Pfizer** researchers were developing a compound (UK-92480, later named sildenafil) as a treatment for angina and hypertension via phosphodiesterase type 5 (PDE5) inhibition and coronary vasodilation. Phase I trials in Merthyr Tydfil, Wales, revealed an unexpected side effect: penile erections.

The pivotal clinical trials were led by **Ian Osterloh** (Pfizer UK) and the drug was developed for ED. **Sildenafil** (brand name: Viagra) received FDA approval on **March 27, 1998** — becoming one of the fastest-selling pharmaceutical products in history.

The mechanism: PDE5 degrades cyclic GMP (cGMP), which mediates smooth muscle relaxation in cavernosal tissue. Sildenafil blocks PDE5, potentiating the nitric oxide (NO)/cGMP pathway — but only in the context of sexual stimulation (requiring NO release). This physiological selectivity distinguished it from non-selective vasoactive agents.<sup>[[9]](#ref9)</sup>

The subsequent approval of **vardenafil** (Levitra, 2003), **tadalafil** (Cialis, 2003), and **avanafil** (Stendra, 2012) completed the PDE5 inhibitor class — which became the first-line treatment for ED globally, profoundly reducing referrals for both ICI and prosthesis surgery.

## Surgical Treatment of Peyronie's Disease

### Henri François LePeyronier (1743)

**François de la Peyronie** (1678–1747), chief surgeon to Louis XV of France, provided the first systematic description in 1743 of the condition bearing his name — induration of the penile shaft causing curvature, painful erection, and sexual dysfunction — in *Mémoires de l'Académie Royale de Chirurgie*. His description of fibrous plaques within the tunica albuginea was clinically accurate, and his name was subsequently attached to the condition by convention rather than by his own choice.<sup>[[10]](#ref10)</sup>

### Nesbit Corporoplasty (1965)

**Reed M. Nesbit** (University of Michigan) described the plication technique for correction of congenital penile curvature in 1965 — shortening the convex tunica albuginea with excision ellipses to straighten the erect penis (*J Urol*, 1965). This technique was subsequently adapted for Peyronie's disease (and penile prosthesis-associated curvature), and remains the standard plication/shortening approach for mild-to-moderate curvature. The Nesbit corpoplasty is the most widely referenced historical technique for penile curvature correction.<sup>[[11]](#ref11)</sup>

### Lue Incision/Grafting (1990s)

**Tom Lue** (UCSF) pioneered length-preserving approaches for Peyronie's disease involving tunica incision or excision with grafting — particularly useful for severe curvature where plication would result in unacceptable penile shortening. Lue's group described the H-shaped incision with saphenous vein grafting, advancing the concept of plaque correction without significant length sacrifice.<sup>[[12]](#ref12)</sup>

## Catheters: The Foley Balloon Catheter

### Frederic Foley (1937)

**Frederic E. B. Foley** (1891–1966), American urologist at Ancker Hospital in St. Paul, Minnesota, designed the self-retaining balloon catheter that carries his name in 1929, obtaining a patent in 1937. The concept — a catheter with an inflatable balloon near its tip to prevent withdrawal — solved the problem of catheter displacement without tying or uncomfortable retention mechanisms.

> Foley FEB. "A self-retaining bag catheter." *J Urol.* 1937;38:140–144.

The Foley catheter is the most widely used urological device in the world, with billions of catheter-days placed annually across all specialties of medicine. Though catheter-associated urinary tract infection (CAUTI) is now the most common hospital-acquired infection globally, the device itself remains irreplaceable in surgical and critical care settings.<sup>[[13]](#ref13)</sup>

## Penile Reconstruction and Phalloplasty

### Bogoras and the First Phalloplasty (1936)

**N.A. Bogoras** (Moscow) performed the first recorded total penile reconstruction in 1936 using a **tubed abdominal pedicle skin flap** (the "filatov tube") for a man who had lost his penis to trauma. He incorporated a rib cartilage segment to provide rigidity — a forerunner of the modern osseous fibula free flap in phalloplasty.

### Radial Forearm Free Flap Phalloplasty — Chang and Hwang (1984)

The modern era of phalloplasty began with **Chang** and **Hwang** (China, 1984), who described the **radial forearm free flap (RFFF)** as the tissue source for total phallic construction — providing thin, pliable, sensate skin ideally suited for the complex geometry of phalloplasty. The RFFF phalloplasty became, and remains, the workhorse procedure for gender-affirming male genitalia construction and penile reconstruction after traumatic loss.<sup>[[14]](#ref14)</sup>
