## Biography

Jack Robertson encountered female urinary disorders at a time when clinicians often examined women with instruments designed for men.[^1] In 1968 he persuaded instrument maker Karl Storz to make a narrow female urethroscope that permitted carbon dioxide insufflation, giving gynecologists a dedicated instrument for examination.[^1]

Robertson also saw that few gynecologists or urologists had been taught to manage female genitourinary disorders.[^1] He and his wife Janice organized weekend seminars that he taught around the world, while he arranged workshops for the American College of Surgeons and the American College of Obstetricians and Gynecologists.[^1] Drutz also remembered Janice Robertson's work as an artist and as the organizer of those courses.[^1] Robertson later taught gynecology interns at several California medical schools and travelled regularly to Nevada to lecture.[^1]

Harold Drutz joined him at Los Angeles County–USC Medical Center in 1975 and recalled teaching that drew on practical experience with complex repairs and treated the female pelvis as a connected problem rather than a set of specialty territories.[^1] In 1976 Robertson and Axel Ingelman-Sundberg gathered colleagues at the FIGO meeting in Mexico City to establish the International Urogynecological Association, which he later led as president from 1981 to 1984.[^1][^3] He then helped form the Gynecologic Urology Society, which became the American Urogynecologic Society, and served as its president in 1979–1980.[^1][^4]
