# History of Urethral Surgery

The history of urethral surgery spans more than three millennia, from urethral dilation by Ayurvedic surgeons in ancient India to robotic-assisted urethroplasty in the 21st century. It follows the management of [urethral stricture disease](/docs/clinical-conditions/03b-voiding-outlet/urethral-stricture), [hypospadias](/docs/special-populations/05f-lifelong-care/hypospadias-epispadias) and traumatic injury, with contributions from general and military surgeons and, from the mid-20th century, from surgeons who specialized in urethral reconstruction.

---

## Ancient and Classical Period (Pre-600 AD)

### Ancient India — The Ayurvedic Tradition (~600 BC)

The earliest recorded treatment for urethral stricture disease is attributed to Sushruta (Suśruta), the central figure of Ayurvedic surgery, traditionally dated to about 600 BC. The *Sushruta Samhita* describes some 200 surgical instruments, including catheters, and the treatment of stricture with graduated dilators of metal or wood; later accounts describe reed catheters lubricated with ghee.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup>

### Ancient Egypt (~1550 BC)

The Ebers Papyrus (c. 1550 BC) records urological treatments dating to earlier millennia. Urethral probes and dilators fashioned from wood, plant stalks, feathers and papyrus rolls were used for instrumentation of the lower urinary tract, and copper instruments for urethral calibration have been documented archaeologically. Lead urethral dilators from the last century BC have been found, and Roman finds in France indicate that some form of urethrotomy was attempted, suggesting that the techniques spread broadly through the ancient world.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup>

### Ancient Greece

Aristotle (4th century BC) is credited in some histories with an early description of hypospadias. Erasistratus (c. 304–250 BC) used an elongated S-shaped metal catheter for stricture, a shape that remained standard for centuries.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup> Epicurus (341–270 BC) is recorded by Diogenes Laertius as dying after a 14-day illness with urinary obstruction attributed to a stone, one of the earliest recorded accounts of urinary retention.

Heliodorus (1st–2nd century AD), an Alexandrian surgeon, gave the first detailed surgical description of hypospadias repair: a circumferential oblique amputation distal to the ectopic meatus, with hemorrhage controlled by vinegar-soaked bandages and cauterization. He distinguished balanic, penile and perineal-scrotal variants and considered only the balanic form surgically treatable. Antyllus (2nd century AD) described the same technique and added that the amputation did not impair fertility.<sup>[[2]](#ref2)</sup>

Galen (c. 130–201 AD) is credited with the first use of the term *hypospadias*, from the Greek *hypo* (under) and *spadon* (rent or tear). Translation of the original manuscripts shows that he recommended stretching and suturing for glanular hypospadias rather than amputation; he was more conservative in his operative recommendations than earlier histories reported. Ventral penile curvature was recognized as functionally significant in antiquity, although the term chordee dates only from the 18th century.<sup>[[2]](#ref2)[[3]](#ref3)</sup>

### Ancient Rome

Aulus Cornelius Celsus (25 BC–50 AD), in *De Medicina*, described an S-shaped catheter and the complications of urethral stricture,<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup> and described external urethrotomy for an impacted urethral stone, an early surgical description of urethrotomy. Paul of Aegina (c. 625–690 AD) continued the classical tradition of hypospadias description and classification.

## Medieval Period (600–1500 AD)

Physicians writing in Arabic preserved Greco-Roman technique through the medieval period, when European surgical practice stagnated. In the 10th century Rhazes of Baghdad and his contemporaries performed external urethrotomy or perineal puncture, and Albucasis recommended silver instruments and flexible lead sounds for urethral dilation.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup> Haly Abbas (al-Majusi, 10th century) wrote a comprehensive medical text in this tradition, and Constantine the African (c. 1015–1087) translated it and other Arabic medical works into Latin. The transmission of Greek surgical knowledge through Arabic scholarship set the stage for Renaissance rediscovery.

The first recorded serious epidemic of gonorrhea, in 1520, renewed European attention to urethral stricture disease. Henry IV of France was operated on for stricture by Mayerne in 1603,<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup> and historical anecdote holds that Napoleon Bonaparte also had a urethral stricture.

---

## The Renaissance: Ambroise Paré (1510–1590)

Ambroise Paré, the French surgeon regarded by many historians as the father of modern surgery, revived urethral surgery in France after it had fallen out of practice. He devised instruments for scraping "carnosities" (the abnormal tissue growths then thought to cause urethral obstruction) from the urethra, an early endourethral debridement concept,<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup> and tubular silver catheters with long gentle curves (coudé-type) to permit easier passage around the prostatic angle. He was known for operating gently to limit pain.<sup>[[4]](#ref4)</sup>

## 17th and 18th Centuries

### The Lanceolate Catheter (1795)

A silver catheter from which a cutting blade could be protruded to stab through a stricture was introduced in 1795. It was an early form of internal urethrotomy performed without direct visualization, remained popular for a long period and preceded the later internal urethrotomes.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup>

### John Hunter (1728–1793)

John Hunter, Scottish-born London surgeon and founder of pathological anatomy in England, wrote on venereal disease and stricture. He described caustic (silver nitrate) treatment of urethral stricture, applied through a cannula, in his *Treatise on the Venereal Disease* (1786). He classified strictures as permanent, spasmodic or mixed, and found up to six strictures in a single urethra.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[5]](#ref5)</sup>

## The 19th Century: The Classical Era of Stricture Surgery

### Jean Civiale (1792–1867) and Internal Urethrotomy

Jean Civiale, a Parisian physician, produced an internal urethrotome in 1817. A terminal bulb was passed through the stricture, a blade was protruded and the instrument was withdrawn, dividing the stricture from within outwards. Because the bulb had to traverse the stenosis, the instrument suited only fairly wide strictures, and Civiale turned to lithotrity (transurethral crushing of bladder stones), the work for which he is better known.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup>

### Maisonneuve (1848)

Jacques-Gilles Maisonneuve (1809–1897) improved internal urethrotomy in 1848 by adding a filiform guide, established in the urethra first, to which the urethrotome could be screwed, enabling safer passage through tight strictures.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup> His blind internal urethrotome, alongside the later Otis instrument, remained in clinical use for over a century.

### Sir Henry Thompson (1820–1904)

Born in Framlingham, Suffolk, Sir Henry Thompson has been called the first British urologist for the dedication of his practice to genitourinary conditions. In 1852 he won the Jacksonian Prize of the Royal College of Surgeons for his essay on urethral stricture. His 1854 monograph, *The Pathology and Treatment of Stricture of the Urethra* (4th ed. 1885), was the standard Victorian reference text, and he held that most strictures lay in the bulbomembranous region. He learned lithotrity directly from Civiale in Paris, and his treatment of King Leopold of Belgium's bladder stone by lithotrity in 1863 brought him international fame and a knighthood.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[6]](#ref6)[[7]](#ref7)</sup>

### Carl Thiersch and the Tubularization Principle (1869)

Carl Thiersch (1822–1895), German surgeon, described tubularization of local penile skin *in situ* for repair of epispadias in 1869; Liston had used the urethral plate in epispadias in 1838. Anger and Duplay extended the principle to hypospadias five years later, and it underlies modern urethral plate urethroplasty.<sup>[[3]](#ref3)</sup>

### Simon Duplay (1874 and 1880)

Simon Duplay (French surgeon) described tubularization of the urethral plate distal to the urethral orifice for hypospadias repair in 1874. By 1880, he had observed that the urethral plate could epithelialize over a catheter without complete sutured closure, foreshadowing the buried-strip technique. The Thiersch-Duplay principle became the conceptual ancestor of all subsequent urethral plate tubularization procedures, including the modern TIP (Snodgrass, 1994).<sup>[[3]](#ref3)</sup>

### Fessenden N. Otis and the Urethrameter (1872/1876)

Fessenden N. Otis (1825–1900), American surgeon in New York, showed that the normal urethral calibre was larger than then assumed and developed the *urethrameter* (urethrometer), a calibrating instrument for determining sound size. His dilating urethrotome, which appeared in about 1870, was expanded within the stricture before a concealed blade was raised and drawn through it, cutting at the 12 o'clock position once the stricture was stretched to the desired caliber. Otis set out his methods in *Stricture of the Male Urethra, Its Radical Cure* (1878).<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[8]](#ref8)</sup> The Otis urethrotome remained in use into the endoscopic era.<sup>[[9]](#ref9)</sup>

### First Use of Oral Mucosal Grafts: Sapezhko (1894)

Kirill Sapezhko (1894) is credited with the first reported use of oral mucosa for urethral stricture, the earliest conceptual ancestor of modern buccal mucosal grafting. The technique was not adopted and was largely forgotten for nearly a century.<sup>[[10]](#ref10)</sup> Tyrmos (1902) applied oral mucosal grafts for urethral fistula repair, the second recorded use.

## Early 20th Century (1900–1950)

### Marion and Heitz-Boyer: Excision and Primary Anastomosis (1912, 1922)

Excision of a stricture with sutured anastomosis was first performed by Heusner in 1883, with poor early results. G. Marion applied excision of the stenosed segment with end-to-end anastomosis in 1912, and M. Heitz-Boyer in 1922, with suprapubic urinary diversion improving the results.<sup>[[11]](#ref11)</sup> This work established the conceptual basis for [excision and primary anastomosis (EPA)](/docs/surgical-techniques/04a-urethral-reconstruction/excision-primary-anastomosis) urethroplasty, still used for short-segment bulbar strictures today.

### Hamilton Russell (1900)

Hamilton Russell (Melbourne) published an operation for severe hypospadias in the *British Medical Journal* in 1900, and Denis Browne later credited him with the buried skin strip principle. The concept of leaving an intact epithelial strip to epithelialize ventrally over a catheter without complete tubularization underpins both the Browne repair and, conceptually, the TIP procedure.<sup>[[3]](#ref3)[[12]](#ref12)</sup>

### G. Humby and Buccal Mucosa — First Modern Use (1941)

Graham Humby (1941), a London surgeon, revived oral mucosal grafting by harvesting buccal mucosa for urethral reconstruction in an 8-year-old with hypospadias, the first modern documented use. The technique again fell largely out of favor and was not widely adopted for another half century.<sup>[[10]](#ref10)[[13]](#ref13)</sup>

## Mid-20th Century: The Staged Urethroplasty Era (1950–1975)

### Bengt Johanson and the Two-Stage Urethroplasty (1953)

The Swedish surgeon Bengt Johanson introduced the two-stage urethroplasty that bears his name. In 1953, he published "Reconstruction of the male urethra in strictures" (*Acta Chirurgica Scandinavica*, supplement 176), describing the buried intact epithelium principle.<sup>[[14]](#ref14)</sup> The technique:

- **Stage 1.** The diseased urethra is opened ventrally and laid open as a skin-lined plate; scrotal or perineal skin covers the repair area.
- **Stage 2.** After the first-stage plate has healed satisfactorily, retubularization and closure. For staged penile repair, the EAU guideline recommends an interval of at least four to six months before this stage (weak recommendation).<sup>[[69]](#ref69)</sup>

Johanson's principle was applied to hypospadias as well as to urethral stricture. In a Vienna series covering 1951–1974, urethroplasty by Johanson's principle was performed in 467 patients, with complete repair in 391.<sup>[[15]](#ref15)</sup> Johanson also proposed suprapubic cystostomy with delayed urethral repair for posterior urethral injuries from pelvic fracture, an early statement of staged management for traumatic urethral disruption. The [two-stage approach](/docs/surgical-techniques/04a-urethral-reconstruction/johanson-two-stage) remains in use for complex strictures with lichen sclerosus, prior failed repairs and severely compromised tissue beds.<sup>[[14]](#ref14)[[16]](#ref16)</sup>

### Denis Browne and the Buried Strip (1930s–1950s)

Denis Browne, British pediatric surgeon at Great Ormond Street Hospital, London, popularized the *buried skin strip* (buried intact epithelium) principle for hypospadias repair. Though the concept traces to Duplay (1880) and Hamilton Russell (1900), Browne's advocacy and operative results spread the technique internationally. His buried-strip repair is the conceptual precursor of the modern TIP procedure.<sup>[[3]](#ref3)[[17]](#ref17)</sup>

---

## Direct Vision Internal Urethrotomy: Sachse (1971/1974)

Endoscopic urethrotomy under vision was first reported in 1865. Hans Sachse (German urologist) introduced the sharp cold-knife urethrotome for optical (direct-vision) internal urethrotomy in 1971 and published his experience in 1974. The technique used a sharp blade under direct endoscopic visualization, avoiding the tissue necrosis associated with electrocautery. Schultheiss and colleagues describe cold-knife urethrotomy as the gold standard that followed; it replaced blind techniques and established [direct vision internal urethrotomy (DVIU)](/docs/surgical-techniques/04a-urethral-reconstruction/dviu) as the first-line endoscopic procedure.<sup>[[18]](#ref18)[[19]](#ref19)</sup>

## The Modern Reconstructive Era (1960–2000)

### Richard Turner-Warwick (1925–2020)

[Richard Turner-Warwick](/docs/roots/surgeons/s-z/richard-turner-warwick) (1925–2020) worked at the Middlesex Hospital, London, where he made contributions to urethral reconstruction over four decades:

- 1960: Published "A technique for posterior urethroplasty" (*J Urol*, PMID 13839971).<sup>[[20]](#ref20)</sup>
- 1977: Published "Complex traumatic posterior urethral strictures" (*J Urol*), on the anatomy and repair of pelvic fracture urethral distraction defects.<sup>[[21]](#ref21)</sup>
- 1976: Described the omental pedicle graft in urinary tract reconstruction, used to provide vascular support to the transpubic bulboprostatic anastomosis, a technique that remains relevant in irradiated and complex pelvic reconstruction.<sup>[[22]](#ref22)</sup>
- Also described the [scrotal inlay urethroplasty](/docs/surgical-techniques/04a-urethral-reconstruction/turner-warwick-scrotal-inlay) and multiple refinements to posterior urethral reconstruction.

His perineal-abdominal transpubic urethroplasty was widely used for pelvic fracture urethral distraction defects in the 1970s and 1980s.

### Waterhouse Transpubic Urethroplasty (1973)

Waterhouse and colleagues (1973) described the [combined abdomino-perineal transpubic approach](/docs/surgical-techniques/04a-urethral-reconstruction/abdominoperineal-urethroplasty): pubic bone division with a Gigli saw (wedge removal), perineal incision, bulbar urethral mobilization and anastomosis to the prostatic urethra. Mean hospitalization was 28 days. The procedure was widely adopted before being gradually superseded by elaborated perineal approaches.<sup>[[23]](#ref23)[[24]](#ref24)</sup>

### Ahmad Orandi — Penile Skin Flap Urethroplasty (1968/1972)

Ahmad Orandi described 10 cases of longitudinal ventral fasciocutaneous island penile flap urethroplasty in the *British Journal of Urology* in December 1968, with an expanded 21-patient series in the *Journal of Urology* in 1972.<sup>[[25]](#ref25)[[26]](#ref26)</sup> The [Orandi flap](/docs/surgical-techniques/04a-urethral-reconstruction/orandi-flap), based on dartos fascia, became a widely used one-stage procedure for anterior penile urethral strictures and is still used for non-obliterative penile strictures without lichen sclerosus.

### Blandy and Tresidder — Scrotal Flap Urethroplasty (1967/1968)

John Blandy (1927–2011), British urologist at the London Hospital, with Singh and Tresidder published "Urethroplasty by scrotal flap for long urethral strictures" in the *British Journal of Urology* in 1968 (PMID 4872409). The technique employed an inverted Y-shaped perineal incision with a mobile scrotal skin flap advanced to the bulbar urethra, based on dartos fascia vascularity.<sup>[[27]](#ref27)</sup> In a long-term review of 211 one-stage dartos-pedicled island patch urethroplasties performed between 1970 and 1987, strictures recurred in 14 of the 194 patients followed for 3 to 20 years (7%), some as late as 15 years after surgery.<sup>[[28]](#ref28)</sup> Blandy also wrote a review of stricture management in the *Postgraduate Medical Journal* in 1980.<sup>[[29]](#ref29)</sup>

### Webster and Goldwasser — Elaborated Perineal Approach (1986–1991)

[George D. Webster](/docs/roots/surgeons/s-z/george-webster) at Duke University refined the elaborated perineal approach for posterior urethral strictures. Webster and Goldwasser (1986) described wedge excision of the inferior pubis, which allowed one-stage perineal repair of prostatomembranous strictures 2 to 5 cm long in 4 patients.<sup>[[30]](#ref30)</sup> Webster and Ramon (1991) published repair of pelvic fracture posterior urethral defects using this approach: experience with 74 cases (PMID 2005693). Drawing on principles from Marion, Turner-Warwick and Waterhouse, Webster's elaborated perineal approach became the dominant technique for [pelvic fracture urethral distraction defects](/docs/special-populations/05a-trauma-emergencies/pfui) in the 1990s and remains the basis of perineal repair today.<sup>[[31]](#ref31)</sup>

### McAninch Circular Fasciocutaneous Flap (1985–1993)

[Jack McAninch](/docs/roots/surgeons/h-r/jack-mcaninch) (UCSF) first described reconstruction of extensive urethral strictures using a [circular fasciocutaneous penile flap](/docs/surgical-techniques/04a-urethral-reconstruction/mcaninch-circular-flap) in 1993. Unlike the Orandi longitudinal flap, the circular technique helped reconstruct 8–21 cm strictures in the initial 10-patient series, but the flap itself supplied up to 15 cm of skin; the 18- and 21-cm repairs required supplemental free penile-skin and bladder-mucosa grafts, respectively.<sup>[[32]](#ref32)</sup> In the initial 10 patients, no stricture recurred at a mean of 14.4 months.<sup>[[32]](#ref32)</sup> McAninch and Morey later reported 66 patients with a mean follow-up of 41 months: the initial success rate was 79% (52 of 66), with recurrence after 13% of onlay and 58% of tubularized repairs, and the flap became an option for extensive anterior strictures.<sup>[[33]](#ref33)</sup>

## The Buccal Mucosa Revolution (1992–1996)

The revival and establishment of [buccal mucosal grafting (BMG)](/docs/foundations/surgical-principles/grafts-gu-reconstruction) in the 1990s changed anterior urethral reconstruction.

Bürger et al. (1992) and Dessanti et al. (1992) independently reported buccal mucosa for urethral surgery, Dessanti in hypospadias repair.<sup>[[34]](#ref34)[[35]](#ref35)</sup> El-Kasaby et al. (1993) published an early adult clinical series of buccal mucosa patch grafts for anterior urethral stricture.<sup>[[36]](#ref36)</sup>

[Allen F. Morey](/docs/roots/surgeons/h-r/allen-morey) and McAninch (1996) reported 13 men with complex, refractory bulbar strictures repaired in one stage with a nontubularized buccal mucosa onlay graft, harvested by a second team while the perineal team exposed the stricture. Among these 13 men, eight also underwent another reconstructive technique; none needed further dilation or instrumentation at a median of 18 months, and operating time with the two-team approach was shorter than with other forms of substitution urethroplasty.<sup>[[37]](#ref37)</sup>

The biological properties of buccal mucosa that suit it to urethral reconstruction:
- Thick epithelium resistant to the wet urinary environment
- Thin, highly vascularized lamina propria enabling rapid imbibition
- Ready accessibility from the inner cheek
- Minimal donor site morbidity

Naha and colleagues describe oral mucosal grafting as the gold standard for anterior urethral stricture repair.<sup>[[10]](#ref10)</sup>

### Guido Barbagli — Dorsal Onlay Urethroplasty (1995–1996)

[Guido Barbagli](/docs/roots/surgeons/a-g/guido-barbagli) (International Center for Reconstructive Urethral Surgery, Arezzo, Italy) introduced the [dorsal onlay graft urethroplasty](/docs/surgical-techniques/04a-urethral-reconstruction/dorsal-onlay-omg) in 1995–1996, combining Monseur's concept of dorsal urethrotomy with [Charles Devine](/docs/roots/surgeons/a-g/charles-devine)'s free graft technique.<sup>[[38]](#ref38)[[39]](#ref39)</sup> The Barbagli procedure for bulbar strictures:

1. Lithotomy position; midline perineoscrotal incision
2. Bulbar urethra completely mobilized and rotated 180°
3. Urethra incised along its dorsal surface
4. Graft (preputial skin or buccal mucosa) quilted to the tunica albuginea of the underlying corpora cavernosa
5. The corpora serve as a richly vascularized graft bed, maximizing take

In the comparison by [Daniela Andrich](/docs/roots/surgeons/a-g/daniela-andrich), Leach and [Anthony Mundy](/docs/roots/surgeons/h-r/anthony-mundy) (2001), titled *"The Barbagli procedure gives the best results for patch urethroplasty of the bulbar urethra"*, strictures recurred in 5% after the Barbagli procedure versus 14% after traditional ventral stricturotomy at 5 years (42 Barbagli and 29 ventral patients; PMID 11564027).<sup>[[40]](#ref40)</sup>

Barbagli subsequently described multiple refinements: fibrin glue in dorsal onlay urethroplasty (2006); muscle- and nerve-sparing bulbar urethroplasty (2008); outcomes of perineal urethrostomy, including the [Blandy flap](/docs/surgical-techniques/04a-urethral-reconstruction/blandy-perineal-urethrostomy), for anterior stricture disease (2009); and the dorsal Orandi skin flap modification for penile urethroplasty (2019).<sup>[[41]](#ref41)[[42]](#ref42)[[43]](#ref43)[[44]](#ref44)</sup> He also reported anterior urethroplasty with the MukoCell® tissue-engineered oral mucosa graft, with success in 32 of 38 patients (84%) at a median of 55 months in a 2018 multicenter series.<sup>[[45]](#ref45)</sup> He has also published an operative atlas and a large body of papers on urethral reconstruction.

### Sanjay Kulkarni — One-Sided Anterior Urethroplasty (2009)

[Sanjay Kulkarni](/docs/roots/surgeons/h-r/sanjay-kulkarni) (Pune, India) described the *"one-sided anterior urethroplasty"* in 2009, a technique designed for long-segment and panurethral strictures that preserves normal anatomy. Dissection is limited to one side of the urethra, sparing the lateral vascular supply on the intact side, the central tendon of the perineum, the bulbospongiosus muscle and its perineal innervation.<sup>[[46]](#ref46)</sup>

Combined with penile invagination (the phallus pulled into the perineal field), Kulkarni's approach permits [single-stage reconstruction of the entire anterior urethra](/docs/surgical-techniques/04a-urethral-reconstruction/kulkarni-one-stage) using two dorsally placed oral mucosal grafts. Initial mixed series: 12 bulbar-only and 12 panurethral strictures; 22/24 patients (92%) needed no postoperative instrumentation at a mean follow-up of 22 months, and 12/24 had lichen sclerosus.<sup>[[46]](#ref46)</sup> The technique is used for panurethral lichen sclerosus, where the entire anterior urethra may require reconstruction in one sitting.

## Modern Hypospadias Surgery

### W.K. Snodgrass and Tubularized Incised Plate (TIP) Urethroplasty (1994)

Warren K. Snodgrass (1994) introduced the Tubularized Incised Plate (TIP) urethroplasty, deepening the midline urethral plate with a relaxing incision and then tubularizing it over a stent. The procedure builds directly on the Thiersch-Duplay tubularization principle; in the first 16 boys there was no meatal stenosis or fistula at a median of 22 months. TIP became one of the most widely used hypospadias repairs for distal and midshaft variants.<sup>[[47]](#ref47)</sup>

### John Duckett — MAGPI and Preputial Island Flap (1980/1981)

John Duckett (Children's Hospital of Philadelphia) described the MAGPI (Meatal Advancement and Glanuloplasty Incorporated) in 1981 for subcoronal hypospadias, advancing the meatus to the glans tip.<sup>[[48]](#ref48)</sup> In his later series of 1,111 patients, 1.2% needed a second procedure.<sup>[[49]](#ref49)</sup> In 1980, he described the transverse preputial island flap for proximal hypospadias, raising the inner prepuce as a tubularized pedicle flap transposed ventrally, a widely adopted technique for one-stage proximal repair.<sup>[[50]](#ref50)</sup>

## Lichen Sclerosus and Urethral Stricture

Stühmer (1928) described balanitis xerotica obliterans (BXO) after circumcision ("post operationem"), an early recognition of [lichen sclerosus (LS)](/docs/clinical-conditions/03g-genital-scrotal/lichen-sclerosus) affecting the male genitalia.<sup>[[51]](#ref51)</sup> Venn and Mundy (1998) reported that one-stage pedicled penile skin flap urethroplasty failed from recurrent BXO in all 12 patients so treated, against one failure among 16 two-stage free-graft repairs with nongenital skin.<sup>[[52]](#ref52)</sup> The recognition that LS recurs in genital skin led to the use of non-genital tissue, chiefly oral mucosa, for LS strictures in the 2000s and 2010s. A retrospective multicenter LS series of 215 men treated with several procedures reported success (no recurrence or further instrumentation) in 8 of 8 one-stage penile oral-mucosa graft repairs and 11 of 15 staged penile repairs; these small, nonrandomized subgroups do not establish superiority for all penile or panurethral LS strictures.<sup>[[53]](#ref53)</sup>

## The Urolume Era and Lessons Learned (1987–2000s)

The Urolume urethral stent, developed in 1985 and first used clinically in 1987–1988, offered an endoscopic alternative to open reconstruction for recurrent bulbomembranous strictures. In the first report by Milroy et al., all eight patients had a good-calibre urethra at a mean of 8 months. In a later 50-patient series, intrastent narrowing developed in 8 (16%); 27 patients were followed for at least 5 years, 5 for 4 years and 18 for shorter periods.<sup>[[54]](#ref54)[[55]](#ref55)</sup> Longer experience showed tissue ingrowth, stent migration and difficult explantation; a 2026 systematic review included 43 studies (3,847 patients), with a quantitative pool of 2,730 patients yielding 37.9% restenosis or tissue ingrowth and 8.7% explantation.<sup>[[56]](#ref56)</sup> The Urolume experience became a cautionary tale against permanent endoluminal implants for stricture disease, and the device was withdrawn from the market (it was available from 1988 to 2007).<sup>[[56]](#ref56)[[57]](#ref57)</sup>

## The 21st Century: Refinement, Robotics, and Regeneration

The modern era has been characterized by:

- **Expanded indications for EPA.** Morey and Kizer (2006) reported anastomotic repair of proximal bulbar strictures 2.6 to 5.0 cm long (mean 3.78 cm) in 11 men, with success in 10 at a mean of 22 months, and concluded that defects up to 5 cm may be excised and primarily repaired in select young men.<sup>[[58]](#ref58)</sup>
- **Non-transecting (vessel-sparing) EPA.** [Gerald H. Jordan](/docs/roots/surgeons/h-r/gerald-jordan) (Eastern Virginia Medical School, 2007) introduced [vessel-sparing excision and primary anastomosis (VS-EPA)](/docs/surgical-techniques/04a-urethral-reconstruction/jordan-vessel-sparing-epa), preserving the intrinsic vascular supply of the corpus spongiosum by not fully transecting it, potentially reducing erectile dysfunction and penile shortening.<sup>[[59]](#ref59)</sup> Andrich and Mundy (2012) published a preliminary report of [non-transecting anastomotic bulbar urethroplasty](/docs/surgical-techniques/04a-urethral-reconstruction/non-transecting-bulbar) in 22 patients (PMID 21933325); none of the 16 patients with at least 1 year of follow-up had a recurrent stricture.<sup>[[60]](#ref60)</sup>
- **Robotic urethroplasty.** Robotic-assisted proximal perineal urethroplasty was described by Unterberg et al. (2019), and [robotic posterior urethral reconstruction](/docs/surgical-techniques/04a-urethral-reconstruction/robotic-posterior-urethroplasty) has since been reviewed by Boswell et al. (2020) and Vereecken et al. (2025), mostly in single-center series.<sup>[[61]](#ref61)[[62]](#ref62)[[63]](#ref63)</sup>
- **Tissue engineering.** Raya-Rivera, [Anthony Atala](/docs/roots/surgeons/a-g/anthony-atala) (Wake Forest Institute for Regenerative Medicine) and colleagues reported human implantation of engineered urethral constructs seeded with autologous urothelial and smooth muscle cells (2011, *Lancet*) in five boys with urethral defects; the cells were expanded and seeded onto tubularized PGA:PLGA scaffolds.<sup>[[64]](#ref64)</sup>
- **Outcome standardization.** The TURNS (Trauma and Urologic Reconstructive Network of Surgeons) multicenter collaboration has produced prospective multicenter outcome data in urethroplasty.
- **Buccal graft optimization.** Dorsal versus ventral onlay, augmented anastomotic repair, and dorsally quilted grafting described by Barbagli, Kulkarni, [Christopher Chapple](/docs/roots/surgeons/a-g/christopher-chapple) and [Joel Gelman](/docs/roots/surgeons/a-g/joel-gelman).
- **Scar modulation.** Radial urethrotomy with intralesional mitomycin C for recurrent [bladder neck contracture](/docs/clinical-conditions/03b-voiding-outlet/bladder-neck-stenosis) gave patency in 72% of 18 patients after one procedure and 89% after two at a median follow-up of 12 months ([Alex Vanni](/docs/roots/surgeons/s-z/alex-vanni) and colleagues, 2011).<sup>[[65]](#ref65)</sup> The Optilume paclitaxel [drug-coated balloon](/docs/surgical-techniques/drug-coated-balloon) received FDA approval in December 2021 for adult men with symptomatic anterior urethral strictures ≤3 cm.<sup>[[70]](#ref70)</sup> In ROBUST III, [Sean Elliott](/docs/roots/surgeons/a-g/sean-elliott) and colleagues enrolled 127 men with recurrent anterior strictures ≤3 cm after at least two prior endoscopic treatments; six-month anatomical success was 50/67 (75%) with Optilume versus 11/41 (27%) with DVIU or dilation among patients in the primary-endpoint analysis.<sup>[[66]](#ref66)</sup> A tacrolimus-eluting stent has been proposed, and botulinum toxin A injection after urethrotomy has been tested in a pilot randomized trial.<sup>[[67]](#ref67)[[68]](#ref68)</sup>
