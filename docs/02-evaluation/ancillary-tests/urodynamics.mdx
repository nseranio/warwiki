# Urodynamics

**Urodynamics (UDS)** is the dynamic study of the transport, storage, and evacuation of urine through the measurement of relevant physiological parameters. The primary clinical goal of urodynamic evaluation is to reproduce a patient's symptomatic complaints in a laboratory setting while collecting objective physiological data to provide a pathophysiological explanation for those symptoms.

Unlike most diagnostic tests, urodynamics is an interactive study, not a passive measurement. Urodynamics is indicated when the result could change treatment or upper-tract surveillance and the relevant physiology cannot be resolved from history, examination and noninvasive tests alone, including complex lower urinary tract dysfunction or concern for upper-tract risk. Treatment failure or planned surgery alone is insufficient: routine testing can be omitted in clearly demonstrated index SUI, and randomized trials did not show benefit from routine testing in women with refractory OAB or urgency-predominant mixed incontinence, or in men with uncomplicated LUTS considering outlet surgery.<sup>[[5]](#ref5)[[24]](#ref24)[[27]](#ref27)[[28]](#ref28)</sup>

**Core principle.** Symptoms and laboratory observations answer different questions. Low flow may reflect outlet obstruction, reduced detrusor contraction, or an unrepresentative void; pressure-flow testing helps distinguish them. A normal filling study does not exclude clinical OAB and does not establish a neurological cause. Interpret a representative, technically sound study alongside symptoms, examination and anatomy.<sup>[[4]](#ref4)[[6]](#ref6)</sup>

## Normal Reference Values (Quick Lookup)

Urodynamic "normal values" are reference ranges and not diagnostic cutoffs. Age, sex, voided volume, catheter size, position, anxiety, medications, neurologic disease, and the reason for testing all change the numbers. In one healthy-volunteer study, more than 70% of asymptomatic subjects had at least one urodynamic finding that could be labeled "pathologic," so the tracing must still be interpreted against symptoms and anatomy.<sup>[[16]](#ref16)</sup>

|---|---|---|---|
| **Supine** | 5–20 cm H₂O | −5 to +5 cm H₂O | Cough should produce similar synchronous rises in Pves and Pabd, leaving Pdet approximately unchanged.<sup>[[2]](#ref2)</sup> |

## Components of Urodynamic Testing

The ICS standard urodynamic protocol comprises clinical history with symptom scores, physical examination, 3-day voiding diary, uroflowmetry with post-void residual (PVR), and cystometry with pressure-flow study.<sup>[[2]](#ref2)</sup>

### 1. Uroflowmetry (Non-invasive)

A non-invasive measurement of urine flow rate over time during spontaneous voiding.

- **Normal pattern**: Smooth, bell-shaped arc with rapid rise to peak and symmetric descent
- **Plateau or prolonged low flow**: May suggest outlet resistance, but is not diagnostic
- **Intermittent flow**: May reflect abdominal straining, intermittent outlet relaxation, reduced contraction or test inhibition

![Illustrative free-flow curves: Synthetic flow-versus-time graph](/img/diagrams/uroflowmetry.svg)

*A smooth bell-shaped example reaches about 25 mL/s; a lower plateau reaches about 8 mL/s and lasts longer. Both are invented shapes. Low flow is nonspecific and cannot distinguish obstruction from weak detrusor contraction alone. The 15 mL/s line is a reference, not a diagnostic cutoff; voided volume and context matter. (Original WARWIKI schematic; see the figure source record and review limits.)*

**Limitation.** Cannot distinguish between bladder outlet obstruction and detrusor underactivity; both produce low flow. Invasive pressure studies are required for differentiation.

Combined with PVR (by ultrasound or catheterization), uroflowmetry screens for voiding dysfunction and guides the need for further testing.

**Penile cuff test.** A pneumatic cuff interrupts flow in men with LUTS; the maximum cuff-interruption pressure combined with Qmax is read against a nomogram to estimate the probability of obstruction. A 2017 systematic review found that the penile cuff test subgroup (7 studies, 546 men) had median sensitivity 88.9% and specificity 70.2% against pressure-flow studies, with wide ranges and heterogeneous thresholds and obstruction definitions; the review described overall evidence quality as typically moderate. This is an investigational screening adjunct, not a replacement for invasive pressure-flow testing.<sup>[[47]](#ref47)</sup>

### 2. Cystometry

Graphic depiction of bladder and abdominal pressure relative to infused volume during filling, storage, and voiding. The multichannel cystometrogram uses:

- **Pves.** Intravesical pressure (from bladder catheter)
- **Pabd.** Intra-abdominal pressure (from rectal or vaginal catheter)
- **Pdet.** Detrusor pressure (calculated: Pves − Pabd)

**Zeroing and reference**: For external, fluid-filled systems, zero to atmospheric pressure with the transducers at the pubic symphysis. Follow the system-specific setup for other catheter technologies.<sup>[[2]](#ref2)[[4]](#ref4)</sup>

**Normal baseline pressures by position**:

| Position | Pves / Pabd (cm H₂O) | Pdet (cm H₂O) |
|---|---|---|
| Supine | 5–20 | −5 to +5 |
| Sitting | 15–40 | −5 to +5 |
| Standing | 30–50 | −5 to +5 |

**Filling phase assessments:**
- **Sensation**: First Sensation of Filling (FSF), First Desire to Void (FDV), Strong Desire to Void (SDV)
- **Compliance**: ΔVolume ÷ ΔPdet (normal: large volume change with minimal pressure rise)
- **Detrusor overactivity**: Involuntary phasic or terminal contractions during filling
- **Cystometric capacity**: Bladder volume at the end of filling; record why filling stopped. Reconcile infused volume, leakage, diuresis, voided volume and PVR rather than assigning a diagnosis from capacity alone.

**Baseline PVR before filling.** Draining a measured baseline residual before starting cystometry is routine, but it can be withheld by design in a selected patient when the clinical question is whether current emptying leaves the bladder at unsafe storage pressure, such as NLUTD without regular self-catheterization or hydronephrosis possibly related to high bladder pressure. Removing the residual, or filling too fast, can itself change the observed compliance, DO and capacity. When residual is intentionally retained, measure it separately by ultrasound and state what was done; contrast filling may still require partial drainage if residual urine would dilute the contrast.<sup>[[48]](#ref48)</sup>

![Illustrative filling cystometrogram: Synthetic multichannel urodynamic traces](/img/diagrams/cystometrogram.svg)

*Pdet is Pves minus Pabd. The example cough raises vesical and abdominal pressures together; a later phasic detrusor rise is not matched by abdominal pressure. FSF, FDV and SDV mark reported filling sensations. All curves and milestone volumes are invented teaching examples, not patient observations or normal-reference thresholds. Measurement artifacts must be excluded clinically. (Original WARWIKI schematic; see the figure source record and review limits.)*

### 3. Pressure-Flow Study (Voiding Phase)

Simultaneous measurement of detrusor pressure and flow rate during voiding.

**Parameters:**
- **Qmax.** Maximum flow rate; interpret with voided volume and detrusor pressure
- **Pdet at Qmax.** Detrusor pressure at the moment of maximum flow
- **Opening detrusor pressure.** Pdet when flow begins; check pressure–flow synchronization
- **Delay before flow.** Record the time from permission to void to flow onset and whether the patient experienced inhibition

**Confirm a genuine void before classifying outlet resistance.** After terminal detrusor overactivity or leakage, a patient may be voluntarily holding the sphincter closed to prevent further loss; this can produce high-pressure, low-flow conditions in which the sphincter, not a fixed anatomical outlet, is the limiting structure. Wait for the contraction to settle when possible, or give an unambiguous instruction to void and interpret the pressure only afterward. If pressure remains high after a clear, unambiguous void, retain uncertainty about the source of resistance rather than calculating BOOI from a trace obtained during voluntary holding.<sup>[[48]](#ref48)</sup>

**Nomograms for obstruction grading in men:**
- **Abrams-Griffiths nomogram**: Classifies as obstructed, equivocal, or unobstructed based on Pdet@Qmax versus Qmax. In the 1979 report on 117 men over 55, maximum flow alone classified about half, Qmax with Pdet@Qmax about two-thirds, and the remaining third needed a plot of detrusor pressure against flow throughout voiding; combining the two values into a single urethral resistance factor did not help.<sup>[[65]](#ref65)</sup>
- **ICS bladder outlet obstruction index (BOOI)**: BOOI = Pdet@Qmax − 2(Qmax); &gt;40 = obstructed; 20–40 = equivocal; &lt;20 = unobstructed in men. The Schäfer nomogram is a separate obstruction-grading system.<sup>[[21]](#ref21)</sup>
- **Bladder contractility index (BCI)**: BCI = Pdet@Qmax + 5(Qmax); assesses detrusor contractile strength

![Male ICS Abrams-Griffiths pressure-flow nomogram with obstructed, equivocal, and unobstructed zones](/img/diagrams/pressure-flow-nomogram.svg)

*The **male** ICS / Abrams-Griffiths pressure-flow nomogram plots detrusor pressure at maximum flow (Pdet@Qmax) against maximum flow rate (Qmax). BOOI = Pdet@Qmax − 2 × Qmax: **above 40** is obstructed, **20–40 inclusive** is equivocal, and **below 20** is unobstructed. These thresholds are not a female BOO classification, and low pressure with low flow does not by itself establish detrusor underactivity. Dots are hypothetical examples, not study observations. (Original WARWIKI schematic; source: ICS adult male terminology, section 5.12.)*<sup>[[21]](#ref21)</sup>

### 4. Videourodynamics (VUDS)

Combines multichannel pressure-flow studies with simultaneous fluoroscopic imaging of the bladder and urethra.

**Additional information provided:**
- Anatomical localization of bladder outlet obstruction (bladder neck versus sphincter level versus urethra)
- Visualization of vesicoureteral reflux (VUR) during filling
- Morphology of the bladder (trabeculation, diverticula)
- Urethral anatomy during voiding

**Indications for VUDS over standard UDS:**
- Selected neurogenic lower urinary tract dysfunction when simultaneous anatomy, reflux or outlet localization adds information to pressure testing
- Complex posterior urethral pathology
- Evaluation after failed surgery
- Suspected vesicoureteral reflux

**High-grade VUR can mask poor bladder compliance.** When reflux is severe, the refluxing upper tract can act as a second reservoir during filling, so a routine cystometrogram measures the combined bladder-plus-upper-tract system and can under-read true bladder wall stiffness; this matters when the plan depends on the bladder's own storage properties, for example before transplantation or when considering removal of the refluxing unit. In a pediatric cohort of 18 children with high-grade VUR studied with and without ureteral balloon occlusion, mean compliance fell 33% with occlusion (initial and terminal compliance, both p &lt; 0.005) and median cystometric capacity fell 16%. Ureteral occlusion during urodynamics, usually done under anesthesia, is a selected technique for this specific question, not a routine addition to VUDS.<sup>[[49]](#ref49)</sup>

### 5. Urethral Function Tests

**Urethral pressure profile (UPP)** and **Valsalva or cough leak point pressure (VLPP/CLPP)**:
- Assess outlet resistance and sphincteric competence
- Demonstrate considerable test-retest variation and overlap between normal and pathological measurements
- VLPP correlates weakly with incontinence severity and does not reliably predict surgical outcomes
- No reliable cutoff predicts continence surgery failure<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup>
- In 125 women with stress incontinence, the abdominal pressure required to cause leakage was unrelated to maximum urethral pressure, so maximum urethral pressure says little about urethral resistance to abdominal pressure<sup>[[66]](#ref66)</sup>

**Clinical utility**: Limited as standalone tests; most useful in context of full multichannel study.

---

### 6. Electromyography (EMG)

Measures neuromuscular activity of pelvic floor musculature and urethral sphincter during filling and voiding.

**Role**: Support assessment of outlet coordination, including suspected **detrusor-sphincter dyssynergia (DSD)** in an appropriate neurological setting. Surface patches are commonly used but record a broader pelvic-floor signal and are susceptible to movement, straining and contact artifacts. Increased patch activity alone does not prove DSD; correlate it with pressures, flow and, when needed, fluoroscopy.<sup>[[4]](#ref4)[[16]](#ref16)</sup>

Surface (kinesiologic) EMG during voiding and concentric-needle EMG answer different questions. Needle EMG samples motor-unit activity to assess denervation or reinnervation and is a specialist investigation reserved for selected patients with suspected sacral-root, pudendal or other peripheral motor lesions after a neurological history and examination; its predictive value for treatment outcome after an isolated vaginal delivery or in otherwise undefined idiopathic incontinence is not established, so routine use for those indications is not supported.<sup>[[50]](#ref50)</sup>

## Pediatric Urodynamics

Technique, comfort and interpretation each need pediatric-specific adjustment beyond simply scaling adult protocols.

- **Filling rate.** A long-standing pediatric recommendation caps the fill rate at no more than 10% of predicted bladder capacity per minute, a threshold more recent UMPIRE-group publications continue to support. Treat this as the pediatric technical ceiling, not a rate for every patient or study purpose.<sup>[[57]](#ref57)[[58]](#ref58)</sup>
- **Comfort and preparation.** In a study of 139 pediatric urodynamic tests, the median pain score was 2/10 and 41% reported moderate-or-worse pain (score ≥4/10); high pretest anxiety, lower-extremity sensory-motor alteration, and difficulty with catheterization independently predicted higher pain scores.<sup>[[59]](#ref59)</sup> A separate study identified catheter placement as the most anxiety-provoking part of testing. Experienced staff, caregiver presence and distraction (a screen or interactive device) are reasonable measures. When catheter placement is exceptionally difficult, placing the catheter under general anesthesia and performing the study once the child is awake is a selected practical option, not a routine pathway or a proven way to improve test validity.<sup>[[59]](#ref59)[[60]](#ref60)</sup>
- **Repeat filling cycles.** In a study of 80 children with myelodysplasia who underwent three same-session filling cycles, maximum cystometric capacity, compliance and postvoid residual were comparable across runs, and detrusor overactivity was present in all three cycles in 85%. Maximum detrusor pressure and detrusor leak-point pressure were highest on the first fill and fell on later fills. The authors favored interpreting the first, generally more adverse, result to protect the upper tract, while noting that repeated cycles lengthen testing and may increase anxiety; there is no validated rule for selecting or combining cycles.<sup>[[61]](#ref61)</sup>

## The "9 Cs" Framework for UDS Interpretation

A systematic approach to reviewing a urodynamic study:

| C | Parameter | What You're Assessing |
|---|---|---|
| **Contractions** | Detrusor overactivity | Involuntary phasic or terminal detrusor contractions during filling |
| **Compliance** | Bladder wall viscoelasticity | ΔV/ΔPdet; interpret sustained detrusor pressure with capacity and neurological/upper-tract risk |
| **Continence** | Sphincteric competence | Leak with cough/Valsalva in absence of detrusor contraction = SUI |
| **Capacity** | Cystometric capacity | Volume at end of filling and reason for stopping; pain, urgency, leakage and compliance may contribute |
| **Sensation** | Sensory thresholds | FSF, FDV, SDV and their relationship to usual symptoms; sensation reports are not a standalone neurological diagnosis |
| **Contractility** | Detrusor strength | Contraction pressure, duration, flow and emptying; indices alone do not diagnose underactivity |
| **Coordination** | DSD assessment | Interpret EMG with flow, pressure, neurological context and artifacts |
| **Complete Emptying** | Post-void residual | Elevated PVR after voiding = incomplete emptying |
| **Clinical Obstruction** | BOO grading | BOOI, Abrams-Griffiths, Schäfer nomogram |

## Classic Urodynamic Signatures

### Bladder Outlet Obstruction (BOO)

**Hallmark**: High Pdet in the presence of low Qmax.

- BOOI &gt;40 = obstructed; 20–40 = equivocal (men)
- May also see high bladder capacity, detrusor overactivity (secondary to obstruction), reduced compliance
- Etiology may be clarified by examination, endoscopy and/or VUDS: benign prostatic enlargement (most common in older men), urethral stricture, bladder neck contracture, dysfunctional voiding
- BOO in females: less common; urethral stenosis, prior anti-incontinence surgery, or pelvic organ prolapse

---

### Detrusor Overactivity (DO)

Involuntary phasic or terminal detrusor contractions during filling, with or without associated urgency or incontinence.

- **Idiopathic DO**: No identified neurological cause
- **Neurogenic DO (NDO)**: Associated with known neurological condition (suprasacral spinal cord injury, multiple sclerosis, Parkinson's, stroke)

NDO in spinal cord injury is associated with significant upper tract risk when high-amplitude contractions, DSD and reduced compliance coexist.

---

### Low Bladder Compliance

A sustained rise in Pdet relative to added volume, after excluding detrusor contractions, rectal artifacts and technical problems. Reduced compliance may have several causes; the tracing alone does not establish histological fibrosis.

**Where the 40 cm H₂O reference comes from.** McGuire et al. followed 42 patients with myelodysplasia. Of 22 whose intravesical pressure at leakage exceeded 40 cm H₂O, 15 had reflux and 18 had ureteral dilatation; among 20 at or below 40, none had reflux and two had dilatation. This influential association supports attention to pressure-related risk, but it does not validate a universal cutoff for all adults, every transient pressure rise, or isolated Pves without abdominal-pressure context.<sup>[[3]](#ref3)</sup>

A procedure that raises outlet resistance, such as an artificial urinary sphincter or a sling, can unmask previously silent poor compliance once the bladder can no longer decompress by leaking. This is a reason for postoperative upper-tract surveillance in a patient with borderline storage pressures before the added resistance, not evidence for applying the myelodysplasia-derived 40 cm H₂O figure, or any other fixed compliance number, to non-neurogenic patients after incontinence surgery.<sup>[[3]](#ref3)</sup>

---

### Detrusor Underactivity (DUA)

A detrusor contraction of reduced strength or duration, resulting in prolonged voiding, incomplete emptying, or inability to void.

- A low contraction index supports assessment but is not sufficient for diagnosis
- Low-pressure, low-flow voiding may reflect reduced contraction or situational inhibition. BOO and reduced contraction can coexist; do not treat them as mutually exclusive
- Clinically important before offering surgical outlet reduction procedures in men
- **Bladder diverticulum as a pressure sink.** During a detrusor contraction, urine can flow into a low-pressure diverticulum, lowering measured bladder pressure and making contractility appear weaker than it is; an elevated postvoid residual may likewise include urine sequestered in the diverticulum rather than reflecting true underactivity or obstruction. In a comparison of men with large bladder diverticula against men with BPH without a diverticulum, carefully measured contractility was not significantly different between groups. Do not diagnose detrusor underactivity from an apparent low-pressure pattern alone when a diverticulum is present.<sup>[[62]](#ref62)[[63]](#ref63)</sup>

---

### Detrusor-External Sphincter Dyssynergia (DSD)

Involuntary contraction of the external urethral sphincter coinciding with a detrusor contraction during voiding. Characteristic of suprasacral spinal cord lesions.

Consequences:
- Functional outlet obstruction that produces high voiding pressures
- Upper tract risk (hydronephrosis, VUR, renal failure)
- Autonomic dysreflexia in cervical or high thoracic injuries

Assess with pressure-flow findings, sphincter activity and, where useful, fluoroscopic outlet behavior. Exclude surface-EMG artifacts and distinguish neurogenic DSD from non-neurogenic dysfunctional voiding.

The videourodynamic diagnosis of DSD itself has limited reproducibility: in a rating study of fluoroscopic VUDS tracings from patients with known neurogenic bladder, agreement between the two expert neurourologists was 82.1% but kappa was only 0.26, and sensitivity and specificity across rater pairs ranged from 28–75% and 64–93%. Read a positive or negative DSD call with this reliability limit in mind, particularly when it is not corroborated by the clinical picture.<sup>[[51]](#ref51)</sup>

---

### Stress Urinary Incontinence (SUI)

Involuntary leakage of urine during filling cystometry associated with increased abdominal pressure (cough, Valsalva, heel drop) in the absence of a detrusor contraction.

On the tracing: Pves and Pabd rise simultaneously, Pdet is unchanged, and leakage occurs via the urethra.

Document the provocation and whether a detrusor contraction accompanies leakage. UDS can help when the mechanism is uncertain, but is not routinely required when uncomplicated SUI is clearly demonstrated clinically.

---

### Fowler's Syndrome

A syndrome of otherwise unexplained, often painless urinary retention in women, associated with abnormal urethral sphincter needle-EMG findings. Fowler's original studies described complex repetitive discharges and decelerating bursts. Specialist evaluation must exclude anatomical, medication-related and neurological causes. EMG findings require clinical correlation, and sacral neuromodulation is an option for selected refractory functional retention, and response is not guaranteed.<sup>[[30]](#ref30)[[31]](#ref31)</sup>

These same discharge patterns are not specific to unexplained retention. In one series of women investigated for unexplained retention, both patterns were found in 33 of 57 (58%)<sup>[[30]](#ref30)</sup>; in a separate study of healthy, asymptomatic volunteers restricted to the early-follicular or luteal phase, the same activity was present in 53% (8 of 15) at one or both phases of the menstrual cycle.<sup>[[52]](#ref52)</sup> This overlap with an asymptomatic population reinforces that an abnormal needle-EMG finding needs clinical correlation and cannot establish the diagnosis on its own.<sup>[[30]](#ref30)[[52]](#ref52)</sup>

## Technical Standards and Quality Control

Per ICS Good Urodynamic Practices 2016–2018:<sup>[[2]](#ref2)[[4]](#ref4)</sup>

- Liquid-filled catheters connected to pressure transducers positioned at the level of the pubic symphysis with atmospheric pressure as zero reference
- Quality of recording must be monitored throughout the study, not just reviewed at the end
- Provocations (cough tests, positional changes, running water) should reproduce the patient's specific symptoms
- Artifacts must be identified and not allowed to confound interpretation: rectal contractions (false Pdet drops), catheter movement artifacts ("knock" artifacts), signal dampening
- Equipment must be calibrated per manufacturer specifications

**Local interpretation is not automatically reliable.** In an ICS-standards audit of a random sample of traces from two large UK multicenter studies of male LUTS, 28% of participating departments reported no calibration checks, reference zero was not set to atmospheric pressure in 27% of reviewed urodynamic traces, and bladder outlet obstruction was misdiagnosed locally in 6 of 110 reviewed studies (5.5%), an error that could lead to unnecessary surgery. This is a reason to review the trace directly rather than accept a site report at face value; it does not estimate error rates for every laboratory.<sup>[[53]](#ref53)</sup>

**Troubleshooting an unequal cough test, a flat or drifting channel, or a suspiciously nonphysiologic spike:** verify zero and transducer height; check taps, connections, leaks, kinks or external compression; flush fluid-filled lines and reassess catheter position. If transmission is still poor with an empty bladder at the start of the study, instilling a small volume (about 50 mL) and repeating the cough test can help before assuming catheter malfunction. Nearly identical Pves and Pabd traces should prompt confirmation that the two catheters truly sit in separate compartments. A pressure spike coinciding with a line flush or an air-charge cycle is a recording artifact, not a patient maximum, and should be marked and excluded rather than used to select Qmax or Pdet.<sup>[[54]](#ref54)</sup>

**Reporting:** Identify flow onset, Qmax and its simultaneous Pdet on synchronized traces. The 2023 ICS pressure-flow standards refine analysis and reporting of outlet resistance, contraction and voiding efficiency; arbitrary A–D letters are not a universal tracing convention.<sup>[[22]](#ref22)[[23]](#ref23)</sup>

**IUGA reporting proforma.** The IUGA writing group proposes a standardized report for female urodynamics that states the clinical question the study should answer and correlates it with the symptoms, examination and a bladder diary (ideally at least 3 days). The report records technique: patient position, fluid type, temperature and filling rate, catheter type and size, site of abdominal pressure measurement, whether the bladder was empty at the start, and how PVR was measured. It grades the quality of zeroing and subtraction as perfect, suboptimal for part of the test, or suboptimal throughout, and documents artifacts. Provocative maneuvers are recorded with the volume and position, and with prolapse reduced and unreduced when prolapse is present. The interpretation states whether the symptoms were reproduced, whether the question was answered, whether a urodynamic diagnosis was made using IUGA/ICS standardized diagnoses, and whether the result changed management. This is a proposed proforma based on working-group consensus.<sup>[[36]](#ref36)</sup>

## Clinical Indications and Guidelines

### When UDS Should NOT Be Routinely Performed

**Uncomplicated stress urinary incontinence (SUI) in women**:

The VALUE trial (630 women) found office evaluation non-inferior to office evaluation plus UDS for one-year surgical outcomes in uncomplicated, demonstrable SUI. AUA/SUFU allows omission in the index patient, which can include stress-predominant mixed incontinence when SUI is clearly demonstrated; mixed symptoms alone do not invariably mandate testing.<sup>[[5]](#ref5)[[24]](#ref24)</sup>

**OAB initial evaluation.** AUA/SUFU advises against routine UDS initially. Testing can be useful when diagnostic uncertainty or inadequate response raises a question that will change management; a normal tracing does not rule out OAB.<sup>[[6]](#ref6)</sup>

**Low-risk NLUTD initial evaluation.** Routine UDS is not advised after appropriate risk classification (Statement 5, Moderate Recommendation, Grade C). Patients who are not classified as low risk after history, examination, PVR and urinalysis are unknown-risk and need multichannel UDS plus upper-tract assessment (Statement 6). The guideline notes that SCI, spina bifida, transverse myelitis and MS (in men) can present with widely variable findings, so the diagnosis alone does not settle risk.<sup>[[7]](#ref7)</sup>

### When UDS Can Add Value

| Scenario | Decision to resolve |
|---|---|
| **Unclear incontinence type, significant emptying symptoms, recurrent SUI, or complex prolapse** | Whether findings would change treatment selection or counseling; consider the individual phenotype and planned intervention.<sup>[[8]](#ref8)[[24]](#ref24)[[25]](#ref25)</sup> |
| **Selected men considering outlet surgery** | Is obstruction present, is contraction reduced, and was non-invasive assessment sufficient? Routine testing of every man is not supported.<sup>[[27]](#ref27)</sup> |
| **Unknown-risk NLUTD** | Establish storage/voiding risk alongside renal function and upper-tract assessment.<sup>[[7]](#ref7)</sup> |
| **Established moderate/high-risk NLUTD** | Repeat testing for relevant clinical change or to assess treatment and risk; timing depends on the risk profile.<sup>[[7]](#ref7)</sup> |
| **Refractory symptoms or major reconstructive decisions** | Resolve diagnostic uncertainty, quantify unsafe storage or evaluate emptying when the result can change an invasive treatment plan.<sup>[[6]](#ref6)[[25]](#ref25)</sup> |

## Evidence on UDS and Outcomes

### High-Impact Randomized Evidence

| Study | Population and finding | Clinical meaning |
|---|---|---|
| **VALUE (2012)** | 630 women with uncomplicated demonstrable SUI. At 12 months, composite success (at least a 70% reduction in Urogenital Distress Inventory score and a “much better” or “very much better” Patient Global Impression of Improvement response) was 76.9% with UDS vs 77.2% with office evaluation alone; the trial met its non-inferiority criterion.<sup>[[5]](#ref5)</sup> | Routine preoperative UDS can be omitted in this selected setting. |
| **FUTURE (2025)** | 1,099 women with refractory OAB or urgency-predominant mixed incontinence being considered for invasive treatment. At final follow-up (15 or 24 months after randomization), patient-reported success among participants with outcome data was 117/496 (23.6%) after UDS plus comprehensive clinical assessment vs 114/503 (22.7%) after clinical assessment alone; adjusted OR 1.12 (95% CI 0.73–1.74).<sup>[[28]](#ref28)</sup> | The superiority trial did **not demonstrate added benefit** from routine UDS. This is not proof of equivalence or a reason to omit testing for a separate neurogenic, obstructive or diagnostic question. |
| **UPSTREAM five-year follow-up (2025)** | From 820 randomized men with bothersome LUTS considering further treatment, IPSS difference was 0.41 points (95% CI −1.10 to 1.93), with no detected difference in surgery (OR 0.96, 95% CI 0.71–1.28). Questionnaires were returned by only about half of each arm; routine data were available for 801/820.<sup>[[27]](#ref27)[[29]](#ref29)</sup> | The results do not support adding UDS routinely for every man. **Five-year non-inferiority was not established** because of uncertainty around the symptom estimate; selective testing remains appropriate. |

The earlier 2020 meta-analysis of seven female-incontinence RCTs (1,149 participants) and the 2013 Cochrane review also found no clear improvement in continence outcomes from routine UDS. These reviews overlap in their older trial evidence. Changes in management or physician confidence do not by themselves establish patient benefit.<sup>[[9]](#ref9)[[10]](#ref10)</sup>

### Impact on Physician Confidence

Despite limited outcome improvement in uncomplicated cases, UDS influences clinical thinking. In a prospective tertiary referral study (n=102), UDS resulted in a management change in 78% of patients, with mean physician confidence increasing from 2.9 to 4.1 (on a 0–5 scale).<sup>[[11]](#ref11)</sup>

### Predictive Value

Preoperative DO or voiding abnormalities can inform counseling, but their predictive performance is inconsistent. An association with postoperative symptoms does not demonstrate that routinely testing and changing treatment improves outcomes.<sup>[[8]](#ref8)[[25]](#ref25)</sup>

In a secondary analysis of the SISTEr trial (655 women randomized to Burch colposuspension or pubovaginal sling), 57 developed voiding dysfunction, defined as catheter use beyond 6 weeks or surgical takedown; all 19 takedowns followed a sling. No preoperative urodynamic parameter (PVR, free or pressure-flow Qmax, or vesical, abdominal or detrusor pressure at Qmax) was associated with voiding dysfunction. The trial excluded women with PVR above 150 mL or free Qmax below 12 mL/s unless advanced prolapse was present, and the authors noted that these exclusions may limit the finding.<sup>[[67]](#ref67)</sup>

## Complications and Tolerability

Catheterization can cause discomfort, dysuria, bacteriuria, symptomatic infection or retention. Incidence depends on the population and outcome definition. In the older 119-patient study, the 4.8% retention figure applied to the 63 men, not all participants; all affected men had significant BOO. The 154-patient questionnaire study found average pain 2.27/10 and embarrassment 2.59/10, with most willing to repeat testing.<sup>[[12]](#ref12)[[13]](#ref13)</sup>

**Infection prevention:** Screen for suspected symptomatic UTI and defer elective testing when present. The 2012 Cochrane review (nine trials, 973 participants) found reduced bacteriuria but uncertain symptomatic-UTI benefit (RR 0.73, 95% CI 0.52–1.03). EAU recommends no antibiotic prophylaxis for UDS; the SUFU 2017 policy instead recommends prophylaxis for defined risk groups, including known relevant NLUTD, elevated PVR, immunosuppression, age &gt;70, asymptomatic bacteriuria, an indwelling catheter, an external urinary collection device, or intermittent catheterization.<sup>[[32]](#ref32)[[33]](#ref33)</sup> A history of recurrent UTI alone was not an indication under that SUFU policy. The AUGS 2025 consensus statement (Q7), which draws on the SUFU policy rather than independent trial data, likewise advises prophylaxis for women with asymptomatic bacteriuria and neurogenic bladder dysfunction undergoing UDS, whatever the cause of the neurogenic dysfunction; outside urodynamic testing, ASB in neurogenic bladder does not need antibiotics. The AUA best practice statement (2020) advises no prophylaxis for urodynamics in healthy adults without infection or a urologic infection risk. It advises a single dose (trimethoprim-sulfamethoxazole is named; first- or second-generation cephalosporin, amoxicillin-clavulanate or an aminoglycoside are alternatives) for NLUTD, immunosuppression, a known or suspected urinary tract abnormality with recent instrumentation, or recent antimicrobial use.<sup>[[37]](#ref37)</sup> Apply the relevant local protocol with these guideline differences in view.<sup>[[14]](#ref14)[[32]](#ref32)[[33]](#ref33)[[35]](#ref35)</sup>

**Autonomic dysreflexia:** Monitor hemodynamics in susceptible NLUTD patients during UDS. If dysreflexia occurs, stop the study, drain the bladder and continue monitoring; persistent dysreflexia requires pharmacological management and/or escalation of care.<sup>[[7]](#ref7)</sup>

**Catheterization when a stricture or prior urethroplasty is known or suspected.** Review the prior anatomy and repair before passing a UDS catheter. Use a small-caliber catheter with generous lubricant, advanced gently; do not force lubricant or the catheter against resistance. For a near-obliterative lesion, pass the filling line under endoscopic or fluoroscopic vision, over a guidewire if needed. If passage fails or meets substantial resistance, stop and characterize the stricture with retrograde or voiding urethrography before reattempting access. Forceful instrumentation after urethroplasty risks bleeding, a false passage or graft injury, with downstream risk of recurrence or infection.<sup>[[55]](#ref55)</sup>

**Reading an unexpected PVR, and a caution specific to the artificial urinary sphincter.** Measure PVR promptly after voiding, since rapid post-hydration diuresis or return of refluxed urine can raise the apparent residual after an otherwise complete void, and confirm an unexpected finding with imaging that identifies the anatomy rather than treating the number in isolation. In a patient with an AUS, identify the device's fluid reservoir on imaging before considering needle aspiration of a presumed pelvic fluid collection; needle aspiration of an AUS reservoir mistaken for a cyst has been reported to render the device nonfunctional.<sup>[[56]](#ref56)</sup>

## Ambulatory Urodynamic Monitoring (AUM)

**AUM** uses physiological anterograde filling (natural diuresis) and longer observation periods compared to traditional retrograde-fill laboratory UDS.<sup>[[15]](#ref15)</sup>

**Indications**: Unexplained urinary symptoms with non-diagnostic standard UDS; suspected DO not captured in brief laboratory study.

**Limitations**: Retrograde calibration more difficult; longer duration; requires patient cooperation.

**Telemetric AUM (TAUM)**: Wireless systems differ in the signals they record. The FDA-cleared Glean system records bladder pressure during natural filling; its April 2026 abdominal-sensor addition permits multichannel pressure recording and calculation of detrusor pressure. The FDA-cleared indication is for adults in a clinic; uroflow volume is not continuous intravesical volume measurement.<sup>[[34]](#ref34)</sup> Clearance and small feasibility studies do not establish superior clinical outcomes or replacement of every conventional UDS component. See the [Glean device hub](/docs/foundations/tools/biomaterials/adjunct-specialty/glean-urodynamics) for current hardware and evidence limitations.<sup>[[34]](#ref34)</sup>

## Summary of Guideline Recommendations

| Setting | Practical recommendation |
|---|---|
| **Clearly demonstrated index SUI** | Routine preoperative UDS may be omitted; assess non-index features individually.<sup>[[24]](#ref24)</sup> |
| **Initial uncomplicated OAB** | Do not perform routine UDS.<sup>[[6]](#ref6)</sup> |
| **Refractory OAB** | FUTURE does not support testing everyone before invasive treatment; retain testing for a specific decision or diagnostic uncertainty.<sup>[[6]](#ref6)[[28]](#ref28)</sup> |
| **Male LUTS** | Use selectively when the result can change management, rather than routinely before all outlet surgery. EAU 2026 weakly recommends urodynamics before invasive treatment only for specific indications: prior unsuccessful invasive treatment, inability to void more than 150 mL, bothersome predominantly voiding LUTS with Qmax above 10 mL/s, and predominantly voiding LUTS at age over 80 or under 50. With Qmax below 10 mL/s EAU considers obstruction likely and urodynamics not necessarily needed, though the cutoff is a weak discriminator on its own: among men with paired uroflowmetry and pressure-flow data in the ICS-'BPH' study (1,271 recruited), a free-flow Qmax below 10 mL/s had 70% specificity, a 70% positive predictive value and only 47% sensitivity for a pressure-flow diagnosis of bladder outlet obstruction. In exploratory UPSTREAM analyses cited by EAU, surgery helped more in men with IPSS above 16, age under 74, Qmax below 10 mL/s, BOOI above 47.6 and BCI above 123.<sup>[[26]](#ref26)[[27]](#ref27)[[45]](#ref45)</sup> |
| **NLUTD** | Initial unknown-risk assessment includes multichannel UDS; low-risk patients do not need routine initial UDS. Subsequent testing depends on risk and clinical change.<sup>[[7]](#ref7)</sup> |
| **Technical performance** | Apply ICS good-practice recommendations and the 2023 pressure-flow standards.<sup>[[4]](#ref4)[[22]](#ref22)[[23]](#ref23)</sup> |
