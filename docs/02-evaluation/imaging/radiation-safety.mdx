# Radiation Safety in Reconstructive Urology

Reconstructive urologists use fluoroscopy routinely: RUG and VCUG, antegrade and retrograde pyelography, stent placement, fluoroscopic urethroplasty workup, and the endourologic adjuncts that accompany complex reconstruction (PCNL, antegrade ureteroscopy). Exposure depends on equipment, workload, positioning and protection; the principles below (ALARA, time–distance–shielding, and equipment optimization) apply across every fluoroscopic moment in the operating room. For patients who could be pregnant, follow the local pregnancy-screening policy before a planned potentially high-dose fluoroscopic procedure.<sup>[[26]](#ref26)</sup>

For the patient-side consequences of therapeutic pelvic radiation (radiation cystitis, urethral stricture in the irradiated bed, fistula in the irradiated field, AUS durability after RT, HBOT for radiation injury), see [Radiation & Tissue Effects](/docs/foundations/surgical-principles/radiation-tissue-effects).

## Occupational Dose Limits and Local Policy

Separate radiation-protection recommendations from the law that applies to a particular workplace. ICRP/IAEA guidance and US NRC rules do not use identical limits. US states regulate the use of diagnostic X-ray equipment; the NRC table below describes its rules for licensed activities and is a comparison and not a substitute for the local radiation-safety officer's requirements.<sup>[[15]](#ref15)[[16]](#ref16)[[18]](#ref18)</sup>

| Quantity | ICRP/IAEA adult occupational guidance | US NRC rules for licensed activities |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Effective dose / total effective dose equivalent | 20 mSv/year averaged over 5 years; no more than 50 mSv in any year | 50 mSv/year total effective dose equivalent |
| Lens of the eye | 20 mSv/year averaged over 5 years; no more than 50 mSv in any year | 150 mSv/year lens dose equivalent |
| Skin and extremities | 500 mSv/year | 500 mSv/year shallow-dose equivalent |
| Embryo/fetus after pregnancy notification | Working conditions/monitoring should limit additional fetal exposure to about 1 mSv | 5 mSv over the entire pregnancy of a declared pregnant worker; avoid substantial variation above a uniform monthly rate |

These are exposure limits and not working targets or patient diagnostic-dose limits. Apply ALARA and local investigation levels well below the ceiling. NCRP recommendations, including its lower lens recommendation, are distinct from NRC regulations.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[3]](#ref3)[[15]](#ref15)[[16]](#ref16)</sup>

## Typical Exposure During Urologic Procedures

The FLASH retrospective UK study (3,651 patients, 12 hospitals) proposed reference levels from the 75th percentile of hospital median values. These are audit benchmarks and not maximum safe doses, average patient doses or staff-dose measurements:<sup>[[5]](#ref5)</sup>

| Procedure | DAP (Gy·cm²) | Fluoroscopy time |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Ureteric stent insertion | 2.3 | 49 s |
| Ureteroscopy | 2.8 | 57 s |
| PCNL | 24.1 | 431 s |

High-volume PCNL centers (more than 50 cases per year) reported substantially lower DAP than low-volume centers (4.2 versus 15.0 Gy·cm², p < 0.001). This is an observational association that may reflect technique, case mix and equipment as well as experience.<sup>[[5]](#ref5)</sup> In a three-hospital series of 243 urethrograms, outside-apron chest dose averaged 80 μGy at the 210-examination site, 60 μGy at the 18-examination site and 790 μGy at the 15-examination site (overall range 4–1,750 μGy). The paper's 310 μGy 'overall mean' averages the three site means without weighting by examination count.<sup>[[6]](#ref6)</sup> Report air-kerma or absorbed-dose values separately from personal effective dose in μSv and collar-badge limits.<sup>[[6]](#ref6)</sup> A 1996 study reported rates as high as 1,100 mrem per fluoroscopy hour under its measured conditions; this is a historical observation and not an estimate for a modern protected operator.<sup>[[7]](#ref7)</sup> A 2024 systematic review of 21 studies found the highest operator exposure with PCNL, particularly prone PCNL, but substantial variation between studies. Actual risk must be assessed from local dosimetry.<sup>[[8]](#ref8)</sup>

## Dose-Reduction Strategy — Time, Distance, Shielding, Optimization

### Shielding

- Wear a properly fitted lead-equivalent apron and thyroid collar, selected and inspected through the local radiation-protection program. Attenuation depends on material, thickness, photon energy, fit and gaps; a single percentage is not guaranteed.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[9]](#ref9)</sup>
- Use leaded eyewear with side protection and a correctly positioned ceiling-mounted shield where available. In the six-center IAEA-SEEGUR study, eyewear was used at only half of centers; the highest estimated annual eye dose was 13.5 mSv, in the busiest department using an over-table tube without a ceiling screen. The six-center IAEA-SEEGUR study identifies a preventable exposure pattern in one high-volume department.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[9]](#ref9)[[10]](#ref10)</sup>
- Use table-mounted or under-table shielding to intercept scatter without entering the primary beam or compromising the sterile field. In a simulated lithotomy study, the *combined package* of pulse, distance, collimation and shielding changes reduced mannequin trunk, genital and leg doses by 95%, 99% and 97%.<sup>[[11]](#ref11)</sup> Those effects cannot be attributed to one drape alone; one shield configuration also increased patient skin dose.<sup>[[9]](#ref9)[[11]](#ref11)</sup>
- Routine gonadal or pelvic shielding of the *patient* is no longer recommended for abdominal and pelvic fluoroscopy or radiography, including in pregnancy; US radiation-protection bodies now advise discontinuing it. A shield placed over the pelvis can sit inside the field that automatic exposure control is reading, and a cadaver study under automated settings found that shielding the uterus lowered its own dose but raised the dose to the adjacent unshielded ureter and ipsilateral kidney by about twofold as the system compensated. Confirm current local policy; this is a shift from a decades-old default.<sup>[[22]](#ref22)[[23]](#ref23)</sup>

### Distance and positioning

- Increase distance from the irradiated patient when practical. Inverse-square falloff is a useful approximation, but the patient is an extended scatter source and room geometry changes the actual reduction. For a lateral beam, exposure is generally lower on the detector side; an under-table tube is usually preferable when the procedure permits. Keep hands out of the primary beam.<sup>[[2]](#ref2)[[10]](#ref10)</sup>
- A phantom or mannequin lithotomy experiment found a median 17% higher operator dose while sitting and a 78% higher genital dose. The simulation supports checking shielding and posture for seated operating in the actual room.<sup>[[11]](#ref11)</sup>

### Equipment settings

- A prospective before-and-after study of 44 versus 50 patients changed defaults to 12 pulses/second and half-dose. PCNL entrance skin dose fell 33% (p < 0.001); the smaller URS and retrograde-study reductions were not statistically significant. The before-and-after study supports testing lower-dose defaults while assessing diagnostic quality locally; it does not establish a universal frame rate.<sup>[[12]](#ref12)</sup>
- Use low-dose or pulsed modes, collimation and the lowest image rate that answers the question, with a radiographer- or physicist-approved protocol. A specific OEC 9900 cadaver or mannequin study found reduced scatter with AEC off and manual settings; that experiment does not justify routinely disabling AEC on every machine. Preserve diagnostic image quality and use equipment-specific training.<sup>[[2]](#ref2)[[13]](#ref13)</sup>
- Use last-image hold to review without continuing exposure; use the stored-fluoro loop rather than additional spot films when documentation is the only goal.<sup>[[2]](#ref2)</sup>
- A newer flat panel detector is not automatically lower-dose than an older image intensifier. In a cadaver ureteroscopy simulation, the flat panel unit's lowest available pulse rate was 4 pulses/second versus 1 pulse/second on the image intensifier, and at each system's own lowest setting the flat panel delivered significantly more radiation, with no difference in image quality; confirm actual dose output on the specific machine rather than assuming a newer detector is inherently safer.<sup>[[19]](#ref19)</sup>
- Remove unnecessary instruments, cords and wires from the primary beam. With automatic brightness control active, a cadaver ureteroscopy simulation found radiation exposure rose above a no-equipment control (18.5 mGy) with a straight camera/light cord (19.3 mGy), a Kelly clamp (19.4 mGy), a coiled cord (20.2 mGy), a flexible ureteroscope (21.0 mGy) and a rigid cystoscope (21.2 mGy) left in the field, because AEC increases output to compensate for the added attenuation.<sup>[[20]](#ref20)</sup>

### Monitoring and education

- Use personal dosimetry as directed by the radiation-safety program. An outside-apron collar badge estimates unshielded exposure; an under-apron badge may be needed to estimate effective dose. Add eye or extremity monitoring when the exposure pattern warrants it. Read and act on results; a collar measurement is not the same as whole-body effective dose.<sup>[[10]](#ref10)</sup>
- Urology studies report gaps in training, monitoring and protective-equipment use. Education and protocol changes can reduce exposure, but an uncontrolled before-and-after improvement does not isolate the effect of education alone.<sup>[[8]](#ref8)[[14]](#ref14)</sup> Among 272 UK respondents in a multicenter survey of urology theater staff, lead apron use was near-universal (99.3%), but thyroid shield use was 52.2% and lead glasses use was 7.4%; lack of availability was cited by 208/272 (76.5%) respondents as a reason for noncompliance.<sup>[[21]](#ref21)</sup> Eyewear and thyroid shielding, not the apron, are the gaps most worth auditing locally.<sup>[[21]](#ref21)</sup>

## Procedure-Specific Pearls

| Procedure | Dose-reduction pearl |
| --- | --- |
| **RUG / VCUG** | Use the dynamic loop for the brief filling/voiding phase only; capture stills off the loop rather than continuous fluoro. Optimize locally; the cited urethrography range is reported in μGy and must not be treated as an effective-dose range.<sup>[[6]](#ref6)</sup> |
| **Ureteroscopy / stent insertion** | Confirm wire position with single short pulses, not continuous fluoro. FLASH reference DAP 2.3–2.8 Gy·cm².<sup>[[5]](#ref5)</sup> |
| **PCNL** | Often the highest-dose endourological procedure. Use a validated low-dose/pulse protocol, favorable tube geometry and appropriate eye/table shielding. In selected stone cases with experienced teams, ultrasound-guided PCNL can avoid intraoperative fluoroscopic radiation; a meta-analysis of 11 randomized PCNL studies (1,370 patients) reported similar stone-free odds and lower overall complication odds than fluoroscopy-guided PCNL.<sup>[[28]](#ref28)[[29]](#ref29)</sup> Keep fluoroscopy available for conversion when needed.<sup>[[29]](#ref29)</sup> Audit DAP and operator dose separately.<sup>[[5]](#ref5)[[12]](#ref12)</sup> |
| **Urethroplasty workup / fluoroscopic dilation** | Short bursts, last-image hold and collimation. Check operator posture and shield coverage; the reported sitting effect comes from a simulation.<sup>[[11]](#ref11)</sup> |
| **Pregnant team member** | Arrange confidential counseling and a dose/working-condition review with the radiation-safety officer; use an under-apron abdominal badge when indicated. A 2025 cadaver PCNL model found 96% lower uterine dose at 1 versus 30 pulses/second and 56% lower dose with low-dose mode; these are equipment- and model-specific estimates, not a safe caseload allowance.<sup>[[27]](#ref27)</sup> No universal double-apron requirement or automatic PCNL reassignment follows from the cited guidance. Adjust duties if needed to meet local limits and the worker's circumstances.<sup>[[4]](#ref4)[[16]](#ref16)[[17]](#ref17)</sup> |
| **Pregnant patient needing a fluoroscopic procedure** (e.g., stent placement, retrograde pyelography) | Susceptibility to high-dose fetal tissue effects is greater during organogenesis (about 2–7 weeks post-conception) and the early fetal period (about 8–15 weeks), but risk also depends on absorbed dose.<sup>[[24]](#ref24)</sup> Do not defer clinically necessary imaging solely because of pregnancy. When fluoroscopy is indicated, consult a qualified medical physicist for dose planning and fetal-dose estimation; minimize beam time, collimate tightly, and estimate and document the dose from the procedure.<sup>[[25]](#ref25)[[26]](#ref26)</sup> |

## Key Takeaways

- **Optimize patient and staff protection together.** DAP, air kerma, lens dose and effective dose measure different things.
- **Use trained, equipment-specific technique:** brief exposures, appropriate pulse rate, collimation, favorable geometry and last-image hold.
- **Fit, position and inspect shielding**, and keep hands out of the primary beam.
- **Monitor actual exposure.** Investigate unexpected results instead of assuming published percentages describe the local room. If a fluoroscopic case exceeds the facility's substantial radiation dose level, follow its policy for patient information and documented follow-up for delayed tissue reactions.<sup>[[26]](#ref26)</sup>
- **Plan pregnancy protection individually**, using the applicable rules, measured exposure and the worker's preferences.
