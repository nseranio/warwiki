# Cystography

Retrograde cystography evaluates bladder integrity; voiding cystourethrography (VCUG) adds urethral and reflux assessment. CT and conventional cystography are both suitable for suspected bladder rupture when the bladder is adequately filled. EAU summarizes their diagnostic performance as comparable: sensitivity approximately 90–95%, specificity 100%. These estimates do not guarantee exclusion of every leak, particularly in a reconstructed reservoir.<sup>[[23]](#ref23)</sup>

See also: [RUG & VCUG](/docs/evaluation/imaging/rug-vcug) for urethral imaging and [CT Urogram](/docs/evaluation/imaging/ct-urogram) for upper-tract assessment.

## Choosing the study

| Study | Main question | Practical distinction |
|---|---|---|
| **CT cystography** | Is the bladder leaking, and where does contrast travel? | Convenient during trauma CT; provides cross-sectional detail of the pelvis and associated injuries |
| **Conventional cystography** | Is the bladder leaking during retrograde filling or after drainage? | Useful for initial assessment or follow-up; allows real-time observation and targeted projections |
| **Fluoroscopic VCUG** | Is there reflux, a bladder/urethral abnormality, or an abnormal voiding outlet? | Requires filling and voiding images; remains a reference examination for reflux grading and urethral anatomy |
| **Radionuclide cystography** | Does known reflux persist? | Sensitive surveillance option with limited anatomic detail; radiation depends on the protocol and comparator |
| **Contrast-enhanced voiding urosonography (ceVUS)** | Is there vesicoureteral reflux? | Radiation-free ultrasound alternative where expertise is available; select the study according to whether detailed urethral evaluation is also needed |

CT is not automatically superior for every indication. A low-dose conventional examination may answer a follow-up leak question without repeating a pelvic CT. Neither a routine contrast-enhanced abdominal CT nor its excretory phase substitutes for a properly performed retrograde cystogram.<sup>[[2]](#ref2)[[4]](#ref4)</sup>

VCUG images the bladder during filling and emptying, the urethra during voiding, and refluxing upper tracts when present. Reflux grade is only one factor in management; age, febrile infections, renal findings and bladder and bowel dysfunction also matter.<sup>[[7]](#ref7)[[8]](#ref8)</sup>

ceVUS is an established option. Lumason has a US intravesical indication for evaluating suspected or known VUR in children. It still requires catheterization, label-specified contrast preparation, an experienced operator and the local pediatric imaging protocol. Lumason is contraindicated with hypersensitivity to sulfur hexafluoride lipid microspheres or components including polyethylene glycol; trained personnel and resuscitation equipment must be readily available.<sup>[[25]](#ref25)</sup>

## Contrast and filling technique

### Contrast agents

Use a water-soluble agent and dilution appropriate to the examination. Examples of products labeled for retrograde cystography include:

- **Cystografin** contains diatrizoate meglumine 30%; a separate Cystografin Dilute 18% product is available.<sup>[[9]](#ref9)[[10]](#ref10)</sup> These are retrograde preparations, not intravenous products. Cystografin is contraindicated with hypersensitivity to diatrizoate salts, and its label calls for caution during active urinary infection.<sup>[[9]](#ref9)[[10]](#ref10)</sup>
- **Cysto-Conray II:** iothalamate meglumine 17.2%, with administration volume adapted to age and bladder capacity.<sup>[[11]](#ref11)</sup>

Product concentration, instilled volume and CT dilution are different quantities. Follow the radiology protocol rather than applying a manufacturer's maximum volume to every patient. Use sterile catheterization and avoid excessive instillation pressure.<sup>[[9]](#ref9)[[11]](#ref11)</sup>

### Adult trauma examination

1. Establish safe bladder access; investigate suspected urethral injury before instrumentation when indicated.
2. Instill dilute contrast retrograde by gravity. EAU specifies retrograde filling with a minimum of 300–350 mL of dilute contrast. The ACS 2025 guideline likewise sets a minimum of 300 mL, or until the patient reaches discomfort or contrast stops flowing, and specifies dilute contrast of about 2–5 g iodine/100 mL for CT cystography (for example, 50 mL iohexol in 500 mL saline) against about 8–25 g iodine/100 mL for conventional cystography. AUA describes gravity filling to at least 300 mL or patient tolerance (Statement 14a discussion). Record the actual volume and any limitation; an underfilled examination may miss a tear.<sup>[[23]](#ref23)[[1]](#ref1)[[13]](#ref13)</sup>
3. CT cystography obtains pelvic images with the bladder distended. Additional intravenous contrast is unnecessary for the cystographic component, although other trauma questions may require it.
4. Conventional cystography includes adequately filled anteroposterior and bilateral oblique views and a post-drainage image, with a lateral view when concern persists without a visible leak.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup> Drainage can reveal leakage obscured by the contrast-filled bladder. Routine post-drainage CT is generally unnecessary.<sup>[[5]](#ref5)</sup>

An adult trauma volume is not forced into a child, a painful low-capacity bladder, a fresh repair or an augmented reservoir. Agree on a tailored filling endpoint with the treating team. Clamping a catheter to collect renally excreted contrast does not provide a reliable trauma cystogram.<sup>[[4]](#ref4)[[5]](#ref5)[[13]](#ref13)</sup>

### Children

Use a pediatric protocol with low-dose pulsed fluoroscopy or an appropriate ultrasound technique. Age formulas such as (age + 2) × 30 mL are estimates, not targets that must be reached. Bael and colleagues' analysis of 386 children with grade III–IV VUR found a nonlinear age-capacity relationship and wide reference ranges; a single linear formula cannot define normal capacity for an individual child.<sup>[[12]](#ref12)</sup> Cyclic filling can improve reflux detection in selected infants; avoid overdistension.<sup>[[6]](#ref6)</sup>

## Trauma indications and interpretation

In a stable patient, visible hematuria with pelvic fracture requires retrograde cystography (AUA Statement 14a; Standard, Grade B). Selected patients with microscopic hematuria and high-risk fracture anatomy, penetrating pelvic injury, or other clinical indicators also warrant evaluation. Microscopic hematuria or pelvic fracture alone is not a universal indication.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[13]](#ref13)</sup>

AUA also recommends retrograde cystography for gross hematuria with a concerning mechanism or pelvic-ring fracture with clinical indicators of rupture (Statement 14b; Recommendation, Grade C). Concerning findings include inability to void, suprapubic pain, abdominal distension, unexplained low urine output, urinary ascites or otherwise unexplained pelvic fluid.<sup>[[13]](#ref13)</sup> A cystogram does not evaluate renal or ureteral injury adequately; choose upper-tract imaging separately.<sup>[[4]](#ref4)</sup>

If urgent pelvic angioembolization is needed, coordinate sequencing: extravasated cystographic contrast may interfere with angiography. Hemorrhage control takes priority.<sup>[[4]](#ref4)</sup>

| Pattern | Typical contrast appearance | Management implication in blunt trauma |
|---|---|---|
| **Extraperitoneal** | Perivesical leakage tracking through pelvic tissue planes | Uncomplicated injuries usually receive catheter drainage; bladder-neck involvement, bone fragments, rectal/vaginal injury or bladder-wall entrapment favor repair |
| **Intraperitoneal** | Contrast around bowel loops or freely within the peritoneal cavity | Surgical exploration and repair |
| **Combined** | Both patterns | Surgical repair |
| **Contusion** | No full-thickness leak demonstrated | Management depends on the complete clinical assessment; an inadequately filled negative study cannot establish a contusion |

<sup>[[23]](#ref23)[[13]](#ref13)</sup>

In a retrospective registry of 140 blunt bladder ruptures, 82 (58.6%) were extraperitoneal, 49 (35.0%) intraperitoneal and 9 (6.4%) combined.<sup>[[21]](#ref21)</sup>

ACS describes two-layer repair as standard practice; EAU notes that superiority over a watertight single-layer closure has not been demonstrated. Selected small uncomplicated endoscopic intraperitoneal perforations have a different management pathway from blunt traumatic rupture.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[23]](#ref23)</sup>

## Technical pitfalls

| Pitfall | Consequence | Response |
|---|---|---|
| Passive filling from IV contrast with the catheter clamped | Insufficient intravesical distension; false-negative study | Fill retrograde through the catheter |
| Underfilling | A small perforation is missed | Fill to tolerated distension and record the volume |
| No post-drainage image on conventional cystography | Leakage hidden by the contrast-filled bladder | Obtain a post-drainage film |
| Posterior-wall injury on an AP view | Missed leak; a lateral view is rarely feasible with pelvic fracture | CT cystography, or oblique projections on fluoroscopy |
| Cystography before angioembolization | Extravasated contrast degrades the angiogram | Coordinate sequencing; hemorrhage control takes priority |
| Forceful syringe instillation | Iatrogenic rupture or extension of an existing injury | Fill by gravity and stop at discomfort or reflux |

## Pediatric reflux: when to image

The AAP 2025 perinatal UTD report supports risk-based lower-tract imaging:<sup>[[6]](#ref6)</sup>

| Postnatal category | Lower-tract imaging approach |
|---|---|
| **UTD P1** | No routine VCUG/ceVUS or prophylactic antibiotics; repeat ultrasound in 3–6 months |
| **UTD P2** | Individualize VCUG/ceVUS and prophylaxis; repeat ultrasound in 1–3 months |
| **UTD P3** | Recommend VCUG/ceVUS, prophylaxis and functional assessment; repeat ultrasound at 1 month |

Ureteral dilation ≥7 mm supports VCUG or ceVUS and prophylaxis. Febrile UTI in a child with known UTD also prompts reflux evaluation. Suspected bladder outlet obstruction needs urgent specialist assessment rather than waiting for routine surveillance. For P3 without suspected outlet obstruction, lower-tract imaging can occur during the birth admission or at the repeat ultrasound. When renal scintigraphy is indicated, AAP advises deferring it until 6–12 weeks of age when clinically feasible because earlier MAG3 measurements may misstate function.<sup>[[6]](#ref6)</sup>

A first-versus-recurrent UTI rule alone is insufficient. NICE NG224 uses age, atypical infection and ultrasound and clinical findings: infants under 6 months with atypical or recurrent UTI receive MCUG; in children 6 months to under 3 years it is not routine but may be considered with dilation, poor flow, non-*E. coli* infection or a family history of VUR. Older children do not routinely receive MCUG solely because of recurrent UTI.<sup>[[24]](#ref24)</sup>

For established VUR, follow-up combines ultrasound and selective repeat reflux imaging. The AUA guideline recommends renal ultrasound every 12 months to monitor growth and scarring, and voiding cystography (radionuclide or low-dose fluoroscopy) between 12 and 24 months, with longer intervals for grades III–V, bladder and bowel dysfunction and older age. Cystography becomes optional if the child is managed by observation, and for grades I–II after one year of age. DMSA is recommended when ultrasound is abnormal, when scarring is a concern (breakthrough UTI, grades III–V) or when creatinine is elevated. After endoscopic injection the guideline recommends voiding cystography, and after any reflux surgery it sets renal ultrasound as a standard to look for obstruction.<sup>[[8]](#ref8)</sup> Beyond these intervals, the modality depends on grade, age, bladder and bowel dysfunction, infections and whether the result would change management.

## Fistulas and reconstructed bladders

### Suspected fistula

Select imaging for the suspected communication and its cause. CT abdomen/pelvis helps evaluate bowel disease, abscess and malignancy in suspected colovesical fistula; cystography may provide additional tract or leak information.<sup>[[16]](#ref16)</sup>

Historical small cohorts illustrate why a negative cystogram is insufficient. Melchior's 49-patient surgical series detected CVF with cystography in 5/30, CT in 25/41, and the poppy-seed test in 35/37 tested patients.<sup>[[14]](#ref14)</sup> These are cohort-specific yields with different denominators, not universal accuracy estimates or evidence that the poppy-seed test replaces anatomic investigation. It does not define the tract or exclude cancer.<sup>[[14]](#ref14)</sup> Najjar's 12-patient series likewise favored CT followed by evaluation for colonic malignancy.<sup>[[15]](#ref15)</sup>

For vesicovaginal fistula, examination, dye testing and cystoscopy usually provide the primary localization; cystography is an adjunct. Routine VCUG is not indicated for uncomplicated recurrent UTI in women. In suspected structural disease, choose targeted imaging for the actual question rather than ordering VCUG for recurrent infection or prolapse alone.<sup>[[3]](#ref3)</sup>

### Intraoperative assessment

Direct inspection of the bladder is part of emergency laparotomy when bladder injury is suspected. Retrograde instillation of dilute dye (for example methylene blue) through the catheter helps identify the perforation site.

### Suspected augmented-bladder perforation

New abdominal symptoms or unexplained systemic illness after augmentation require urgent urologic assessment, and a negative cystogram does not exclude perforation. Sensory impairment may blunt abdominal findings. Braverman's historical series found leakage on 12/13 cystograms, whereas Glass and Rushton's four-patient series demonstrated an abnormal cystogram in only one case despite confirmed rupture; ultrasound detected free fluid in all four.<sup>[[17]](#ref17)[[19]](#ref19)</sup>

CT/ultrasound may reveal fluid or other complications when contrast leakage is absent. Persistent clinical concern, peritonitis or instability requires immediate surgical decision-making; additional imaging must not delay needed treatment. Tailor filling to reservoir anatomy and the clinical situation. A historical recommendation for maximal reservoir filling is not a mandate to pressurize a suspected perforation.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[18]](#ref18)[[20]](#ref20)</sup>

## Follow-up after bladder injury or repair

Guidelines differ on routine imaging after a simple repair. EAST's 2019 systematic guideline makes the following recommendations, all based on very-low-certainty evidence:<sup>[[2]](#ref2)</sup>

| Clinical group | EAST recommendation |
|---|---|
| Simple IP or EP injury surgically repaired, without concerning symptoms | Conditionally omit routine follow-up cystography |
| Complex IP injury surgically repaired | Obtain follow-up cystography; strong recommendation |
| Simple EP injury treated with catheter drainage | Obtain follow-up cystography; strong recommendation |

The underlying cohorts had 0/175, 2/22, and 34/200 leaks, respectively. The guideline's additional “per 1,000” estimates were diagnostic models, not observed trial results.<sup>[[2]](#ref2)</sup>

Johnsen's retrospective 140-patient series found one leak among 49 imaged patients after cystorrhaphy; 10/56 patients managed with catheter drainage had persistent extravasation, and seven of those ten required operations for related complications. These findings support selective postoperative imaging and closer assessment after drainage-only treatment; they do not establish a universal leak probability.<sup>[[21]](#ref21)</sup>

**Timing and local protocol.** ACS 2025 recommends cystography for most operative repairs, beginning at least 7 days after repair and potentially 3–4 weeks for complex injury. AUA describes 2–3 weeks of drainage with cystographic confirmation of healing for uncomplicated EP injury; it advises cystography after complex repair while allowing omission after selected simple repairs (Statements 15–17 discussion). EAU 2026 allows catheter removal five to ten days after simple repair in a healthy patient without cystography, plans cystography at approximately ten days for conservatively treated injuries, and advises cystography after complex repair or with impaired-healing risks.<sup>[[23]](#ref23)</sup> Use the injury, repair, healing risks and clinical course to agree on catheter removal; document which pathway is being followed.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[13]](#ref13)</sup>

After colovesical fistula repair, a postoperative cystogram may be reserved for a large defect or complex repair; the supporting 2025 Pau study was retrospective, with only 18 postoperative cystograms.<sup>[[22]](#ref22)</sup> After augmentation or neobladder construction, the operating team's reconstruction-specific pathway determines whether and when cystography or pouchography is needed.

### Bladder-neck position after prostatectomy

Static cystography can also be read for bladder-neck position, not just for leak. The bladder-neck-to-pubic-symphysis (BNPS) ratio — the distance from the superior pubic-symphysis border to the bladder neck, divided by total pubic-symphysis height — is lower in continent men and rises with worse post-prostatectomy incontinence. In a retrospective series of 611 consecutive RARP patients, 215 had a postoperative cystogram: mean BNPS at 3 months was 0.39 in continent men versus 0.49 in incontinent men; at 12 months it was 0.40 versus 0.60 (12-month incontinence 2.8% in the lowest BNPS tertile versus 19.4% in the highest).<sup>[[26]](#ref26)</sup> A separate 203-patient laparoscopic/robotic cohort found a BNPS ratio above 0.59 was the only factor significantly associated with persistent incontinence at every follow-up interval to 12 months.<sup>[[27]](#ref27)</sup> Treat the BNPS ratio as an association from these two retrospective cohorts, not a validated diagnostic cutoff for an individual patient.
