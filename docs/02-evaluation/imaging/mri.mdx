# MRI in Reconstructive Urology

**MRI** shows the tissue around the urethra and pelvic floor, which contrast studies of the lumen do not. It can clarify periurethral and pelvic soft-tissue anatomy when examination, endoscopy, or lumen-based imaging leaves a specific reconstructive question unanswered.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[2]](#ref2)</sup> The study is ordered around that question and the relevant prior operation. An image-derived length, angle, or signal characteristic is not, by itself, an instruction to perform a particular repair.

**At a glance:** MRI is used selectively for complex posterior urethral disease, female urethral diverticulum, difficult fistulas, or selected pelvic-floor and postoperative problems. The implant and contrast plan is established before scanning. Findings are interpreted alongside examination, symptoms, endoscopy, and prior imaging.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[2]](#ref2)[[3]](#ref3)[[4]](#ref4)</sup>

## 1. Overview: What MRI Adds

<span id="unmatched-soft-tissue-resolution"></span>
<span id="no-ionizing-radiation"></span>
<span id="multiplanar-acquisition"></span>
<span id="functional-protocols"></span>

| Clinical question | Useful MRI contribution | Companion evaluation |
|---|---|---|
| Posterior urethral stenosis or complex PFUI | Periurethral anatomy and associated fistula or other pathology | [RUG/VCUG](/docs/evaluation/imaging/rug-vcug); selected antegrade/retrograde endoscopy |
| Female urethral diverticulum | Extent, configuration, and relationship to urethra and sphincter | Examination and urethroscopy; histology when tissue is removed |
| Selected prolapse or defecatory dysfunction | Dynamic compartment relationships | Symptoms, POP-Q examination, and bowel assessment |
| Difficult postoperative pelvic problem | Deep tissue, collection, tract, or inflammatory change | Operation/device details and targeted endoscopy or ultrasound |

These are indications for problem-solving, not requirements for every patient with these diagnoses.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[2]](#ref2)[[5]](#ref5)[[6]](#ref6)</sup>

MRI has four practical advantages in reconstruction. It shows soft tissue (periurethral scar, levator muscle, a diverticular sac, a fistula tract) rather than only the contrast-filled lumen. It uses no ionizing radiation, which matters for young patients who need serial studies. It is acquired in true multiple planes, so the urethral axis or a diverticulum can be imaged along its own orientation. Dynamic, diffusion-weighted and contrast-enhanced sequences add functional information. It complements fluoroscopic urethrography and urodynamics and does not replace them.<sup>[[8]](#ref8)[[14]](#ref14)</sup>

## 2. Pelvic Fracture Urethral Injury (PFUI)

<span id="full-mri-protocol-for-pfui"></span>
<span id="mri-vs-rug-comparative-table"></span>
<span id="surgical-approach-decision-thresholds"></span>

<span id="background"></span>

### When MRI helps

Combined RUG and VCUG remain central for a nearly obliterated or obliterated posterior urethra. The EAU recommends considering MRI urethrography as an ancillary test in posterior stenosis, particularly when associated pathology or complex spatial anatomy matters.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup>

A prospective study of 25 men with complete posterior stenosis found MRI measurements correlated more closely with operative length than conventional urethrography. In that study the mean measurement error was 0.4 ± 0.4 cm for MR urethrography and 1.4 ± 1.1 cm for RUG with VCUG (p &lt; 0.001). The study supports an adjunctive role, but it does not establish a universal correction in millimeters to add to RUG measurements.<sup>[[7]](#ref7)</sup>

In that series the MR urethrogram was acquired after aseptic lubricant was infused through the meatus to dilate the distal urethra up to the stricture, with axial and sagittal T2 turbo spin-echo, sagittal T1 and contrast-enhanced sagittal T1 images.<sup>[[7]](#ref7)</sup> A scar bridge between the urethral ends can appear as a low-signal band on T2, and diffusion or post-contrast sequences are sometimes added to separate fibrosis from residual lumen. These are technique descriptions, not a validated protocol.

<span id="what-to-measure-on-mri"></span>

### Request and reporting checklist

Request a dedicated urethral or pelvic protocol and provide the trauma, prior repair, catheter, and endoscopy history. Thin-section T2 images in sagittal, axial and coronal planes aligned to the relevant anatomy are useful; contrast and additional sequences depend on the question. A phased-array pelvic surface coil is the usual choice. The bladder is moderately filled, and a clamped suprapubic catheter can help outline the bladder neck. Document the following:

- the visible ends of the urethra, the estimated intervening distance and the plane of measurement;
- prostatic position, including superior or posterior displacement;
- the angle between the urethral remnants and the bladder neck axis;
- any low-signal band that could represent a scar bridge;
- the pubic symphysis (diastasis, marrow edema or osteitis on fluid-sensitive images);
- the bladder neck appearance, and associated abnormalities. If the proximal lumen or bladder neck is inadequately assessed, the report says so and does not infer normality.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[7]](#ref7)</sup>

**Operative limit:** gap length and stump geometry contribute to planning, but the evidence does not support a universal 3-cm gap or 30-degree angle that mandates pubectomy or an abdominal approach. Imaging is reviewed with the reconstructive surgeon, and bladder-neck appearance on a static scan is not a continence test.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup>

| Feature | RUG with VCUG | MRI |
|---|---|---|
| Luminal detail and dynamic filling | Yes | Limited |
| Gap length | Underestimated in some series; depends on filling | Closer to operative length in a small series<sup>[[7]](#ref7)</sup> |
| Prostatic position, bladder neck, symphysis | Limited | Shown directly |
| Fibrosis and associated pathology | Not shown | Shown |
| Radiation | Yes | No |
| Availability and cost | Universal; low | Center-dependent; higher |

## 3. Dynamic (Defecation) MRI for Pelvic Organ Prolapse

<span id="pubo-coccygeal-line-pcl-reference"></span>
<span id="compartment-descent-grading-table"></span>
<span id="dietz-levator-ani-grading-avulsion-scale"></span>
<span id="clinical-utility-summary"></span>

<span id="indications"></span>

### Select the clinical question

Do not routinely obtain imaging simply to document prolapse already demonstrated on examination. When imaging is needed for complex compartment symptoms, defecatory dysfunction, or unresolved postoperative findings, MR defecography is one option; fluoroscopic and ultrasound examinations answer overlapping questions.<sup>[[5]](#ref5)[[6]](#ref6)</sup>

<span id="protocol"></span>

### Acquisition and reporting

The ESUR/ESGAR consensus recommends static images plus coached squeeze, strain, and evacuation sequences, obtained on 1.5 T or 3 T systems with a surface coil centered low on the pelvis. The patient is trained in the maneuvers beforehand. Inadequate effort or incomplete evacuation is recorded because either can conceal findings.<sup>[[8]](#ref8)</sup>

| Element | Consensus recommendation |
|---|---|
| Bladder | Moderately filled; the patient voids about 2 hours before |
| Rectum | Ultrasound gel distension, 120–250 mL depending on the patient (larger volumes help evacuation) |
| Vagina | Gel opacification (about 20 mL) helps but did not reach consensus |
| Bowel preparation | Cleansing enema helps but did not reach consensus |
| Contrast | No oral or intravenous contrast is necessary |
| Phases | Rest, squeeze, strain and evacuation, in the sagittal plane; dynamic sequences are single-slice, fast T2 |
| Position | Supine with the knees flexed; the patient wears a pad for comfort during evacuation |

The pubococcygeal line (PCL) runs from the inferior pubic symphysis to the last coccygeal joint. Organ descent is measured perpendicular to it at rest and at maximum strain. The report states the reference line, organ landmark, phase, and displacement. The consensus grading differs by compartment:<sup>[[8]](#ref8)</sup>

| Finding | Grading |
|---|---|
| Bladder base or cervix/vaginal vault ("rule of three") | Graded from 1 cm below the PCL: mild 1–3 cm, moderate 3–6 cm, severe more than 6 cm. The bladder base may descend up to 1 cm below the PCL on straining in continent women, which is not reported as a cystocele. |
| Rectocele ("rule of two") | Grade I under 2 cm, grade II 2–4 cm, grade III over 4 cm. It is reported as pathological from grade II, because grade I is common in parous women. |
| Anorectal junction descent | Grade I 3–5 cm below the PCL, grade II 5 cm or more |
| Enterocele | The contents of the peritoneal sac are reported (fat, small bowel or sigmoid) |
| Intussusception | Small rectal wall intussusception is a common normal finding during defecation |

Levator defects are described with the method used. A muscle defect is not, alone, a mandate for mesh surgery.<sup>[[8]](#ref8)</sup>

Use the report to reconcile symptoms and examination, then return to the [POP clinical pathway](/docs/clinical-conditions/03c-pelvic-support/pelvic-organ-prolapse). An abnormal image without corresponding bother or functional relevance is not a stand-alone indication for repair.<sup>[[5]](#ref5)</sup>

## 4. Urethral Diverticulum

<span id="key-sequence-and-findings"></span>
<span id="surgical-planning-checklist-from-mri"></span>

<span id="why-mri-is-the-gold-standard"></span>

### Role and limits

MRI is a preferred study for characterizing female urethral diverticulum and planning surgery. Ask for location, dimensions, simple versus multiloculated or circumferential configuration, visible communication with the urethra, and relationship to the sphincter. Fluid-sensitive images can show a small lesion; the ostium is not always demonstrable. Record prior bulking injections because they can mimic pathology.<sup>[[2]](#ref2)[[9]](#ref9)</sup>

In a single-institution study of 60 women referred for suspected diverticulum, MRI had a sensitivity and specificity of 100% for diverticula (20 patients). The diverticula were mostly round with sharp margins and homogeneous high T2 signal, and the ostium was seen in 23 of 27 (85%). MRI also identified an alternative diagnosis in 13 of the 28 women (46%) who did not have a diverticulum. An endoluminal vaginal coil gave the best image quality in that study.<sup>[[9]](#ref9)</sup> Thin-section T2 images in axial, sagittal and coronal planes show the sac, and post-contrast T1 and diffusion sequences are added when a solid component is suspected. A horseshoe or circumferential sac wrapping the urethra on axial images has been described as a characteristic configuration.

**MRI is not infallible.** In Chung and colleagues' operated series, 10 of 41 patients who had preoperative MRI had a diagnostic or anatomical discrepancy; cancer within the diverticulum was missed in two. This selected retrospective series is a caution about false reassurance, not an accuracy estimate transferable to every imaging service.<sup>[[10]](#ref10)</sup>

<span id="internal-complexity-and-carcinoma-risk"></span>

### Complexity and surgical planning

Simple fluid is T2 bright without enhancement. Proteinaceous debris or a calculus changes the signal pattern, and a solid component that enhances on post-contrast images raises the possibility of carcinoma, which in diverticula is most often adenocarcinoma in published series. Wall thickening, debris, or an enhancing component requires clinical interpretation. Neither an enhancing nodule nor restricted diffusion proves malignancy, and a reassuring MRI cannot exclude it. Persistent clinical concern needs specialist assessment and appropriate tissue diagnosis. Do not assign a continence procedure or a biopsy route from a neck-width cutoff alone.<sup>[[2]](#ref2)[[10]](#ref10)</sup>

Items worth reporting for surgical planning are the clock-position and level of the neck along the urethra, the relationship of the neck to the sphincter, unilateral versus horseshoe or circumferential extent, any tract to the vaginal wall, and any solid enhancing component.<sup>[[2]](#ref2)[[9]](#ref9)</sup> A proximal neck and a neck close to the sphincter are generally regarded as raising the continence risk of excision.

## 5. Vesicovaginal Fistula (VVF)

<span id="role-of-mri"></span>
<span id="sequences-for-vvf"></span>
<span id="fistula-tract-characteristics"></span>
<span id="surgical-approach-determination"></span>

Examination, bladder dye testing, and cystoscopy are often the starting point. The EAU supports late-excretory CT and/or MRI when diagnosis is difficult or a ureterovaginal fistula is suspected.<sup>[[2]](#ref2)</sup> For MRI, specify whether the unanswered question concerns a tract, collection, radiation change, or adjacent organ involvement. These may require a different protocol from uncomplicated prolapse imaging.<sup>[[6]](#ref6)</sup>

Common protocol elements are T2-weighted sagittal, axial and coronal images (the tract is T2 bright and linear), with post-contrast T1 images when inflammation or radiation change is a concern. Coronal images show the relationship of the fistula to the ureteric orifices and bladder dome. Radiation-related fistulas may show diffuse wall thickening and fibrosis with a larger, irregular tract, which indicates poorer tissue quality. The report should describe visible tract anatomy and uncertainty, and it should state the distance from the fistula to the ureteric orifices. It should not assign a vaginal versus abdominal repair, interposition flap, or ureteral stent on the basis of a fixed millimeter distance alone. Examination, exposure, ureteral involvement, tissue quality, and prior treatment remain part of the [VVF assessment](/docs/clinical-conditions/03f-fistulas/in-females/vesicovaginal).<sup>[[2]](#ref2)</sup>

## 6. MRI Urethrogram

<span id="indications-1"></span>
<span id="spongiofibrosis-visualization"></span>
<span id="posterior-urethra"></span>
<span id="female-urethral-stricture"></span>

MRI may add information about periurethral tissues, fistulation, or complex posterior anatomy. Routine anterior strictures usually begin with conventional urethral assessment; MRI is not required simply because urethroplasty is planned.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup>

On T2 images the corpus spongiosum is bright, and fibrosis replaces that signal with lower signal. Compared with RUG, MRI can show periurethral fibrosis, the surrounding muscle and the posterior urethra, and it can be used to look for an associated mass. Sonourethrography shows spongiofibrosis in part, and RUG shows only the lumen.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup> After radical prostatectomy the anastomotic scar appears as a low-signal band, and contrast enhancement alone does not separate scar from recurrent tumor. Describe narrowing and surrounding signal abnormalities without equating MRI signal with histological fibrosis depth. There is no validated universal MRI grade in the cited guidance that independently selects endoscopic treatment, anastomotic repair, onlay, or staged reconstruction. For anterior disease, [sonourethrography](/docs/evaluation/imaging/ultrasound) can be a useful adjunct, with its own limitations.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup>

In women, MRI is particularly useful when another periurethral lesion is suspected. It does not replace clinical, endoscopic, and functional evaluation of obstruction.<sup>[[2]](#ref2)</sup>

## 7. Peyronie's Disease

<span id="role-of-mri-1"></span>
<span id="peyronies-mri-protocol"></span>
<span id="plaque-characteristics-on-mri"></span>
<span id="surgical-relevance"></span>

MRI is not a routine investigation for Peyronie's disease in current EAU guidance. History, examination, objective erection and curvature assessment, and selected ultrasound or duplex studies guide evaluation. MRI signal should not be used to label an oral treatment effective or to assign grafting from plaque length alone.<sup>[[11]](#ref11)</sup> Where it is used, MRI has been described as showing plaque location and thickness, calcification (signal void on T1 and gradient-echo images), an hourglass waist and, on post-contrast images, enhancement that some authors associate with active disease. Scans are usually flaccid, on a small surface coil, with thin axial and sagittal T2 images and fat-suppressed T1 images. Erect curvature is documented separately by history, photographs or a supervised erection.

Use the [Peyronie's treatment atlas](/docs/surgical-techniques/04j-sexual-dysfunction/peyronies-disease) and [penile Doppler page](/docs/evaluation/imaging/penile-doppler-ultrasound) for the relevant clinical workflow. This MRI page does not provide an erection-induction drug protocol.

## 8. VI-RADS for Bladder Cancer

<span id="overview"></span>
<span id="mpmri-sequences-used-in-vi-rads"></span>
<span id="vi-rads-scoring-system"></span>
<span id="relevance-to-reconstructive-urologists"></span>

VI-RADS is a five-point MRI assessment of the likelihood of muscle invasion, using T2-weighted, diffusion-weighted, and dynamic contrast-enhanced findings in its original multiparametric protocol. The score runs from 1 (muscle invasion very unlikely) to 5 (very likely), with 3 equivocal. It does not assign fixed universal percentage risks to each category or replace endoscopy and histopathology. Bladder filling is moderate, because overdistension thins the wall and underfilling obscures wall layers.<sup>[[12]](#ref12)</sup>

For reconstructive practice, its relevance is coordination with the oncology team when a known or suspected bladder lesion affects planning. Examples are an irradiated or contracted bladder before augmentation, a neurogenic bladder with hematuria, and a lesion at the bladder neck before outlet reconstruction. A low VI-RADS score is not clearance for augmentation, diversion, or treatment of an unexplained bladder abnormality.<sup>[[12]](#ref12)</sup>

## 9. Upper Tract / MR Urography (MRU)

<span id="indications-for-mru-over-ct-or-mag3"></span>
<span id="pediatric-advantage"></span>

<span id="mru-protocol"></span>

### Choose the technique for the question

| Technique | What it provides | Important limit |
|---|---|---|
| Static-fluid MRU | Heavily T2-weighted depiction of urinary fluid; no injected contrast required | Dilatation does not establish functionally important obstruction |
| Excretory/functional MRU | Contrast excretion, anatomy, and protocol-dependent functional information | Depends on excretion, acquisition, processing, and local expertise |

Hydronephrosis workup depends on symptoms, prior imaging, pregnancy, renal function, and the likely cause. CT urography, MRU, and MAG3 are alternative or complementary investigations; no single study is automatically preferred for every dilated system.<sup>[[13]](#ref13)[[14]](#ref14)</sup>

MRU avoids ionizing radiation and can be useful for complex congenital anatomy (duplicated systems, ectopic ureters, ureteroceles), for children who would otherwise accumulate radiation from repeated studies, and for planning before ureteral reconstruction, but examination time, motion, and possible sedation matter. Split renal function is quantified more reliably with Tc-99m MAG3 than with MRU. In excretory protocols, furosemide is given after contrast (the F+15 pattern) to test washout. A diuretic protocol must be named and followed as designed; “F+15” and “F−15” are not interchangeable. Interpret drainage with the full examination rather than calling any delayed washout an obstruction.<sup>[[14]](#ref14)</sup> See [MAG3 renal scintigraphy](/docs/evaluation/imaging/mag3-renal-scintigraphy) for the dedicated functional evaluation.

## 9.5. Mesh and Sling Complications

<span id="indications-where-mri-is-preferred-over-pelvic-floor-ultrasound"></span>
<span id="where-ultrasound-is-preferred-over-mri"></span>
<span id="imaging-protocol-considerations"></span>

Obtain the operative report and device information. MRI can assess deep postoperative pelvic problems; ultrasound can show many slings and accessible mesh segments. Modality selection depends on the suspected complication and local expertise. ACR rates contrast-enhanced MRI as usually appropriate for selected subacute or chronic complications after pelvic-floor repair, a different question from uncomplicated recurrent prolapse.<sup>[[6]](#ref6)</sup>

MRI is the usual choice for components ultrasound cannot reach: the retropubic space, the obturator foramen and ischiorectal fossa, sacrocolpopexy mesh, deep abscess or osteomyelitis, presacral hematoma and peripheral nerve involvement (MR neurography). Ultrasound is often preferred for suburethral and vaginal-wall mesh, because polypropylene is echogenic and can be hard to separate from scar on MRI, and it allows real-time assessment during Valsalva. Useful MRI sequences are T2 in three planes, fat-suppressed post-contrast T1 for inflammation, and diffusion imaging for abscess. See [mesh complications and removal](/docs/surgical-techniques/04g-prolapse-repair/mesh-complications) and [ultrasound](/docs/evaluation/imaging/ultrasound).

Neither a normal MRI nor a normal ultrasound excludes every mesh complication. Suspected urinary tract exposure still needs appropriate endoscopic evaluation, and vaginal exposure is assessed clinically. Avoid claiming universal diagnostic sensitivity for a device or using visible mesh position alone as proof that it caused pain.<sup>[[5]](#ref5)</sup>

## 11. Practical Protocol Tips

<span id="magnet-strength-15t-vs-3t"></span>
<span id="coil-selection"></span>
<span id="bladder-filling-protocol"></span>
<span id="rectal-contrast-for-prolapse-mri"></span>

<span id="implant-safety"></span>

### Implants and MRI safety

For a sacral neuromodulator or another active implanted system, identify the exact generator, leads, and any retained or abandoned components. Apply the current device-specific MR conditions, including field strength, permitted scan region, programming, and other restrictions. “MR Conditional” does not mean safe under every protocol. Unknown or unmet conditions require the radiology service's formal MR risk assessment; a prior uneventful scan is not substitute clearance.<sup>[[3]](#ref3)</sup>

<span id="contrast-safety"></span>

### Gadolinium, renal impairment, and pregnancy

The ACR contrast manual distinguishes agents by NSF risk. When gadolinium is necessary in a patient at risk, Group II agents are preferred; renal impairment is not a blanket contraindication to every gadolinium agent. Use the lowest dose that provides a diagnostic examination under the agent's labeling and institutional protocol, rather than an automatic half-dose rule. ACR does not recommend initiating or altering dialysis solely because Group II contrast was administered.<sup>[[4]](#ref4)</sup>

During pregnancy, consider a noncontrast examination or another adequate test first. Gadolinium requires a documented judgment that the expected clinical benefit justifies the uncertain fetal risk; pregnancy is not a reason to casually postpone necessary diagnosis.<sup>[[4]](#ref4)</sup>

<span id="patient-preparation"></span>

### Field strength, coil and antispasmodic

A 3 T system gives higher signal-to-noise and resolution but more susceptibility artifact, so 1.5 T is often adequate for dynamic pelvic studies. A phased-array pelvic surface coil is the default. An endorectal coil deforms anatomy and is used for prostate imaging, not for PFUI or prolapse. An endoluminal vaginal coil has been reported to improve urethral and periurethral detail.<sup>[[9]](#ref9)</sup> An antispasmodic (hyoscine butylbromide or glucagon) is sometimes given to reduce bowel motion. Hyoscine is avoided in glaucoma and tachyarrhythmia, and the choice is made with the radiology service.

### Request quality

Include the clinical question, symptom pattern, prior operations, device details, pregnancy status when relevant, and contrast-risk history. Let the MR service tailor bladder filling, coil, field strength, motion reduction, and rectal preparation. A fixed bladder volume or universal antispasmodic prescription is not appropriate across PFUI, diverticulum, defecography, and MRU protocols.<sup>[[3]](#ref3)[[4]](#ref4)[[8]](#ref8)[[14]](#ref14)</sup>
