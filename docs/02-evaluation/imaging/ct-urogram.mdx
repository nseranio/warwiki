# CT Urogram & CT-Based Imaging in Reconstructive Urology

CT urography (CTU) maps the urinary tract from the renal parenchyma to the ureteral orifices in three dimensions. It is a principal study for the soft-tissue envelope around the tract, intrinsic urothelial lesions and the spatial relationships of the retroperitoneum and pelvis, where fluoroscopic and nuclear studies are limited. It shows anatomy, not function. It cannot quantify split renal function or drainage kinetics, which need diuretic renography.

CTU answers an anatomic question. Phases are chosen around the suspected lesion and the decision the result will change. A full CTU is not required for every stone, stricture, episode of microscopic hematuria or postoperative follow-up.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[2]](#ref2)</sup>

## At a glance

| Clinical question | Useful study | Limitation |
|---|---|---|
| High-risk microhematuria | Cystoscopy plus axial upper-tract imaging; usually CTU if suitable | CTU does not replace bladder inspection.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup> |
| Intermediate-risk microhematuria | Renal/bladder ultrasound with the guideline's cystoscopy pathway | Routine CTU adds exposure without being the default recommendation.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup> |
| Suspected stone | Ultrasound or noncontrast CT according to presentation | Contrast/excretory phases are added for a separate anatomic question, not automatically.<sup>[[7]](#ref7)</sup> |
| Suspected ureteral injury or urinary leak | Contrast CT with appropriately delayed images in a stable patient | Poor excretion can limit localization; further delayed or direct studies may be needed.<sup>[[5]](#ref5)</sup> |
| Suspected bladder rupture | **Retrograde CT or conventional cystography** | Excreted IV contrast passively filling the bladder is insufficient to exclude rupture.<sup>[[5]](#ref5)[[6]](#ref6)</sup> |
| Differential renal function / drainage | Diuretic renography, usually MAG3 | Interpret function, drainage, hydration and bladder emptying together.<sup>[[8]](#ref8)</sup> |

## 1. Indications and Alternatives

### Hematuria and urothelial lesions

Apply the AUA/SUFU 2025 risk assessment. There is no universal pre-urethroplasty CTU rule. In a patient with a urethral or ureteral stricture and gross or persistent microscopic hematuria, the hematuria evaluation still applies, because upper-tract urothelial carcinoma can coexist with stricture disease and a missed lesion delays oncologic care. Low- or negligible-risk microhematuria generally begins with repeat urinalysis; intermediate-risk patients usually receive renal ultrasound and a cystoscopy discussion; high-risk patients receive cystoscopy and axial upper-tract imaging. When CTU is unsuitable, MR urography is an alternative; when both are unsuitable, retrograde pyelography with noncontrast imaging or ultrasound may be used. Gross hematuria requires a separate complete clinical evaluation.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup>

CTU may demonstrate filling defects, urothelial thickening, papillary lesions or obstruction with hydroureteronephrosis above a discrete lesion, but imaging alone does not establish histology. A suspected upper-tract lesion may require ureteroscopic assessment and tissue sampling. A negative scan does not exclude all urothelial disease.<sup>[[10]](#ref10)</sup> When contrast is contraindicated, retrograde pyelography with ureteroscopy and biopsy, or MR urography, is the alternative.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup>

### Stone disease with stricture

Unenhanced CT is the standard test for urolithiasis.<sup>[[7]](#ref7)</sup> When a stone coexists with a ureteral stricture, and stone passage may itself have caused the stricture, one CTU can identify the stones by size and location, localize the transition point, show hydronephrosis and perinephric stranding or abscess, and give the operative map for ureteroscopy with dilation or formal ureteroplasty.

### Ureteral reconstruction and congenital anatomy

Specify the operative question: narrowing location, opacified length, collecting-system duplication, relationship to a mass, or vascular anatomy. Thin sections with multiplanar and maximum-intensity reconstructions localize the transition point of a ureteral stricture and the periureteral tissue around it. Stricture length can be measured when both the dilated proximal segment and the collapsed distal segment are seen. Poorly excreting or completely obstructed segments may remain unopacified, so a retrograde or antegrade study can supply additional luminal information.<sup>[[2]](#ref2)[[5]](#ref5)</sup>

A crossing vessel on CT does not, by itself, prove the cause or functional severity of UPJ obstruction. Reconstructive selection also uses symptoms, serial anatomy and functional assessment. Neither a cortical-thickness measurement nor a delayed nephrogram is a substitute for a measured differential renal function.<sup>[[8]](#ref8)</sup> An arterial or corticomedullary acquisition with thin sections and 3D reconstruction shows a lower-pole vessel crossing anterior to the UPJ, and dedicated CT or MR angiography can be requested for complex cases such as a redundant pelvis, secondary vessels or horseshoe kidney.

Periureteral stranding, thickening and enhancement indicate inflammation. Dense soft tissue encasing the ureter suggests retroperitoneal fibrosis (see below), and diffuse thickening within a radiation field suggests radiation fibrosis. Before radical cystectomy with diversion, CTU documents ureteral caliber, position and any existing stricture, hydronephrosis, ureteral length and course for the planned anastomosis, and the presence of an upper-tract lesion.

**Congenital anatomy.** Before pyeloplasty in a horseshoe kidney, CTU with 3D reconstruction maps the isthmus relative to the aorta and mesenteric vessels, aberrant renal arteries and the ureteral course, which usually passes anterior to the isthmus. In a pelvic kidney it shows the anomalous vascular supply and the relationship of the ureter to pelvic structures. In a duplex system it shows the ureteral insertions (the upper-moiety ureter usually inserts ectopically, below and medial to the lower-moiety orifice, the Weigert-Meyer rule), any ureterocele and coexisting lower-moiety UPJ obstruction.

### Fistula, collection and postoperative complication

Request an excretory phase when the question is a urinary leak and a retrograde cystogram when bladder integrity is the question. Communicate prior surgery, diversion anatomy, stents, drains, renal function and the suspected communication. Delayed contrast in a collection supports a urinary source; absent opacification does not reliably exclude a small leak in a poorly functioning unit.<sup>[[5]](#ref5)[[6]](#ref6)</sup>

Complex pelvic fistulae may also need MRI, endoscopy or direct contrast studies. CT cannot certify flap viability, tissue vascularity or that an irradiated repair will succeed. See [MRI](/docs/evaluation/imaging/mri) and the [fistula repair atlas](/docs/surgical-techniques/04h-fistula-repair).

For a vesicovaginal fistula, an excretory or cystographic phase can show contrast in the vaginal vault, the relation of the ureteral orifices to the fistula margin, ureteral dilation from trigonal distortion and residual bladder capacity. For rectourethral fistula, CTU with a CT cystogram can show the communication, surrounding radiation fibrosis, associated ureteral or bladder involvement and pelvic abscess. In the irradiated pelvis, CT shows bladder wall thickness and capacity, ureteral dilation and the relation of a fistula to non-irradiated tissue and adjacent bowel, which informs interposition-flap planning.

## 2. CTU Protocol — Match the Acquisition to the Question

A CTU protocol is a radiology prescription, not a fixed three-phase requirement. The ESUR technical consensus describes selecting one, two or three phases according to indication and patient characteristics.<sup>[[2]](#ref2)</sup>

| Acquisition | Main contribution | Planning point |
|---|---|---|
| Unenhanced | Stones, renal and ureteral wall calcification, phleboliths, baseline lesion attenuation | Include when that information is needed. Split-bolus protocols can also include an unenhanced scan.<sup>[[2]](#ref2)</sup> |
| Nephrographic | Renal parenchyma, renal masses, ureteral wall enhancement, retroperitoneal soft tissue | A corticomedullary (arterial) acquisition is added for vascular detail such as a UPJ crossing vessel. Coordinate lesion-specific phases with radiology.<sup>[[2]](#ref2)</sup> |
| Excretory | Contrast-filled collecting systems and ureters: filling defects, stricture transition point, duplication, ureterocele, dilation, bladder wall and diverticula | Timing depends on excretion and the clinical question. Incomplete opacification is not automatically a stricture.<sup>[[2]](#ref2)[[3]](#ref3)</sup> |
| Split-bolus combined phase | Nephrographic and excretory information in fewer acquisitions | May reduce exposure, but additional delayed images can offset savings.<sup>[[3]](#ref3)</sup> |

In the split-bolus technique a first contrast bolus is followed by a wait to allow excretion into the collecting system and then a second bolus, and a single acquisition captures excretory and nephrographic information together. This removes one acquisition. It suits urothelial and collecting-system questions without a dedicated stone or mass question. A separate unenhanced series, the corticomedullary phase for vascular mapping and a full three-phase protocol are chosen when stone detection, mass enhancement or pre-pyeloplasty vascular mapping is the main question.

Low-dose IV furosemide given with the contrast has been used to distend the ureter and improve mid-ureteral opacification, a common technical failure that can be mistaken for obstruction. It is omitted when the aim is to preserve the obstructed anatomy. Oral contrast is not routinely used because it can obscure ureteral filling. Water as negative oral contrast improves bowel delineation. Prone positioning can improve opacification of the ureterovesical junction.

Dose is protocol-dependent. Scan length, patient size, scanner settings and repeat phases change exposure materially, and dose per examination is not a fixed value. The ESUR consensus proposes lower-dose acquisitions for benign indications and standard dose when malignancy is possible.<sup>[[2]](#ref2)</sup> In a retrospective single-center comparison of 241 CTU examinations, a single-bolus protocol in patients aged 50 or older needed a repeat excretory phase less often (32/112, 28.6%) than two split-bolus groups (25/48, 52.1%, and 37/80, 46.3%) and had a shorter mean scan time (678 s vs 978 to 1,046 s). Its mean dose-length product was higher (1,422 vs 1,041 and 1,137 mGy·cm), and image quality did not differ.<sup>[[3]](#ref3)</sup> This is a protocol comparison at one center, not a general dose estimate. Record the protocol and dose in the report for longitudinal tracking, especially in young patients and those needing serial imaging.

Provide prior studies to avoid duplicating acquisitions. Use the lowest exposure that still answers the diagnostic question. Ultrasound, MR urography or MAG3 renography may be preferable for repeated assessment in an appropriate patient.<sup>[[2]](#ref2)[[7]](#ref7)</sup>

## 3. Iodinated Contrast

### Renal risk and hydration

Distinguish **contrast-associated AKI** (temporal association) from **contrast-induced AKI** (causal attribution). The ACR–NKF consensus does not treat an eGFR below 30 as an absolute prohibition on a clinically necessary contrast CT.<sup>[[4]](#ref4)</sup>

| Situation | Practical interpretation |
|---|---|
| Stable eGFR ≥30 mL/min/1.73 m² | Routine IV saline prophylaxis is not indicated for every patient. |
| eGFR 30–44 with recent AKI or other high-risk circumstances | Consider prophylaxis individually. |
| AKI or eGFR &lt;30, not on maintenance dialysis | Discuss diagnostic benefit and alternatives; isotonic saline prophylaxis is indicated when not contraindicated by volume risk. |
| Maintenance dialysis | Do not initiate or reschedule dialysis solely to clear IV iodinated contrast; consider residual renal function and fluid burden. |

These recommendations concern intravenous contrast CT, not every intra-arterial exposure. Contrast is not reduced below a diagnostic dose to create a nominally safer but nondiagnostic study.<sup>[[4]](#ref4)</sup> Low-osmolality or iso-osmolality nonionic agents are standard. N-acetylcysteine has no supported role in prevention. In the PRESERVE trial of high-risk patients undergoing angiography (intra-arterial contrast, with findings generally applied to IV contrast), neither sodium bicarbonate nor acetylcysteine reduced the risk of death, dialysis or persistent kidney-function decline compared with saline or placebo.<sup>[[13]](#ref13)</sup>

### Metformin and prior reactions

ACR 2026 does not require withholding metformin for IV contrast when there is no AKI and eGFR is ≥30. With AKI or severe CKD below 30, hold it at or before exposure and for 48 hours, then reassess renal function before restarting; specified arterial catheter procedures have additional considerations. FDA labeling or local policy may be more restrictive.<sup>[[9]](#ref9)</sup>

Document the actual prior contrast reaction and agent. Shellfish or povidone-iodine allergy alone is not an iodinated-contrast allergy. For a prior same-class allergic-like reaction, coordinate agent selection, alternatives and any premedication with radiology. Premedication regimens combine a corticosteroid started many hours before the scan with an antihistamine, and the regimen and its timing follow the ACR Manual and local protocol. Premedication does not eliminate breakthrough reactions and should not delay an urgent diagnosis without weighing the harm of the delay. After a prior severe reaction, consider whether contrast is necessary or whether MR urography, retrograde pyelography or noncontrast CT with nuclear imaging answers the question.<sup>[[9]](#ref9)</sup>

### Sequencing with MAG3

The SNMMI/EANM procedure standard does not set a universal wait after iodinated contrast or a mandatory CTU-first sequence. Supply recent imaging, kidney-function changes and medication history to nuclear medicine, and schedule according to the diagnostic question and the department's protocol. Acute renal deterioration after any event may affect interpretation.<sup>[[8]](#ref8)</sup>

## 4. Trauma: CT, Cystography and Urethrography Have Different Roles

Resuscitation takes priority. CT is appropriate when the trauma patient's condition allows it; hemodynamic instability is not an instruction to delay definitive hemorrhage management for a CT scan.<sup>[[5]](#ref5)</sup>

Pelvic fracture, hematoma or superior bladder displacement can raise concern for urethral injury, but none alone proves complete transection. Blood at the meatus after pelvic trauma is an indication for RUG. Repeated blind catheter attempts are avoided; urinary drainage and any realignment decision depend on the injury and the treating trauma or urology team.<sup>[[5]](#ref5)</sup>

### What CT shows in pelvic fracture urethral injury

CT is obtained for the fracture and hemorrhage, and it adds information relevant to later reconstruction.<sup>[[5]](#ref5)[[14]](#ref14)</sup>

- **Ring pattern.** Fractures that disrupt the posterior pelvic ring, and displaced or vertically unstable patterns (for example Tile C or Young-Burgess APC-III and vertical shear), are associated with posterior urethral injury more often than isolated anterior arch fractures. The association is one of risk, not a substitute for urethrography.
- **Bladder and hematoma.** A bladder displaced superiorly by a large pelvic hematoma (the “pie-in-the-sky” appearance) raises concern for complete posterior urethral disruption. Suprapubic drainage is then usually chosen over blind transurethral catheterization, with urethrography once the patient is stable.
- **Hematoma.** Volume, distribution and active extravasation (a contrast blush on the arterial phase) direct hemorrhage control. A large posterior hematoma may indicate denser scar at later perineal surgery.
- **Bone fragments.** Retropubic fragments can injure the bladder neck or urethra, can be incorporated into the obliterative scar, and can obscure urethrographic anatomy.
- **Associated injuries.** Bladder laceration (intraperitoneal or extraperitoneal, shown by retrograde CT cystography) and rectal injury (wall defect, perirectal stranding, free air) change the operative plan. Unrecognized rectal injury complicates urethroplasty and diversion decisions.

**Planning delayed urethroplasty.** For a posterior urethral distraction defect, coronal and sagittal reconstructions show the position of the fractured pubic arch and prostate, the thickness of the inferior pubic arch, retropubic fibrosis, and the integrity of the ischiopubic rami and levator ani. Marked posterior displacement of the arch may lead to inferior pubectomy, and very long defects may need an abdominoperineal approach. These are planning aids and do not replace RUG and VCUG for defect length.

For suspected bladder injury, actively fill retrograde by gravity with dilute water-soluble contrast using an appropriate cystography protocol. EAU describes 300–350 mL for adult trauma evaluation when tolerated; fresh repairs, small bladders and children need an individualized volume. Passive excretion from IV contrast cannot provide an adequate negative study. Conventional and CT cystography have comparable diagnostic performance when properly performed; CT also shows surrounding injuries.<sup>[[6]](#ref6)</sup>

### CT retrograde urethrography

CT-RUG combines retrograde urethral contrast injection with CT acquisition during or immediately after injection, giving cross-sectional and 3D views of the urethra and periurethral tissue. The urologist manages injection and urethral filling in the CT suite while radiology controls acquisition timing, so the two teams agree on technique, contrast dilution and reconstructions beforehand.

It has been described for problems that fluoroscopic RUG and VCUG leave unresolved:

- spongiofibrosis extent and periurethral tissue characterization
- false passages, distinguishing the true lumen from a periurethral tract
- complex rectourethral or urethrocutaneous fistula anatomy and the tissue between the structures
- recurrent stricture after prior urethroplasty with distorted anatomy
- posterior urethral distraction defects in which prostate position and defect length are unclear
- urethral duplication or complex congenital anomalies

The technique is not standardized for acquisition, contrast concentration or reporting, the evidence is limited, and it exposes the patient to more radiation than fluoroscopic RUG. It is a problem-solving study. It does not replace the established RUG and VCUG pathway for routine stricture planning. MRI can add information in selected posterior stenoses and complex periurethral disease.<sup>[[11]](#ref11)</sup>

## Comparison With Other Modalities

| Feature | CTU | MAG3 renography | MR urography | Retrograde pyelography |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Anatomic detail | Excellent, 3D | Functional map only | Excellent, best soft-tissue contrast | Collecting system only |
| Function and drainage | None | Split function and drainage kinetics | Limited | None |
| Radiation | Yes | Yes, low relative to CT | None | Yes, fluoroscopy |
| Urothelial lesions | Filling defects and wall thickening | Cannot detect | Wall thickening and mass | Filling defects; ureteroscopy allows biopsy |
| Stones | Excellent on unenhanced CT | Poor | Poor | Radiolucent stones are missed |
| UPJ crossing vessel | Good with arterial phase | Cannot detect | Good with MR angiography | Cannot detect |
| Retroperitoneal fibrosis | Good | Cannot characterize | Very good | Suggests medial deviation |
| Effect of poor renal function | Contrast decision; poor excretion limits opacification | Curve quality falls | Gadolinium considerations | Unaffected |

Qualitative ratings summarize the general roles described above. Local availability and expertise decide the choice.

## 5. Retroperitoneal Fibrosis

Retroperitoneal fibrosis is a fibro-inflammatory process that forms a soft-tissue mantle around the infrarenal aorta and IVC and encases the ureters, and CT or MRI shows it. The typical CT appearance is homogeneous soft tissue around the aorta, most often in the lower lumbar segments, with ureteral encasement, hydronephrosis and medial deviation of the mid-ureters. Medial deviation contrasts with the lateral displacement produced by lymphadenopathy or retroperitoneal tumor. Enhancement varies with activity: active inflammatory disease enhances, and chronic fibrotic tissue enhances little.

Imaging distribution, clinical history and, when indicated, biopsy help distinguish idiopathic or IgG4-related disease from malignant and other secondary causes. FDG-PET/CT shows metabolic activity of the mantle and has been used to assess activity and to look for relapse. FDG avidity is not a stand-alone diagnosis or an automatic steroid prescription. Medical therapy, drainage and ureterolysis are complementary options selected according to the cause and renal threat.<sup>[[12]](#ref12)</sup>

Before ureterolysis, CT documents the level and length of encasement, whether the disease is unilateral or bilateral, ureteral perfusion, whether stenting has decompressed the collecting system, and omental vascularity and pedicle reach for a wrap.

Keep drug regimens and operative indications on their dedicated pages: [ureterolysis](/docs/surgical-techniques/04d-upper-tract-reconstruction/ureterolysis) and [ureteral stricture](/docs/clinical-conditions/03e-upper-tract/ureteral-stricture).

## 6. Follow-up and Reporting

No universal schedule applies for CTU after urethroplasty or a fixed wait for a suspected leak. New fever, pain, renal deterioration or concerning drain output calls for timely clinical assessment and the study best suited to the suspected complication. Stable postoperative surveillance is procedure- and risk-specific, and persistent dilatation alone does not prove failed reconstruction.<sup>[[5]](#ref5)[[8]](#ref8)</sup>

After ureteral reconstruction, pyeloplasty or diversion, CTU with an excretory phase can show extravasation along the ureter or around the renal pelvis, a perinephric urinoma that accumulates contrast over time, its size and communication with the collecting system, and the position of drains and stents. Comparing density before and after contrast helps separate urinoma from hematoma or lymphocele. Recurrent hydronephrosis after reconstruction is evaluated for an anastomotic stricture: an abrupt caliber change at the anastomosis, stricture length, periureteral scar and upstream dilation. Early postoperative studies can show edema, stent-related change and dilation from manipulation that overstate obstruction, so anatomic assessment of an anastomosis is usually more reliable after the stent is out and edema has settled.

Hydronephrosis that predated urethroplasty because of outlet obstruction is expected to improve after successful reconstruction, and the time course varies with severity and duration. Persistent mild dilatation with a normal MAG3 drainage curve represents residual dilation, not ongoing obstruction. CTU is preferred when the question is anatomic, for example persistent hydronephrosis after a technically successful urethroplasty that suggests an independent ureteral or UPJ process. MAG3 is preferred for functional questions and for serial surveillance, because it avoids repeated CT exposure.<sup>[[8]](#ref8)</sup>

A useful report identifies the indication, acquisitions and contrast, limitations of opacification, lesion or transition-point location, upstream findings, leaks or collections, stent or drain position and comparison with prior studies. Separate observed anatomy from inference about function or causation. If uncertainty will affect reconstruction, state which additional study could resolve it.<sup>[[2]](#ref2)[[8]](#ref8)</sup>
