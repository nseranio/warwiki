# Penile Doppler Ultrasound & Cavernosometry

**Penile Doppler ultrasound (PDUS)** is the primary minimally invasive imaging modality for evaluating penile hemodynamics, used to differentiate arteriogenic, venogenic, and mixed vasculogenic erectile dysfunction (ED), as well as to assess [Peyronie disease](/docs/clinical-conditions/03g-genital-scrotal/peyronies-disease) plaques, [penile fracture](/docs/special-populations/05a-trauma-emergencies/penile-fracture), and [priapism](/docs/special-populations/05a-trauma-emergencies/priapism).<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[2]](#ref2)</sup> **Dynamic infusion cavernosometry and cavernosography (DICC)** is a specialist invasive assessment with important false-positive and interpretation limitations. It is not routinely required to diagnose or treat ED.<sup>[[3]](#ref3)[[4]](#ref4)</sup>

For the broader ultrasound primer (sonourethrogram, renal US, pelvic floor US), see [Ultrasound in Reconstructive Urology](/docs/evaluation/imaging/ultrasound). For the clinical conditions evaluated by these tests, see [Erectile Dysfunction](/docs/clinical-conditions/03g-genital-scrotal/erectile-dysfunction) and the [PDE5 inhibitor](/docs/foundations/pharmacology/sexual-medicine-andrology/pde5-inhibitors), [intracavernosal injection agent](/docs/foundations/pharmacology/sexual-medicine-andrology/intracavernosal-injection-agents), and [priapism management](/docs/foundations/pharmacology/sexual-medicine-andrology/priapism-management) hubs.

## Physiology of Erection — Basis for Hemodynamic Testing

Normal erection involves three hemodynamic phases that produce characteristic Doppler waveform changes:<sup>[[5]](#ref5)</sup>

1. **Arterial inflow phase.** Cavernosal artery dilation leads to increased peak systolic velocity (PSV) and high diastolic flow.
2. **Tumescence phase.** Expanding sinusoids compress subtunical venules, leading to a progressive decrease in end-diastolic velocity (EDV).
3. **Full erection or rigid phase.** Complete veno-occlusion leads to EDV approaching zero or becoming negative (reversed diastolic flow), and the resistive index (RI) approaches 1.0.

Waveforms must be interpreted with the erection phase, rigidity and technical quality; an abnormal value does not uniquely identify the cause of ED.<sup>[[5]](#ref5)[[6]](#ref6)</sup>

## Penile Doppler Ultrasound (PDUS)

### Indications

- Selected ED with suspected vascular disease or an unclear mechanism when the result would change counseling or treatment; failure of an oral drug does not automatically require Doppler.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[7]](#ref7)</sup>
- Pre-surgical evaluation for Peyronie disease (plaque characterization, calcification, vascular integrity).<sup>[[8]](#ref8)[[9]](#ref9)</sup>
- **Penile fracture** with equivocal clinical findings.<sup>[[10]](#ref10)</sup>
- **Priapism.** Differentiation of ischemic and non-ischemic subtypes; localization of arteriovenous fistulae.<sup>[[11]](#ref11)[[12]](#ref12)</sup>
- Post-revascularization follow-up.<sup>[[2]](#ref2)</sup>
- Young men with post-traumatic ED being considered for penile revascularization.<sup>[[13]](#ref13)</sup>

### Equipment and Setup

- **High-frequency linear transducer** (7.5–15 MHz).<sup>[[2]](#ref2)[[6]](#ref6)</sup>
- Patient supine or in lithotomy position.
- Gray-scale, color Doppler, and spectral (pulsed-wave) Doppler modes.
- Private, comfortable environment to minimize anxiety-related sympathetic tone, which impairs smooth muscle relaxation and produces false-positive results.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup>

## Vasoactive Agents and Dosing Protocols

For elective vascular ED testing, adequate cavernosal smooth-muscle relaxation is necessary. Do not administer an erectogenic drug to evaluate an already prolonged erection or an acute suspected fracture. Those examinations answer different questions.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[2]](#ref2)</sup>

| Agent | Diagnostic context |
|---|---|
| **Alprostadil** | Caverject has a US diagnostic-adjunct indication. For diagnostic Doppler testing, its label calls for a single individualized dose producing a rigid erection using the vasculogenic/psychogenic/mixed ED titration regimen, which starts at 2.5 µg; the 1.25 µg start applies to treatment titration for purely neurogenic ED. A fixed 10–20 µg diagnostic starting dose is not the label regimen. The label contraindicates it in men prone to priapism (sickle cell disease or trait, multiple myeloma, leukemia), with penile implants, and for ED treatment with penile fibrosis or Peyronie's disease. It also advises avoiding Caverject in known cavernosal venous leakage because systemic alprostadil exposure may cause hypotension.<sup>[[14]](#ref14)</sup> |
| **Compounded combinations, such as Trimix** | Used in specialist protocols; concentrations and administered doses vary. These combinations are not FDA-approved formulations<sup>[[44]](#ref44)</sup> |
| **Papaverine, with or without phentolamine** | Historical/off-label intracavernosal protocols; account for prolonged erection and fibrosis risks |

<sup>[[7]](#ref7)[[14]](#ref14)[[15]](#ref15)</sup>

Published protocols differ. Bassiem's 100-patient study began alprostadil at 5 µg, escalating to 10 and 20 µg when needed.<sup>[[16]](#ref16)</sup> Flores describes a rigidity-based institutional protocol with redosing. Record dose, timing, stimulation and erection hardness; reaching a protocol's maximum dose does not prove full relaxation if rigidity remains inadequate. Report such limitations rather than assign definite vascular disease.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup>

Use the [intracavernosal-agent hub](/docs/foundations/pharmacology/sexual-medicine-andrology/intracavernosal-injection-agents) for drug preparation, contraindications and treatment dosing. Diagnostic redosing requires a trained team and a detumescence or rescue plan; it is not a home-injection instruction.

## Technique — Step by Step

1. **Baseline scan.** Gray-scale assessment of penile anatomy (cavernosal artery diameter, normal approximately 0.3–0.5 mm flaccid and typically greater than 0.7 mm in the normal erect state; tunica albuginea, septum, plaques, structural abnormalities).<sup>[[2]](#ref2)[[6]](#ref6)</sup>
2. **Intracavernosal injection (ICI).** For Caverject, inject into one corpus cavernosum at the right or left lateral proximal third of the penis using a 29–30-gauge half-inch needle perpendicular to the long dorsolateral penile axis. Avoid visible veins and compress the site for 5 minutes. Follow the administration instructions for other preparations.<sup>[[14]](#ref14)</sup>
3. **Serial Doppler measurements.** Time-based protocols commonly sample at intervals after injection; Flores uses a rigidity-based assessment. Record timing and hardness, and measure bilaterally at a standardized location. A clock interval alone does not establish maximal response.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[6]](#ref6)</sup>
4. **Doppler parameters recorded**:
   - **Peak systolic velocity (PSV)** at peak systole.
   - **End-diastolic velocity (EDV)** at end-diastole.
   - **Resistive index (RI)** is (PSV − EDV) / PSV.
   - **Acceleration time.** Time from onset of systole to peak systole.
   - **Cavernosal artery diameter** change (pre- and post-injection).
5. **Angle correction.** Doppler angle should be ≤ 60° for accurate velocity measurements.<sup>[[6]](#ref6)</sup>
6. **Post-procedure monitoring.** Confirm detumescence before discharge. A persistent penetration-rigid erection may need intracavernosal phenylephrine under the supervised rescue protocol, with blood pressure and heart-rate monitoring; refractory cases may require aspiration. Distinguish early post-injection prolonged erection from established ischemic priapism, which requires urgent treatment. See the [priapism management hub](/docs/foundations/pharmacology/sexual-medicine-andrology/priapism-management).<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[11]](#ref11)</sup>

## Sampling Location — A Critical Variable

The anatomic sampling site can materially change the result. Pagano and Stahl studied 52 men (104 cavernosal arteries): mean PSV was 52.9 cm/s at the crus, 29.5 proximally and 21.6 at mid-artery.<sup>[[20]](#ref20)</sup> EDV differences were not significant in that cohort; another 210-patient study did find location-related EDV differences.<sup>[[19]](#ref19)[[20]](#ref20)</sup>

The site, Doppler angle and erection phase are standardized and documented. The sampling site is not changed to obtain the diagnosis suspected clinically. Reference values must match the technique.

## Interpretation — Normal Values and Diagnostic Criteria

EAU describes PSV &gt;30 cm/s, EDV &lt;3 cm/s and RI &gt;0.8 as commonly normal. Other specialist protocols use EDV &lt;5 cm/s and different RI thresholds. State the criteria used rather than combine different protocols into one definitive classification.<sup>[[40]](#ref40)[[1]](#ref1)</sup>

| Parameter | Interpretation |
|---|---|
| **PSV** | Low values suggest impaired arterial inflow when stimulation and technique are adequate; borderline values and incomplete relaxation require caution |
| **EDV** | Persistent positive flow, commonly &gt;5 cm/s in published protocols, may suggest impaired veno-occlusion when arterial inflow and relaxation are adequate |
| **RI** | Derived from PSV and EDV; an adjunct rather than a stand-alone venous-leak diagnosis |
| **Acceleration / artery diameter** | Supplementary measurements; neither an isolated timing cutoff nor post-injection diameter establishes arterial disease |

A low PSV plus positive EDV during a suboptimal erection does not reliably prove “mixed arterial and venous disease.” Normal hemodynamics do not establish a psychogenic or neurogenic diagnosis; assess the broader history and examination.<sup>[[4]](#ref4)[[6]](#ref6)[[22]](#ref22)[[23]](#ref23)</sup>

Historical cohorts are useful for understanding measurement, not population prevalence. Kaufman's 743-patient DICC series reported 58.9% combined abnormalities in a detailed 124-patient single-center subgroup; it does not describe the entire series.<sup>[[13]](#ref13)</sup> Pathak's 259 men were selected from 2,043 referrals for sustained rigidity and normal Doppler parameters; their age-related PSV association does not define normality for all men.<sup>[[18]](#ref18)</sup>

## Diagnostic Accuracy of PDUS for Venous Leak

Chen and colleagues compared three composite Doppler criteria with cavernosography in 54 selected men with ED, 36 of whom were cavernosography-positive. Continuous deep dorsal vein flow with peak velocity &gt;3 cm/s plus PSV &gt;30 and EDV &gt;5 cm/s had reported sensitivity 91.7%, specificity 70.6% and accuracy 84.9%. Adding RI &lt;0.89 or &lt;0.80 increased specificity but reduced sensitivity and overall accuracy; the study did not evaluate an RI &lt;0.75 add-on.<sup>[[21]](#ref21)</sup> These estimates are study-specific and depend on an imperfect invasive reference standard.

**Venous-compression Doppler remains investigational.** Gutwein's proof-of-concept study included 40 men, but only 17 underwent compression and only 10 had CT comparison. The reported 90% versus 20% accuracy therefore comes from a small selected comparison. A nonsignificant P value of 0.096 does not establish equivalence to CT cavernosography.<sup>[[24]](#ref24)</sup>

## PDUS in Peyronie Disease

The AUA Peyronie Disease Guideline recommends an in-office ICI test with or without duplex Doppler ultrasound prior to invasive intervention such as intralesional therapy, prosthesis placement or surgery (Statement 2, Expert Opinion).<sup>[[8]](#ref8)</sup> When duplex is added, plaque size and density, calcified versus non-calcified plaque and penile vascular integrity can also be assessed.<sup>[[8]](#ref8)</sup> PDUS provides:<sup>[[9]](#ref9)[[25]](#ref25)[[26]](#ref26)</sup>

- **Plaque characterization.** Document plaque location, number, calcification and approximate size; ultrasound plaque-size measurement is inaccurate and operator-dependent, and calcification extent can affect the feasibility of the intended therapy.<sup>[[46]](#ref46)[[9]](#ref9)</sup>
- **Vascular assessment.** In Masterson’s 108-man cohort, 48/87 (55%) with questionnaire-defined ED and 20/33 (61%) with severe questionnaire scores had normal Doppler parameters. Masterson's data show discordance without assigning a psychological or mechanical cause.<sup>[[27]](#ref27)</sup>
- **Fibrosis.** Intracavernosal fibrosis was associated with difficulty maintaining erection in the cited cohort; do not extend that result to every fibrotic finding or infer causation.<sup>[[26]](#ref26)</sup>
- **Surgical planning.** PDUS helps determine whether plication, grafting, or [penile prosthesis](/docs/surgical-techniques/04j-sexual-dysfunction) is most appropriate.<sup>[[25]](#ref25)</sup>

## PDUS in Priapism

The AUA/SMSNA permits penile duplex Doppler when ischemic versus non-ischemic priapism remains indeterminate (Expert Opinion), whereas EAU 2026 strongly recommends penile and perineal color duplex before corporal aspiration; Doppler findings include:<sup>[[11]](#ref11)[[12]](#ref12)[[28]](#ref28)[[47]](#ref47)</sup>

- **Ischemic priapism.** Absent or severely diminished cavernosal artery flow; diastolic flow may be absent or reversed.<sup>[[11]](#ref11)</sup>
- **Non-ischemic priapism.** Normal to high cavernosal artery velocities; turbulent flow at the site of arteriovenous fistula; color aliasing. A single case report described cavernosal artery peak systolic velocity above 50 cm/sec with elevated end-diastolic velocity, arterialized draining-vein waveforms and a high-velocity, turbulent focus at the fistula itself; this specific cutoff is illustrative, not a validated diagnostic threshold.<sup>[[41]](#ref41)</sup>
- **Fistula localization.** PDUS identifies the location and size of arterio-lacunar fistulae, guiding selective embolization planning.<sup>[[11]](#ref11)</sup> One described acquisition scans the perineum in color-flow mode with the patient frog-legged, beginning at the proximal corpora cavernosa and moving distally, together with examination of the entire penis.<sup>[[42]](#ref42)[[11]](#ref11)</sup>

The AUA/SMSNA Priapism Guideline states that, in diagnosed non-ischemic priapism, the clinician should consider penile duplex ultrasound to assess fistula location and size (Statement 24, Expert Opinion). Doppler ultrasound may be used when the ischemic versus non-ischemic diagnosis is indeterminate (Statement 3, Expert Opinion); it is not a routine prerequisite to urgent ischemic-priapism treatment.<sup>[[11]](#ref11)</sup>

After shunting, persistent rigidity can reflect reactive hyperemia or conversion to non-ischemic priapism. For persistent erection after shunting for acute ischemic priapism, AUA/SMSNA advises corporal blood gas testing or color duplex Doppler before repeat surgery to assess cavernous oxygenation or arterial inflow (Statement 13, Moderate Recommendation, Grade C); examination alone is insufficient.<sup>[[11]](#ref11)</sup>

The STIFF protocol (Sonography to Identify Forward Flow) was described in a single case report using glans compression during Doppler. It has not been validated to predict response or justify withholding phenylephrine; it should not delay established ischemic-priapism treatment.<sup>[[29]](#ref29)[[11]](#ref11)</sup>

## PDUS in Penile Fracture

The 2020 AUA Urotrauma Guideline permits ultrasound when penile-fracture findings are equivocal (Statement 28; Expert Opinion).<sup>[[10]](#ref10)</sup> Ultrasound can:<sup>[[10]](#ref10)[[30]](#ref30)</sup>

- Identify tunica albuginea disruption (discontinuity of the hyperechoic tunica).
- Differentiate intracavernosal from extracavernosal hematomas.

Routine ultrasound is unnecessary when penile fracture is clinically clear; MRI is an alternative when ultrasound is equivocal or unavailable, and surgical exploration should follow if imaging is equivocal or the diagnosis remains in doubt.<sup>[[10]](#ref10)</sup> In penile fracture with blood at the meatus, gross hematuria or inability to void, AUA Statement 29 requires evaluation for urethral injury (Standard, Grade B) by urethroscopy or retrograde urethrography.<sup>[[45]](#ref45)</sup>

## Dynamic Infusion Cavernosometry and Cavernosography (DICC)

### Overview

DICC measures pressure and flow relationships and can map venous drainage. Its positive predictive value and false-positive rate in potent men remain uncertain; the 2013 SOP explicitly recognizes these limitations.<sup>[[3]](#ref3)[[4]](#ref4)</sup> However, its use has declined substantially as venous ligation surgery has demonstrated poor long-term outcomes.<sup>[[4]](#ref4)[[31]](#ref31)</sup>

### Technique and reported parameters

Specialist protocols use separate corporal access for pressure measurement and infusion after pharmacologic relaxation. They record equilibrium pressure, **flow-to-maintain (FTM)**, pressure decay after stopping infusion, and sometimes contrast drainage pathways. Document the infusion pressure, drug regimen, units and image timing.<sup>[[4]](#ref4)[[13]](#ref13)[[17]](#ref17)</sup>

The historical Kaufman protocol assessed pressure decay from 150 mmHg over 30 seconds and performed cavernosography at 90 mmHg. Other studies used different target pressures. FTM values obtained at different pressures are not interchangeable. Likewise, mL/second and mL/minute differ sixtyfold.<sup>[[13]](#ref13)[[31]](#ref31)</sup>

Gao's 147-patient observational protocol used flow expressed in mL/second and pressure-decay criteria. Its treatment-linked severity categories are not a consensus grading system. Do not use these study-specific categories as automatic indications for embolization or prosthesis placement.<sup>[[3]](#ref3)</sup> The small controlled Lowe study found substantial overlap in flow and resistance between potent and impotent men, reinforcing the need to interpret the entire study.<sup>[[32]](#ref32)</sup>

### Limitations of DICC<sup>[[4]](#ref4)[[31]](#ref31)</sup>

- **Invasive.** Requires corporal puncture, contrast injection, and fluoroscopy.
- **False positives.** Incomplete smooth muscle relaxation is the most common cause of false-positive results.<sup>[[4]](#ref4)[[33]](#ref33)</sup>
- **Cavernosography adds limited value.** 46% of men with abnormal FTM values had a normal cavernosogram, undermining the utility of the radiographic component, particularly in low-grade venous leak.<sup>[[31]](#ref31)</sup>
- **Declining clinical relevance.** As venous ligation surgery has fallen out of favor due to poor long-term outcomes, the anatomic information from cavernosography is less actionable.<sup>[[31]](#ref31)[[34]](#ref34)</sup>

### Gravity Cavernosometry

A 1991 comparison in 96 men found comparable diagnostic value for gravity and pump techniques, with practical advantages for gravity infusion. Incomplete relaxation limited both.<sup>[[33]](#ref33)</sup>

## Pudendal Arteriography

Selective internal pudendal arteriography defines arterial anatomy in selected patients being considered for penile revascularization surgery, often young men with post-traumatic ED.<sup>[[13]](#ref13)[[22]](#ref22)[[40]](#ref40)</sup> In the multi-institutional DICC study, 169 patients underwent arteriography, and 105 proceeded to arterial bypass surgery.<sup>[[13]](#ref13)</sup> It is not used as a screening tool due to its invasiveness.

## Advanced Imaging Modalities

| Modality | Role | Advantages | Limitations |
|---|---|---|---|
| **CT cavernosography** | Venous leak mapping; validation of PDUS findings | 3D anatomic detail; identifies venous drainage patterns | Radiation; contrast; invasive |
| **CT angiography** | Pudendal artery anatomy for embolization planning (NIP) | Vascular roadmap | Radiation; contrast |
| **MRI** | Penile fracture (equivocal US); tumor staging; cavernosal necrosis in ischemic priapism | Superior soft-tissue contrast; no radiation | Cost; availability; limited hemodynamic data |
| **Time-resolved MRA** | Arteriovenous shunting; venous leak localization | Temporal + spatial resolution; 85.2% concordance with Doppler for shunt/AVM in a selected 26-man study<sup>[[35]](#ref35)</sup> | Requires contrast + ICI; limited availability |
| **Penile angiography** | Pre-revascularization arterial mapping; therapeutic embolization (NIP) | Detailed arterial mapping<sup>[[22]](#ref22)[[40]](#ref40)</sup>; therapeutic capability | Invasive; radiation; contrast |

Sources: <sup>[[12]](#ref12)[[13]](#ref13)[[24]](#ref24)[[35]](#ref35)[[36]](#ref36)[[37]](#ref37)[[11]](#ref11)</sup>

MRI assessment of corporal viability is a selected later question; it must not delay initial treatment of acute ischemic priapism.<sup>[[11]](#ref11)</sup>

Doppler is the usual hemodynamic examination; MRI can answer selected structural or oncologic questions. Historical reports of plaque detection do not make MRI a routine Peyronie test.<sup>[[36]](#ref36)</sup>

## PDUS as a Cardiovascular Risk Marker

ED is increasingly recognized as an early manifestation of systemic vascular disease. A 2026 retrospective study of 275 men used PSV &lt;35 cm/s and found an association with estimated QRISK3 risk. It was not independent after adjustment for age and diabetes, discrimination was modest (AUC 0.60), and adding PSV barely changed the clinical model. It does not validate PDUS as a cardiovascular screening test or demonstrate prediction of future events.<sup>[[38]](#ref38)</sup>

## Practical Pearls and Pitfalls

- **Incomplete smooth muscle relaxation** can produce false-positive results on both PDUS and DICC. Consider stimulation and supervised redosing when appropriate; respect drug-specific limits and document an indeterminate response.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[4]](#ref4)[[33]](#ref33)</sup>
- **Anxiety-mediated sympathetic tone** can produce falsely low PSV and falsely elevated EDV. A private, comfortable environment is necessary.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup>
- **Sampling location matters.** The large mean differences reported in one study illustrate the need for a consistent documented site; they are not correction factors to add to an individual result.<sup>[[20]](#ref20)</sup>
- **Asymmetric cavernosal artery flow** (significant PSV difference between right and left) suggests unilateral arterial disease.<sup>[[23]](#ref23)</sup>
- **Direct venous visualization is not the same as functional assessment.** The cited 1990 study of 13 men could not visualize cavernosal venous leaks.<sup>[[39]](#ref39)</sup>
- **Peyronie patients.** Questionnaire severity can reflect curvature-related intercourse difficulty as well as erectile function. Interpret discordant Doppler findings individually.<sup>[[27]](#ref27)</sup>

---

## Summary of Diagnostic Algorithm

1. Begin with history, examination, symptom assessment and appropriate metabolic or hormonal evaluation. For predominantly vasculogenic ED, assess cardiovascular risk independently of the Doppler result; Princeton IV recommends estimating 10-year ASCVD risk.<sup>[[43]](#ref43)</sup>
2. Add PDUS when a vascular question would change management, including selected post-traumatic ED or Peyronie planning.
3. Reserve DICC or cavernosography for a specific specialist question. It is not the automatic next step after Doppler, and venous ligation is not recommended as routine ED treatment.
4. Use pudendal arteriography for selected revascularization candidates; it is not general screening.

Most men do not need an escalating sequence of vascular tests. Discuss treatment according to goals and clinical findings, without requiring an invasive test merely to authorize effective ED care.<sup>[[7]](#ref7)[[40]](#ref40)</sup>
