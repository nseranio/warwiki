# Ultrasound in Reconstructive Urology

**Ultrasound** images tissue in real time without ionizing radiation. It assesses bladder emptying, upper-tract dilatation, anterior urethral anatomy and selected pelvic-floor problems.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[5]](#ref5)[[7]](#ref7)</sup> Its usefulness depends on the clinical question, technique and operator. A bright scar, an enlarged hiatus or a dilated collecting system is an observation to interpret, not an indication to operate.

**At a glance:** bladder ultrasound measures PVR, renal ultrasound follows upper-tract dilatation according to symptoms and risk, and sonourethrography or pelvic-floor ultrasound is added when the extra anatomy can change the evaluation. Neither a single residual volume nor sonographic fibrosis depth is a universal treatment threshold.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[5]](#ref5)[[8]](#ref8)</sup>

## 1. Overview: Why Ultrasound in Reconstructive Urology?

The retrograde urethrogram (RUG) shows the contrast-filled lumen. It does not show the periurethral tissue, so it cannot show spongiofibrosis, the fibrous replacement of the corpus spongiosum in stricture disease. Sonourethrography (SUG) distends the urethra with saline and images the lumen and surrounding spongiosum together.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[2]](#ref2)[[16]](#ref16)</sup>

| Examination | Main reconstructive question | Companion |
|---|---|---|
| Bladder ultrasound/PVR | Is emptying incomplete? | Voided volume, symptoms, repeat measurement when needed |
| Renal ultrasound | Is there collecting-system dilatation or parenchymal change? | Clinical history, renal function, comparison imaging |
| Sonourethrogram (SUG) | What are the anterior stricture's length and surrounding appearance? | [RUG/VCUG](/docs/evaluation/imaging/rug-vcug) and operative findings |
| Pelvic-floor ultrasound | Is there a specific support, levator or implant-related question? | Examination and symptom correlation |
| Penile duplex / endoanal ultrasound | Selected vascular or anal-sphincter assessment | Dedicated specialist protocols |

RUG and SUG differ in what each shows:

| Feature | RUG | SUG |
|---|---|---|
| Lumen | Direct contrast opacification | Good when distension is adequate |
| Periurethral tissue and spongiofibrosis | Not shown | Shown as distensibility and echogenicity change |
| Stricture length | Underestimates bulbar length in comparative series | Closer to operative length in comparative series |
| Radiation and iodinated contrast | Yes | None (saline distension) |
| Posterior urethra | Assessed with VCUG | Not adequately imaged |
| Operator dependence | Moderate | High; requires specific training |

This page describes indications and interpretation. It does not replace supervised acquisition training.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[5]](#ref5)[[7]](#ref7)[[11]](#ref11)[[12]](#ref12)[[13]](#ref13)</sup>

## 2. Sonourethrogram (SUG): Overview and Indications

<span id="what-sug-shows-that-rug-cannot"></span>
<span id="when-to-order-sug"></span>

SUG depicts the distended anterior urethra and adjacent tissues. It identifies stricture site, number and caliber, and it shows calculi, diverticula and false passages.<sup>[[3]](#ref3)</sup> Spongiofibrosis appears as loss of urethral distensibility during saline instillation, and posterior shadowing has been described in severe post-traumatic strictures. In complex or reoperative anterior strictures, sonographic staging also shows calculi, urethral hair, false passages and stent encrustation.<sup>[[4]](#ref4)</sup> The EAU describes SUG as a three-dimensional, outpatient, radiation-free assessment of location, length and associated spongiofibrosis.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup>

Length is the main practical gain:

- In the original 17-patient series, SUG length was consistently closer to the open-urethroplasty measurement than RUG length in the 7 patients compared.<sup>[[2]](#ref2)</sup>
- In 101 patients (123 paired studies), the penile-urethra correlation with operative length was similar for the two tests (0.91 vs 0.98). In the bulb, SUG correlated better (0.69 for RUG, 0.89 for SUG). SUG indicated a different reconstructive procedure from the RUG-based plan in 16%.<sup>[[3]](#ref3)</sup>
- In 67 men with bulbar strictures of 25 mm or less on RUG, sonographic measurements were frequently twice the RUG length. SUG changed the chosen procedure in 15 of 41 men (37%) whose RUG length was 11 to 25 mm, to flap or graft procedures. It changed nothing in the 26 men with strictures of 10 mm or less, all of whom had excision and end-to-end anastomosis.<sup>[[14]](#ref14)</sup>
- In 52 men with anterior strictures, mean length was 2.0 cm on RUG, 3.8 cm on SUG and 3.5 cm at surgery.<sup>[[15]](#ref15)</sup>
- The EAU summarizes that the preoperative SUG findings changed a RUG-based surgical plan in 31% of cases.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup>

SUG is an adjunct that requires expertise and is not required before every urethroplasty.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[4]](#ref4)</sup> It is most useful when the surgical choice may change with the tissue findings:

- complex or reoperative strictures, including prior failed dilation, urethrotomy or urethroplasty;
- bulbar strictures of intermediate length on RUG, where excision versus substitution is undecided;
- suspected calculus, false passage, diverticulum or periurethral pathology;
- a clinical picture (symptoms, flow rate, cystoscopy) that suggests a longer or more severe stricture than RUG shows.

A short first-time stricture in a man without prior instrumentation can be planned from RUG and cystoscopy.<sup>[[14]](#ref14)</sup> SUG is used more often in lichen sclerosus and post-hypospadias strictures because these are usually complex, although the cited series do not test that indication separately.

The main domain is anterior disease. For nearly obliterative stenosis or PFUI, combined RUG and VCUG and selected endoscopy or MRI remain necessary because conventional SUG assesses the posterior urethra poorly.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[2]](#ref2)</sup>

## 3. SUG: Equipment

Use equipment suited to superficial urethral imaging: a high-frequency linear transducer (7.5 MHz or higher is typical), B-mode imaging and a trained operator. A curved low-frequency abdominal probe lacks the resolution to separate the layers of the urethral wall. The study also needs an aseptic method of gentle urethral distension and a way to document longitudinal and transverse images. Color or power Doppler is an optional adjunct. Probe selection and distal versus bulbar windows depend on the target segment.<sup>[[2]](#ref2)[[4]](#ref4)[[16]](#ref16)</sup>

Set-ups described in practice use sterile saline as the distension medium (lidocaine gel gives analgesia but adds artifact) and either a Foley catheter with the balloon inflated in the fossa navicularis or a penile clamp to hold the meatus. Sterile gloves, a transducer cover and acoustic gel complete the sterile field. These details come from technique descriptions rather than comparative studies.

## 4. SUG: Technique

<span id="patient-preparation-and-positioning"></span>
<span id="scanning-planes-and-probe-positions"></span>

<span id="distension-and-imaging-protocol"></span>

### Acquisition principles

SUG evaluates the anterior urethra during retrograde fluid distension. The EAU notes that distension requires intraurethral anesthesia.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup> The steps below are a commonly described sequence and should be adapted to the local trained-service protocol for anesthesia, sterile preparation and meatal sealing.<sup>[[2]](#ref2)[[4]](#ref4)</sup>

1. The patient lies supine with the penis stretched along the lower abdomen. This aligns the penile urethra for a longitudinal scan. The scrotum is retracted to expose the perineum for bulbar imaging.
2. The catheter tip or clamp seals the meatus.
3. Sterile saline is injected slowly and continuously while scanning. Under-distension leaves the walls collapsed, and collapsed walls look falsely echogenic.
4. Injection stops if the patient has pain or the resistance is high. The purpose is imaging, not dilation.

Catheter size, balloon volume, injection pressure and total volume are not fixed by the literature. Some strictures pass only a small volume. Inadequate distension is recorded as a limitation rather than interpreted as a precise scar boundary.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[4]](#ref4)</sup>

**Penile urethra.** The probe sits on the ventral penile surface. The longitudinal view shows the tubular urethra with a continuous mucosal line, and irregularity, narrowing or hyperechogenicity marks a stricture. The transverse view shows the corpus spongiosum around the lumen, and fibrosis appears as asymmetric or circumferential echogenicity in place of normal spongiosum.<sup>[[2]](#ref2)[[4]](#ref4)</sup>

**Bulbar urethra.** The bulbar urethra curves into the perineum and is not seen from the ventral penile surface. The probe is placed in the midline perineum between the posterior scrotum and the anus and angled anterosuperiorly. A slightly parasagittal orientation often gives the best longitudinal view. McAninch and colleagues also described a transscrotal approach.<sup>[[2]](#ref2)[[4]](#ref4)</sup> The proximal bulbar urethra near the perineal body can be difficult to image.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup>

<span id="what-to-document-at-each-stricture-site"></span>

### Reporting checklist

| Parameter | How it is described | Use |
|---|---|---|
| Segment, number and length of narrowings | Longitudinal scan, proximal to distal extent of the abnormal segment | Repair selection (excision versus onlay or substitution) |
| Luminal distensibility and caliber | Transverse scan at the narrowest point | Severity |
| Surrounding echogenicity and depth | Transverse scan, from mucosa toward the tunica albuginea | Extent of spongiofibrosis |
| Calcification | Hyperechoic foci with posterior shadowing | Severe, often post-traumatic disease |
| Extension beyond the spongiosum | Abnormal tissue crossing the tunica albuginea | Complex reconstruction |
| False passage, diverticulum, stone, hair, stent or implant | Direct visualization | Associated findings |
| Proximal extent | Relation to the perineal body and membranous urethra | Need for perineal access |

Describe measurement conditions and any disagreement with RUG. Measure the abnormal segment, not only the narrowed lumen. Some surgeons add a margin to the imaging length when planning the incision or graft. That practice is not validated, so the imaging measurement is not automatically increased by a fixed distance to set graft length or resection margins.<sup>[[3]](#ref3)[[4]](#ref4)</sup>

## 5. Normal Ultrasound Anatomy of the Urethra

Fluid distension outlines the lumen within the corpus spongiosum. The layers are recognized by echogenicity:

| Structure | Echogenicity | Appearance |
|---|---|---|
| Urethral mucosa | Hyperechoic | Thin bright line around the distended lumen |
| Distended lumen | Anechoic | Smooth fluid-filled channel of consistent caliber |
| Corpus spongiosum | Intermediate, homogeneous | Uniform tissue around the urethra |
| Tunica albuginea | Hyperechoic | Thin bright line at the outer margin of the spongiosum |
| Corpora cavernosa | Intermediate | Paired bodies dorsal and lateral to the spongiosum, separated by the septum |
| Bulbospongiosus and ischiocavernosus (perineal view) | Intermediate | Muscle layers around the bulb |

Focal or diffuse hyperechogenicity in the spongiosum, an irregular mucosal line or a narrowed lumen indicates disease. Appearance varies with scan plane and acquisition conditions, and the fossa navicularis is naturally more fibrous, so comparison with adjacent urethra helps interpretation.<sup>[[2]](#ref2)[[4]](#ref4)</sup>

The sonographic border of an abnormal segment is not a histological margin. The operative assessment of tissue and reconstructive feasibility remains necessary.<sup>[[3]](#ref3)</sup>

<span id="6-spongiofibrosis-grading-mcaninch--morey-classification"></span>

## 6. Spongiofibrosis: Descriptive Assessment

<span id="grading-pearls"></span>

Periurethral abnormality and distensibility are best described rather than forced into a universal four-grade "McAninch/Morey" scale that dictates treatment. The literature supports sonographic staging but does not validate a depth-to-operation algorithm.<sup>[[2]](#ref2)[[3]](#ref3)[[4]](#ref4)</sup>

The direct evidence is limited. Nash and colleagues found SUG unreliable at predicting the depth of spongiofibrosis against full-depth biopsies in 36 patients. In 52 men, Gupta and colleagues reported SUG accuracy for spongiofibrosis of 42%, 56% and 83% for mild, moderate and severe degrees. The EAU states that the clinical relevance of assessing spongiofibrosis preoperatively remains to be established, and it describes sonourethrography with real-time elastography as a qualitative and quantitative option under investigation.<sup>[[3]](#ref3)[[15]](#ref15)[[1]](#ref1)</sup>

Echogenicity, circumferential involvement or an elastography value therefore does not mandate an endoscopic, excisional, onlay or staged operation. See the [urethral stricture pathway](/docs/clinical-conditions/03b-voiding-outlet/urethral-stricture) for treatment selection.

## 7. Evidence: SUG vs. RUG

<span id="key-studies"></span>
<span id="comparative-summary-table"></span>

| Primary study | Design and sample | What it supports | What it does not establish |
|---|---|---|---|
| McAninch et al., 1988 | Preliminary series: 17 patients; operative length comparison in 7, full-depth biopsy in 5 | Feasibility and closer length agreement than RUG; classification of spongiofibrosis by distensibility | Population-wide accuracy or definitive fibrosis grading |
| Nash et al., 1995 | 123 paired examinations in 101 patients with 110 strictures | Better length correlation in the bulbar subgroup; detection of calculi, diverticula and false passages | Reliable histological fibrosis depth; superiority in every segment |
| Morey and McAninch, 1997 | 67 men with bulbar strictures of 25 mm or less on RUG | Sonographic length often about twice RUG length; procedure changed in 37% of intermediate-length strictures | Improved outcomes after the changed procedure |
| Gupta et al., 2006 | 52 men with anterior strictures, compared with operative findings | Mean length 2.0 (RUG), 3.8 (SUG) and 3.5 cm (surgery); modest spongiofibrosis accuracy | Reliable grading of mild or moderate fibrosis |

These studies support complementary imaging in selected patients. They do not show that RUG underestimates length by a fixed percentage in all contemporary cases, or that every SUG-driven change improves clinical outcomes.<sup>[[2]](#ref2)[[3]](#ref3)[[14]](#ref14)[[15]](#ref15)</sup> RUG remains the imaging of the whole urethra, including the posterior segment, and most reconstructive surgeons use the two tests together when SUG is available.

## 8. SUG: Limitations

| Limitation | Detail |
|---|---|
| Operator dependence | SUG needs specialized training in ultrasound and is not in widespread use.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup> |
| Bulbar segment | The EAU lists lower sensitivity for detecting bulbar strictures. The proximal bulbar urethra is hard to image, particularly through the perineum in larger patients.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[3]](#ref3)</sup> |
| Posterior urethra | Membranous and prostatic segments are not adequately imaged. PFUI needs RUG and VCUG, with selected endoscopy or MRI.<sup>[[2]](#ref2)[[1]](#ref1)</sup> |
| Distension | It requires intraurethral anesthesia, and patients who cannot tolerate adequate volume give limited images.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup> |
| Fibrosis depth | Not reliable against biopsy in the largest available comparison.<sup>[[3]](#ref3)</sup> |

An incompletely visualized segment is recorded as such rather than reported as normal, and further imaging depends on the unresolved question.

## 9. Renal Ultrasound

<span id="technical-considerations"></span>
<span id="when-to-upgrade-to-mag3-renal-scintigraphy"></span>
<span id="doppler-ultrasound-for-renovascular-assessment"></span>

<span id="role-in-reconstructive-urology"></span>

### Indications and report

Renal ultrasound is a first-line, radiation-free study when upper-tract dilatation or parenchymal change is relevant to obstruction, reconstruction or a surveillance plan.<sup>[[7]](#ref7)</sup> Typical reconstructive settings are:

- baseline assessment in stricture, bladder outlet obstruction or neurogenic bladder;
- follow-up after urinary diversion, pyeloplasty or ureteral reconstruction;
- new or worsening dilatation after urethroplasty or reimplantation;
- transplant ureteral anastomosis.

The examination scans in supine and decubitus positions through different acoustic windows, using a low-frequency curvilinear probe. Report laterality, extent of calyceal or pelvic dilatation, visible ureteral change, cortical thickness, parenchymal appearance and relevant bladder findings. Compare prior studies and document conditions that may affect interpretation, such as bladder fullness.<sup>[[7]](#ref7)</sup>

<span id="hydronephrosis-grading-sfu--esur-system"></span>

### Hydronephrosis is not synonymous with obstruction

Dilatation may persist after a successful repair, and its importance depends on the clinical setting.<sup>[[7]](#ref7)</sup> The Society for Fetal Urology system is the grading most often used. It is qualitative and was developed for infants: grade 0 is normal; grade 1 is renal pelvic dilatation only; grade 2 adds some calyces; grade 3 involves all calyces with normal parenchyma; grade 4 adds parenchymal thinning.<sup>[[17]](#ref17)</sup> It does not define adult anteroposterior pelvic-diameter cutoffs, and no universal adult diameter table should be applied. If a formal grade is reported, the report names the system and its intended population.

Ultrasound cannot measure differential function or drainage. When the unresolved issue is drainage or split function, [MAG3](/docs/evaluation/imaging/mag3-renal-scintigraphy) answers it. When anatomy or cause remains unclear, [CT urography](/docs/evaluation/imaging/ct-urogram) or [MRU](/docs/evaluation/imaging/mri#9-upper-tract--mr-urography-mru) may help. The ACR lists ultrasound with Doppler, CT urography, MR urography and MAG3 as viable initial studies in symptomatic patients with hydronephrosis of unknown cause. Escalation depends on symptoms, renal function, prior surgery and change over time, not on ultrasound grade alone.<sup>[[7]](#ref7)</sup>

<span id="upper-tract-surveillance"></span>

### Risk-based follow-up

For adult NLUTD, the AUA/SUFU guideline recommends no surveillance upper-tract imaging in low-risk patients with stable signs and symptoms, imaging every 1 to 2 years in moderate-risk patients and annual imaging in high-risk patients (Moderate Recommendations, evidence level C). New symptoms, infections, stones or renal deterioration prompt reassessment. The EAU strongly recommends regular upper-tract assessment in high-risk neurogenic patients.<sup>[[8]](#ref8)</sup> These intervals belong to the defined NLUTD risk groups and are not copied to every BPH, stricture or diversion patient.

Renal artery duplex needs a vascular question and a laboratory-validated protocol. In 313 patients (606 arteries) compared with angiography, a peak systolic velocity of 180 cm/s or more with a renal-to-aortic ratio of 3.5 or more detected 60% or greater stenosis with sensitivity 72% and specificity 81%, and the best single cutoffs were 285 cm/s and 3.7. The authors advised validation in each vascular laboratory. A resistive index alone is not proof of obstruction and does not replace evaluation of the collecting system and renal function.<sup>[[7]](#ref7)[[21]](#ref21)</sup>

<span id="post-void-residual"></span>

### Bladder ultrasound and PVR

The EAU recommends measuring PVR at the initial assessment of LUTS (Strong) and prefers ultrasound to catheterization for the risk-benefit profile. NICE also prefers a bladder scan to catheterization in women with suspected voiding dysfunction or recurrent UTI.<sup>[[5]](#ref5)[[6]](#ref6)</sup> Timing and voided volume are recorded from a representative void where practical. In women, the EAU identifies no consensus threshold for a significant PVR and prefers bladder voiding efficiency, VV/(VV + PVR) × 100. An unexpected value is reconciled with the clinical findings rather than used alone to diagnose obstruction or prescribe catheterization.<sup>[[5]](#ref5)</sup>

NICE advises against imaging in routine assessment of uncomplicated female urinary incontinence and against ultrasound other than for residual urine. Specific concerns are investigated rather than screened with pelvic MRI or ultrasound.<sup>[[6]](#ref6)</sup>

## 10. Pelvic Floor Ultrasound (Transperineal / Translabial)

<span id="equipment-and-setup"></span>
<span id="levator-ani-avulsion-the-dietz-technique"></span>
<span id="rectocele-assessment"></span>
<span id="comparison-to-dynamic-mri"></span>

<span id="overview-and-role-in-reconstructive-urology"></span>

### Selective clinical use

Transperineal (translabial) ultrasound shows levator morphology, bladder-neck mobility and pelvic-organ descent in real time through the perineum. It is used for selected support, levator, or sling and mesh questions. Routine imaging to document clinically demonstrable prolapse is unnecessary. When imaging is useful, ultrasound and dynamic MRI provide different views, and performance depends on technique and expertise.<sup>[[6]](#ref6)[[11]](#ref11)</sup>

A curved-array transducer (about 3.5 to 8 MHz) is placed on the perineum with the patient in dorsal lithotomy or supine. Three- and four-dimensional volumes are needed for levator assessment. Acquisitions are usually made at rest, on maximal Valsalva and on pelvic-floor contraction, with bladder filling adjusted to the question. These are conventional set-up details rather than trial-tested parameters.<sup>[[9]](#ref9)[[10]](#ref10)</sup>

<span id="levator-hiatus-assessment"></span>

### Hiatus and levator injury

The methods and maneuver are stated when hiatal dimensions are reported. Dietz and colleagues proposed a Valsalva hiatal area above 25 cm² as "ballooning" in a retrospective tertiary-clinic dataset of 544 women. For symptomatic prolapse, that cutoff had sensitivity 0.55 and specificity 0.77, and the area under the ROC curve for Valsalva hiatal area was 0.71. It is an association-based imaging definition, not a stand-alone diagnosis or a treatment threshold.<sup>[[9]](#ref9)</sup>

Levator avulsion (detachment of the pubovisceral muscle from the pelvic sidewall) is assessed on three-dimensional or tomographic translabial images by inspecting the muscle insertion in serial slices. In a prospective series of 61 nulliparous women, avulsion was found in 14 of 39 women (36%) after vaginal delivery and was associated with stress incontinence 3 months postpartum. In 262 women referred with lower urinary tract dysfunction or prolapse, 19% had avulsion (unilateral or bilateral), and defect score was higher in women with prolapse symptoms and associated with cystocele but not with incontinence.<sup>[[18]](#ref18)[[10]](#ref10)</sup> Report side, extent, method and uncertainty. A defect is not translated directly into an indication for mesh repair, and a single loosely specified levator-urethra gap is not enough to diagnose avulsion.<sup>[[10]](#ref10)</sup>

<span id="bladder-neck-mobility-and-urethral-hypermobility"></span>

### Mobility and posterior compartment

Bladder-neck position relative to the symphysis, descent on Valsalva, and funneling of the proximal urethra can be documented. Bladder-neck movement describes mobility and does not independently establish symptomatic stress incontinence. In the posterior compartment, ultrasound can show rectal herniation on Valsalva and can separate an enterocele (echogenic bowel content that moves) from a rectocele. An image of rectal bulging needs correlation with bowel symptoms and examination. A universal 10-mm bladder-neck or rectocele threshold is not an adequate basis for surgery.<sup>[[5]](#ref5)[[6]](#ref6)[[11]](#ref11)</sup>

| Feature | Transperineal ultrasound | Dynamic pelvic MRI |
|---|---|---|
| Real-time dynamic imaging | Yes, including 4D | Limited by acquisition time |
| Levator avulsion | Well shown on 3D/4D | Well shown |
| Multi-compartment and apical assessment | Limited | Better |
| Fascial planes | Limited | Better |
| Patient tolerance and cost | Well tolerated, low cost | Longer study, higher cost |
| Operator dependence | High | Moderate |

Dynamic MRI is preferred for complex multi-compartment or apical questions, and ultrasound for serial follow-up, dynamic assessment and levator mapping. The table summarizes common practice and is not drawn from a head-to-head trial.<sup>[[11]](#ref11)</sup>

<span id="mesh-and-sling-evaluation"></span>

### Mesh and sling evaluation

The implant type and operation history are provided with the request. Ultrasound can locate accessible sling or mesh material and assess its relation to adjacent structures. Polypropylene is echogenic, and synthetic mesh can be hard to separate from scar on MRI.<sup>[[6]](#ref6)[[11]](#ref11)</sup> Two studies quantify performance:

- In 100 women with lower urinary tract symptoms after mesh or sling surgery, combined translabial and transvaginal ultrasound had 100% sensitivity, 98% to 100% specificity and 100% negative predictive value against cystourethroscopy for lower-urinary-tract exposure. Exposure was uncommon (3%), and positive predictive value was 33% to 50% for the urethra. The authors concluded that it is a screening test to exclude exposure.<sup>[[19]](#ref19)</sup>
- In 124 women with suspected mesh complications after midurethral sling (12% with erosion into the urethra or bladder at surgery), translabial ultrasound had sensitivity 53% and specificity 100% when only intraluminal mesh counted, and sensitivity 93% and specificity 72% when intramural mesh also counted. Cystourethroscopy had sensitivity 67%.<sup>[[20]](#ref20)</sup>

Retropubic, obturator and sacrocolpopexy components are less well seen, and deeper or unresolved problems may need MRI. No modality excludes every complication. Clinical examination evaluates vaginal exposure, and suspected urinary tract exposure requires endoscopy. Imaging position alone does not prove the cause of pain. See [Mesh Complications & Removal](/docs/surgical-techniques/04g-prolapse-repair/mesh-complications) for evaluation and management.<sup>[[6]](#ref6)[[11]](#ref11)</sup>

<span id="11-endorectal-ultrasound"></span>

## 11. Endoanal Ultrasound

<span id="indications-in-reconstructive-urology"></span>

Dedicated endoanal ultrasound assesses anal-sphincter anatomy in selected patients with fecal incontinence, prior obstetric sphincter injury or a repair complication. The external sphincter appears as a hyperechoic ring and the internal sphincter as a hypoechoic ring inside it. Defects appear as discontinuity or thinning and are recorded by clock-face position and arc. Interpret anatomy alongside symptoms, examination and, where available and indicated, anorectal physiology. An anatomical defect alone does not determine symptoms or the need for another repair.<sup>[[13]](#ref13)</sup>

The 2026 IUGA OASI guideline uses endoanal ultrasound in specialist assessment and follow-up pathways. Where unavailable, appropriately performed transperineal ultrasound can serve as a screening alternative, with suspected defects referred for endoanal confirmation. This is a dedicated anal-sphincter pathway. Endorectal ultrasound has no routine role in evaluating a Peyronie's plaque.<sup>[[13]](#ref13)[[12]](#ref12)</sup>

## 12. Doppler Ultrasound: Penile Duplex

<span id="role-before-urethral-reconstruction"></span>
<span id="technical-protocol"></span>
<span id="key-parameters-and-thresholds"></span>
<span id="relevance-to-urethroplasty-approach"></span>

Penile duplex is a selected second-level test for suspected vasculogenic ED when the result can change management. The usual protocol images both cavernosal arteries at the penile base with a high-frequency linear probe, records baseline velocities, gives an intracavernosal vasoactive agent (alprostadil or a papaverine-based mixture) and repeats measurements at intervals over 20 to 30 minutes. Response depends on adequate pharmacological stimulation, timing, technique and anxiety, and an abnormal study can be falsely positive.<sup>[[12]](#ref12)[[22]](#ref22)</sup>

Protocols and criteria vary. In a review of published studies, 39% defined a normal peak systolic velocity as 30 cm/s or more and 57% used an end-diastolic velocity of 5 cm/s or less as normal, and no consensus criteria exist. Low peak velocity is read as arterial insufficiency, and a persistently raised end-diastolic velocity as venous leak, with the thresholds taken from the local protocol.<sup>[[22]](#ref22)</sup>

Use the [dedicated penile Doppler article](/docs/evaluation/imaging/penile-doppler-ultrasound) for acquisition and interpretation. There is no general requirement for duplex before every urethroplasty. Baseline sexual function is documented before bulbar surgery, and duplex is considered when a vascular question is clinically relevant. Pre-existing ED does not justify avoidable vascular injury or replace careful reconstructive planning.<sup>[[12]](#ref12)</sup>

## 13. Practical Pearls

- **State the question.** Residual urine, upper-tract change, stricture anatomy, levator morphology and an implant complication each need a different examination.
- **State the conditions.** Voided volume and timing for PVR, distension for SUG and the maneuver for pelvic-floor imaging make measurements interpretable.
- **Use the transperineal window for the bulb.** The bulbar urethra is not seen from the ventral penile surface, and a bulbar stricture imaged only from the penis is underestimated or missed.<sup>[[2]](#ref2)[[4]](#ref4)</sup>
- **Order SUG when the plan can change.** The gain is largest for bulbar strictures of intermediate length and for complex or reoperative disease.<sup>[[14]](#ref14)</sup>
- **Keep observation separate from treatment.** Fibrosis appearance, collecting-system dilatation and hiatal enlargement need clinical context.
- **Escalate for uncertainty that matters.** The next test is chosen to answer the unresolved question. Renal ultrasound is morphological and cannot quantify split function or drainage.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[5]](#ref5)[[7]](#ref7)[[9]](#ref9)</sup>
