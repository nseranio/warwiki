# Handgrip Strength

Handgrip strength (HGS) measures maximal voluntary grip force. It is a practical assessment of muscle strength and a useful prompt to investigate frailty, sarcopenia or impaired recovery. **It complements the preoperative assessment; it does not replace it or establish malnutrition by itself.**<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[2]](#ref2)[[3]](#ref3)</sup>

## Measurement

Use a calibrated dynamometer and a reproducible protocol with appropriate reference data. Record device, handle setting, posture, tested hand, number of attempts and whether the result is a maximum or mean. A common hydraulic-dynamometer protocol uses a seated patient, neutral shoulder/forearm and elbow flexed to 90°; protocols differ, so comparisons require matching methods.<sup>[[4]](#ref4)[[1]](#ref1)</sup>

AWGS 2025 recommends **two to three attempts in both hands and the maximum value**, with positioning appropriate to the mechanical spring or hydraulic device. Allow recovery between efforts and consistent encouragement. Pain, arthritis, focal nerve injury, stroke, cognition and inability to follow instructions can limit validity. Consider a suitable lower-limb strength assessment when the hands cannot be tested.<sup>[[2]](#ref2)[[1]](#ref1)</sup>

In 497 patients with cancer, protocol choice changed measured values substantially, although the prevalence classified as weak did not change significantly. This supports standardization rather than a universal conversion between devices or positions.<sup>[[5]](#ref5)</sup>

## Reference Thresholds

| Framework and population | Low HGS in men | Low HGS in women | How it is used |
|---|---|---|---|
| **EWGSOP2**, principally older European populations | &lt;27 kg | &lt;16 kg | Low strength supports probable sarcopenia; assess muscle quantity/quality for confirmation |
| **AWGS 2025**, Asian adults ≥65 years | &lt;28 kg | &lt;18 kg | Diagnosis requires concurrent low muscle mass |
| **AWGS 2025**, Asian adults 50–64 years | &lt;34 kg | &lt;20 kg | New age-specific threshold; assess muscle mass with the corresponding criteria |

These are framework-specific thresholds, not universal normal ranges or surgical cancellation criteria. AWGS 2025 retains the older-adult grip thresholds but changes the diagnostic framework and adds middle-aged adults.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[2]](#ref2)</sup>

Age, body size, sex, geography and measurement method affect reference values. For example, pooled data from **12 British studies / 49,964 participants** showed peak median HGS of 51 kg in men aged 29–39 and 31 kg in women aged 26–42. Those population medians are not minimum acceptable values for an individual patient.<sup>[[6]](#ref6)</sup>

## Sarcopenia Assessment

**EWGSOP2: Find → Assess → Confirm → Severity.** Find cases through SARC-F or clinical suspicion; assess strength with grip or chair stands; confirm with low muscle quantity/quality; assess severity using physical performance. A low SARC-F score does not reliably exclude sarcopenia when symptoms or examination raise concern.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup>

**AWGS 2025:** low strength **and** low muscle mass establish sarcopenia. Physical performance remains clinically valuable but is treated as an outcome; the previous separate severe-sarcopenia category was removed. Age-specific DXA/BIA thresholds and further detail are in the original consensus. Do not mix thresholds or diagnosis stages from different frameworks.<sup>[[2]](#ref2)</sup>

For malnutrition, the **GLIM 2025 update explicitly states that muscle strength cannot substitute for muscle mass**. HGS is functional information. Use weight loss, BMI or an appropriate muscle-mass assessment to establish a GLIM phenotype, together with an etiologic criterion.<sup>[[7]](#ref7)</sup>

## What the Outcome Evidence Shows

| Evidence | Finding | Clinical interpretation |
|---|---|---|
| **UK Biobank**, 502,293 participants | Each 5 kg lower HGS associated with higher all-cause mortality: HR 1.16 in men and 1.20 in women | Population association; does not prove that increasing grip by 5 kg causes the inverse mortality reduction |
| **GI tumor surgery meta-analysis**, 8 observational studies / 2,291 patients | Low HGS associated with complications OR 2.23 (1.43–3.50), pneumonia OR 5.16 (3.17–8.38), ileus OR 2.48 (1.09–5.65), short-term mortality OR 7.28 (1.90–27.92) | Heterogeneous definitions and wide mortality interval; these are not validated risk multipliers for GU reconstruction |
| **Choi cohort**, 648 older general-surgery patients | Multidimensional frailty score discriminated complications better than HGS (AUC 0.750 vs 0.566); HGS did not improve age- or ASA-based models significantly | A single strength measurement should not replace comprehensive assessment |
| **2026 abdominal-surgery cohort**, 223 patients | Higher preoperative HGS associated with shorter stay; acute perioperative HGS changes were not associated with outcomes | Supports baseline functional assessment, without validating a postoperative grip target |

Sources: UK Biobank,<sup>[[8]](#ref8)</sup> GI surgery review,<sup>[[9]](#ref9)</sup> Choi,<sup>[[3]](#ref3)</sup> and Montanari.<sup>[[10]](#ref10)</sup> In Choi's fully adjusted analyses, only the multidimensional frailty score predicted six-month mortality; the study supports comprehensive assessment rather than superiority of grip strength.

## Exercise, Nutrition and Follow-up

Resistance exercise, often combined with balance or aerobic training and individualized nutrition, is central to sarcopenia management. Tailor the program to medical stability, baseline ability and rehabilitation goals.<sup>[[2]](#ref2)</sup>

A **42-RCT network meta-analysis / 3,728 older adults** found resistance-plus-balance exercise with nutrition improved HGS by **4.19 kg versus usual care** (95% CI 2.55–5.83; moderate certainty). The point estimate was **below the review's 5 kg minimally important difference**, although the interval crossed it. This was not a 4.19 kg incremental benefit over exercise alone. Quality-of-life and functional effects differed by intervention; mortality evidence was sparse.<sup>[[11]](#ref11)</sup>

Use repeat measurements under the same conditions to contribute to follow-up, together with intake, weight, mobility and patient goals. There is no established universal exercise dose that guarantees a particular grip gain, and no validated requirement to cross a cutoff or demonstrate three months of improvement before reconstruction.

## Reconstructive Application

Consider HGS when weakness, weight loss, advanced age, chronic disease or a major planned operation raises concern about physiological reserve. A low value can trigger a fuller assessment and dietetic, physiotherapy or geriatric input. Decide operative timing from the whole clinical picture, urgency and expected benefit, rather than a grip number alone.

In CKD, dialysis or recent trauma, interpret current HGS alongside illness effects and functional history. A measurement after injury is **not a measurement of preinjury reserve**. For hand disability or neurological weakness, select another appropriate test rather than applying population grip cutoffs mechanically.
