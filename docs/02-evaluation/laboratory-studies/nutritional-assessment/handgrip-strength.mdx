# Handgrip Strength

Handgrip strength (HGS) measures maximal voluntary grip force. It is a practical assessment of muscle strength and a useful prompt to investigate frailty, sarcopenia or impaired recovery. It complements the preoperative assessment and does not establish malnutrition by itself.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[2]](#ref2)[[3]](#ref3)</sup>

## Measurement

Use a calibrated dynamometer and a reproducible protocol with appropriate reference data. Record device, handle setting, posture, tested hand, number of attempts and whether the result is a maximum or mean. A common hydraulic-dynamometer protocol uses a seated patient, neutral shoulder and forearm and elbow flexed to 90°; protocols differ, so comparisons require matching methods.<sup>[[4]](#ref4)[[1]](#ref1)</sup>

AWGS 2025 recommends two to three attempts in both hands and the maximum value, with positioning appropriate to the mechanical spring or hydraulic device. Allow recovery between efforts and consistent encouragement. Pain, arthritis, focal nerve injury, stroke, cognition and inability to follow instructions can limit validity. Consider a suitable lower-limb strength assessment when the hands cannot be tested.<sup>[[2]](#ref2)[[1]](#ref1)</sup>

In 497 patients with cancer, protocol choice changed measured values, and the difference was potentially clinically meaningful in over 60% of men and 40% of women. The classification of weakness was not significantly affected. This supports standardizing the protocol. It does not support a universal conversion between devices or positions.<sup>[[5]](#ref5)</sup>

## Reference Thresholds

| Framework and population | Low HGS in men | Low HGS in women | How it is used |
|---|---|---|---|
| **EWGSOP2**, principally older European populations | &lt;27 kg | &lt;16 kg | Low strength supports probable sarcopenia; assess muscle quantity/quality for confirmation |
| **AWGS 2025**, Asian adults ≥65 years | &lt;28 kg | &lt;18 kg | Diagnosis requires concurrent low muscle mass |
| **AWGS 2025**, Asian adults 50–64 years | &lt;34 kg | &lt;20 kg | New age-specific threshold; assess muscle mass with the corresponding criteria |

These are framework-specific thresholds, not universal normal ranges or surgical cancellation criteria. AWGS 2025 retains the older-adult grip thresholds but changes the diagnostic framework and adds middle-aged adults.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[2]](#ref2)</sup>

Age, body size, sex, geography and measurement method affect reference values. For example, pooled data from 12 British studies with 49,964 participants showed peak median HGS of 51 kg in men aged 29–39 and 31 kg in women aged 26–42. Those population medians are not minimum acceptable values for an individual patient.<sup>[[6]](#ref6)</sup> Grip strength falls progressively with age. In the Jerusalem Longitudinal Cohort, mean HGS declined between ages 70 and 90 from 35.3 to 19.5 kg in men and from 21.3 to 11.5 kg in women.<sup>[[7]](#ref7)</sup>

## Sarcopenia Assessment

**EWGSOP2 (Find, Assess, Confirm, Severity).** Find cases through SARC-F or clinical suspicion; assess strength with grip or chair stands; confirm with low muscle quantity or quality; assess severity using physical performance. A low SARC-F score does not reliably exclude sarcopenia when symptoms or examination raise concern.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup>

**AWGS 2025.** Low strength and low muscle mass establish sarcopenia. Physical performance remains clinically valuable but is treated as an outcome; the previous separate severe-sarcopenia category was removed. Age-specific DXA or BIA thresholds and further detail are in the original consensus. Do not mix thresholds or diagnosis stages from different frameworks.<sup>[[2]](#ref2)</sup>

For malnutrition, the GLIM 2025 update explicitly states that muscle strength cannot substitute for muscle mass. HGS is functional information. Use weight loss, BMI or an appropriate muscle-mass assessment to establish a GLIM phenotype, together with an etiologic criterion.<sup>[[8]](#ref8)</sup>

## What the Outcome Evidence Shows

| Evidence | Finding | Clinical interpretation |
|---|---|---|
| **UK Biobank**, 502,293 participants | Each 5 kg lower HGS associated with higher all-cause mortality: HR 1.16 in men and 1.20 in women | Population association; does not prove that increasing grip by 5 kg causes the inverse mortality reduction |
| **GI tumor surgery meta-analysis**, 8 observational studies / 2,291 patients | Low HGS associated with complications OR 2.23 (1.43–3.50), pneumonia OR 5.16 (3.17–8.38), ileus OR 2.48 (1.09–5.65), short-term mortality OR 7.28 (1.90–27.92) | Heterogeneous definitions and wide mortality interval; these are not validated risk multipliers for GU reconstruction |
| **Choi cohort**, 648 older general-surgery patients | Multidimensional frailty score discriminated complications better than HGS (C-index 0.750 vs 0.566); HGS did not improve age- or ASA-based models significantly | A single strength measurement should not replace comprehensive assessment |
| **2026 abdominal-surgery cohort**, 223 patients | Higher preoperative HGS associated with shorter stay; acute perioperative HGS changes were not associated with outcomes | Supports baseline functional assessment, without validating a postoperative grip target |

Sources: UK Biobank,<sup>[[9]](#ref9)</sup> GI surgery review,<sup>[[10]](#ref10)</sup> Choi,<sup>[[3]](#ref3)</sup> and Montanari.<sup>[[11]](#ref11)</sup> In Choi's fully adjusted analyses, only the multidimensional frailty score predicted six-month mortality; the study supports comprehensive assessment and does not show superiority of grip strength. The frailty score's discrimination was measured as a C-index (0.750, compared with 0.566 for grip strength).

### Population Cohorts, Function and Disability

In PURE (139,691 participants aged 35 to 70 in 17 countries; median follow-up 4.0 years), each 5 kg lower grip strength was associated with higher all-cause mortality (HR 1.16, 95% CI 1.13–1.20) and cardiovascular mortality (HR 1.17, 1.11–1.24). Grip strength predicted all-cause and cardiovascular mortality more strongly than systolic blood pressure.<sup>[[12]](#ref12)</sup> In UK Biobank, each 5 kg lower grip strength was also associated with cardiovascular mortality (HR 1.19 in women, 1.22 in men) and cancer mortality (HR 1.17 in women, 1.10 in men; colorectal, lung and breast cancer, but not prostate cancer). Adding grip strength to an office-based risk score increased the C-index by 0.013 for all-cause mortality.<sup>[[9]](#ref9)</sup> Marginal structural models in 121,116 adults aged 50 years or older in the SHARE survey associated a 5 kg increase in grip strength with lower all-cause mortality (HR 0.86) and cardiovascular mortality (HR 0.86). These are observational estimates.<sup>[[13]](#ref13)</sup>

Low grip strength is also associated with loss of independence. In 17,747 older US adults, each 5 kg decrease was associated with higher odds of limitation in eating (20%), walking (14%), bathing (14%), dressing (9%), transferring (8%) and toileting (6%).<sup>[[14]](#ref14)</sup> In the Jerusalem cohort, lowest-quartile grip strength predicted later dependence in activities of daily living from age 78 to 85 (OR 2.68, 95% CI 1.04–6.89) and from 85 to 90 (OR 2.31, 1.01–5.30) after adjustment for education, self-rated health, physical activity, diabetes, depression and cognition.<sup>[[7]](#ref7)</sup> In 1,765 older Japanese adults, grip strength showed an inverse dose-response relationship with disability that was independent of body composition. Mortality in men depended more on muscle mass.<sup>[[15]](#ref15)</sup>

### Nutritional Status and Kidney Disease

Muscle function reacts early to nutritional deprivation, and HGS is used as a marker of nutritional status and as an outcome in nutrition trials.<sup>[[16]](#ref16)</sup> In one hospital cohort of 490 patients (mean age 69.5 years), the HGS cutoff for severe GLIM malnutrition was 20 kg in men younger than 70 (AUC 0.82) and 11 kg in women older than 70 (AUC 0.78). These are single-institution values, not validated thresholds.<sup>[[17]](#ref17)</sup>

Low HGS is more common in chronic kidney disease. In a Korean national survey, low HGS was present in 25.2% of participants with CKD and 6.2% of those without, and CKD was independently associated with low HGS (OR 1.91 in men, 1.57 in women).<sup>[[18]](#ref18)</sup> In 789 community-dwelling older men, mild-to-moderate renal impairment (eGFR below 60 mL/min/1.73 m²) was associated with faster decline in grip strength over five years despite preserved muscle mass.<sup>[[19]](#ref19)</sup> In a small study of 75 participants (control 16, CKD 17, ESRD 42), low grip strength was most frequent in ESRD (41.6%) and was associated with hospitalization over 600 days. Serum indoxyl sulfate was associated with low grip strength (adjusted OR 8.5, 95% CI 1.8–40.2).<sup>[[20]](#ref20)</sup>

## Exercise, Nutrition and Follow-up

Resistance exercise, often combined with balance or aerobic training and individualized nutrition, is central to sarcopenia management. Tailor the program to medical stability, baseline ability and rehabilitation goals.<sup>[[2]](#ref2)</sup>

A meta-analysis of 24 trials in 3,018 healthy community-dwelling older adults found small effects of exercise training on grip strength (SMD 0.28, 95% CI 0.13–0.44), with task-specific and multimodal training giving the largest transfer.<sup>[[21]](#ref21)</sup> In sarcopenic older adults, a Bayesian network meta-analysis of 13 studies (711 participants) estimated the best-performing resistance-training dose at 3 sessions per week at 49% of one-repetition maximum for about 19 weeks (about 1,400 repetitions per week), with estimated gains of about 7 to 8 kg in grip strength. The effective ranges were wide (2–5 sessions per week, 30–75% of one-repetition maximum, 4–24 weeks).<sup>[[22]](#ref22)</sup>

A 42-RCT network meta-analysis of 3,728 older adults found resistance-plus-balance exercise with nutrition improved HGS by 4.19 kg versus usual care (95% CI 2.55–5.83; moderate certainty). The point estimate was below the review's 5 kg minimally important difference, although the interval crossed it. This was not a 4.19 kg incremental benefit over exercise alone. Quality-of-life and functional effects differed by intervention; mortality evidence was sparse.<sup>[[23]](#ref23)</sup> In a cluster randomized trial of 245 community-dwelling older adults, home-based exercise with protein counseling did not change the primary physical-performance outcome. Muscle strength improved by 2.32 kg at 6 months and remained improved at 12 months compared with control.<sup>[[24]](#ref24)</sup>

Use repeat measurements under the same conditions to contribute to follow-up, together with intake, weight, mobility and patient goals. The network-derived doses above are estimates. No exercise dose is established to guarantee a particular grip gain, and no validated requirement to cross a cutoff or demonstrate three months of improvement before reconstruction.

## Reconstructive Application

Consider HGS when weakness, weight loss, advanced age, chronic disease or a major planned operation raises concern about physiological reserve. A low value can trigger a fuller assessment and dietetic, physiotherapy or geriatric input.

Settings where a bedside grip test is a reasonable first step include cystectomy with diversion, complex prolapse or pelvic floor reconstruction, staged gender-affirming surgery, urethroplasty with buccal grafting and repair of radiation-related or post-pelvic-cancer fistula. The surgical outcome data come from gastrointestinal tumor and abdominal operations, and no grip threshold has been validated for these genitourinary settings. A dynamometer takes about a minute and needs no laboratory test or imaging, which makes it practical when CT or bioimpedance body composition is unavailable in clinic. Operative timing depends on the whole clinical picture, urgency and expected benefit. A grip number alone does not set it.

In CKD, dialysis or recent trauma, interpret current HGS alongside illness effects and functional history. A measurement after injury is not a measurement of preinjury reserve. For hand disability or neurological weakness, select another appropriate test rather than applying population grip cutoffs mechanically.
