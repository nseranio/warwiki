# Zinc

Zinc supports enzyme activity, gene regulation, immune function, and tissue repair. In reconstructive practice, the useful questions are whether a patient has a credible deficiency risk, whether supplementation is appropriate, and whether excessive zinc has caused **copper deficiency**. Zinc's biological role in healing does not establish that extra zinc improves graft take, fistula closure, or flap survival.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[2]](#ref2)[[7]](#ref7)</sup>

## Physiology and Dietary Requirements

Zinc has catalytic, structural, and regulatory roles. Zinc fingers stabilize many proteins involved in transcription; the often-quoted thousands of zinc-dependent proteins are not all transcription factors. Most body zinc is in muscle and bone. Absorption and gastrointestinal losses regulate balance; serum zinc represents a small, rapidly changing compartment.<sup>[[2]](#ref2)</sup>

For adults, the US RDA is **11 mg/day for men and 8 mg/day for women**, increasing to **11 mg in pregnancy and 12 mg during lactation**. The adult tolerable upper intake level is **40 mg/day**, excluding higher-dose treatment under medical supervision. Meat, shellfish, dairy, legumes, nuts, and fortified foods contribute zinc; phytate reduces absorption from plant foods. A plant-based diet calls for appropriate dietary planning, not a diagnosis of deficiency.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup>

**Prescribe elemental zinc.** The mass of zinc sulfate, gluconate, acetate, or oxide is not the mass of zinc it contains. Check the product's elemental content before converting doses.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[6]](#ref6)</sup>

## Assessment of Zinc Status

Interpret serum or plasma zinc alongside diet, symptoms, inflammation, albumin, and the collection conditions. There is no single sensitive test that reliably diagnoses every individual with mild deficiency.<sup>[[2]](#ref2)</sup>

The BOND review gives the following **population risk cutoffs**, rather than a universal severity classification:<sup>[[2]](#ref2)</sup>

| Sample conditions | Females ≥10 years | Males ≥10 years |
|---|---|---|
| Morning, fasting | &lt;70 μg/dL | &lt;74 μg/dL |
| Morning, nonfasting | &lt;66 μg/dL | &lt;70 μg/dL |
| Afternoon | &lt;59 μg/dL | &lt;61 μg/dL |

- **A low result may reflect redistribution or reduced binding.** Inflammation, hypoalbuminemia, pregnancy, recent meals, and time of day affect the measurement. Use trace-element collection procedures and the laboratory's reference interval.<sup>[[2]](#ref2)[[3]](#ref3)</sup>
- **70 μg/dL is not a validated universal cutoff for severe deficiency.** Wessells et al. examined **50 μg/dL** in experimental severe restriction and acrodermatitis enteropathica; those selected populations do not establish an all-patient diagnostic threshold.<sup>[[4]](#ref4)</sup>
- NHANES 2011–2014 found no association with reported dietary/supplemental intake in its adjusted cross-sectional analysis. This does **not** mean serum zinc never responds to supplementation: the updated systematic review of intervention studies confirmed a response, with substantial heterogeneity.<sup>[[3]](#ref3)[[5]](#ref5)</sup>
- Hair zinc, nonspecific enzyme activity, and unvalidated commercial mineral panels should not replace clinical assessment. Growth response is a functional indicator used in children, not an adult preoperative diagnostic test.<sup>[[2]](#ref2)</sup>

## Deficiency: Who Needs Assessment?

Consider inadequate intake, alcohol use disorder, malabsorption, chronic diarrhea, short bowel, bariatric surgery, high gastrointestinal losses, and chronic liver disease. Poor appetite or taste, dermatitis, alopecia, delayed healing, and impaired growth can occur, but are nonspecific. Do not assign severity solely from a symptom list or diagnose zinc deficiency from hyperammonemia alone.<sup>[[2]](#ref2)[[6]](#ref6)[[10]](#ref10)</sup>

**Acrodermatitis enteropathica** is an inherited SLC39A4-related absorption disorder, classically associated with acral/periorificial dermatitis, alopecia, and diarrhea. Acquired deficiency can resemble it. Suspected inherited disease needs specialist assessment and sustained replacement with monitoring; a short general nutritional course may be inadequate.<sup>[[2]](#ref2)</sup>

### After Bariatric Surgery

ASMBS recommends **at least annual zinc assessment after RYGB and BPD/DS**, with additional testing for compatible symptoms or chronic diarrhea after any bariatric procedure. Its routine supplement ranges are:<sup>[[6]](#ref6)</sup>

| Procedure | Elemental zinc in the routine daily supplement |
|---|---|
| Sleeve gastrectomy or gastric band | 8–11 mg |
| RYGB | 8–22 mg |
| BPD/DS | 16–22 mg |

Balance supplementation with copper; ASMBS suggests approximately **8–15 mg zinc per 1 mg copper**. This is a prevention framework, not a substitute for treating established copper deficiency. ASMBS found insufficient evidence for a universal zinc repletion dose and cautioned against its older 60 mg twice-daily regimen. Choose treatment and reassessment according to the cause, degree of deficiency, and copper status.<sup>[[6]](#ref6)</sup>

GLP-1 therapy alone is not an established indication for routine zinc testing before every operation. Assess persistent poor intake, vomiting, weight loss, malabsorption, and the planned procedure as part of a broader nutritional assessment.

## Wounds and Reconstructive Surgery

Correct nutritional deficiency as part of comprehensive care, including adequate energy and protein, perfusion, infection control, pressure relief, and management of the wound's cause. Avoid presenting zinc as a proven standalone healing treatment.<sup>[[7]](#ref7)</sup>

The **2024 Cochrane review** found very uncertain evidence for zinc supplementation in pressure-ulcer healing. Its zinc-alone evidence came from two small older trials; evidence for combinations of protein, arginine, zinc, and antioxidants was also uncertain. These findings do not establish improved urethroplasty, vaginal-cuff, fistula, or flap outcomes. A change in a collagen marker in a multinutrient trial cannot identify zinc's effect or establish fewer clinical wound failures.<sup>[[7]](#ref7)</sup>

Topical zinc oxide can protect moisture-exposed surrounding skin. Older mechanistic and wound literature is not evidence that zinc paste should be placed on every surgical wound or that it prevents infection in a normally nourished patient. **An Unna boot is a leg-ulcer dressing/compression approach, not a standard pelvic or intravaginal wound treatment.**<sup>[[8]](#ref8)</sup>

For **peristomal skin beneath an adhesive appliance**, address fit and leakage first. WOCN guidance uses appropriately applied barrier powder, with optional alcohol-free barrier film, for moist skin injury. Do not automatically substitute zinc cream beneath the wafer; select products compatible with the appliance and involve an ostomy nurse.<sup>[[9]](#ref9)</sup>

## Other Established or Studied Uses

### Liver Disease and Hepatic Encephalopathy

The **2025 ACG guideline's key concept** is to consider supplementation in liver disease with hypozincemia and/or compatible deficiency findings. Low serum zinc in cirrhosis is also influenced by albumin and disease severity; one cohort's deficiency prevalence should not be generalized to every patient.<sup>[[10]](#ref10)</sup>

In the 2019 meta-analysis, four trials entered quantitative synthesis; the number-connection-test comparison of zinc plus lactulose versus lactulose alone used **three trials, 227 patients**. Performance improved, but heterogeneity was high, one trial included other antioxidants, and pooled ammonia and digit-symbol results were not significantly different. Hospitalization outcomes and adverse events were not reported. The small **69-patient Zinc-MHE trial** subsequently supported psychometric benefit over 12 weeks. Neither establishes prevention of perioperative encephalopathy, mortality benefit, or routine zinc-plus-lactulose treatment for all cirrhotic surgical patients.<sup>[[11]](#ref11)[[12]](#ref12)</sup>

### Wilson Disease

Zinc induces intestinal metallothionein and reduces copper absorption. AASLD guidance permits use in selected asymptomatic patients and maintenance therapy; **EASL-ERN 2025 recommends chelators as the primary choice for significant hepatic disease**. Zinc should not be described as universally equivalent to penicillamine.<sup>[[13]](#ref13)[[14]](#ref14)</sup>

The US **GALZIN** indication is maintenance after initial chelation; the usual adult dose is **50 mg elemental zinc three times daily**, taken at least one hour before or two to three hours after meals. Pediatric, pregnancy, and combination regimens require specialist selection and copper-balance monitoring. Do not transplant a dose from a different country's product label into a US prescription.<sup>[[15]](#ref15)</sup>

### Age-Related Macular Degeneration

For intermediate AMD or advanced AMD in one eye, AREDS antioxidants plus zinc reduced progression to advanced disease by approximately **25% over five years**. The usual AREDS2 formula contains **80 mg elemental zinc and 2 mg copper**; it is not general AMD prevention. AREDS2 did not detect a difference between 25 and 80 mg zinc, which does not prove dose equivalence. Lutein/zeaxanthin replaced beta-carotene, particularly relevant to current or former smokers.<sup>[[16]](#ref16)[[17]](#ref17)</sup>

### Common Cold and Childhood Diarrhea

- **Common cold:** the 2024 Cochrane review found little or no preventive effect. Treatment may shorten symptoms by about 2.4 days, with low-certainty, highly heterogeneous evidence and more nonserious adverse effects. This does not justify indefinite high-dose supplementation.<sup>[[18]](#ref18)</sup>
- **Childhood diarrhea:** WHO's **2024 guideline** recommends adjunctive oral zinc for acute watery or persistent diarrhea in children up to 10 years, suggesting **5 mg daily for up to 14 days** (dose recommendation conditional, low-certainty evidence). This updates the older 20 mg regimen; zinc accompanies appropriate rehydration.<sup>[[19]](#ref19)</sup>

## Excess Zinc, Copper Deficiency, and Interactions

Sustained supplemental zinc can inhibit copper absorption. **50 mg/day or more for weeks** can be problematic; 100–300 mg/day is not a threshold below which copper deficiency is excluded. Gastrointestinal intolerance may occur earlier.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[21]](#ref21)</sup>

With unexplained cytopenias, sensory ataxia, paresthesias, or myelopathy, ask about supplements and zinc-containing denture adhesive. Check copper, ceruloplasmin, zinc, and B12 as appropriate. Copper-deficiency myelopathy can resemble B12 deficiency, but urinary symptoms occur in a **minority**, and an elevated zinc concentration is not required to diagnose copper deficiency. Stop an excessive nonessential zinc source and arrange copper replacement; severe deficiency or neurologic disease may need parenteral treatment. Hematologic recovery is generally better than neurologic recovery.<sup>[[20]](#ref20)[[21]](#ref21)</sup>

Zinc can reduce absorption of quinolone/tetracycline antibiotics and penicillamine. Follow the particular drug's labeling for separation; Wilson disease regimens need specialist coordination rather than a generic interaction interval. Review total zinc from all products, including a prescribed AREDS formulation, before adding another supplement.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[13]](#ref13)[[16]](#ref16)</sup>
