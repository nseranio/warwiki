# Vitamin A

Vitamin A is an essential fat-soluble nutrient for vision, epithelial integrity, immunity, and development. In reconstructive practice, the main issues are malabsorption and deficiency, toxicity and pregnancy risk, and the limited evidence for high-dose supplementation to improve wound healing.<sup>[[13]](#ref13)[[15]](#ref15)</sup>

Vitamin A has been used to counter steroid-impaired healing, but this is not a proven routine perioperative intervention. Treat established deficiency and address overall nutrition; do not automatically prescribe high-dose retinol to every patient taking corticosteroids.

## Biochemistry and Metabolism

"Vitamin A" encompasses a family of fat-soluble retinoid compounds:<sup>[[13]](#ref13)</sup>

- **Preformed dietary vitamin A** (retinol and retinyl esters). Found in animal-source foods, fortified foods, and supplements. Retinyl esters are hydrolyzed to retinol in the intestine, absorbed with dietary fat, and transported to the liver via chylomicrons.
- **Provitamin A carotenoids** (β-carotene, α-carotene, β-cryptoxanthin). Plant-derived pigments enzymatically cleaved to retinal in the intestine. Conversion varies with food matrix and absorption. Dietary reference calculations use 12 μg dietary β-carotene or 24 μg dietary α-carotene or β-cryptoxanthin per 1 μg retinol activity equivalent (RAE); adequate intake is possible from a suitably planned diet.<sup>[[13]](#ref13)</sup>

Most body vitamin A is stored in the liver, predominantly as retinyl esters in hepatic stellate cells (HSCs).<sup>[[2]](#ref2)[[3]](#ref3)</sup> When needed, retinyl esters are hydrolyzed to retinol, which is released bound to retinol-binding protein (RBP) for delivery to target tissues. Sufficient hepatic stores can maintain requirements for months to years.<sup>[[4]](#ref4)</sup>

Three active metabolites mediate vitamin A's biological functions:<sup>[[13]](#ref13)</sup>

- **Retinal (retinaldehyde).** Needed for vision; 11-cis-retinal is the chromophore in rhodopsin and cone opsins.
- **Retinoic acid (all-trans and 9-cis).** The primary transcriptionally active form; binds RAR/RXR nuclear receptors to regulate cell differentiation, proliferation, apoptosis, and immune function.
- **Retinol.** The principal circulating transport form and precursor of retinal and retinoic acid; storage is mainly as retinyl esters.

## Dietary Sources and Requirements

Preformed vitamin A is found in liver, fish oils, dairy products, and eggs, with the contribution varying by diet. Provitamin A carotenoids are found in dark green leafy vegetables, carrots, sweet potatoes, mangoes, and red palm oil.<sup>[[13]](#ref13)</sup>

| Population | RDA (μg RAE/day) | UL (μg/day preformed) |
|---|---|---|
| Adult men | 900 | 3,000 (~ 10,000 IU) |
| Adult women | 700 | 3,000 (~ 10,000 IU) |
| Pregnant adults | 770 | 3,000 |
| Lactating adults | 1,300 | 3,000 |
| Children 1–3 years | 300 | 600 |
| Children 4–8 years | 400 | 900 |

RAE accounts for differences between preformed vitamin A and carotenoids. The adult UL is 3,000 μg RAE/day of preformed vitamin A, equivalent to 10,000 IU retinol; it is not a recommended intake. Fat malabsorption can impair uptake.<sup>[[13]](#ref13)</sup>

## Assessment of Vitamin A Status

Assessing vitamin A status is challenging because serum retinol is homeostatically regulated across a wide range of liver stores and may remain normal despite depleted stores; inflammation can also lower the measurement without equivalent depletion.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[4]](#ref4)</sup>

- **Serum retinol.** Most commonly used biomarker. Common thresholds are &lt;0.70 μmol/L (20 μg/dL) and &lt;0.35 μmol/L (10 μg/dL) for severe biochemical deficiency; these are not stand-alone individual diagnostic rules.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[13]](#ref13)</sup>
- **Confounders.** Infection, inflammation, low transport proteins, liver disease, and pregnancy complicate interpretation. Oral contraceptives can increase plasma vitamin A; kidney disease can also distort retinol or RBP interpretation. As with albumin and zinc, inflammation significantly reduces measured levels independent of true stores.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[10]](#ref10)</sup>
- **Retinol-binding protein (RBP).** Less expensive alternative to serum retinol; shares homeostatic, inflammatory, hepatic, and renal limitations; it cannot reliably exclude deficiency.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup>
- **Liver biopsy and retinol isotope dilution.** Reference tests for total body vitamin A stores, but impractical for routine use.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[4]](#ref4)</sup>
- **Clinical assessment.** Night blindness (earliest sign), Bitot's spots, conjunctival xerosis, corneal xerosis and keratomalacia are features warranting clinical assessment for deficiency; corneal involvement requires urgent ophthalmic care.<sup>[[4]](#ref4)</sup>

**2025 Cochrane diagnostic review:** 40 studies, none designed as diagnostic-accuracy studies. Serum retinol had low and uncertain sensitivity; against isotope-dilution liver stores at a 0.1 μmol/g cutoff, sensitivity was 10% (95% CI 2–38) and specificity 92% (85–96). These estimates vary by reference standard. A normal blood result cannot safely rule out deficiency in a symptomatic high-risk patient, and a low result during inflammation is not proof of depleted stores.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup>

## Causes of Deficiency

| Category | Examples |
|---|---|
| **Dietary insufficiency** | Inadequate intake of both preformed vitamin A and provitamin A sources; restrictive diets or food insecurity |
| **Malabsorption** | Celiac disease, IBD, chronic pancreatitis, cystic fibrosis, short bowel syndrome, **bariatric surgery (especially BPD/DS, RYGB)** |
| **Liver disease** | Cirrhosis, alcoholic liver disease (impaired storage and RBP synthesis) |
| **Increased demand** | Pregnancy (especially third trimester), lactation, rapid growth in infancy |
| **Infections** | Measles, diarrheal illness, HIV (increased catabolism and urinary losses) |

## Clinical Manifestations of Deficiency

**Ocular.** The hallmark of vitamin A deficiency is xerophthalmia, a spectrum of progressive eye disease:<sup>[[4]](#ref4)</sup>

1. **Night blindness (nyctalopia).** Earliest symptom; impaired dark adaptation from insufficient 11-cis-retinal for rhodopsin regeneration.
2. **Conjunctival xerosis.** Drying and keratinization of the conjunctiva.
3. **Bitot's spots.** Triangular, foamy deposits on bulbar conjunctiva; a characteristic sign requiring assessment in context.
4. **Corneal xerosis.** Drying and haziness of the cornea.
5. **Corneal ulceration / keratomalacia.** Liquefactive necrosis of the cornea; medical emergency that can lead to permanent blindness.

Corneal injury can progress rapidly. Do not wait for laboratory confirmation before arranging urgent ophthalmic assessment and treatment when xerophthalmia is suspected.

**Immune.** Deficiency impairs both innate and adaptive immunity, including epithelial barrier integrity, neutrophil function, NK-cell activity and T-cell differentiation. Susceptibility to measles, diarrheal diseases and respiratory infections is increased.<sup>[[13]](#ref13)[[5]](#ref5)</sup>

**Other.** Growth retardation, impaired hematopoiesis (anemia), follicular hyperkeratosis, increased childhood mortality.<sup>[[13]](#ref13)</sup>

## Clinical Applications

### Childhood Mortality and Measles

Population prevention and treatment of an ill child use different schedules. Cochrane 2022 included 47 trials (approximately 1.22 million community-dwelling children aged six months to five years). Its mortality meta-analysis included 19 trials and found a 12% relative reduction, RR 0.88 (95% CI 0.83–0.93). The older 24% estimate came from an earlier review. These trials excluded children already hospitalized or ill; they do not establish an adult wound-healing benefit.<sup>[[5]](#ref5)[[6]](#ref6)</sup>

**Measles:** current CDC guidance permits supervised vitamin A for children with measles and recommends it for severe or hospitalized cases. If used, give immediately and repeat the next day: 50,000 IU below six months, 100,000 IU at 6–11 months, or 200,000 IU from 12 months. Vitamin A does not replace vaccination or prevent measles infection.<sup>[[20]](#ref20)</sup>

### Retinoids in Dermatology

Vitamin A derivatives are among the most widely used pharmacologic agents in dermatology:<sup>[[7]](#ref7)[[8]](#ref8)</sup>

- **Isotretinoin** (oral). Disease-modifying treatment for severe nodulocystic acne; suppresses sebum, normalizes keratinization, modulates innate immunity. Teratogenic (see Safety below).
- **Tretinoin, adapalene, tazarotene, trifarotene** (topical). First-line for acne; also used for photoaging and melasma.
- **Acitretin** (oral). Psoriasis, pityriasis rubra pilaris, Darier disease, ichthyoses.
- **Bexarotene** (oral / topical). Cutaneous T-cell lymphoma.

### Retinoids in Oncology

**All-trans retinoic acid (ATRA)** is a differentiation treatment for acute promyelocytic leukemia within hematology-directed combination regimens. These drug effects should not be extrapolated to nutritional vitamin A supplementation for GU cancer prevention.<sup>[[9]](#ref9)</sup>

## Treatment of Vitamin A Deficiency

**Suspected xerophthalmia or corneal involvement requires urgent specialist treatment.** Dose and route depend on age, pregnancy, absorption, and ocular findings. Scheduled population supplementation every 4–6 months is not treatment for acute eye disease.

| Context | Verified regimen and limits |
|---|---|
| **Post-bariatric deficiency without corneal changes** | ASMBS describes **10,000–25,000 IU orally daily until clinical improvement, usually 1–2 weeks**; reassess absorption and response. |
| **Post-bariatric deficiency with corneal changes** | ASMBS describes **50,000–100,000 IU IM daily for three days**, then **50,000 IU IM daily for two weeks**. Urgent ophthalmic/nutrition supervision is required. |
| **Adult deficiency requiring parenteral treatment** | AQUASOL A labeling specifies **100,000 IU IM daily for three days**, then **50,000 IU IM daily for two weeks**, followed by replacement appropriate to absorption and response. **This product must not be given IV.** |

These are high-dose deficiency-treatment regimens, not preventive prescriptions, and should not be applied to pregnancy without specialist assessment. Evaluate concurrent deficiencies, especially iron and copper after bariatric surgery.<sup>[[10]](#ref10)[[14]](#ref14)</sup>

**Maintenance after bariatric surgery:** ASMBS lists 5,000 IU/day after banding, 5,000–10,000 IU/day after sleeve or RYGB, and 10,000 IU/day after duodenal switch, individualized to procedure, history, levels, and pregnancy status. Prevention and repletion are separate; higher doses are not automatically better.<sup>[[14]](#ref14)</sup>

## Toxicity (Hypervitaminosis A)

Excess preformed vitamin A can cause hepatotoxicity, skin changes, alopecia, headache or intracranial hypertension, bone symptoms, and PTH-independent hypercalcemia. Dose, duration, formulation, hepatic or renal disease, and concurrent retinoids affect risk. The dietary UL is not a sharp threshold below which toxicity is impossible or above which everyone develops toxicity.<sup>[[10]](#ref10)[[11]](#ref11)[[13]](#ref13)</sup>

β-carotene does not ordinarily cause classic retinol toxicity; excess dietary carotenoids may discolor skin. High-dose β-carotene supplements are not harmless, however. In ATBC (29,133 male smokers), β-carotene increased lung-cancer incidence. In CARET (18,314 smokers, former smokers and asbestos-exposed participants), β-carotene plus retinyl palmitate increased lung cancer, RR 1.28 (1.04–1.57), and all-cause mortality, RR 1.17 (1.03–1.33). Do not use these supplements for cancer prevention; these findings do not apply to ordinary fruit and vegetable consumption.<sup>[[18]](#ref18)[[19]](#ref19)</sup>

## Teratogenicity — A Critical Safety Concern

**Excess preformed vitamin A and systemic retinoid medicines can cause fetal harm.** Vitamin A at nutritional intake is essential; avoid describing every exposure to ordinary dietary retinol as teratogenic. Check supplements, liver or fish-liver-oil products, and prescriptions together.<sup>[[12]](#ref12)[[13]](#ref13)</sup>

- **Isotretinoin:** follow the current iPLEDGE requirements for patients who can become pregnant, including pregnancy tests and required contraception or program-defined continuous abstinence. Avoid pregnancy during treatment and for one month afterward. Do not extrapolate that interval to every retinoid or vitamin A preparation.
- **2026 iPLEDGE change:** FDA has delayed implementation of the newly approved modifications to November 15, 2026. Pre-treatment tests remain in a medical setting; the FDA describes enforcement discretion for prescriber-supervised home testing during or after treatment. Use the current program requirements rather than a fixed checklist copied here.<sup>[[16]](#ref16)</sup>
- **Topical retinoids:** systemic exposure is much lower, but EMA advises avoidance during pregnancy and when planning pregnancy.<sup>[[17]](#ref17)</sup>
- **Nutritional deficiency in pregnancy:** requires obstetric and nutrition assessment. WHO population supplementation guidance is restricted to settings of severe vitamin A deficiency to prevent night blindness; it is not a general high-dose pregnancy recommendation.<sup>[[21]](#ref21)</sup>

An inadvertent exposure warrants teratology counseling. Fixed fetal-risk percentages from heterogeneous reports should not be presented as a precise prognosis.

## Vitamin A and Liver Disease

Liver disease can alter retinol storage, transport proteins, and blood levels. A low serum result does not alone establish the size of hepatic stores. Excess supplementation can itself injure the liver; do not infer that every low retinol measurement in cirrhosis needs high-dose replacement. Coordinate suspected deficiency with nutrition and hepatology.<sup>[[2]](#ref2)[[3]](#ref3)[[11]](#ref11)</sup>

## Reconstructive Relevance

### Corticosteroids and Wound Healing

Vitamin A has a biologic rationale and historical experimental use for steroid-impaired healing. A wound-healing review explicitly describes the human evidence as limited and the supplementation recommendation as expert opinion without rigorous therapeutic trials.<sup>[[15]](#ref15)</sup> It does not establish that 25,000 IU/day improves urethroplasty, fistula closure, graft take, or prolapse outcomes.

**Do not use an automatic oral or compounded topical vitamin A protocol solely because a patient takes steroids.** Assess nutrition and the cause of poor healing, correct documented deficiency, and coordinate steroid management with the prescribing clinician. High-dose vitamin A has hepatic and reproductive risks and should not be used to deliberately counter necessary immunosuppression.

### Malabsorption and Nonhealing Wounds

Review the patient's bariatric operation, intake, existing supplementation, visual symptoms, liver or renal disease, and evidence of malabsorption. Follow the bariatric surveillance schedule and obtain targeted tests when deficiency is plausible. A vitamin A level is not a mandatory preoperative test for every wound or reconstructive operation.<sup>[[14]](#ref14)</sup>

### Medication and Pregnancy Review

Identify systemic or topical retinoids and high-dose supplements during preoperative medication review. Preserve the drug-specific pregnancy-prevention plan and arrange specialist review of exposures; avoid a generic one-month washout for all vitamin A products.
