# Copper

Copper is an essential trace element that serves as a cofactor for approximately **25 cuproenzymes** critical to oxidative metabolism, antioxidant defense, neurotransmitter synthesis, connective tissue formation, iron metabolism, and immune function.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[2]](#ref2)[[3]](#ref3)</sup> The adult body contains approximately **75–100 mg of copper**, with the liver as the primary storage organ.<sup>[[4]](#ref4)</sup> Copper occupies a unique position in medicine as the element central to two well-characterized genetic diseases with diametrically opposite pathophysiology: **Menkes disease** (deficiency) and **Wilson disease** (toxicity).<sup>[[5]](#ref5)[[6]](#ref6)</sup>

For reconstructive practice, consider copper deficiency in **myeloneuropathy or unexplained cytopenias with a relevant risk factor**: gastric/bariatric surgery, malabsorption, prolonged nutrition support, or excessive zinc exposure. Urinary symptoms occur in a **minority** of reported copper-deficiency myelopathy cases; isolated urgency or retention is not a reason for routine copper screening. B12 deficiency and other neurologic causes must also be assessed.<sup>[[7]](#ref7)[[9]](#ref9)[[27]](#ref27)</sup>

---

## Biochemistry and Homeostasis

Dietary copper is absorbed primarily in the **duodenum and proximal jejunum** via the copper transporter CTR1 (SLC31A1), which imports reduced Cu⁺ into enterocytes.<sup>[[4]](#ref4)</sup> From enterocytes, copper is exported into the portal circulation by the copper-transporting ATPase **ATP7A** and travels to the liver bound to albumin and histidine. Within hepatocytes, copper is incorporated into **ceruloplasmin** (which carries ~ 90% of circulating copper), delivered to cuproenzymes, or excreted into bile via **ATP7B** — the primary route of elimination.<sup>[[4]](#ref4)</sup>

### Key Copper-Dependent Enzymes

| Enzyme | Function | Copper-Related Clinical Relevance |
|---|---|---|
| **Cytochrome c oxidase** | Terminal electron-transport-chain enzyme; mitochondrial respiration | Mitochondrial dysfunction, lactic acidosis |
| **Cu/Zn superoxide dismutase (SOD1)** | Antioxidant defense; scavenges superoxide radicals | Oxidative stress; SOD1-associated ALS is a separate genetic disease, not a diagnosis of nutritional copper deficiency |
| **Ceruloplasmin (ferroxidase)** | Iron oxidation (Fe²⁺ → Fe³⁺) for transferrin loading | Impaired iron handling and anemia; aceruloplasminemia is a separate genetic disorder |
| **Lysyl oxidase** | Crosslinks collagen and elastin in connective tissue | Vascular fragility, bone abnormalities, skin laxity |
| **Dopamine-β-hydroxylase** | Converts dopamine → norepinephrine | **Dysautonomia, orthostatic hypotension** (Menkes) |
| **Tyrosinase** | Melanin synthesis | Hypopigmentation of hair and skin |
| **Peptidylglycine α-amidating monooxygenase (PAM)** | Amidation of neuropeptides | Impaired neuropeptide signaling |

## Dietary Sources and Requirements

Copper is found in **organ meats, shellfish, nuts, seeds, chocolate, legumes, and whole grains**. The adult RDA is **0.9 mg/day** and the adult tolerable upper intake level is **10 mg/day** from food and supplements; the upper limit does not govern medically supervised deficiency treatment. Routine adult parenteral nutrition generally provides **0.3–0.5 mg/day IV**, adjusted for losses and hepatobiliary function.<sup>[[28]](#ref28)[[29]](#ref29)</sup>

## Assessment of Copper Status

- **Serum copper and ceruloplasmin** — Interpret together with clinical risk, the laboratory's reference intervals and **CRP/inflammation**. Both can rise with inflammation, estrogen exposure and pregnancy; a normal value does not reliably exclude depletion in these settings. Low values also occur in conditions other than nutritional deficiency. Total serum copper alone cannot diagnose Wilson disease.<sup>[[4]](#ref4)[[28]](#ref28)[[30]](#ref30)</sup>
- **24-hour urinary copper** — Usually low in nutritional deficiency. Excretion above **100 μg/day** is typical in symptomatic Wilson disease; values above **40 μg/day** may warrant further investigation, particularly in children or asymptomatic individuals. Neither threshold establishes the diagnosis alone.<sup>[[4]](#ref4)[[10]](#ref10)[[30]](#ref30)</sup>
- **Non-ceruloplasmin-bound copper (NCC)** — The subtraction estimate is assay dependent and can produce biologically impossible negative values. Do not use a calculated “free copper” result as a stand-alone diagnostic test. **EASL–ERN 2025** incorporates the Leipzig score and relative exchangeable copper, where available, into a specialist diagnostic assessment.<sup>[[30]](#ref30)</sup>
- Functional cuproenzyme assays are research/specialist methods rather than routine first-line clinical tests.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup>

## Causes of Deficiency

| Category | Examples |
|---|---|
| **Zinc-induced** | Excessive zinc supplements, **zinc-containing denture adhesives**, coin ingestion |
| **Malabsorption** | **Bariatric surgery (RYGB, BPD/DS)**, celiac disease, short bowel syndrome, IBD |
| **Inadequate intake** | TPN without copper supplementation, severe malnutrition |
| **Increased losses** | Prolonged continuous renal-replacement therapy |
| **Genetic** | **Menkes disease (ATP7A mutations)** |

**Zinc-induced copper deficiency** deserves particular emphasis. Excess zinc upregulates **metallothionein** in enterocytes, which has a higher affinity for copper than zinc. When these enterocytes are sloughed, copper is lost into the GI tract — a functional copper malabsorption state.<sup>[[12]](#ref12)</sup> A 2026 systematic review of 37 cases found zinc-induced hematologic toxicity most commonly resulted from **oral supplements, denture-adhesive creams, and coin ingestion**, with daily elemental zinc doses ranging from ~ 50 mg to &gt; 1,500 mg.<sup>[[13]](#ref13)</sup> In a Scottish national review, **9 of 16 copper-deficient patients were using zinc-containing dental fixatives** at diagnosis.<sup>[[12]](#ref12)</sup> A study of 70 patients prescribed zinc found 62% received doses sufficient to cause copper deficiency, yet copper status was measured in only 2 patients. Unexplained anemia (9%) and compatible neurologic symptoms (7%) were signals in this retrospective audit, not confirmed incidence estimates of zinc-induced copper deficiency.<sup>[[14]](#ref14)</sup>

## Clinical Manifestations of Deficiency

### Hematologic — The MDS Mimic

Copper deficiency can cause **anemia** (macrocytic, normocytic, or microcytic), **neutropenia**, and less commonly thrombocytopenia or pancytopenia.<sup>[[7]](#ref7)[[12]](#ref12)[[15]](#ref15)</sup> Bone marrow findings include **vacuolated myeloid and erythroid precursors, ring sideroblasts, and multilineage dysplasia** — potentially mimicking **myelodysplastic syndrome (MDS)**.<sup>[[12]](#ref12)[[13]](#ref13)</sup> In the 16-patient Scottish review, cytopenias **responded** to replacement in 93%; this does not mean that all abnormalities resolved or that the same response rate applies to every patient. Include copper testing in the evaluation of otherwise unexplained cytopenias when malabsorption or excess zinc is plausible.<sup>[[12]](#ref12)</sup>

### Neurologic — Myeloneuropathy Mimicking B12 SCD

**Myeloneuropathy mimicking subacute combined degeneration** (vitamin B12 deficiency) is the most important neurologic manifestation, peaking in the 5th–6th decades with a female predominance.<sup>[[16]](#ref16)[[9]](#ref9)</sup> Patients present with:

- Posterior column dysfunction (**sensory ataxia, loss of vibration / proprioception**)
- Spasticity and hyperreflexia (UMN signs)
- Peripheral neuropathy (large fiber &gt; small fiber)
- Urinary dysfunction in some patients, **less commonly than gait and sensory abnormalities**.<sup>[[27]](#ref27)</sup>

MRI may show dorsal cervical/thoracic cord T2 hyperintensity resembling B12-related subacute combined degeneration; a normal MRI does not exclude the condition. Imaging was abnormal in **47%** of cases in an older 55-case review.<sup>[[27]](#ref27)</sup> A 2024 systematic review assembled **198 reported cases**, including 55 with prior gastric surgery. It reported neurologic improvement in 24% and return to baseline in 5.1%, but these are selected published cases with variable follow-up, not reliable individual prognostic probabilities. The consistent clinical message is to diagnose and treat promptly because neurologic recovery may be incomplete.<sup>[[9]](#ref9)</sup>

### Other

Hypopigmentation of hair, impaired wound healing, osteoporosis, increased susceptibility to infections (neutropenia + impaired phagocytic function).<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[8]](#ref8)[[17]](#ref17)</sup>

## Immune Function

Copper is essential for both innate and adaptive immunity. Neutropenia is a hallmark of deficiency, and even marginal deficiency impairs neutrophil and macrophage phagocytosis, oxidative burst, and microbial killing.<sup>[[17]](#ref17)</sup> Macrophages actively accumulate copper in phagosomes via ATP7A translocation to kill intracellular pathogens — a mechanism particularly important in defense against mycobacteria.<sup>[[18]](#ref18)[[19]](#ref19)</sup>

## Treatment of Copper Deficiency

Replacement depends on severity, absorption and ongoing losses. **ASMBS post-bariatric guidance** recommends oral elemental copper **3–8 mg/day** for mild-to-moderate deficiency, and initial IV copper **2–4 mg/day for approximately 6 days** for severe deficiency, with reassessment and subsequent oral treatment as appropriate. These are low-certainty recommendations for that population. **ESPEN 2024** general clinical-nutrition guidance gives a different severe-deficiency regimen (**4–8 mg/day IV by slow infusion**); select the regimen with the nutrition/pharmacy team rather than combining them.<sup>[[20]](#ref20)[[28]](#ref28)[[31]](#ref31)</sup>

Remove an excessive zinc source and investigate the underlying cause. Monitor copper indices, blood counts and neurologic response; hematologic recovery generally occurs over weeks, while neurologic improvement may be partial. After normalization, ASMBS recommends copper monitoring every 3 months in patients treated for deficiency.<sup>[[7]](#ref7)[[12]](#ref12)[[15]](#ref15)[[31]](#ref31)</sup>

## Post-Bariatric Surgery Monitoring

- **RYGB or BPD/DS:** routine supplementation generally supplies **2 mg/day** copper; screen with serum copper and ceruloplasmin at least annually.
- **Sleeve gastrectomy or adjustable gastric band:** routine supplementation generally supplies **1 mg/day** copper.
- Assess sooner for unexplained anemia, neutropenia, myeloneuropathy, poor healing or excess zinc exposure.
- The preventive ratio of **8–15 mg elemental zinc per 1 mg copper** belongs to bariatric supplementation guidance. It is not an instruction to add copper to Wilson disease treatment.<sup>[[20]](#ref20)[[31]](#ref31)</sup>

## Genetic Disorders of Copper Metabolism

### Menkes Disease

X-linked recessive disorder caused by mutations in **ATP7A**, resulting in impaired intestinal copper absorption and defective copper delivery to cuproenzymes.<sup>[[11]](#ref11)[[21]](#ref21)</sup> Clinical features reflect decreased activity of copper-dependent enzymes:

- Presents at **2–3 months** of age with hypotonia, seizures, failure to thrive, progressive neurodegeneration.
- Characteristic **sparse, kinky ("steely") hair**, hypopigmentation, connective-tissue abnormalities.
- Low serum copper and ceruloplasmin (though unreliable in neonates).
- Without treatment, death typically by **age 3 years**.

**FDA approved Zycubo (copper histidinate) on January 12, 2026 for pediatric Menkes disease**, the first US-approved treatment. Early subcutaneous treatment improves survival, but neurologic outcome depends on timing and genotype; prevention of neurodegeneration is not guaranteed. The approval does not include occipital horn syndrome.<sup>[[21]](#ref21)[[22]](#ref22)[[32]](#ref32)</sup>

Persistent dopamine-β-hydroxylase dysfunction can contribute to dysautonomia. A 2026 droxidopa crossover study enrolled only **three men** (two with Menkes disease and one with occipital horn syndrome), primarily to assess safety. Its preliminary orthostatic findings do not establish routine treatment efficacy.<sup>[[23]](#ref23)</sup>

### Wilson Disease

Autosomal recessive disorder caused by mutations in **ATP7B**, resulting in impaired biliary copper excretion and toxic copper accumulation in liver, brain, cornea, kidneys.<sup>[[4]](#ref4)[[6]](#ref6)[[10]](#ref10)</sup> Key features:

- Progressive liver disease (hepatitis, cirrhosis, acute liver failure).
- Neuropsychiatric manifestations (dystonia, tremor, dysarthria, psychiatric symptoms).
- **Kayser-Fleischer rings** (corneal copper deposition in Descemet's membrane).
- Low ceruloplasmin (&lt; 20 mg/dL), elevated 24-hour urine copper (&gt; 100 μg/day), elevated hepatic copper on biopsy.<sup>[[10]](#ref10)[[4]](#ref4)</sup>

Treatment requires specialist selection of **chelators (D-penicillamine or trientine)** or zinc and lifelong monitoring for under- and overtreatment. **EASL–ERN 2025 recommends chelators for significant liver disease**; absence of symptoms does not automatically make zinc the appropriate initial treatment. Zinc reduces intestinal copper absorption through metallothionein induction.<sup>[[4]](#ref4)[[30]](#ref30)</sup>

## Cuproptosis — An Emerging Concept

**Cuproptosis** (2022) is a recently described form of copper-dependent regulated cell death distinct from apoptosis, necrosis, and ferroptosis.<sup>[[24]](#ref24)[[25]](#ref25)</sup> Excess intracellular copper directly binds to **lipoylated proteins in the TCA cycle** (particularly DLAT), causing aggregation and destabilizing iron-sulfur cluster proteins → mitochondrial proteotoxic stress and cell death.<sup>[[24]](#ref24)[[26]](#ref26)</sup> Particularly active in cells relying on oxidative phosphorylation — a potential therapeutic vulnerability in certain cancers, currently preclinical.<sup>[[25]](#ref25)</sup>

## Reconstructive Relevance

| Reconstructive scenario | Practical action |
|---|---|
| **Myeloneuropathy with relevant nutritional risk**, with or without bladder dysfunction | Check copper, ceruloplasmin, zinc and B12 status as part of a neurologic evaluation; consider coexisting deficiencies. Do not assume B12/MMA must be normal or MRI must be abnormal. Address excessive zinc and arrange prompt replacement when deficient.<sup>[[7]](#ref7)[[27]](#ref27)</sup> |
| **Previous bariatric surgery** | Verify the procedure-specific supplement plan and that scheduled surveillance is current; obtain additional testing when symptoms, poor intake, malabsorption or prior deficiency warrant it. A remote bariatric operation alone is not a universal preoperative copper-panel requirement.<sup>[[20]](#ref20)[[31]](#ref31)</sup> |
| **Anemia with neutropenia or marrow dysplasia** | Consider copper deficiency alongside the hematologic differential, particularly after gastric surgery or with excessive zinc. Do not postpone other indicated evaluation on the assumption that copper explains all cytopenias.<sup>[[12]](#ref12)[[15]](#ref15)</sup> |
| **Long-term parenteral nutrition or prolonged CRRT** | Review copper provision and monitoring with nutrition support; adjust for high losses or cholestasis. CRRT losses occur in the **effluent**, not necessarily through urinary excretion.<sup>[[7]](#ref7)[[28]](#ref28)</sup> |
| **Wilson disease undergoing reconstruction** | Agree on a perioperative plan with hepatology and avoid unplanned treatment cessation. **Penicillamine may need temporary dose reduction to support wound healing**; do not prescribe an automatic switch to trientine or a generic combined zinc/chelator schedule.<sup>[[4]](#ref4)[[33]](#ref33)</sup> |
