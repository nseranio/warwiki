# Serum Albumin

Low serum albumin is associated with postoperative complications and mortality. It is useful for risk assessment, but does not diagnose malnutrition, measure muscle mass or show that nutrition support is adequate.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup>

## Thresholds and Their Limits

- **< 3.5 g/dL** is a common definition of hypoalbuminemia; use the reporting laboratory's reference interval.
- **< 3.0 g/dL**, without hepatic or renal dysfunction, is one ESPEN 2025 criterion for high metabolic risk before surgery. It is not a stand-alone malnutrition diagnosis.<sup>[[2]](#ref2)</sup>
- **≤ 3.3 g/dL** was a data-derived risk split in a retrospective plastic-surgery cohort. It is not a validated threshold for cancelling every reconstructive operation.<sup>[[3]](#ref3)</sup>

## What Changes the Concentration

Albumin has an approximately 20-day half-life under usual conditions. Inflammation changes synthesis, capillary permeability, distribution and turnover. Fluid administration, liver disease and renal or gastrointestinal protein loss also affect the concentration.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[4]](#ref4)[[5]](#ref5)</sup>

Albumin may remain normal despite profound nutritional deprivation. Conversely, acute illness can produce a low concentration in someone whose intake was previously adequate. Malnutrition and inflammation often coexist; neither a normal albumin nor a normal CRP excludes malnutrition.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup>

In a prospective cohort of 2,465 emergency-department patients, CRP > 20 mg/L and nutritional risk were independently associated with hypoalbuminemia (adjusted ORs 10.51 and 2.87, respectively). Combining albumin, CRP and NRS-2002 predicted 30-day mortality with AUC 0.82. This is an acute-care prognostic association and not a test for the cause of low albumin in every patient.<sup>[[6]](#ref6)</sup>

## What the Reconstructive Evidence Shows

| Study | Finding | Interpretation |
|---|---|---|
| **Alfertshofer 2026:** 200,015 plastic / reconstructive surgery patients in NSQIP | Complications occurred in 36.5% with albumin ≤ 3.3 g/dL versus 10.4% with albumin > 3.3 g/dL; albumin was the strongest predictor among the studied preoperative laboratory variables | Retrospective association; not evidence that raising albumin alone prevents complications |
| **Herzog 2024:** 3,038 head-and-neck free-flap patients | Flap failure 6.3% versus 2.1% with versus without hypoalbuminemia; adjusted OR 3.37 (95% CI 1.38–8.21) | Increased odds in this population, not a universal 3.4-fold risk across GU reconstruction |
| **Panayi 2024:** 34,571 free-tissue-flap records | Combined frailty and hypoalbuminemia identified increased postoperative risk; albumin was available in 16,250 records | Free-flap cohort, not a radical-cystectomy / urinary-diversion cohort; missing albumin limits generalizability |

Sources: Alfertshofer,<sup>[[3]](#ref3)</sup> Herzog,<sup>[[7]](#ref7)</sup> and Panayi.<sup>[[8]](#ref8)</sup> Observational changes in albumin during hospitalization also track disease severity and outcomes, but do not establish a target-driven nutrition treatment strategy.<sup>[[9]](#ref9)</sup>

## Clinical Approach

1. **Investigate a low result:** review inflammation or infection, fluid balance, hepatic synthesis and protein losses alongside the nutrition history.
2. **Assess nutrition independently:** a validated risk screen leads to dietetic assessment, SGA or a GLIM-based diagnosis. Screening and diagnosis are different steps.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[10]](#ref10)</sup>
3. **Treat identified problems:** provide appropriate nutrition and manage the underlying disease. A rise in albumin may reflect resolving inflammation; it does not establish that calorie or protein delivery was sufficient.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup>
4. **Plan the operation in context:** ESPEN recommends preoperative nutrition for severe malnutrition and/or high metabolic risk, considering 10–14 days even if surgery is postponed. Urgent source control or other disease-related urgency may change the balance. Do not require normalization of albumin as the treatment endpoint.<sup>[[2]](#ref2)</sup>

Albumin infusion solely to correct a low laboratory concentration is not nutritional rehabilitation. Any albumin infusion should have a separate clinical indication.<sup>[[4]](#ref4)[[5]](#ref5)</sup>
