# Iron & Ferritin

Iron deficiency can occur without anemia. Ferritin estimates iron stores, but inflammation, infection and liver injury can raise it. Interpret ferritin with the CBC, transferrin saturation (TSAT), symptoms and clinical setting; a low serum iron alone does not establish deficiency. Iron-deficiency anemia (IDA) requires both anemia and evidence of iron deficiency.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[3]](#ref3)</sup>

For major reconstruction, the practical questions are whether anemia is present, what caused it, and whether treatment can improve the patient's readiness for surgery. An abnormal ferritin is not by itself an indication for IV iron or a reason to postpone every operation.

## Biochemistry

Ferritin is a hollow shell of 24 H (heavy) and L (light) subunits that stores iron as ferric oxyhydroxide. Iron regulates its synthesis through iron-responsive proteins that bind iron-responsive elements on ferritin mRNA. Inflammatory cytokines also raise ferritin synthesis independent of iron status, which is why ferritin behaves as a positive acute-phase reactant. Serum ferritin is a small, mostly iron-poor fraction that tracks tissue stores.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup>

## Diagnostic thresholds

Ferritin 1 ng/mL = 1 μg/L. Diagnostic thresholds, treatment-entry criteria and treatment targets answer different questions.

| Setting | Interpretation |
|---|---|
| General clinical assessment | ASH 2026 conditionally suggests ferritin ≤30 ng/mL to diagnose iron deficiency in nonpregnant adults who do not menstruate and in menstruating individuals. Its remarks consider ≤50 ng/mL appropriate for nonpregnant adults who do not menstruate with iron-deficiency symptoms or ongoing risk factors, and for menstruating individuals with heavy menstrual bleeding, other excessive abnormal uterine bleeding, iron-deficiency symptoms, added risk factors, upcoming surgery or planned pregnancy.<sup>[[15]](#ref15)</sup> In nine studies of nonhealthy adults (512 participants) using ferritin &lt;30 ng/mL, the Cochrane review found pooled sensitivity 79% (95% CI 58–91%) and specificity 98% (95% CI 91–100%), with low-certainty evidence.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup> |
| GI evaluation **in a patient with anemia** | AGA recommends &lt;45 ng/mL rather than &lt;15 for diagnosing iron deficiency.<sup>[[4]](#ref4)</sup> |
| Surgical anemia with inflammation | ICCAMS uses ferritin &lt;30 ng/mL and/or TSAT &lt;20% to identify iron deficiency, and ferritin &lt;100 ng/mL may be appropriate in inflammation. Low TSAT with raised ferritin may reflect iron restriction or mixed disease.<sup>[[3]](#ref3)</sup> |
| CKD | Use the 2026 KDIGO treatment criteria below, with dialysis status specified.<sup>[[5]](#ref5)</sup> |
| Heart failure | Common trial criteria are ferritin &lt;100 ng/mL, or 100–299 with TSAT &lt;20%. These criteria identify iron deficiency, which need not include anemia.<sup>[[11]](#ref11)</sup> |

Laboratory reference intervals can have lower limits below clinically useful decision thresholds. A 2024 systematic review found substantial methodological weaknesses in how many ferritin reference intervals were established; an automatically reported normal result should not end the evaluation when deficiency remains likely.<sup>[[2]](#ref2)</sup>

## Interpreting ferritin in inflammation

Ferritin can be normal or elevated despite depleted stores. Review CRP when inflammation is uncertain and consider recent infection, liver disease, transfusion and iron treatment. TSAT helps assess iron availability, but inflammation also lowers transferrin and can complicate its interpretation. Ferritin 30–100 with low TSAT is not automatically pure functional deficiency; absolute and inflammatory mechanisms can coexist.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[3]](#ref3)</sup>

Reticulocyte hemoglobin or soluble transferrin receptor may help when routine results conflict. The sTfR/log-ferritin index is assay dependent and needs a locally validated interpretation.<sup>[[3]](#ref3)</sup>

## CKD: KDIGO 2026

For anemia with CKD, KDIGO suggests starting iron under these conditions; the recommendations are 2D, reflecting very low-certainty evidence.<sup>[[5]](#ref5)</sup>

| Population | Treatment-entry criteria | Route |
|---|---|---|
| Hemodialysis | Ferritin ≤500 ng/mL AND TSAT ≤30% | IV preferred |
| No dialysis or peritoneal dialysis | Ferritin &lt;100 AND TSAT &lt;40%, OR ferritin ≥100 to &lt;300 AND TSAT &lt;25% | Oral or IV, individualized |

Routine iron is generally withheld when ferritin is &gt;700 ng/mL or TSAT is ≥40%. These are thresholds for withholding iron, not treatment targets. Monitor Hb, ferritin and TSAT every 3 months without hemodialysis and every 1–3 months with hemodialysis, sooner after bleeding or treatment changes. Consider pausing during systemic infection. Poor oral tolerance or an inadequate response after 1–3 months supports switching to IV treatment. Profound deficiency without anemia (ferritin &lt;30 with TSAT &lt;20%) can justify treatment after shared decision-making; it does not mandate immediate IV iron for everyone.<sup>[[5]](#ref5)</sup>

## Preoperative patient blood management

Screen for anemia before surgery beyond minor procedures with negligible blood loss, ideally early enough to evaluate and treat the cause. ICCAMS supports Hb &lt;13 g/dL in both sexes as a preoperative anemia threshold and assessment about four weeks before surgery when feasible. A short interval should still prompt evaluation. Look for ongoing bleeding, iron deficiency, renal disease, B12 or folate deficiency and other hematologic causes rather than prescribing iron for every low Hb.<sup>[[3]](#ref3)</sup>

Treat confirmed IDA. The 2024 AGA expert review advises oral iron at most once daily, with every-other-day dosing sometimes better tolerated; it advises IV iron when oral iron is not tolerated, ferritin fails to improve after an oral trial, or absorption is unlikely.<sup>[[16]](#ref16)</sup> IV iron may also suit a preoperative interval too short for oral repletion; ICCAMS generally favors IV iron for preoperative IDA. The decision to delay an elective operation depends on anemia severity, expected blood loss, treatment response and the consequences of delay.<sup>[[3]](#ref3)</sup>

The PREVENTT trial was a blinded, multicenter trial that randomized 487 anemic adults before major open abdominal surgery to ferric carboxymaltose 1,000 mg or placebo 10–42 days preoperatively.<sup>[[9]](#ref9)</sup> From randomization through 30 days after surgery, transfusion or death occurred in 69/237 (29%) versus 67/237 (28%), respectively (RR 1.03, 95% CI 0.78–1.37).<sup>[[9]](#ref9)</sup> Preoperative Hb increased, but neither this composite nor the coprimary transfusion count improved. The study did not require iron deficiency for entry, and its lower eight-week readmission rate was a secondary finding. PREVENTT did not show that IV iron prevents transfusion or death in its abdominal-surgery population.<sup>[[9]](#ref9)</sup> A 2026 network meta-analysis of 34 perioperative randomized trials (4,688 participants) found lower transfusion requirements with ferric carboxymaltose versus placebo (OR 0.72, 95% credible interval 0.55–0.91); mixed surgical populations limit direct application to reconstruction.<sup>[[14]](#ref14)</sup>

### IV preparations are not interchangeable

The following are US adult IDA regimens, not the separate heart-failure dosing schedule. Check the current product label and indication.<sup>[[7]](#ref7)[[8]](#ref8)</sup>

| Agent | Labeled course |
|---|---|
| **Ferric carboxymaltose (Injectafer)** | At ≥50 kg: 750 mg twice, at least seven days apart (1,500 mg total); alternatively 15 mg/kg, maximum 1,000 mg, once per course. Below 50 kg: 15 mg/kg twice, at least seven days apart. 1,500 mg is not a single dose.<sup>[[7]](#ref7)</sup> |
| **Ferric derisomaltose (Monoferric)** | At ≥50 kg: 1,000 mg once. Below 50 kg: 20 mg/kg once. Infused over at least 20 minutes.<sup>[[8]](#ref8)</sup> |

Check product-specific contraindications before infusion: Injectafer is contraindicated with hypersensitivity to it or its components, and Monoferric with a history of serious hypersensitivity to it or its components. Administer where serious hypersensitivity can be treated, and monitor blood pressure after each Injectafer dose.<sup>[[7]](#ref7)[[8]](#ref8)</sup> Both US labels require observation during administration and for at least 30 minutes afterward, until clinically stable.<sup>[[7]](#ref7)[[8]](#ref8)</sup>

:::warning[Injectafer and phosphate depletion]
The FDA added a boxed warning on September 1, 2026 for symptomatic hypophosphatemia after single or multiple ferric carboxymaltose doses, including cases with normal baseline phosphate and no apparent risk factors; severe, prolonged hypophosphatemia can cause osteomalacia and fractures.<sup>[[7]](#ref7)</sup> Risk factors in the Injectafer label include gastrointestinal disorders associated with malabsorption of fat-soluble vitamins or phosphate, inflammatory bowel disease, current or prior use of drugs affecting proximal renal tubular function, hyperparathyroidism, vitamin D deficiency, malnutrition and hereditary hemorrhagic telangiectasia. Correct preexisting hypophosphatemia; monitor serum phosphate in patients at risk for chronic low phosphate and before a repeat course in patients at risk for low serum phosphate or anyone retreated within three months.<sup>[[7]](#ref7)</sup> Severe symptomatic or persistent hypophosphatemia may require permanent discontinuation. New weakness, fatigue or bone pain after treatment can reflect phosphate depletion rather than persistent anemia.<sup>[[6]](#ref6)</sup>
:::

## Finding the cause

- **GU or gynecologic bleeding.** Quantify and investigate hematuria or heavy menstrual bleeding. Fistula or prior reconstruction does not by itself indicate iron deficiency, and iron replacement does not stop the bleeding source.
- **Poor intake or malabsorption.** Consider restrictive intake, bariatric surgery and celiac disease. Other deficiencies may coexist.
- **Diversion or bowel reconstruction.** Evaluate anemia according to the bowel segment, renal function, blood loss and laboratory pattern. Diversion-related anemia has several causes; iron infusion has no universal schedule and depends on the cause and laboratory pattern.
- **Unexplained IDA.** AGA recommends bidirectional endoscopy for asymptomatic men and postmenopausal women. In asymptomatic premenopausal women it is a conditional suggestion, with the balance of investigation and an initial iron trial discussed in context. In adults with IDA and no identifiable cause after bidirectional endoscopy, AGA conditionally suggests noninvasive *H. pylori* testing and treatment if positive. For asymptomatic adults with IDA and plausible celiac disease, it conditionally suggests serology first and small-bowel biopsy only if positive. In uncomplicated asymptomatic adults with IDA after negative bidirectional endoscopy, it conditionally suggests an initial iron trial before routine video capsule endoscopy.<sup>[[4]](#ref4)</sup>

## Hyperferritinemia

A high ferritin alone does not establish iron overload. Review inflammation or infection, liver injury, metabolic disease, alcohol, malignancy, transfusions and TSAT. EASL recommends genetic evaluation for the appropriate clinical and biochemical pattern (raised ferritin with elevated TSAT or otherwise unexplained persistently high TSAT), with consent and attention to ancestry and family history. In patients with high TSAT and elevated ferritin but other HFE genotypes, EASL requires hepatic iron overload on MRI or liver biopsy to diagnose haemochromatosis. Do not prescribe venesection solely because ferritin is elevated.<sup>[[10]](#ref10)</sup>

Very high ferritin in a systemically ill patient should raise concern for severe infection, acute liver injury or HLH/MAS, among other causes. No single ferritin threshold, such as 9,000 or 25,000 μg/L, diagnoses HLH/MAS. Assessment integrates the syndrome, serial results and specialist evaluation.<sup>[[13]](#ref13)</sup>

## Heart failure: keep the evidence in context

FAIR-HF2 (2025, 1,105 patients) did not meet its prespecified multiplicity-adjusted primary endpoints despite a nominal HR 0.79, P=.04 for cardiovascular death or first HF hospitalization.<sup>[[11]](#ref11)</sup> The updated six-trial meta-analysis (7,175 patients) found fewer recurrent HF hospitalizations or cardiovascular deaths over complete follow-up (RR 0.81, 95% interval 0.63–0.97), but the cardiovascular-mortality estimate alone remained inconclusive (HR 0.87, 0.73–1.04).<sup>[[12]](#ref12)</sup>
