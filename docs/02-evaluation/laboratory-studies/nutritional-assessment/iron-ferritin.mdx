# Iron & Ferritin

**Iron deficiency can occur without anemia.** Ferritin estimates iron stores, but inflammation, infection and liver injury can raise it. Interpret ferritin with the CBC, transferrin saturation (TSAT), symptoms and clinical setting; a low serum iron alone does not establish deficiency. **Iron-deficiency anemia (IDA) requires both anemia and evidence of iron deficiency.**<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[3]](#ref3)</sup>

For major reconstruction, the practical questions are whether anemia is present, what caused it, and whether treatment can improve the patient's readiness for surgery. An abnormal ferritin is not by itself an indication for IV iron or a reason to postpone every operation.

## Diagnostic thresholds

Ferritin **1 ng/mL = 1 μg/L**. Diagnostic thresholds, treatment-entry criteria and treatment targets answer different questions.

| Setting | Interpretation |
|---|---|
| General clinical assessment | **Ferritin &lt;30 ng/mL** strongly supports depleted stores. The Cochrane review found **79% sensitivity and 98% specificity**, with wide confidence intervals and low-certainty evidence, in nine studies of nonhealthy adults using this threshold; performance is not universal. <sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup> |
| GI evaluation **in a patient with anemia** | AGA recommends **&lt;45 ng/mL** rather than &lt;15 for diagnosing iron deficiency. This does not redefine anemia or mean every nonanemic person below 45 needs endoscopy. <sup>[[4]](#ref4)</sup> |
| Surgical anemia with inflammation | ICCAMS uses ferritin **&lt;30 ng/mL and/or TSAT &lt;20%** to identify iron deficiency; ferritin **&lt;100 ng/mL** may be appropriate in inflammation. Low TSAT with raised ferritin may reflect iron restriction or mixed disease. <sup>[[3]](#ref3)</sup> |
| CKD | Use the **2026 KDIGO treatment criteria below**, with dialysis status specified. <sup>[[5]](#ref5)</sup> |
| Heart failure | Common trial criteria are ferritin **&lt;100 ng/mL**, or **100–299 with TSAT &lt;20%**. These identify iron deficiency, which need not include anemia. <sup>[[11]](#ref11)</sup> |

Laboratory reference intervals can have lower limits below clinically useful decision thresholds. A 2024 systematic review found substantial methodological weaknesses in how many ferritin reference intervals were established; an automatically reported “normal” result should not end the evaluation when deficiency remains likely.<sup>[[2]](#ref2)</sup>

## Interpreting ferritin in inflammation

Ferritin can be normal or elevated despite depleted stores. Review CRP when inflammation is uncertain and consider recent infection, liver disease, transfusion and iron treatment. TSAT helps assess iron availability, but inflammation also lowers transferrin and can complicate its interpretation. **Ferritin 30–100 with low TSAT is not automatically pure functional deficiency**; absolute and inflammatory mechanisms can coexist.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[3]](#ref3)</sup>

Reticulocyte hemoglobin or soluble transferrin receptor may help when routine results conflict. The sTfR/log-ferritin index is assay dependent; use a validated local interpretation rather than a universal ratio above 2 or a fixed sensitivity claim.<sup>[[3]](#ref3)</sup>

## CKD: KDIGO 2026

For **anemia with CKD**, KDIGO suggests starting iron under these conditions; the recommendations are **2D**, reflecting very low-certainty evidence.<sup>[[5]](#ref5)</sup>

| Population | Treatment-entry criteria | Route |
|---|---|---|
| Hemodialysis | Ferritin **≤500 ng/mL AND TSAT ≤30%** | IV preferred |
| No dialysis or peritoneal dialysis | Ferritin **&lt;100 AND TSAT &lt;40%**, **OR** ferritin **≥100 to &lt;300 AND TSAT &lt;25%** | Oral or IV, individualized |

Generally withhold routine iron when **ferritin &gt;700 ng/mL OR TSAT ≥40%**; these are stopping considerations, not targets. Monitor Hb, ferritin and TSAT every **3 months** without hemodialysis and every **1–3 months** with hemodialysis, sooner after bleeding or treatment changes. Consider pausing during systemic infection. Poor oral tolerance or an inadequate response after **1–3 months** supports switching to IV treatment. Profound deficiency without anemia—ferritin **&lt;30 with TSAT &lt;20%**—can justify treatment after shared decision-making; it does not mandate immediate IV iron for everyone.<sup>[[5]](#ref5)</sup>

## Preoperative patient blood management

Screen for anemia before surgery beyond minor procedures with negligible blood loss, ideally early enough to evaluate and treat the cause. ICCAMS supports **Hb &lt;13 g/dL in both sexes** as a preoperative anemia threshold and assessment about four weeks before surgery when feasible. A short interval should still prompt evaluation. Look for ongoing bleeding, iron deficiency, renal disease, B12/folate deficiency and other hematologic causes rather than prescribing iron for every low Hb.<sup>[[3]](#ref3)</sup>

Treat confirmed IDA. IV iron is often useful when oral therapy is poorly tolerated, poorly absorbed or unlikely to work within the available interval; ICCAMS favors it for preoperative IDA. The decision to delay an elective operation depends on anemia severity, expected blood loss, treatment response and the consequences of delay.<sup>[[3]](#ref3)</sup>

**PREVENTT prevents overpromising:** this blinded, multicenter trial randomized **487** anemic adults before major open abdominal surgery to ferric carboxymaltose 1,000 mg or placebo **10–42 days** preoperatively. Transfusion or death occurred in **69/237 (29%) versus 67/237 (28%)**, respectively (**RR 1.03, 95% CI 0.78–1.37**). Hb increased, but neither coprimary transfusion outcome improved. The study did not require iron deficiency for entry, and its lower eight-week readmission rate was a secondary finding. It supports realistic counseling rather than a promise that IV iron prevents transfusion or death in every reconstructive procedure.<sup>[[9]](#ref9)</sup>

### IV preparations are not interchangeable

The following are **US adult IDA regimens**, not the separate heart-failure dosing schedule. Check the current product label and indication.<sup>[[7]](#ref7)[[8]](#ref8)</sup>

| Agent | Labeled course |
|---|---|
| **Ferric carboxymaltose (Injectafer)** | At ≥50 kg: **750 mg twice, at least seven days apart** (1,500 mg total); alternatively **15 mg/kg, maximum 1,000 mg**, once per course. Below 50 kg: **15 mg/kg twice**, at least seven days apart. **1,500 mg is not the standard single dose.** |
| **Ferric derisomaltose (Monoferric)** | At ≥50 kg: **1,000 mg once**; below 50 kg: **20 mg/kg once**, infused over **at least 20 minutes**. |

Administer where serious hypersensitivity can be treated. Both US labels require observation during administration and for **at least 30 minutes afterward**, until clinically stable.<sup>[[7]](#ref7)[[8]](#ref8)</sup>

:::warning[Injectafer and phosphate depletion]
The FDA added a **boxed warning on September 1, 2026** for symptomatic hypophosphatemia, which can cause osteomalacia and fractures. Correct preexisting low phosphate; monitor patients at risk and check phosphate before a repeat course in anyone at risk **or receiving another course within three months**. Severe symptomatic or persistent hypophosphatemia may require permanent discontinuation. New weakness, fatigue or bone pain after treatment can reflect phosphate depletion rather than persistent anemia.<sup>[[6]](#ref6)</sup>
:::

## Finding the cause

- **GU or gynecologic bleeding:** quantify and investigate hematuria or heavy menstrual bleeding. Neither a fistula diagnosis nor a history of reconstruction establishes iron deficiency; replacement does not stop the bleeding source.
- **Poor intake or malabsorption:** consider restrictive intake, bariatric surgery and celiac disease. Other deficiencies may coexist.
- **Diversion or bowel reconstruction:** evaluate anemia according to the bowel segment, renal function, blood loss and laboratory pattern; do not assume all anemia is iron deficiency or impose a universal iron-infusion schedule.
- **Unexplained IDA:** AGA recommends bidirectional endoscopy for asymptomatic men and postmenopausal women. In asymptomatic premenopausal women it is a **conditional suggestion**, with the balance of investigation and an initial iron trial discussed in context. After negative endoscopy, H. pylori testing and other evaluation depend on the clinical picture; suspected celiac disease warrants serologic assessment.<sup>[[4]](#ref4)</sup>

## Hyperferritinemia

A high ferritin alone does **not** establish iron overload. Review inflammation/infection, liver injury, metabolic disease, alcohol, malignancy, transfusions and TSAT. EASL recommends genetic evaluation for the appropriate clinical/biochemical pattern—raised ferritin with elevated TSAT or otherwise unexplained persistently high TSAT—with consent and attention to ancestry and family history. Hepatic MRI can help confirm overload when the genotype does not settle the diagnosis. Do not prescribe venesection solely because ferritin is elevated.<sup>[[10]](#ref10)</sup>

Very high ferritin in a systemically ill patient should raise concern for severe infection, acute liver injury or **HLH/MAS**, among other causes. A threshold near 9,000 or 25,000 μg/L is not a universal diagnostic rule or a closed differential. HLH/MAS assessment integrates the syndrome, serial results and specialist evaluation; ferritin alone is insufficient.<sup>[[13]](#ref13)</sup>

## Heart failure: keep the evidence in context

**FAIR-HF2 (2025, 1,105 patients)** did not meet its prespecified multiplicity-adjusted primary endpoints despite a nominal **HR 0.79, P=.04** for cardiovascular death or first HF hospitalization. The updated six-trial meta-analysis (**7,175 patients**) found fewer recurrent HF hospitalizations/cardiovascular deaths overall (**RR 0.81, 95% interval 0.63–0.97**), but the cardiovascular-mortality estimate alone remained inconclusive (**HR 0.87, 0.73–1.04**). These are HF-specific findings, not proof of a mortality or surgical benefit for all patients with a low ferritin.<sup>[[11]](#ref11)[[12]](#ref12)</sup>
