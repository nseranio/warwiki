# Transferrin

Transferrin is a liver-synthesized iron-transport protein with an approximately 7–8-day half-life. Each molecule can bind two ferric ions. Serum concentrations are influenced by iron status, inflammation, hepatic function and protein losses; transferrin is not a stand-alone nutritional-status test.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[2]](#ref2)[[3]](#ref3)</sup>

## Iron Studies

- **Total iron-binding capacity (TIBC)** relates to transferrin concentration. It often rises in iron deficiency and falls with inflammation or impaired hepatic synthesis.
- **Transferrin saturation (TSAT)** is serum iron divided by TIBC, multiplied by 100, using compatible units.
- Interpret TSAT with ferritin, CBC, the clinical context and the laboratory method. Iron overload and iron deficiency pathways use different thresholds; a single “normal range” does not answer every clinical question.<sup>[[3]](#ref3)[[4]](#ref4)</sup>

Iron deficiency may raise transferrin while concurrent inflammation lowers it. Therefore, a normal concentration does not exclude deficiency. Similarly, a low TIBC can make calculated TSAT appear relatively high despite reduced circulating iron.<sup>[[3]](#ref3)[[5]](#ref5)</sup>

### Preoperative Anemia

ICCAMS recommends investigating the cause of anemia before surgery. Its iron-deficiency criteria include **ferritin < 30 ng/mL and/or TSAT < 20%**; with inflammation, **ferritin < 100 ng/mL** may indicate deficiency. These are diagnostic aids, rather than a universal instruction to give IV iron to every patient with low TSAT. The panel favors IV over oral iron for preoperative iron-deficiency anemia, with treatment individualized to the clinical context and time available.<sup>[[6]](#ref6)</sup>

Use the [Iron & Ferritin](/docs/evaluation/laboratory-studies/nutritional-assessment/iron-ferritin) and [Preoperative Labs](/docs/evaluation/laboratory-studies/preoperative-labs) pages for the wider workup. Dialysis, heart failure and other inflammatory diseases have their own treatment frameworks; do not transplant their ferritin thresholds into every surgical pathway.

### Soluble Transferrin Receptor

Soluble transferrin receptor (sTfR), sometimes combined with log ferritin, can help when routine tests leave iron deficiency uncertain in an inflammatory setting. A prospective multicenter study supported its additional diagnostic value.<sup>[[7]](#ref7)</sup> Assay availability, method-specific cutoffs and altered erythropoiesis limit interchangeability. It is an adjunct and not a universal replacement for ferritin and TSAT.<sup>[[3]](#ref3)[[6]](#ref6)</sup>

## Why It Is Not a Nutritional Marker

Like albumin and prealbumin, transferrin is a negative acute-phase protein. Iron deficiency can raise its concentration while inflammation or hepatic disease lowers it, obscuring nutritional interpretation.<sup>[[2]](#ref2)</sup>

Historical studies illustrate this limitation:

- **Roza 1984:** correlation with body cell mass at the group level did not produce reliable individual classification; reported false-positive and false-negative rates were 60% and 31%. Transferrin did not change significantly after two weeks of parenteral nutrition in the treated subgroup.<sup>[[8]](#ref8)</sup>
- **Morlese 1997:** kinetic measurements in 14 children with severe protein-energy malnutrition showed only fair correlations with wasting indices. This is pediatric mechanistic evidence and not an adult operative decision rule.<sup>[[9]](#ref9)</sup>

Screen and assess nutrition using weight and intake history, examination and an accepted clinical framework. Do not titrate nutrition support to transferrin concentration.<sup>[[2]](#ref2)</sup>

## Liver Disease and Dialysis

A mechanistic study including liver and serum samples from 40 patients with advanced liver disease linked transferrin with hepatic HNF4α activity. This is exploratory biomarker evidence; it does not establish routine transferrin testing as an additional preoperative liver-clearance test.<sup>[[10]](#ref10)</sup>

In a 59-patient hemodialysis cohort, lower TIBC was associated with worse SGA classification, and the lower denominator could increase calculated TSAT. This supports interpreting iron indices together, not diagnosing nutrition status or prescribing iron from TIBC alone.<sup>[[5]](#ref5)</sup>

## Comparison with Other Visceral Proteins

| Feature | Albumin | Prealbumin | Transferrin |
|---|---|---|---|
| Approximate half-life | 20 days | 2 days | 7–8 days |
| Negative acute-phase response | Yes | Yes | Yes |
| Measures nutritional intake or muscle stores reliably | No | No | No |
| Important confounding | Distribution, dilution, synthesis and losses | Inflammation and renal / hepatic disease | Iron status, inflammation, hepatic synthesis and losses |
| Clinical use | Risk assessment in context | Selected prognostic information; no routine nutrition-monitoring target | Iron-status assessment as part of a panel |

Use laboratory-specific reference intervals. A short half-life does not make a visceral protein a better therapeutic target.<sup>[[2]](#ref2)[[3]](#ref3)</sup>
