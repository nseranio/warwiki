# Vitamin D

Vitamin D supports calcium absorption and bone mineralization. Preventive supplementation in otherwise healthy people and treatment of established deficiency are different decisions. The 2024 Endocrine Society prevention guideline replaces its 2011 guideline; it does not cover all management of osteoporosis, malabsorption, or other conditions that substantially alter vitamin D physiology.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[2]](#ref2)</sup>

## Metabolism and Testing

Vitamin D3 (cholecalciferol) comes from skin synthesis, food, or supplements; D2 (ergocalciferol) is another dietary or supplemental form. Hepatic hydroxylation produces 25-hydroxyvitamin D [25(OH)D], the usual status marker. Renal conversion produces 1,25-dihydroxyvitamin D (calcitriol), regulated by calcium, phosphate, PTH, and FGF23. A 1,25-dihydroxyvitamin D level is not a substitute for 25(OH)D when assessing nutritional status.<sup>[[3]](#ref3)[[4]](#ref4)</sup>

| Interpretation framework | Meaning |
|---|---|
| NAM nutritional assessment | Below 12 ng/mL indicates risk of deficiency; 12 to below 20 ng/mL may be inadequate; at least 20 ng/mL is adequate for most people. Multiply ng/mL by 2.5 for nmol/L. |
| Endocrine Society 2024 prevention guidance | Does not establish a universal blood target for disease prevention. |
| Condition-specific treatment | Osteoporosis, bariatric malabsorption, and CKD require their own assessment and treatment context. |

Thresholds are not interchangeable across these settings. Interpret an actual result alongside symptoms, calcium, kidney function, nutrition, and the reason for testing.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[3]](#ref3)[[4]](#ref4)</sup>

## Prevention: What to Recommend

For generally healthy adults aged 19–74, the Endocrine Society suggests against routine supplementation beyond dietary reference intake, and against routine 25(OH)D screening. Obesity or dark complexion alone does not establish a screening indication. These recommendations do not preclude testing for a clinical indication such as hypocalcemia.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[2]](#ref2)</sup>

The usual adult RDA is 600 IU/day through age 70 and 800 IU/day after 70, counting food and supplements.<sup>[[3]](#ref3)</sup>

The guideline conditionally supports empiric supplementation in these groups:<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup>

| Population | Rationale and limits |
|---|---|
| Ages 1–18 | Rickets prevention and possible respiratory-infection benefit. |
| Age ≥75 | Possible small mortality benefit; overall recommendation has moderate-certainty evidence. |
| Pregnancy | Possible obstetric/neonatal benefit; low-certainty evidence. |
| High-risk prediabetes | Possible reduction in diabetes progression **alongside lifestyle intervention**. |

Trial doses varied substantially. Reported averages of roughly 2,500 IU/day in pregnancy or 3,500 IU/day in prediabetes are not universal prescribed doses. For adults ≥50 with an indication for supplementation or treatment, the guideline favors lower daily dosing over intermittent large doses.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup>

## Large Trials and Clinically Important Outcomes

### Fractures

- **VITAL:** 25,871 participants; D3 2,000 IU/day; median 5.3 years. Total fracture risk was not significantly reduced: HR 0.98 (95% CI 0.89–1.08). Participants were not selected for deficiency, low bone mass, or osteoporosis; this does not settle treatment of osteomalacia or severe deficiency.<sup>[[5]](#ref5)</sup>
- **D-Health:** 21,315 randomized, 20,326 included in the linked-data fracture analysis; D3 60,000 IU/month. Over median 5.1 years, at least one fracture occurred in 5.6% versus 5.9%; HR 0.94 (0.84–1.06). This tertiary outcome did not establish fracture prevention.<sup>[[6]](#ref6)</sup>

### Diabetes Prevention

A 2023 individual-participant meta-analysis of three randomized trials (4,190 people with prediabetes) found an adjusted diabetes HR of 0.85 (0.75–0.96), with 3.3 percentage points absolute risk reduction over three years; NNT approximately 30. This is a pooled result and not a statistically significant primary D2d result alone. Two trials used cholecalciferol and one used the active analogue eldecalcitol.<sup>[[7]](#ref7)</sup>

Analyses associating achieved 25(OH)D ≥50 ng/mL with larger benefit did not randomize patients to blood-level targets. They do not justify escalating everyone to that concentration. Safety events were uncommon, with imprecise estimates; supplementation does not replace weight, activity, or other indicated diabetes-prevention care.<sup>[[7]](#ref7)</sup>

### Cardiovascular Disease and Cancer

VITAL did not significantly reduce major cardiovascular events (MI, stroke, or cardiovascular death), HR 0.97 (0.85–1.12), or invasive cancer, HR 0.96 (0.88–1.06). Overall cancer mortality was also not significantly lower; the favorable HR of 0.75 came from an analysis excluding the first two years, and should not be presented as the overall primary finding.<sup>[[8]](#ref8)</sup>

The 2025 D-Health cancer analysis included 21,308 participants and found no reduction in cancer incidence, HR 1.02 (0.95–1.10). Mortality hypotheses are not evidence that supplementation prevents GU cancer or substitutes for cancer treatment.<sup>[[9]](#ref9)</sup>

### Autoimmune Disease

VITAL's prespecified ancillary study initially reported fewer confirmed autoimmune diagnoses with vitamin D, HR 0.78 (0.61–0.99). The 2024 follow-up both extended observation and identified additional cases: the revised randomized-period estimate was 0.85 (0.70–1.04); over approximately seven years it was 0.98 (0.83–1.17). The original 22% reduction should not be presented as an established durable preventive effect.<sup>[[10]](#ref10)[[11]](#ref11)</sup>

## Deficiency Treatment and Safety

Treatment depends on the cause, severity, absorption, calcium balance, and kidney function. Correct deficiency when indicated; general-population negative prevention trials do not negate that treatment.

| Setting | Practical approach |
|---|---|
| **Postmenopausal osteoporosis** | AACE 2020 describes D3 **5,000 IU/day for 8–12 weeks** for low levels, then usually **1,000–2,000 IU/day**, adjusted to the measured response and its condition-specific target above 30 ng/mL. This is an osteoporosis regimen, not a universal prescription for every reconstructive patient. |
| **Bariatric malabsorption** | Use the bariatric team's procedure-specific surveillance and replacement plan; standard nutritional intake may be insufficient. |
| **CKD** | KDIGO permits assessing 25(OH)D and correcting deficiency with nutritional vitamin D. In adults with CKD G3a–G5 **not on dialysis**, calcitriol/analogues are not routine; reserve them for selected severe, progressive hyperparathyroidism, generally in G4–G5. Dialysis regimens require renal-team assessment. |
| **Active granulomatous disease or hypercalcemia** | Repletion needs caution and calcium monitoring; avoid an automatic loading regimen. |

D3 is generally preferred for replacement; D2 is an alternative in appropriate circumstances. When using D2, ensure the assay adequately measures both 25(OH)D2 and 25(OH)D3. Do not prescribe calcitriol simply because someone has kidney or liver disease.<sup>[[4]](#ref4)[[12]](#ref12)[[13]](#ref13)</sup>

The adult tolerable upper intake level is 4,000 IU/day for routine intake. Clinician-directed deficiency treatment can exceed this temporarily with monitoring; 10,000 IU/day is not a general maintenance recommendation. Excess can cause hypercalcemia, hypercalciuria, and kidney injury.<sup>[[3]](#ref3)</sup> In the Calgary trial (311 adults), 4,000 or 10,000 IU/day for three years produced lower radial volumetric bone density than 400 IU/day. This was a bone-density finding, not proof of a corresponding fracture increase.<sup>[[14]](#ref14)</sup>

## Reconstructive Relevance

- **Augmentation or diversion:** evaluate chronic acidosis, kidney function, dietary intake, immobility, and fracture risk. Add vitamin D and mineral-bone investigations when clinically indicated; there is no universal requirement for a 25(OH)D–PTH–phosphate panel in every diversion patient. Follow [Renal Function & Metabolic Surveillance](/docs/evaluation/laboratory-studies/renal-function-metabolic-surveillance).
- **Urogynecology:** use age- and risk-based [osteoporosis screening](/docs/special-populations/05e-womens-health/osteoporosis-screening). Prolapse surgery alone does not mandate DXA plus vitamin D testing.
- **Gender-affirming care:** assess actual bone-risk factors, including prolonged hypogonadism or gonadectomy without adequate hormone replacement; surgery or hormone therapy alone does not establish deficiency.
- **Bariatric or congenital care:** malabsorption, restricted intake, reduced weight bearing, acidosis, and CKD may warrant targeted evaluation. Deficiency is not inevitable after every bariatric operation. Routine FGF23 or 1,25-dihydroxyvitamin D testing is not a standard nutritional screen.<sup>[[12]](#ref12)[[13]](#ref13)</sup>
