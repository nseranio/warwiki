# Folate

Folate (vitamin B9) is a water-soluble B vitamin required for one-carbon metabolism, DNA synthesis, amino-acid homeostasis and epigenetic regulation.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[2]](#ref2)[[3]](#ref3)</sup> It is the primary carrier of one-carbon units required for de novo synthesis of purines and thymidylate (for DNA replication) and for remethylation of homocysteine to methionine, generating S-adenosylmethionine (SAM), the universal methyl donor.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[4]](#ref4)</sup> Folate deficiency remains clinically relevant despite mandatory folic-acid fortification, particularly in pregnancy, alcohol use disorder, and patients on certain medications.

For the reconstructive urologist and urogynecologist, folate matters in three scenarios: (1) preconception counseling in any reproductive-age patient undergoing reconstruction with downstream pregnancy potential, (2) adult congenital or spina bifida transitional-urology patients (NTD pathology context, methotrexate / valproate exposure, possible familial NTD history), and (3) drug-induced deficiency in patients on methotrexate, anticonvulsants, sulfasalazine (IBD), trimethoprim chronic suppression for rUTI, or chronic alcohol use.

---

## Biochemistry and Metabolism

"Folate" refers to the naturally occurring reduced forms of vitamin B9 (pteroylglutamates); **folic acid** is the synthetic, oxidized form used in supplements and fortified foods. Folic acid has approximately 70% higher bioavailability than food folate.<sup>[[5]](#ref5)</sup> After absorption in the proximal jejunum, dietary folate is converted to **5-methyltetrahydrofolate (5-MTHF)**, the predominant circulating form, which donates its methyl group to homocysteine via the B12-dependent enzyme methionine synthase.<sup>[[2]](#ref2)[[3]](#ref3)</sup>

Two metabolic outputs depend on folate:

- **Nucleotide synthesis.** 5,10-methylenetetrahydrofolate is required by thymidylate synthase for conversion of dUMP to dTMP. Folate deficiency causes uracil misincorporation into DNA, leading to strand breaks and impaired DNA repair.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[4]](#ref4)</sup>
- **Methylation reactions.** 5-MTHF feeds the methionine cycle to generate SAM, which methylates DNA, histones, proteins, and lipids. Disruption leads to hyperhomocysteinemia and aberrant DNA methylation.<sup>[[3]](#ref3)[[6]](#ref6)</sup>

## Dietary Sources and Requirements

Folate occurs naturally in dark green leafy vegetables, legumes, citrus fruits and liver.<sup>[[5]](#ref5)[[7]](#ref7)</sup> The RDA for adults is 400 μg dietary folate equivalents (DFE)/day, rising to 600 μg DFE/day in pregnancy.<sup>[[8]](#ref8)[[9]](#ref9)</sup> The tolerable upper intake level (UL) for folic acid from supplements and fortified foods is 1,000 μg/day, set primarily to avoid masking vitamin B12 deficiency.<sup>[[8]](#ref8)[[7]](#ref7)</sup>

Since 1998, the US has mandated folic-acid fortification of enriched cereal grain products, which has substantially reduced the prevalence of both folate deficiency and neural tube defects.<sup>[[8]](#ref8)</sup> NHANES 2007–2018 data show that median natural-food folate intake (222 μg DFE/day) remains below the EAR of 320 μg DFE/day; only approximately 2% of adults exceed the UL.<sup>[[9]](#ref9)</sup>

## Assessment of Folate Status

- **Serum folate.** The usual initial test when deficiency is suspected; interpret using the local assay and recent intake. A concentration below about 3 ng/mL supports deficiency, but decision limits vary by method and clinical context.<sup>[[5]](#ref5)[[10]](#ref10)</sup>
- **RBC folate.** Reflects longer-term exposure but has analytical limitations and generally adds little to serum testing in routine clinical deficiency assessment. It is not a universally preferred test or an exact analogue of HbA1c.<sup>[[10]](#ref10)</sup>
- **Homocysteine and MMA.** Homocysteine may rise in either folate or B12 deficiency. MMA helps assess B12 deficiency because isolated folate deficiency does not usually raise it; renal impairment and coexisting deficiencies complicate interpretation.<sup>[[11]](#ref11)[[12]](#ref12)</sup>

## Causes of Deficiency

| Category | Examples |
|---|---|
| **Dietary insufficiency** | Low intake of vegetables / legumes, alcohol use disorder, poverty, restrictive diets |
| **Malabsorption** | Celiac disease, IBD, tropical sprue, short bowel syndrome |
| **Increased demand** | Pregnancy, lactation, hemolytic anemias, exfoliative dermatitis, rapid growth (adolescence) |
| **Medications** | Methotrexate, phenytoin, carbamazepine, valproate, sulfasalazine, trimethoprim, oral contraceptives, alcohol |
| **Genetic** | Rare disorders of folate transport/metabolism; common MTHFR variants can modestly influence folate indices but are not, by themselves, a diagnosis of deficiency |

Body stores of folate are limited (5–20 mg), and deficiency can develop within 2–4 months of inadequate intake, much faster than B12 deficiency (3–5 years).<sup>[[10]](#ref10)</sup>

## Clinical Manifestations

**Hematologic.** Megaloblastic anemia identical to that of B12 deficiency: macrocytosis (MCV often &gt; 110 fL), oval macrocytes, hypersegmented neutrophils, pancytopenia in severe cases.<sup>[[11]](#ref11)[[12]](#ref12)</sup> Unlike B12 deficiency, folate deficiency does not cause subacute combined degeneration, though some evidence suggests folate deficiency may contribute to peripheral neuropathy and neuropsychiatric symptoms in certain patients.<sup>[[11]](#ref11)[[13]](#ref13)</sup>

**Pregnancy complications.** Folate deficiency can cause megaloblastic anemia in pregnancy.<sup>[[14]](#ref14)</sup> Beyond anemia, inadequate folate is strongly linked to neural tube defects (NTDs), and may contribute to preeclampsia and other adverse outcomes through hyperhomocysteinemia.<sup>[[15]](#ref15)[[16]](#ref16)</sup>

---

## Neural Tube Defect Prevention

The USPSTF 2023 A recommendation is a daily supplement containing 400–800 μg folic acid for people planning to or who could become pregnant, starting at least 1 month before conception and continuing through the first 2–3 months of pregnancy. Its standard-dose recommendation does not cover the highest-risk groups.<sup>[[17]](#ref17)[[18]](#ref18)</sup>

- A Cochrane review of five trials found fewer NTDs with periconceptional folate (RR 0.31, 95% CI 0.17–0.58; 6,708 births with outcome information). It did not establish prevention of all other birth defects.<sup>[[15]](#ref15)</sup>
- After a previous NTD-affected pregnancy, CDC recommends 4 mg/day beginning 1 month before conception and through the first 3 months. Arrange a preconception plan for personal or partner NTD history, malabsorption or other substantial risk; dosing should reflect that specific context.<sup>[[19]](#ref19)[[45]](#ref45)</sup>
- For people with epilepsy using an antiseizure medication, AAN/AES/SMFM 2024 recommends at least 0.4 mg/day before and during pregnancy. The optimal higher dose is uncertain; this does not support automatically prescribing 4 mg to every antiseizure-medication user. Folate does not neutralize valproate's teratogenic risk.<sup>[[46]](#ref46)</sup>

Neural-tube closure usually occurs 26–28 days after fertilization, approximately 6 weeks by obstetric gestational dating, often before pregnancy is recognized. Continue routine prenatal nutrition after the NTD-prevention window.<sup>[[18]](#ref18)</sup>

## MTHFR C677T Polymorphism

Common MTHFR C677T variants can reduce enzyme activity and affect circulating folate and homocysteine. The size of the effect depends on nutritional status and measurement method.<sup>[[20]](#ref20)[[21]](#ref21)</sup> A meta-analysis using microbiologic assays found serum or plasma and RBC folate approximately 13% and 16% lower, respectively, in TT versus CC participants.<sup>[[22]](#ref22)</sup>

Routine MTHFR testing is not recommended for thrombophilia, recurrent pregnancy loss or related adverse pregnancy outcomes. A common variant does not require avoiding folic acid or substituting methylfolate; CDC confirms that 400 μg/day folic acid raises folate levels regardless of common MTHFR genotype. Rare severe metabolic disorders require specialist evaluation.<sup>[[23]](#ref23)[[47]](#ref47)</sup>

## Folate and Cardiovascular Disease

Folic acid lowers homocysteine by approximately 25%.<sup>[[24]](#ref24)</sup> Despite strong observational associations between elevated homocysteine and CVD, large RCTs have been largely negative for major cardiovascular events:

- Trials of homocysteine lowering have not consistently reduced major cardiovascular outcomes.<sup>[[25]](#ref25)</sup>
- **Meta-analysis.** A meta-analysis of 30 RCTs (82,334 participants) reported a 10% reduction in stroke (RR 0.90, 95% CI 0.84–0.96) and a 4% reduction in overall CVD (RR 0.96, 0.92–0.99), with greater benefit in lower-baseline-folate populations.<sup>[[26]](#ref26)</sup>

For patients with ischemic stroke or TIA and hyperhomocysteinemia, AHA/ASA 2021 classifies folate, B6 and B12 supplementation as no benefit for preventing subsequent stroke. Treat a documented nutritional deficiency on its own merits; a pooled primary/secondary-prevention signal is not a reason to prescribe vitamins routinely after stroke.<sup>[[27]](#ref27)</sup>

---

## Folate and Cancer

The folate-cancer relationship — particularly colorectal cancer (CRC) — is complex and follows a dual-modulator hypothesis: adequate folate protects normal mucosa from carcinogenesis, but excess folate may promote growth of pre-existing neoplastic foci.<sup>[[28]](#ref28)[[29]](#ref29)</sup>

- **Observational.** Higher total folate intake associates with a 12–16% reduction in CRC risk (RR 0.84, 95% CI 0.80–0.90), with stronger protection among alcohol consumers.<sup>[[28]](#ref28)[[30]](#ref30)[[31]](#ref31)</sup>
- **Supplementation trials.** Folic acid has not reduced adenoma recurrence in RCTs and may increase risk of advanced adenomas and serrated polyps.<sup>[[29]](#ref29)</sup>
- **B-PROOF follow-up.** Among 2,524 older trial participants, allocation to folic acid 400 μg plus B12 500 μg/day was associated with overall cancer HR 1.25 (95% CI 1.00–1.53) and CRC HR 1.77 (1.08–2.90). This secondary follow-up of a fracture trial cannot isolate an effect of folic acid alone or be directly extrapolated to routine pregnancy supplementation.<sup>[[32]](#ref32)</sup>

The AGA Clinical Practice Update advises against folic acid supplementation specifically for colorectal-neoplasia prevention; its advice does not oppose indicated deficiency treatment or NTD prevention.<sup>[[29]](#ref29)</sup>

## Drug Interactions

Several drug-folate interactions warrant clinical attention:<sup>[[33]](#ref33)[[34]](#ref34)[[13]](#ref13)[[35]](#ref35)</sup>

- **Methotrexate.** Supplement folic or folinic acid during low-dose treatment for non-neoplastic disease according to the prescribing team's regimen. Dose and timing vary; omitting folate on methotrexate day is not a universal label requirement. Cancer regimens, including leucovorin rescue after high-dose methotrexate, require protocol-specific timing. Do not add or withhold supplements independently during cancer treatment.<sup>[[36]](#ref36)</sup>

- **Anticonvulsants.** Phenytoin, carbamazepine, phenobarbital, primidone increase hepatic folate metabolism and impair intestinal absorption, lowering serum folate. Conversely, folic acid supplementation can antagonize the anticonvulsant action of phenytoin, potentially requiring dose adjustment.<sup>[[33]](#ref33)[[34]](#ref34)[[35]](#ref35)</sup>
- **Valproate.** Associated with both folate deficiency and teratogenicity (NTD risk); mechanism may involve disruption of the methionine cycle.<sup>[[37]](#ref37)</sup>
- **Alcohol.** Alcohol inhibits intestinal absorption, hepatic uptake and metabolic utilization of folate and is an important risk factor for deficiency.<sup>[[33]](#ref33)</sup>

---

## Masking of Vitamin B12 Deficiency — A Critical Safety Concern

**Folic acid alone can correct the anemia of B12 deficiency while neurologic injury continues.** Product labeling includes this warning even at doses above 0.1 mg/day; it is a warning against treating B12-deficient anemia with folate alone, not a reason to withhold recommended pregnancy supplementation.<sup>[[34]](#ref34)[[35]](#ref35)[[18]](#ref18)</sup>

The often-quoted NHANES odds ratios (4.9 for anemia and 5.0 for cognitive impairment) compared low-B12/high-folate participants with a normal-B12/normal-folate reference group, not with other B12-deficient participants. They describe cross-sectional prevalence associations, not anemia severity or proof that folic acid caused harm.<sup>[[40]](#ref40)</sup> Proposed depletion of active B12 by excess folic acid remains a hypothesis supported mainly by observational, uncontrolled or experimental evidence.<sup>[[38]](#ref38)[[39]](#ref39)[[41]](#ref41)[[42]](#ref42)[[43]](#ref43)</sup>

Obtain B12 testing before treating unexplained megaloblastic anemia when feasible. If B12 deficiency is suspected, especially with neurologic findings, start B12 promptly and give folate alongside it when needed, rather than delaying treatment while waiting for results.<sup>[[11]](#ref11)[[35]](#ref35)</sup>

## Treatment of Folate Deficiency

- A common US regimen is oral folic acid 1 mg/day; adjust duration to hematologic recovery, restoration of intake and the underlying cause. Resistant cases need reassessment for ongoing malabsorption or another diagnosis rather than indefinite dose escalation.<sup>[[35]](#ref35)</sup>
- After correction, select ongoing supplementation according to continuing risk and pregnancy and bariatric guidance. The adult dietary requirement is not interchangeable with a therapeutic dose.<sup>[[8]](#ref8)[[35]](#ref35)[[44]](#ref44)</sup>
- **Treat coexisting or suspected B12 deficiency concurrently**. Folate monotherapy is inadequate for B12-related neurologic disease.<sup>[[11]](#ref11)[[35]](#ref35)</sup>
- For post-bariatric deficiency, ASMBS recommends 1 mg/day until normalization, then the preventive regimen; routine doses above 1 mg/day are discouraged because of masking risk unless a separate specialist indication applies.<sup>[[48]](#ref48)</sup>

## Reconstructive Relevance

| Scenario | Practical action |
|---|---|
| **Pregnancy potential during reconstructive care** | Ask about reproductive plans, review folic-acid intake and coordinate pregnancy timing with the procedure. Use the standard or high-risk regimen above; an operation does not create a separate folate-dose requirement.<sup>[[18]](#ref18)[[45]](#ref45)</sup> |
| **Adult congenital/spina bifida care** | Personal/partner NTD history warrants preconception counseling. Review antiseizure and potentially teratogenic medicines with their prescribers; folate is not a substitute for medication planning.<sup>[[16]](#ref16)[[19]](#ref19)[[46]](#ref46)</sup> |
| **Methotrexate, sulfasalazine, trimethoprim or anticonvulsants** | Check the indication, nutrition, blood counts and interacting medicines. Follow the relevant drug-monitoring and supplementation plan; do not assume every user is deficient or needs an additional preoperative folate test. Methotrexate is not a routine IC/BPS therapy.<sup>[[33]](#ref33)[[34]](#ref34)[[36]](#ref36)[[37]](#ref37)</sup> |
| **Alcohol-related malnutrition or bariatric surgery** | Assess the broader nutritional picture and adherence to follow-up; replace likely or established deficits, including B12/thiamine when indicated. Continue scheduled bariatric folate surveillance.<sup>[[13]](#ref13)[[44]](#ref44)[[48]](#ref48)</sup> |
| **Ileal diversion or augmentation with macrocytosis** | Evaluate **B12** and other causes before relying on folate. If B12-related neurologic disease is suspected, initiate B12 while completing the work-up.<sup>[[11]](#ref11)</sup> |

See [Vitamin B12](/docs/evaluation/laboratory-studies/nutritional-assessment/vitamin-b12).
