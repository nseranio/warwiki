# Imaging Modalities in Reconstructive Urology

Imaging is central to the evaluation of urethral stricture, pelvic organ prolapse, ureteropelvic junction obstruction, vesicovaginal fistula, pelvic fracture urethral injury, and upper tract pathology. Selecting the correct study — and interpreting it with precision — directly determines surgical planning. This page covers the indications, techniques, and interpretive frameworks for the modalities used most frequently in functional reconstructive urology practice.

---

## Retrograde Urethrogram (RUG)

### Overview

The retrograde urethrogram remains the primary imaging study for male urethral stricture disease. It delineates stricture location, length, and multiplicity and provides an anatomical roadmap for surgical planning. Despite advances in MRI and ultrasound, RUG remains irreplaceable because it provides luminal detail under controlled filling pressure.

:::tip Key Principle
RUG shows the **lumen** — not the spongiosum. A stricture on RUG may underestimate true length because dense periurethral fibrosis (spongiofibrosis) is not visualized. Correlate with sonourethrogram (SUG) or MRI when depth of fibrosis matters.
:::

### Equipment

| Item | Specification |
|------|--------------|
| Foley catheter | 16–18 Fr Foley or dedicated urethral clamp (Brodney clamp) |
| Contrast agent | Omnipaque 240 (iohexol 240 mg I/mL), diluted 50:50 with normal saline |
| Volume | 20–60 mL, injected slowly under fluoroscopic guidance |
| Fluoroscopy | C-arm preferred; static X-ray acceptable for limited resources |
| Patient position | Right posterior oblique (RPO) 45° — penis stretched laterally |
| Radiation dose | 0.1–2 mSv per study |

### Step-by-Step Technique

1. **Position**: Patient supine, rotated 45° right posterior oblique. Right leg straight, left knee flexed. Penis stretched over the right thigh at 45° to the body axis — this opens the bulbar curve and avoids overlap of penile and bulbar urethra.
2. **Catheter placement**: Insert a 16–18 Fr Foley 2–3 cm into the meatus. Inflate balloon to 1–2 mL within the fossa navicularis to create a seal without traumatizing the meatus. Do not advance into the bladder.
3. **Contrast preparation**: Dilute Omnipaque 240 1:1 with saline. Full-strength contrast obscures fine mucosal detail and makes length estimation difficult. Fill a 60 mL piston syringe.
4. **Slow injection**: Inject under continuous fluoroscopy at a steady, slow rate. The urethra should fill retrogradely from meatus to bladder neck. Forceful injection causes spasm and artifactual narrowing.
5. **Fluoroscopic capture**: Obtain images during injection — not before or after. Record early filling (to see meatus/fossa), mid-filling (penile urethra), and full filling (bulbar, membranous, bladder neck).
6. **Voiding phase (VCUG)**: When clinically indicated (posterior urethra, bladder neck pathology, vesicoureteral reflux), follow RUG with VCUG: fill bladder to capacity via catheter or suprapubic tube, then image during voiding.
7. **Documentation**: Record stricture location (meatus, fossa navicularis, penile, bulbomembranous junction, membranous, posterior), estimated length, caliber at narrowest point, number of strictures, and appearance (smooth vs. irregular).

### Combined RUG + VCUG

When posterior urethral pathology is suspected (post-PFUI, bladder neck contracture, post-prostatectomy stenosis), simultaneous imaging from both ends is required:

- Insert suprapubic tube or catheter above the injury
- Fill bladder retrogradely via the suprapubic route while injecting contrast retrogradely via penile catheter
- Two columns of contrast meet at the defect — the gap length is the **estimated stricture/obliteration length**
- Simultaneous imaging is the gold standard for posterior urethral distraction defects

:::warning Pitfall: Gap Length Overestimation
The apparent gap on RUG+VCUG represents luminal discontinuity — but the true surgical gap is shorter because the proximal urethra may not fill completely with contrast under low pressure. MRI provides complementary data on fibrotic tissue between the contrast columns.
:::

### Normal Anatomy by Segment

| Segment | Normal Caliber | Key Landmark |
|---------|---------------|--------------|
| Meatus | ≥18 Fr | Smallest normal point |
| Fossa navicularis | 22–28 Fr | Within glans |
| Penile urethra | 22–28 Fr | Uniform, parallel walls |
| Bulbar urethra | 24–30 Fr | Widest segment; slight dilation normal |
| Membranous urethra | 18–22 Fr | Apparent narrowing normal at external sphincter |
| Verumontanum | Filling defect | Normal; visible on VCUG |
| Bladder neck | Opens smoothly on voiding | Closed at rest |

### Interpretation Checklist

When reviewing a RUG, systematically assess:

- [ ] **Location**: Which anatomical segment(s) are affected?
- [ ] **Length**: Estimate in centimeters; mark landmarks
- [ ] **Severity**: Complete obliteration vs. partial narrowing vs. hairline lumen
- [ ] **Number**: Single vs. panurethral vs. multiple discrete strictures
- [ ] **Appearance**: Smooth/web-like (low spongiofibrosis) vs. irregular/moth-eaten (dense fibrosis, LS)
- [ ] **Proximal anatomy**: Is the posterior urethra and bladder neck normal?
- [ ] **Fistula or false passage**: Any extravasation or branching tracts?
- [ ] **Dilated proximal urethra**: Suggests chronic high-pressure obstruction

### Common Pitfalls

| Pitfall | Mechanism | Solution |
|---------|-----------|----------|
| Apparent narrowing at membranous urethra | External sphincter spasm from rapid injection | Slow injection; correlate with VCUG during voiding |
| Short-appearing stricture | Incomplete urethral filling distal to dense obstruction | Combine with antegrade filling (VCUG/SUG) |
| Extravasation artifact | Foley balloon not seated, torn mucosa from catheter | Re-seat catheter, reduce injection pressure |
| Poor oblique positioning | Penile/bulbar overlap obscures bulbar stricture | Ensure 45° oblique and penile stretch |
| Dilution artifact | Too dilute contrast → poor mucosal detail | Use 50:50 dilution; avoid &gt;70% saline |

## Voiding Cystourethrogram (VCUG)

### Overview

VCUG evaluates the urethra during voiding (antegrade) and provides information complementary to RUG. It is essential for posterior urethra assessment, bladder neck competence, vesicoureteral reflux, and female urethral pathology.

### Indications

- Posterior urethral distraction defect (combined with RUG)
- Bladder neck contracture/stenosis post-prostatectomy or radiation
- Vesicoureteral reflux evaluation
- Female urethral stricture — **wine-glass sign**
- Urethral diverticulum (may show filling of diverticulum neck)
- Post-augmentation cystoplasty surveillance

### VUR Grading (International Reflux Study Committee)

| Grade | Description |
|-------|-------------|
| I | Reflux into ureter only; no dilatation |
| II | Reflux to renal pelvis; no dilatation |
| III | Mild dilatation of ureter/pelvis; mild or absent calyceal blunting |
| IV | Moderate ureteral and pelvicalyceal dilatation; obliteration of sharp calyceal angles |
| V | Gross dilatation; tortuous ureter; intrarenal reflux |

Grades IV–V VUR in a patient undergoing urethral reconstruction warrant preoperative nephrology co-management and surveillance imaging post-repair.

### Female VCUG — The Wine-Glass Sign

In female urethral stricture, VCUG during voiding demonstrates the pathognomonic **wine-glass sign**: the proximal urethra dilates proximally to the obstruction, creating a "wine-glass" or "champagne-flute" silhouette. The dilated proximal segment narrows abruptly at the stricture, with a normal or narrow distal segment below. This pattern distinguishes intrinsic stricture from bladder neck dyssynergia.

:::info Diagnostic Thresholds — Female Urethra
A urethral caliber of **&lt;14 Fr** on calibration or VCUG is considered the diagnostic threshold for female urethral stricture. Normal female urethral caliber is 18–24 Fr. Symptoms alone (dysuria, weak stream, incomplete emptying) are insufficient for diagnosis.
:::

## Sonourethrogram (SUG)

### Overview

Sonourethrogram (ultrasonographic urethrogram) uses real-time ultrasound during urethral filling with saline or gel to image the spongiosum directly. Unlike RUG, which shows only the lumen, SUG visualizes periurethral spongiofibrosis — the fibrotic process within the corpus spongiosum that determines surgical complexity and technique selection.

### Spongiofibrosis Grading (McAninch / Morey)

| Grade | SUG Appearance | Clinical Implication |
|-------|---------------|---------------------|
| 1 | Focal hyperechogenicity, &lt;1 cm | Thin fibrosis; amenable to dilation or DVIU |
| 2 | Segmental hyperechogenicity, 1–3 cm | Moderate fibrosis; EPA or onlay urethroplasty |
| 3 | Diffuse hyperechogenicity, &gt;3 cm | Extensive fibrosis; substitution urethroplasty |
| 4 | Full-thickness echogenic plaque ± calcification | Obliterative; BMG substitution required |

### Evidence: SUG vs. RUG for Stricture Length

Three landmark studies established SUG superiority for stricture length measurement:

- **McAninch et al. (1988)** — the seminal SUG paper: in 45 patients, SUG-measured length matched intraoperative measurement more closely than RUG, which underestimated length in 78% of cases. RUG systematically underestimates stricture length by ~1 cm at the "shoulder" margins where mucosa transitions into normal epithelium.
- **Pavlica et al. (2003)** — mean SUG-to-intraoperative difference 0.3 cm vs. 1.1 cm for RUG; SUG also graded spongiofibrosis unavailable on RUG.
- **Trojan et al. (2001)** — sensitivity 100%, specificity 96% for stricture detection by SUG vs. 84%/88% for RUG.

:::tip For the Reconstructive Urologist
RUG systematically underestimates stricture length. Add ~1 cm buffer when planning graft length based on RUG alone. Order SUG for complex anterior strictures (lichen sclerosus, post-hypospadias, recurrent) when spongiofibrosis depth changes the operative approach.
:::

### SUG vs. RUG

| Feature | RUG | SUG |
|---------|-----|-----|
| Lumen visualization | Excellent | Good |
| Spongiofibrosis depth | Not visible | Direct visualization |
| Stricture length accuracy | May underestimate by ~1 cm | More accurate (within 0.3 cm) |
| Equipment availability | Universal | Requires urethral probe expertise |
| Radiation | Yes (low) | None |
| Best use | Initial evaluation, surgical planning | Preoperative fibrosis mapping, complex/recurrent strictures |

## Magnetic Resonance Imaging (MRI)

MRI is indispensable in reconstructive urology for pelvic floor assessment, posterior urethral injury, urethral diverticulum, Peyronie's disease plaque mapping, and upper tract evaluation. Each clinical scenario uses specific sequences and anatomical planes.

### MRI by Clinical Indication

| Indication | Key Sequences | Primary Findings | Surgical Relevance |
|-----------|--------------|-----------------|-------------------|
| Pelvic fracture urethral injury (PFUI) | T2 axial/sagittal, T1 post-contrast | Distraction gap length, urethral stump position, fibrotic bridge, pubic symphysis status | Determines perineal vs. abdominoperineal approach; gap &gt;3 cm suggests complex repair |
| Pelvic organ prolapse (dynamic MRI) | T2 sagittal at rest/strain, fast imaging | PCL line, bladder descent, rectal descent, levator defects | Identifies multicompartment prolapse; guides concomitant repair vs. staged approach |
| Urethral diverticulum | T2 axial/coronal, STIR | Horseshoe configuration, neck location, extent | Defines complete excision vs. marsupialization approach |
| Vesicovaginal fistula | T2 multiplanar | Fistula tract, bladder wall integrity, proximity to ureters | Transvaginal vs. transabdominal approach; ureteroneocystostomy need |
| Peyronie's disease plaque | T1 + T2 axial with fat saturation ± dynamic | Plaque location, calcification, curvature | Plication site selection, graft sizing |
| Urethral stricture (complex/recurrent) | T2 multiplanar | Fibrosis extent, bladder capacity, periurethral anatomy | Graft vs. flap selection; fistula evaluation |
| Upper tract obstruction | T2 + MRU with furosemide | Hydronephrosis grade, ureteral caliber, transition point | Preoperative planning for pyeloplasty or ureteral reimplantation |

### PFUI: MRI Protocol and Interpretation

Pelvic fracture urethral injury (PFUI) is the most complex posterior urethral pathology. MRI provides information that neither RUG nor cystoscopy can supply:

**Key MRI Findings in PFUI:**
- **Gap length**: Distance between the distal bulbar urethral stump and the proximal posterior urethral stump
- **Fibrotic bridge**: Dense T2-hypointense tissue between the stumps; does not represent functional urethra
- **Urethral stump angulation**: Superior displacement of proximal stump by fibrosis suggests need for inferior pubectomy
- **Pubic symphysis**: Diastasis or posterior plate disruption may require bony resection
- **Bladder neck integrity**: Intact vs. injured bladder neck determines continence prognosis

**MRI Sequences for PFUI:**

| Sequence | Plane | Purpose |
|----------|-------|---------|
| T2 FSE | Sagittal | Gap length, stump angles, bladder neck |
| T2 FSE | Axial | Lateral fibrosis extent, levator anatomy |
| T1 post-gadolinium | Sagittal/axial | Vascular supply, active inflammation |
| DWI (optional) | Axial | Distinguishes viable from fibrotic tissue |

:::info MRI vs. RUG in PFUI
MRI and RUG provide complementary information. RUG shows luminal contrast gap; MRI shows soft tissue anatomy, gap length in three planes, and surgical obstacles (pubic plate, misaligned stumps). Both should be obtained preoperatively for complex posterior urethral repairs.
:::

### Dynamic Pelvic MRI for Prolapse

Dynamic (defecation) MRI is the gold standard for multicompartment pelvic organ prolapse evaluation when clinical exam is discordant with symptoms or when surgical planning requires compartment mapping.

**Protocol:**
- 1.5T or 3T magnet; open MRI systems have lower resolution but allow physiologic defecation
- Patient position: supine (preferred for image quality) or seated
- Bladder: comfortably full; rectum opacified with 180–200 mL ultrasound gel
- Sequences: T2 sagittal at rest → Valsalva → during defecation → after defecation
- Image every 1–2 seconds during strain using fast single-shot T2

**Pubo-Coccygeal Line (PCL):** The reference line drawn from inferior pubic symphysis to last coccygeal joint. Organ descent is measured perpendicular to this line:

| Compartment | Normal | Mild | Moderate | Severe |
|-------------|--------|------|----------|--------|
| Bladder (cystocele) | Above PCL | &lt;3 cm below | 3–6 cm below | &gt;6 cm below |
| Uterus/vault | Above PCL | &lt;3 cm below | 3–6 cm below | &gt;6 cm below |
| Rectum (rectocele) | Above PCL | &lt;3 cm below | 3–6 cm below | &gt;6 cm below |

**Levator Ani Assessment (Dietz grading on MRI):**
- Grade 0: Intact muscle
- Grade 1: &lt;50% thickness loss
- Grade 2: ≥50% thickness loss
- Grade 3: Complete avulsion from pubic symphysis

Bilateral levator avulsion on MRI predicts higher prolapse recurrence after native tissue repair — may favor mesh augmentation or pessary management.

### Urethral Diverticulum: The Horseshoe Sign

Urethral diverticulum (UD) is a focal outpouching of the urethral wall, most common in women at the mid-urethra. MRI is the gold standard for UD evaluation.

**MRI Characteristics:**
- T2-bright cystic structure surrounding the urethra
- **Horseshoe configuration**: extends circumferentially around the urethra — seen on axial T2 (80% of cases)
- Neck location: anterior, posterior, or lateral — critical for surgical approach
- Internal complexity: simple fluid vs. debris vs. calculus vs. solid nodule (carcinoma in UD)
- Relationship to sphincter mechanism

**Surgical Planning Checklist from MRI:**
- [ ] Location relative to external sphincter — proximal extension may require sphincter-sparing dissection
- [ ] Circumferential vs. unilateral — affects completeness of excision
- [ ] Neck location — determines incision placement
- [ ] Internal contents — calculus or solid component changes management
- [ ] Fistula or communication with vaginal wall

### VI-RADS (Vesical Imaging-Reporting and Data System)

VI-RADS is the standardized MRI scoring system for bladder cancer, relevant for reconstructive urologists managing urothelial malignancy, radiation-damaged bladders, and augmentation cystoplasty candidates.

| VI-RADS Score | Meaning | Muscle Invasion Probability |
|---------------|---------|---------------------------|
| 1 | Intact inner muscle layer; no invasion | &lt;10% |
| 2 | Disrupted inner layer; outer layer intact | ~20% |
| 3 | Equivocal — uncertain muscle involvement | ~50% |
| 4 | Disrupted outer muscle layer | ~75% |
| 5 | Extravesical extension or invasion | &gt;90% |

Scores ≥3 prompt repeat biopsy or early radical cystectomy planning. In augmentation candidates, VI-RADS helps confirm benign urothelial pathology before cystoplasty.

### MRI Sequence Quick Reference

| Sequence | Abbreviation | Best For |
|----------|-------------|----------|
| T2-weighted fast spin echo | T2 FSE | Anatomy, fluid, fibrosis |
| Short tau inversion recovery | STIR | Edema, inflammation, fluid-filled structures |
| T1-weighted | T1 | Fat, hemorrhage, post-contrast enhancement |
| Diffusion-weighted imaging | DWI | Tissue restriction (cancer, abscess) |
| Dynamic contrast enhancement | DCE | Vascularity, active inflammation |
| MR urography | MRU | Ureteral anatomy, obstruction level |

## MAG3 Renogram (Mercaptoacetyltriglycine Scintigraphy)

### Overview

Technetium-99m MAG3 renal scintigraphy is the nuclear medicine study of choice for upper tract evaluation in reconstructive urology. It provides **split renal function** and **drainage kinetics** in a single study, using tubular secretion (rather than glomerular filtration) for superior image quality — especially in patients with impaired renal function where DTPA fails.

### Physiology

- MAG3 is secreted by proximal tubular cells (80% first-pass extraction)
- Provides higher count density than DTPA at equal dose
- Preferred in: impaired GFR, infants, horseshoe kidney, transplant kidney
- DTPA (glomerular filtration) is preferred only when absolute GFR measurement is needed

### Three-Phase Renogram Curve

| Phase | Time | What It Reflects |
|-------|------|-----------------|
| Vascular/perfusion | 0–2 min | Renal blood flow; rapid rise in counts |
| Parenchymal/uptake | 2–5 min | Cortical extraction; peak at ~3 min |
| Drainage | 5–30 min | Collecting system and ureteral transit |

A normal renogram curve shows rapid rise, symmetric peak at 2–3 minutes, and smooth exponential decay to baseline by 20–30 minutes.

### F+20 Diuretic Protocol

The standard diuresis renogram uses furosemide injection to differentiate **true obstruction** (drainage failure despite maximal diuresis) from **functional dilation** (dilated system that drains when challenged with increased urine flow).

**Protocol:**
1. Patient hydrated with 500–1000 mL oral water 30–60 minutes before scan
2. IV access; patient positioned supine under gamma camera
3. Inject Tc-99m MAG3 IV; begin imaging immediately
4. At **20 minutes post-tracer injection** (F+20): inject furosemide 0.5 mg/kg IV (max 40 mg)
5. Continue imaging to 40–60 minutes total
6. Calculate T½ from point of furosemide injection

:::info Why F+20?
F+20 protocol injects furosemide at 20 minutes (after tracer has reached collecting system) rather than earlier (F0 or F−15). F+20 avoids false obstruction from a poorly filled pelvis at early time points and is the most widely validated protocol for UPJ obstruction.
:::

### T½ Interpretation

Half-time (T½) is the time from furosemide injection for counts to fall by 50%:

| T½ | Interpretation | Clinical Action |
|----|---------------|-----------------|
| &lt;10 minutes | Unobstructed drainage | No intervention needed |
| 10–20 minutes | Equivocal | Correlate with symptoms, anatomy, split function |
| &gt;20 minutes | Obstructed | Surgical correction indicated (pyeloplasty, endopyelotomy) |

:::warning Confounders of T½
- **Dehydrated patient** — reduced urine flow → falsely prolonged T½
- **Massively dilated pelvis** — large reservoir dilutes diuretic effect; T½ unreliable
- **Impaired renal function** — poor MAG3 uptake → flat curve; split function unreliable
- **Diuretic resistance** — furosemide failure in neonates or chronic renal insufficiency

In these cases, correlate with split function differential, symptoms, and anatomic imaging.
:::

### Split Renal Function

Split function measures each kidney's contribution to total renal function, expressed as a percentage:

| Split Function | Interpretation |
|---------------|---------------|
| 45–55% each | Normal bilateral function |
| 40–45% | Mild differential; monitor |
| 35–40% | Moderate reduction; consider repair |
| &lt;35% | Significant impairment; repair to preserve function |
| &lt;10–15% | Minimal function; nephrectomy may be preferable to reconstruction |

**The McGuire Threshold**: A poorly functioning kidney (&lt;35% split function) in the setting of obstruction warrants surgical intervention — further functional loss is likely with ongoing obstruction even if asymptomatic.

### MAG3 vs. DTPA

| Feature | MAG3 | DTPA |
|---------|------|------|
| Mechanism | Tubular secretion | Glomerular filtration |
| First-pass extraction | ~80% | ~20% |
| Image quality | Superior | Adequate |
| GFR impairment | Reliable | Unreliable |
| Absolute GFR | Not standard | Validated |
| Pediatric use | Preferred | Acceptable |
| Cost | Higher | Lower |
| Best indication | Obstruction evaluation, transplant | Absolute GFR measurement |

### Upper Tract Imaging Hierarchy

| Clinical Question | First-Line Study | Supplementary |
|-------------------|-----------------|---------------|
| Obstruction assessment (UPJ, UVJ) | MAG3 renogram | CT urogram for anatomy |
| Split renal function | MAG3 | — |
| Absolute GFR | DTPA or serum creatinine/cystatin C | — |
| Urothelial carcinoma | CT urogram | Ureteroscopy |
| Anatomic detail (crossing vessel, stone) | CT urogram | MRI if no radiation |
| Post-pyeloplasty follow-up | MAG3 at 3–6 months | Ultrasound for hydronephrosis grade |
| Transplant kidney obstruction | MAG3 | Ultrasound with Doppler |
| Pediatric obstruction | MAG3 (F+20) | Ultrasound |

## CT Urogram (CTU)

### Overview

CT urogram is the primary anatomic imaging modality for upper tract urothelial carcinoma, complex stone disease, and delineation of urinary tract anatomy in patients with congenital anomalies or prior surgery. In reconstructive urology, it is most commonly used for:

- **Pelvic fracture classification**: Tile/Young-Burgess classification of pelvic ring injuries predicts urethral distraction injury pattern and guides surgical approach
- **Complex fistula mapping**: VVF with ureteral involvement; rectourethral fistula with bowel involvement
- **Ureteral stricture**: Transition point, crossing vessels, periureteral fibrosis
- **Post-radiation anatomy**: Bladder capacity, ureteral dilation, fistula tract

### PFUI — Pelvic Fracture Classification

The Tile classification of pelvic ring injuries correlates with urethral injury pattern:

| Tile Type | Pelvic Ring Stability | Urethral Injury Risk |
|-----------|----------------------|---------------------|
| A | Stable | Low |
| B | Rotationally unstable, vertically stable | Moderate — anterior urethra |
| C | Rotationally and vertically unstable | High — posterior urethra, bladder neck |

Type C injuries with significant displacement have the highest rates of posterior urethral distraction defect and the most complex anatomy requiring MRI correlation for surgical planning.

### CT Retrograde Urethrogram (CT-RUG)

An emerging hybrid technique: retrograde urethral contrast injection followed immediately by helical CT acquisition. CT-RUG provides cross-sectional and 3D reconstructions of the urethra with periurethral tissue detail approaching SUG — particularly useful in:

- Complex strictures with prior surgery or suspected false passages
- Complex fistulae (urethrocutaneous, urethrorectal) requiring precise tract mapping
- Patients where SUG expertise is unavailable but spongiofibrosis data is needed
- Simultaneous upper and lower tract evaluation in a single session

**Limitation**: Higher radiation dose than fluoroscopic RUG. Not yet standardized; requires coordination between urology and radiology. Currently reserved for complex cases where standard RUG and SUG are inconclusive.

## Clinical Pearls for the Reconstructive Urologist

1. **Never rely on a single RUG image.** Always obtain multiple fluoroscopic spot images in both oblique projections. A single imperfect image can miss a proximal stricture or misrepresent length.

2. **RUG systematically underestimates stricture length.** Intraoperative measurement is longer than RUG length in &gt;70% of cases. Add ~1 cm buffer when planning graft length.

3. **Order SUG for complex anterior strictures.** Lichen sclerosus, post-hypospadias, recurrent strictures, and any case where spongiofibrosis depth will change technique selection require SUG.

4. **Always perform combined RUG/VCUG in PFUI.** Gap length — the single most important parameter for operative approach — requires both contrast columns.
   - Gap &lt;1 cm: perineal anastomotic urethroplasty usually feasible
   - Gap 1–3 cm: anastomotic with adjunct maneuvers (inferior pubectomy, corporal splitting)
   - Gap &gt;3 cm: complex reconstruction; may require transpubic or combined approach

5. **Assess the bladder neck on every PFUI VCUG.** Bladder neck injury (present in ~10–15% of PFUI) significantly complicates reconstruction and affects continence outcome.

6. **Use pulsed fluoroscopy.** Pulsed mode (7.5 fps) vs. continuous fluoroscopy reduces patient radiation dose by 50–70% without diagnostic compromise.

7. **Post-urethroplasty surveillance RUG at 3 months.** This is the standard interval; combine with uroflowmetry and PVR. Normal RUG with good uroflow at 3 months is highly predictive of durable success.

8. **Female urethral assessment: MRI over double-balloon urethrogram.** For urethral diverticulum, pelvic MRI with T2 sagittal and axial sequences (surface coil) has replaced the double-balloon urethrogram at high-volume centers.

9. **Diluted contrast gives better stricture delineation.** Full-strength (300 mg I/mL) contrast can over-opacify and obscure subtle mucosal irregularities or sinus tracts. Diluted 1:1 with saline (150 mg I/mL) is preferred for stricture evaluation.

10. **SUG for the anterior urethra; MRI for the posterior urethra.** These modalities are complementary: SUG provides spongiofibrosis data unavailable on any other study; MRI provides soft-tissue anatomy of the posterior urethra and pelvis that SUG cannot reach.

## Reference Summary

| Modality | Primary Use in Rec. Urology | Radiation | Contrast | Key Limitation |
|----------|-----------------------------|-----------|----------|----------------|
| RUG | Male urethral stricture mapping | Low (0.1–2 mSv) | Yes (iodinated) | Lumen only; no spongiofibrosis |
| VCUG | Posterior urethra, VUR, female stricture | Low | Yes | Requires voiding cooperation |
| SUG | Spongiofibrosis depth and extent | None | Saline/gel | Operator dependent |
| MRI | PFUI, prolapse, UD, Peyronie's, upper tract | None | Optional | Cost, access, claustrophobia |
| MAG3 | Split function, obstruction kinetics | Low (nuclear) | Tc-99m MAG3 | Radiation, hydration dependence |
| CTU | Anatomy, urothelial Ca, fistula | Moderate–high | Yes (iodinated) | Radiation, nephrotoxicity |
