# Intermittent Catheters and Clean Intermittent Catheterization (CIC)

**Intermittent catheterization** drains the bladder at intervals, with removal of the catheter after each drainage. For adults with neurogenic lower urinary tract dysfunction (NLUTD), AUA/SUFU recommends intermittent over indwelling catheterization when it is feasible (strong recommendation, evidence grade C). Selection still depends on bladder pressures, anatomy, hand function, cognition, caregiver support and the patient's goals; comparative evidence does not establish a universal ranking of infection risk or quality of life for every patient.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[2]](#ref2)</sup>

CIC uses a single-lumen, non-balloon catheter passed through the urethra, or through a continent catheterizable channel, and removed immediately after the bladder empties. It can be performed by the patient (clean intermittent self-catheterization, CISC), a caregiver or a clinician. It is the long-term emptying method for many patients with neurogenic bladder, after augmentation cystoplasty and with continent channels. A continent catheterizable channel has its own anatomical and follow-up requirements; see [Catheterizable Channels](/docs/surgical-techniques/04b-bladder-reconstruction/catheterizable-channels).

## When intermittent drainage is appropriate

Consider intermittent drainage for clinically important incomplete emptying or retention, including selected neurogenic, postoperative, medication-related and chronic non-neurogenic cases. A single postvoid residual threshold does not determine the long-term catheter plan. Symptoms, renal risk, bladder capacity and pressure, residual trend, reversible causes and the ability to catheterize reliably all matter.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[2]](#ref2)</sup>

- **Unsafe storage pressures require treatment as well as emptying.** CIC alone does not guarantee upper-tract protection. Adjust storage treatment and drainage when pressures, reflux, renal deterioration or autonomic dysreflexia indicate an unsafe regimen.
- **A urethral route must be passable without force.** Suspected injury, a false passage or a difficult stricture requires assessment and an alternative access plan; repeated blind attempts can worsen injury.
- **Reconstruction does not impose one universal regimen.** Catheterizable reservoirs or channels require a reliable emptying plan, but not every augmentation requires lifelong CIC. Bladder-neck closure may be paired with a catheterizable channel or suprapubic drainage, depending on the reconstruction.
- **Feasibility includes assistance and supplies.** A caregiver, handling aid or different catheter may make CIC possible. If timely, safe drainage cannot be maintained, discuss an indwelling or surgical alternative.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[2]](#ref2)</sup>

### Typical settings

- **Neurogenic bladder.** Spinal cord injury, multiple sclerosis, Parkinson disease, spina bifida and cauda equina syndrome, and persistent voiding dysfunction after stroke.
- **Retention after treatment.** Retention that follows intradetrusor onabotulinumtoxinA or anticholinergic therapy.
- **Non-neurogenic retention.** Acute or chronic retention without outlet obstruction, and overflow incontinence.
- **Reconstruction.** Augmentation cystoplasty, catheterizable channels (Mitrofanoff, Monti or Yang-Monti), continent cutaneous diversion and bladder-neck closure with a channel.
- **Postoperative retention after pelvic surgery.** In a Markov cost model, CIC was the least costly pathway ($79 per patient, versus $128 for self-removal of an indwelling catheter and $185 for an office voiding trial), but only if patients were taught CIC after surgery. If all patients were taught CIC routinely before surgery, it became the most costly option.<sup>[[19]](#ref19)</sup>
- **Bladder voiding efficiency.** A multidisciplinary consensus statement recommends individualized assessment, including bladder voiding efficiency, to decide when to start CISC.<sup>[[22]](#ref22)</sup>

Relative contraindications include inability to follow a catheterization schedule without caregiver support, abnormal urethral anatomy that prevents safe passage, a high-pressure bladder that needs continuous drainage to protect the kidneys, and autonomic dysreflexia triggered by bladder filling despite catheterization.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[2]](#ref2)</sup>

## Catheter selection

Most intermittent drainage catheters have no retention balloon. Material, coating, length, tip, packaging and approved use life vary by product; neither “PVC” nor “hydrophilic” describes every device.<sup>[[2]](#ref2)</sup>

| Feature | Practical selection |
|---|---|
| **Uncoated** | Requires the compatible lubricant specified for that product. Do not substitute water alone for required lubrication. |
| **Hydrophilic or prelubricated** | May improve handling and comfort. Some require activation; others are ready to use. Follow the package instructions. |
| **Gel-reservoir** | Pre-applied gel lubricant coats the catheter on use, with no water activation. |
| **Sleeve / no-touch / collection-bag system** | Can help avoid touching the insertion surface and assist patients who cannot readily use a toilet. In vitro, no-touch designs transferred about 5 CFU per plate versus 210 to 440 CFU for traditional hydrophilic catheters handled with contaminated gloves. In a simulation with nurses and students, the no-touch method took 92 seconds less and produced fewer sterility errors. Neither result is proof of a clinical UTI reduction.<sup>[[13]](#ref13)[[14]](#ref14)</sup> |
| **Straight or coudé tip** | Select for the patient's anatomy and prior successful technique. A curved tip is not permission to push through resistance. See [Coudé Catheter](/docs/foundations/tools/biomaterials/urinary-catheters/coude-catheter). |
| **Size** | Use a comfortable size that drains effectively. EAUN adult examples are approximately 12–14 Fr for men and 10–14 Fr for women; individual anatomy or a reconstructed channel may require a different prescription. |
| **Length** | Standard male catheters are commonly about 40 cm; shorter female products and purpose-designed compact male products are distinct. **Do not substitute a female-length catheter for a male catheter without a specifically assessed, appropriate device.** |
| **Tip and examples** | Straight (Nelaton) and coudé tips exist. Product examples include SpeediCath (Coloplast) and LoFric (Wellspect), which are hydrophilic; availability varies by market. |
| **Use life** | Check whether the exact product is labeled single-use or reusable, its replacement limit and any cleaning requirements. Coating or appearance alone does not establish this. |

### Coating evidence

The 2021 Cochrane review found low-certainty evidence for symptomatic UTI differences between hydrophilic and uncoated catheters (RR 0.89, 95% CI 0.69–1.14; two studies, 98 participants). A later nine-study meta-analysis found a lower pooled UTI risk with hydrophilic catheters (RR 0.78, 95% CI 0.62–0.97; 525 participants); its adult subgroup estimate was RR 0.83 (95% CI 0.74–0.93). Differences in populations, comparators and outcome definitions limit a single universal estimate.<sup>[[3]](#ref3)[[4]](#ref4)</sup>

In the AUA/SUFU guideline text, hydrophilic catheters may be associated with lower UTI and urethral trauma rates than other catheter types, specifically among patients with spinal cord injury.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup> A systematic review and cost analysis found little difference in symptomatic UTI risk with catheter type, although hydrophilic and gel-reservoir catheters were associated with fewer patients reporting one or more UTIs than sterile non-coated catheters. It concluded that clean non-coated catheters were the most cost effective and advised offering hydrophilic or gel-reservoir catheters as a precaution.<sup>[[12]](#ref12)</sup> The Cochrane review found that uncoated catheters probably slightly reduce urethral trauma and bleeding compared with hydrophilic catheters (RR 1.37, 95% CI 1.01–1.87; moderate certainty), and the nine-study meta-analysis found no hematuria difference (RR 1.02, 95% CI 0.66–1.60).<sup>[[3]](#ref3)[[4]](#ref4)</sup>

EAUN favors hydrophilic or prelubricated catheters for reducing trauma, but comparative reviews do not agree on every bleeding outcome. Choose for comfort, ease of use, access and prior problems; do not promise freedom from infection or stricture.<sup>[[2]](#ref2)[[3]](#ref3)[[4]](#ref4)</sup>

## Training and drainage schedule

**Clean technique is acceptable for chronic intermittent catheterization outside acute care.** The 2021 Cochrane review found no certain difference in symptomatic UTI between aseptic and clean technique (RR 1.20, 95% CI 0.54–2.66; one study, 36 participants; low certainty).<sup>[[3]](#ref3)</sup> Healthcare insertion follows the facility's aseptic technique and single-use supply requirements. Clean home technique still requires hand hygiene, appropriate equipment handling and a taught procedure.<sup>[[2]](#ref2)[[5]](#ref5)</sup>

1. Confirm the prescribed catheter and any lubricant or activation step. Prepare a clean working area and collection method; perform hand hygiene and genital cleansing as taught.
2. Use the patient's practiced position and anatomical landmarks. Avoid touching the part that enters the urethra where the device permits a non-touch technique.
3. Insert gently until urine drains, then advance only as needed to keep the drainage eyes in the bladder. Stop for significant resistance, pain or new bleeding; do not use a fixed insertion depth for every patient.
4. Allow drainage, withdrawing slowly and pausing if more urine appears. Routine abdominal straining or a Credé maneuver is not part of this general technique.
5. Discard a single-use catheter. For a reusable-labeled product, follow its agreed cleaning, storage and replacement protocol. Record volumes when establishing or reassessing the regimen.<sup>[[2]](#ref2)</sup>

Patients who do not void often need four to six catheterizations per day, but frequency is individualized. A common aim is to avoid bladder volumes above roughly 400–500 mL; lower limits may be needed for low capacity, unsafe pressure or reflux. Review unexpectedly large or very small drainage volumes, fluid intake, leakage, overnight needs and missed catheterizations. A volume target is not a substitute for risk-based urodynamic and renal follow-up.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[2]](#ref2)</sup>

Use supervised practice and teach-back, with early follow-up to address dexterity, vision, pain, positioning, access to toilets and supply cost. Provide a backup plan for inability to drain, significant hematuria, febrile illness or autonomic dysreflexia.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[2]](#ref2)</sup>

## Single-use and reusable catheters

The 2021 Cochrane review found no certain difference in symptomatic UTI between single-use and multiple-use catheters (RR 0.98, 95% CI 0.55–1.74; two studies, 97 participants; low certainty).<sup>[[3]](#ref3)</sup> A review of 11 comparative studies found no appreciable evidence that reusable catheters were inferior from an infection or usability standpoint, and it raised the cost and environmental burden of single-use catheters.<sup>[[20]](#ref20)</sup> Reuse is common in some regions (about 50% in Australia, Canada and the United States in one review), with no consensus on cleaning method or reuse limit, and off-label reuse of single-use products is not legally supported.<sup>[[21]](#ref21)</sup> A patient catheterizing five times daily uses about 1,825 single-use catheters a year.

## Reusable catheters: two important 2026 trials

The small, uncertain reuse evidence summarized by Cochrane in 2021 has been supplemented by two larger randomized trials. Both tested specified reusable products, training and cleaning systems, with access to single-use catheters. They support a structured option for selected users, while leaving important tolerability and generalizability limits.<sup>[[3]](#ref3)[[6]](#ref6)[[7]](#ref7)</sup>

| Trial | Primary finding | What limits application |
|---|---|---|
| **COMPARE, Netherlands, 2026** — 386 randomized, 12-month follow-up | Modified intention-to-treat UTI rates were 0.050/month with reusable versus 0.054/month with single-use catheters; difference −0.004 (95% CI −0.025 to 0.019), meeting the prespecified noninferiority margin of 0.07 UTI/month. | That margin exceeded the observed single-use rate. Reusable use was discontinued by 72/185 participants (39%); reported urethral irritation was 20% versus 4.7%. The trial excluded augmentation, nonurethral catheterization and several other complex conditions. |
| **MultICath, UK, 2026** — 578 randomized, 12 months | In the per-protocol analysis, at least one symptomatic, microbiologically confirmed UTI occurred in 49/171 mixed-use versus 84/244 single-use participants: OR 0.77 (95% CI 0.50–1.17), meeting the prespecified OR 2.0 noninferiority margin. | There was substantial differential withdrawal and a smaller mixed-use analysis population. Catheter sticking was reported more often with mixed use (24.6% versus 15.2%). Participants were community adults, predominantly self-catheterizing through the urethra. |

COMPARE used reusable catheters replaced every two weeks, a prescribed disinfection regimen and limited additional single-use catheterization. MultICath used a Cliny silicone catheter labeled for up to 28 days, a supplied cleaning kit with soap and water and chlorine-based disinfection, training and flexible use of the person's usual single-use catheters.<sup>[[6]](#ref6)[[7]](#ref7)[[8]](#ref8)[[9]](#ref9)</sup>

**Clinical implication:** discuss a supported reusable or mixed regimen where an appropriate reusable-labeled product and reliable cleaning arrangements are available. These trials do not authorize reprocessing any single-use catheter, establish an interchangeable household disinfectant recipe, or demonstrate safety in every reconstructed reservoir, child or caregiver-dependent patient. Comfort, sticking, cleaning burden and willingness to continue matter alongside infection outcomes.<sup>[[6]](#ref6)[[7]](#ref7)[[8]](#ref8)[[9]](#ref9)</sup>

## Complications and UTI prevention

**UTI** is the commonest complication. Repeated symptomatic UTIs affect about 25% of people who use CISC.<sup>[[11]](#ref11)</sup> In a cross-sectional NLUTD cohort, UTI prevalence at the visit was 8% (24/299) with intermittent catheterization and 8% (16/206) with indwelling catheters (p = 0.782). The indwelling group was older with more comorbidity, so the study does not overturn the guideline preference.<sup>[[15]](#ref15)</sup>

**Urethral stricture** is associated with long-term CIC in men. In a retrospective spinal cord injury center series, strictures occurred in 25% (95% CI 21–30%) of 415 men using intermittent catheterization, at a median of 5.9 years, versus 14% (95% CI 11–17%) of 629 men using other evacuation methods (p = 0.0001). About one-third had internal urethrotomy, and radiographic recurrence after urethrotomy was 100% at a median of 14 years.<sup>[[16]](#ref16)</sup> A single-institution spinal cord injury cohort of 333 patients reported 4.2% strictures at a mean of 9 years of CISC, which shows the case-mix variability.<sup>[[17]](#ref17)</sup>

**Other complications.** Hematuria is common at initiation. In 28 boys and young men with neurogenic bladder followed for a median of 16 years (438 catheterization-years), 7 major urethral lesions were seen on cystoscopy, including 5 false passages. None occurred during self-catheterization or with catheters of 12 Ch or larger, so both seemed protective.<sup>[[18]](#ref18)</sup> Channel stenosis can occur with Mitrofanoff or Monti channels and may need dilation or revision.

Pain, bleeding, false passage, urethral stricture and symptomatic UTI can occur. New difficulty passing a previously reliable catheter warrants assessment of technique and anatomy. Persistent or substantial hematuria should not automatically be attributed to catheter trauma. A reconstructed channel that becomes difficult to catheterize needs its own specialist access plan.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[2]](#ref2)</sup>

**Asymptomatic bacteriuria is common but not universal.** Do not routinely screen or treat asymptomatic NLUTD patients; pregnancy and selected urologic procedures are exceptions requiring their specific guidance. With compatible new symptoms, obtain appropriate urine testing and evaluate drainage, stones, obstruction or another cause of recurrent infection.<sup>[[10]](#ref10)</sup>

For recurrent symptomatic UTI, first review emptying, catheter handling and the patient's underlying urinary tract. The AnTIC trial randomized 404 adults with recurrent UTI who used intermittent self-catheterization: daily prophylaxis reduced symptomatic antibiotic-treated UTI frequency (IRR 0.52, 95% CI 0.44–0.61), but increased antimicrobial resistance. AUA/SUFU permits prophylaxis in selected NLUTD patients with recurrent UTI after a discussion of that tradeoff; it is not routine treatment for every CIC user.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[11]](#ref11)</sup>

The AnTIC trial also measured resistance at 9–12 months in urinary isolates from participants who had at least one isolate:

| Resistance | Prophylaxis | Control | p |
|---|---|---|---|
| Nitrofurantoin | 12/51 (24%) | 6/64 (9%) | 0.038 |
| Trimethoprim | 34/51 (67%) | 21/64 (33%) | 0.0003 |
| Co-trimoxazole | 26/49 (53%) | 15/62 (24%) | 0.002 |

The guideline statement is conditional: clinicians may offer oral antimicrobial prophylaxis to NLUTD patients who perform CIC and have recurrent UTI after shared decision-making about resistance risk (Conditional Recommendation, Grade C). It also states that cranberry extract has not been demonstrated to reduce UTI rates in NLUTD patients with recurrent UTI (Conditional Recommendation, Grade B), and that clinicians may offer bladder instillations (Expert Opinion).<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[11]](#ref11)</sup>

## Special situations

- **Postoperative retention.** The URECA algorithm, developed by an expert panel with the RAND/UCLA method, prefers intermittent to indwelling catheterization for managing lower bladder volumes. It sets bladder scanner volumes appropriate to prompt catheterization at 300 mL or greater in symptomatic patients and 500 mL or greater in asymptomatic patients.<sup>[[23]](#ref23)</sup>
- **Children.** CIC is widely used in spina bifida, often started in infancy with parental help. Long-term data are in the complications above.<sup>[[18]](#ref18)</sup>
- **Barriers.** Poor hand dexterity (arthritis, tremor, spasticity, weakness), cognitive or visual impairment, obesity, psychological barriers, catheter cost and lack of a clean catheterization space can limit CIC.<sup>[[2]](#ref2)</sup>

For drug selection, methenamine, intravesical regimens and the distinction between uncomplicated recurrent UTI and catheter-associated populations, see [UTI Suppressive & Prophylactic Therapy](/docs/foundations/pharmacology/infection-prophylaxis/uti-suppressive-prophylactic).
