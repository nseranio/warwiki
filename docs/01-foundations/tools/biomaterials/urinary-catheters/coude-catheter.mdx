# Coudé (Tiemann) Catheter

A **coudé catheter** (French *coudé*, "bent" or "elbowed") has a curved tip that can help it pass an angulated prostatic urethra or elevated bladder neck. The same tip geometry is called a **Tiemann tip**. It is useful in selected difficult male catheterizations. It does not dilate a stricture, and it does not make repeated blind attempts safe. Indwelling balloon-retained and intermittent versions are available. Tip curvature, stiffness, size, material and orientation markings vary by product.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup>

## Design

A coudé is a Foley-type catheter whose distal tip curves upward instead of ending in a straight, rounded tip. A raised ridge or bump on the funnel end marks the direction of the curve, and it must stay anterior (12 o'clock) during insertion. Balloon capacity, lumen configuration (2-way or 3-way), size and material (latex, silicone-coated, all-silicone) are set by the product.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup>

| Tip variant | Notes |
|---|---|
| **Coudé (Tiemann) tip** | Smooth angled tip; the most common curved design. |
| **Mercier tip** | Similar angulation with a more bulbous tip. Naming varies by manufacturer. |
| **Council-coudé** | Curved tip with a central end-hole, combining wire-guided placement with prostatic-curve navigation. See [Council Tip Catheter](/docs/foundations/tools/biomaterials/urinary-catheters/council-tip-catheter). |

## Mechanism

The male urethra curves anteriorly at the bulbomembranous junction and again at the prostatic urethra and bladder neck. In BPH, prostatic enlargement can elevate the bladder neck and increase prostatic urethral angulation.<sup>[[6]](#ref6)[[7]](#ref7)</sup> In a transrectal ultrasound series of 281 men with BPH, the mean bladder neck angle was 85.5° in men with severe symptoms and 89.8° in men with Qmax &lt;10 mL/s, and 86.0° in men with acute retention compared with 63.8° in men without.<sup>[[6]](#ref6)</sup> A straight tip can catch on an elevated bladder neck or median lobe and cause resistance, false passage or failed catheterization. The coudé curve is intended to ride over the obstruction by following the anterior curvature into the bladder. The angulation concept in the Cho paper is a hypothesis, not an established cause of BPH.<sup>[[7]](#ref7)</sup>

## Device selection

| Design | Use and limitation |
|---|---|
| **Straight-tip Foley** | Standard indwelling drainage when passage is straightforward. |
| **Coudé / Tiemann-tip catheter** | Curved tip, commonly used for prostatic angulation. Select an appropriate diameter and length; a larger catheter is not automatically safer. Blind placement with the tip oriented anteriorly. |
| **Council-tip catheter** | Has an end-hole for passage over a guidewire, with cystoscopic or fluoroscopic guidance. A curved tip alone does not imply wire compatibility. |
| **Coudé intermittent catheter** | A non-retaining option for an established self-catheterization plan; requires patient instruction on orientation. |

Consult the model's instructions and confirm latex status and balloon requirements when applicable. Coudé designs are used mainly in male catheterization. Sex alone is not a universal contraindication independent of the device label and the patient's anatomy.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup>

## Indications

A trained operator may use a coudé when prostatic angulation is the likely obstacle.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[2]](#ref2)</sup> Typical settings are:

- BPH with an elevated bladder neck or median-lobe enlargement.
- Bladder-neck contracture or vesicourethral anastomotic stenosis after TURP or radical prostatectomy.
- A failed straight-tip Foley, as a second blind attempt before cystoscopic or guidewire techniques. In an emergency-medicine review, the difficult-catheterization algorithm reattempts with a coudé catheter after failed standard placement.<sup>[[2]](#ref2)</sup>
- Routine male catheterization in institutions that have adopted a coudé standard (see the evidence below).<sup>[[5]](#ref5)</sup>

Review prior difficult catheterization, prostate or urethral surgery, known stricture and any continence implant before attempting insertion. A bladder-neck contracture, vesicourethral anastomotic stenosis or suspected false passage should prompt an access plan rather than a trial of progressively greater force.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[2]](#ref2)</sup>

## Insertion and when to stop

1. Use sterile equipment and aseptic technique in acute care, with a compatible lubricant, ideally containing lidocaine.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup>
2. Orient the curve anteriorly, usually the 12 o'clock direction in standard male positioning, using the manufacturer's orientation marker.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[2]](#ref2)</sup>
3. Hold the penis perpendicular to the body with gentle traction to straighten the penile urethra.
4. Advance slowly and steadily. At the prostatic urethra the curve lifts over the elevated bladder neck. One emergency-medicine review describes rotating to the 9 and 3 o'clock positions if resistance persists; other sources keep the tip anterior throughout, so follow the manufacturer's instructions.<sup>[[2]](#ref2)</sup>
5. Stop for persistent resistance, marked pain or bleeding.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup>

:::warning[Balloon safety]
For a male indwelling catheter, urine return alone is insufficient before balloon inflation. Advance freely to the catheter bifurcation or hub and establish bladder placement before inflating with the model's specified fluid and volume. Pain or resistance during inflation requires stopping and reassessing. Do not inflate while the balloon may still lie in the urethra.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[3]](#ref3)</sup>
:::

After a failed attempt, obtain experienced help. Cystoscopy with a guidewire and an appropriate [Council-tip catheter](/docs/foundations/tools/biomaterials/urinary-catheters/council-tip-catheter), or [suprapubic drainage](/docs/foundations/tools/biomaterials/urinary-catheters/suprapubic-catheter), may be appropriate early. In a prospective series of 54 patients unable to pass a urethral catheter, bedside flexible cystoscopy with a guidewire and a Council-tipped catheter achieved drainage in 52 without complications.<sup>[[8]](#ref8)</sup>

### Escalation sequence

A commonly taught escalation order for difficult male catheterization is listed below. It is a practice pattern rather than a guideline, and the safest route may skip steps.<sup>[[2]](#ref2)[[8]](#ref8)</sup>

1. Standard straight-tip Foley with adequate lubrication.
2. Coudé-tip catheter for suspected prostatic obstruction.
3. Guidewire-stiffened Foley (Liss maneuver).
4. Flexible cystoscopy, guidewire and Council catheter.
5. Fluoroscopy-guided Council-tip catheterization.
6. Suprapubic cystostomy.

### The Liss maneuver is a different technique

The 2009 report placed the floppy end of a stiff guidewire inside the Foley lumen to stiffen its body. It did not first establish a wire route into the bladder. Bench testing showed a mean 359% increase in catheter-body bending stiffness with no significant increase in tip stiffness, and the maneuver succeeded in five of six clinical attempts; the failure was an 8 Fr stricture. This limited technique report does not establish equivalence to cystoscopically guided over-wire catheterization. It is not a mandatory step and is not evidence that blind stiffening prevents urethral injury.<sup>[[4]](#ref4)</sup>

## What the institutional evidence shows

Miller and colleagues reported fewer traumatic catheterizations after introducing a combined nursing-education and coudé-kit program for male operating-room patients: 18/601 (3.0%) before versus 4/2,038 (0.2%) after. The historical sample covered three surgical services, whereas the later sample covered male OR catheterizations more broadly. The 18 traumatic events cost $27,793, or $1,544 per patient, in additional procedures, supplies and OR time.<sup>[[5]](#ref5)</sup>

This before-and-after study supports evaluating a structured local safety program. It cannot isolate the effect of the catheter from education, case mix or changes over time. It does not include a comparison of incremental catheter cost against savings, so it does not establish cost-effectiveness, and it does not show that every adult male should receive a coudé.<sup>[[5]](#ref5)</sup>

The URECA algorithm (Chrouser 2024, developed by expert-panel consensus) advises that high-risk patients, such as men older than 55 years or with prostatic enlargement, be catheterized by experienced nurses using the safest catheter type and size. It does not name a specific catheter type in the published text.<sup>[[9]](#ref9)</sup>

## Trauma is a separate pathway

ACS 2025 guidance prefers a straight-tip catheter and does not recommend coudé tips when urethral injury is suspected. With strong suspicion, such as meatal blood accompanying pelvic fracture, urethral assessment, usually retrograde urethrography in a male, should precede catheterization when feasible. The guidance advises aborting the procedure if resistance is met or no urine is obtained. A selected experienced attempt when imaging is unavailable is not permission for repeated attempts.<sup>[[3]](#ref3)</sup> A curved tip could extend a false passage in an injured urethra.

See [Pelvic Fracture Urethral Injury](/docs/special-populations/05a-trauma-emergencies/pfui) and [Acute Urinary Retention](/docs/special-populations/05a-trauma-emergencies/acute-urinary-retention).

## Complications

Coudé catheters share the risks of other urinary catheters: pain, hematuria, false passage, urethral injury, bladder spasm, blockage, encrustation and infection. A curved tip does not eliminate these risks.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup>

- **Tip orientation.** A curve pointing posteriorly can catch on the posterior urethral wall and create a false passage.
- **Strictures.** A coudé is not effective for tight strictures, which need guidewire-assisted [Council-tip](/docs/foundations/tools/biomaterials/urinary-catheters/council-tip-catheter) techniques.

Secure indwelling catheters without traction and use the closed-drainage and removal principles in [Foley Catheter](/docs/foundations/tools/biomaterials/urinary-catheters/foley-catheter).<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup>

## Self-catheterization with coudé tips

Coudé-tip intermittent catheters are available for men doing clean intermittent catheterization who have difficulty with straight catheters, for example with BPH or bladder-neck obstruction. Teach and verify the patient's technique with the exact device prescribed, including keeping the curve facing up. See [Intermittent Catheter](/docs/foundations/tools/biomaterials/urinary-catheters/intermittent-catheter).<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup>
