# Suprapubic Catheter (SPT)

A **suprapubic catheter (SPC or SPT)** is an indwelling catheter placed percutaneously or operatively through the anterior abdominal wall into the bladder, bypassing the urethra. It avoids a continuously indwelling urethral catheter. In appropriately selected adults with neurogenic lower urinary tract dysfunction (NLUTD) who require chronic indwelling drainage, AUA/SUFU recommends suprapubic rather than urethral catheterization (strong recommendation, Grade C evidence). Intermittent catheterization is preferred when feasible and appropriate.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup>

Suprapubic drainage can protect the urethra and make catheter care or sexual activity easier for some patients. It still entails an invasive insertion and ongoing risks of blockage, infection, stones, leakage, spasm and tract loss; neither preserved sexual function nor freedom from infection is guaranteed.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[2]](#ref2)</sup>

## Indications and route selection

Suprapubic drainage is considered when long-term catheterization is needed or urethral catheterization is not feasible or desired.<sup>[[2]](#ref2)</sup> Typical settings are:

- neurogenic bladder (spinal cord injury, multiple sclerosis) when intermittent catheterization is not feasible;
- chronic retention refractory to other management;
- urethral abnormalities such as stricture, false passage, bladder-neck obstruction or urethral fistula;
- pelvic fracture urethral injury, where suprapubic diversion precedes delayed posterior urethroplasty;
- protective diversion during healing after urethral reconstruction;
- perineal skin breakdown worsened by a urethral catheter;
- refractory OAB, where AUA/SUFU advises chronic indwelling urethral or suprapubic catheters only when OAB therapies are contraindicated, ineffective or no longer desired, and always with shared decision-making (Expert Opinion);<sup>[[11]](#ref11)</sup>
- patient preference, for example to spare the urethra or ease sexual activity.

Suprapubic drainage is also used for urethral rest before definitive stricture surgery. The EAU stricture guideline text states that a suprapubic catheter should be inserted if incapacitating obstructive symptoms or retention develop during urethral rest (narrative text, not a strength-rated recommendation).<sup>[[3]](#ref3)</sup>

The decision should include bladder storage pressures, urethral anatomy, hand function, caregiver support, mobility, skin and body habitus, anticipated duration, patient goals, and alternatives. An external collection device does not treat retention. For continence without an emptying indication, an indwelling catheter requires a separate, carefully justified discussion of alternatives and harms.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[2]](#ref2)</sup>

See [Intermittent Catheterization](/docs/foundations/tools/biomaterials/urinary-catheters/intermittent-catheter), [Pelvic Fracture Urethral Injury](/docs/special-populations/05a-trauma-emergencies/pfui), and [Urinary Diversion](/docs/surgical-techniques/04c-urinary-diversion).

## Device and insertion planning

A suprapubic catheter is usually a Foley-type balloon catheter passed through the tract. Dedicated trocar-placed kits and pigtail or self-retaining designs also exist (product names include Cystofix, Bonanno and Stamey). Select a catheter and access system appropriate to the tract, urine and debris burden, anatomy, and product instructions. Balloon inflation fluid and volume and subsequent exchange compatibility remain device-specific.<sup>[[2]](#ref2)</sup>

Peel-away kits are easy to place but often deliver a 12 or 14 Fr catheter that is prone to kinking and may need serial upsizing. Open placement permits a larger initial catheter but is more invasive, particularly in obese patients.<sup>[[12]](#ref12)</sup> No source sets a universal minimum caliber or fixed skin site. One institutional technique places a 17G Tuohy needle about 3 cm above the pubis (generally in the abdominal crease), 1 to 2 cm off midline toward the side where the patient prefers the drainage bag, and angles it to enter the bladder dome in the midline under cystoscopic vision so that the catheter lies flat. A 24 Fr nephrostomy balloon and sheath then allow initial placement of a catheter of 20 Fr or larger. In that 65-placement single-institution series, median operative time was 16 minutes, all placements of 20 Fr or larger succeeded, Clavien I/II complications occurred in 18%, one Clavien IIIb event occurred, and the first clinic exchange succeeded in 95% (2 patients needed replacement under anesthesia).<sup>[[12]](#ref12)</sup>

Before percutaneous insertion, establish a safely accessible bladder and assess for interposed bowel, previous abdominal or pelvic surgery, altered anatomy, scars, mesh or vascular grafts. A bladder that is not palpable may still be localizable by imaging; a bladder that cannot be safely localized or accessed is not a target for a blind puncture. Prior surgery, pelvic radiation, pregnancy, anticoagulation or coagulopathy and infection at the proposed site require specialist planning. Known or suspected bladder cancer and unexplained gross hematuria require particular assessment because a suprapubic route may be unsuitable, including concern about tumor seeding.<sup>[[2]](#ref2)</sup>

| Approach | Role and limitation |
|---|---|
| **Image-guided percutaneous access** | Ultrasound helps identify the bladder, trajectory and overlying structures; fluoroscopy may assist wire/catheter placement. An adequate safe window is required. |
| **Trocar or Seldinger access** | Different access methods selected for the situation and operator expertise. Trocar access is a single step through a puncture cannula; a bedside series of 72 men with a palpable bladder, excluding prior lower abdominal surgery, reported a mean procedure time under five minutes and no complications.<sup>[[13]](#ref13)</sup> Seldinger access uses needle, wire and dilation. Neither method is universally safer in all anatomy. |
| **Cystoscopic assistance** | Confirms intravesical entry and position. Looking inside the bladder does **not** independently exclude bowel between skin and bladder. |
| **Lowsley retractor or Van Buren sound** | The bladder is entered by incision onto a sound or retractor passed per urethra, with cystoscopy. A retrospective series of 44 patients with neurogenic bladder reported a mean time of about 20 minutes and no incorrect placements.<sup>[[14]](#ref14)</sup> A Van Buren sound technique has been described that allows wire exchange of the catheter before a tract is established; it needs an intact urethra and is contraindicated in pelvic trauma.<sup>[[15]](#ref15)</sup> |
| **Open placement** | An option when percutaneous access is unsafe or when placement accompanies another operation. |

Ultrasound is recommended by BAUS whenever possible to identify interposed bowel.<sup>[[16]](#ref16)</sup> The conventional blind technique depends on bladder filling to displace bowel. Its bowel-injury risk has been estimated at up to 2.4%, with a mortality of 1.8%.<sup>[[16]](#ref16)</sup> Contemporary data suggest a lower risk (see below).

When ultrasound shows bowel overlying the anterior bladder wall in a patient unfit for general anesthesia, hydrodissection has been described. In a single case report, the bladder was filled through the urethral catheter, and a 22G needle with 30 mL (10 mL 1% lidocaine plus 20 mL sterile water) was inserted under ultrasound between the pubic symphysis and the overlying bowel. Injection displaced the bowel, an 18G needle then entered the bladder, and the catheter was placed by Seldinger technique with no complications at 6 months.<sup>[[17]](#ref17)</sup>

Do not force distension of a low-capacity or recently repaired bladder to meet a generic target. Plan analgesia or anesthesia and monitoring, including autonomic dysreflexia precautions when relevant. Case-level reports such as this one should not be converted into routine bedside recipes; see the [hydrodissection hub](/docs/foundations/pharmacology/intraoperative-adjuncts/hydrodissection-agents).<sup>[[2]](#ref2)</sup>

### Trocar and Seldinger compared

A retrospective comparison of 125 image-guided cystostomies (Roberts 2020) found both approaches technically successful, with rare major complications. Trocar access was faster and needed less sedation and radiation. Occlusion was more frequent with trocar access, possibly because of a catheter-tract size mismatch.<sup>[[18]](#ref18)</sup>

| Outcome | Trocar (n = 60) | Seldinger (n = 65) | p |
|---|---|---|---|
| Procedure time | 12.4 min | 25.7 min | &lt;.001 |
| Catheter size | 13.1 Fr | 13.9 Fr | .044 |
| Midazolam dose | 1.2 mg | 1.9 mg | .003 |
| Radiation | 20.2 mGy | 100.7 mGy | &lt;.001 |
| Catheter occlusion | 41.7% | 16.9% | .003 |
| Bladder spasm | 1.7% | 13.8% | .018 |

Overall, catheter occlusion was the most common complication (28.8%), followed by UTI (13.6%) and bladder spasm (8.0%).<sup>[[18]](#ref18)</sup>

## Complication evidence: use the right denominator

Hobbs 2022 retrospectively reviewed 1,000 elective insertions, predominantly in patients with spinal cord injury. Cystoscopy-guided puncture or an incision onto a urethral sound followed by cystoscopy accounted for 98% of procedures. Clavien–Dindo grade IIIb or higher complications occurred in 0.6%; no bowel injury was observed, and one patient died within 30 days from pulmonary embolism. Mostly minor complications occurred in 59% over variable follow-up (median 3.3 years). This does not prove cystoscopy is the safest method or that insertion has no bowel-injury risk.<sup>[[4]](#ref4)</sup>

Hall 2019 identified 11,473 insertions in a UK administrative audit. Of 141 subsequent laparotomy cases, information was returned for 114 and one SPC-related bowel injury was identified. The accompanying published-series meta-analysis found 11/1,490 bowel injuries (0.7%); the authors proposed counseling low-risk patients with a risk below 0.25%. The ascertainment limits, different populations and methods prevent treating the national audit as a complete exact risk estimate or attributing a historical decline to one device.<sup>[[5]](#ref5)</sup>

A more recent long-term cohort of 222 patients with at least two years of follow-up reported short-term (30.2%) and long-term (59.9%) complication rates that were mostly minor: Clavien–Dindo grade III or higher occurred in 4.5% and 23%. Ancillary lower-tract treatments (anticholinergics, intradetrusor onabotulinumtoxinA) were used in 46%, and discontinuation was 13.9% at a median of 12 months. Associations between ancillary treatment and catheter retention were observational; they do not establish that prescribing medication pre-emptively prolongs catheter use. Absence of ancillary treatment (OR 56.8) and initial placement by interventional radiology (OR 2.03) predicted discontinuation univariately.<sup>[[6]](#ref6)</sup>

A within-subject retrospective study of 37 elderly patients who moved from a urethral to a suprapubic catheter found lower CAUTI (38% vs 86%, p &lt; 0.001), fewer emergency department visits (35% vs 68%, p &lt; 0.001), fewer obstructions and less hematuria after the change. Dislodgement and leakage did not differ. The design is retrospective and the cohort small.<sup>[[19]](#ref19)</sup>

## Care and catheter exchanges

Use securement without traction, closed drainage, a bag below bladder level, routine hygiene, and individualized hydration advice. Assess constipation, debris, spasm, and catheter position when drainage fails or urine bypasses. Routine antibiotics or antimicrobial washouts are not standard prevention strategies.<sup>[[2]](#ref2)[[7]](#ref7)</sup>

### First and difficult exchanges

The first exchange requires an established tract and an operator capable of managing loss of access. Timing depends on the insertion method, healing, catheter system and local specialist protocol; a calendar date alone does not prove tract maturity. Document who will perform the first change and whether imaging or wire guidance is needed.<sup>[[2]](#ref2)</sup>

- Record the original catheter type, size, balloon instructions, insertion details and planned exchange method.
- Deflate and remove according to the device instructions; do not pull against an inflated or retained balloon.
- Confirm the replacement catheter's intravesical position before balloon inflation. Unexpected depth, pain, resistance, absent drainage, or uncertainty requires reassessment rather than force.
- A wire-assisted exchange requires a compatible catheter and controlled access; it is not achieved by blindly pushing a wire or replacement catheter into an uncertain tract.

**Dislodgement needs urgent assessment because the tract can close rapidly.** An early or lost tract may require image-guided or operative replacement. Do not teach untrained patients or carers to force a catheter into it. Provide a clear emergency contact and backup drainage plan. Tract losses during routine community changes have been reported.<sup>[[2]](#ref2)[[4]](#ref4)</sup>

A multifunctional suprapubic catheter (scale markings and modified drainage holes) reduced catheter occlusion (11.6% vs 37.5%, p = .005) and cystospasm compared with a conventional balloon catheter in a single-center randomized trial of 91 patients.<sup>[[20]](#ref20)</sup>

### Subsequent intervals

Base replacement on clinical need, the manufacturer's maximum dwell time, and the individual's blockage pattern. CDC does not recommend routine fixed-interval changes for every patient. The 2016 Cochrane review found only three small trials totaling 107 participants and insufficient evidence for a universal replacement policy.<sup>[[7]](#ref7)[[8]](#ref8)</sup>

## Long-term surveillance and cancer risk

Catheterization does not eliminate the need to assess renal function, stones, bladder pressure or changing symptoms. AUA/SUFU advises interval catheter and site examination in NLUTD and urinary-tract imaging every 1–2 years for catheter users at risk of calculi, with broader follow-up guided by NLUTD risk category.<sup>[[9]](#ref9)</sup>

Chronic catheter use is associated with bladder cancer in observational cohorts, but the effect of the catheter cannot be cleanly separated from underlying disease and other exposures.

- **Ontario.** The population cohort (36,903 chronically catheterized patients matched to 110,709 controls) reported bladder cancer in 1.1% vs 0.3% (adjusted subdistribution HR 4.80, 95% CI 4.26–5.42) and cancer-specific death HR 8.68. Risk was highest beyond 2.9 years of catheterization. The study combined indwelling and intermittent catheterization and cannot give a suprapubic-specific estimate or a comparison with CIC.<sup>[[10]](#ref10)</sup>
- **Taiwan.** A registry study of 72,971 patients with chronic indwelling catheters reported 213.29 vs 40.4 bladder cancers per 100,000 person-years (adjusted HR 5.16). Another Taiwanese cohort reported HR 9.11 for chronic indwelling catheters without spinal cord injury versus a reference cohort.<sup>[[21]](#ref21)[[22]](#ref22)</sup>
- **Spinal cord injury.** In 3,670 patients with 21 bladder cancers, sole indwelling catheter use carried RR 4.9 (95% CI 1.3–13.8) versus non-indwelling methods.<sup>[[23]](#ref23)</sup> Biopsies in 208 patients with catheters for more than 8.5 years found malignancy in 17 (10 squamous cell carcinomas) and premalignant keratinizing squamous metaplasia or cystitis glandularis in 48 (23%).<sup>[[24]](#ref24)</sup>
- **Screening evidence.** A scoping review found that screening is often recommended in spinal cord injury guidelines, but the evidence for tools and protocols is lacking.<sup>[[25]](#ref25)</sup>

:::info[Screening is different from investigating symptoms]
AUA/SUFU recommends against routine screening or surveillance cystoscopy in asymptomatic NLUTD patients, including those with chronic indwelling catheters. A ten-year catheter duration alone does not create a routine cystoscopy and cytology schedule. Hematuria, recurrent infections, suspected anatomic abnormality or other concerning changes warrant targeted evaluation; known bladder cancer follows its own surveillance pathway.<sup>[[9]](#ref9)</sup>
:::

Do not routinely screen for or treat asymptomatic bacteriuria. Pregnancy and selected urologic procedures breaching the mucosa are exceptions. New fever, flank pain, systemic illness, persistent hematuria, abdominal pain, failure to drain, or autonomic dysreflexia requires assessment rather than attribution to ordinary catheter colonization.<sup>[[7]](#ref7)[[9]](#ref9)</sup>
