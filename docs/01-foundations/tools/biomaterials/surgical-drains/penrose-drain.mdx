# Penrose Drain

A **Penrose drain** is a flexible, open, passive wound drain that allows fluid to travel along its surfaces into a dressing or collection appliance. It has no suction reservoir. The Cardinal product line is latex and radiopaque; check the actual product label rather than assuming all Penrose-type drains have identical materials or imaging properties.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup>

The drain is named for Charles Bingham Penrose, an American gynecologist. It was the standard surgical drain for much of the twentieth century and has been largely replaced by closed-suction systems.<sup>[[4]](#ref4)</sup>

For broader indications and removal decisions, see [JP and wound-drain guidance](/docs/foundations/tools/biomaterials/surgical-drains/jackson-pratt). For a closed silicone channel drain, see [Blake Drain](/docs/foundations/tools/biomaterials/surgical-drains/blake-drain).

## Design

The Cardinal catalog lists sterile radiopaque latex Penrose drains in widths from 1/4 to 1 inch and lengths of 12, 18 and 36 inches.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup> The flat tube collapses when empty and is cut to length at the operation. One end lies in the wound or cavity and the other exits through the skin. Fluid moves along the tube surface by capillary action and gravity into a dressing, so the drain applies no negative pressure.

## When the design is useful

An open passive drain may suit a selected superficial wound or cavity that needs a maintained drainage route. Its usefulness depends on position, the wound opening, the fluid and continued drainage. It does not actively evacuate every recess, treat the underlying infection or replace debridement.

| Feature | Clinical implication |
|---|---|
| **Passive drainage** | No bulb activation is needed, but positioning, dressing care and patency still require attention. |
| **Open exit** | Drainage reaches the dressing or appliance; protect surrounding skin and monitor soiling. The wound is not isolated from external contamination. |
| **No measuring reservoir** | Precise output measurement is harder and may require a suitable collection appliance. Visual dressing assessment is not equivalent to a measured daily volume. |
| **Flexible tube** | Securement is needed to avoid inward migration or displacement. Softness does not remove the risks of retention, tissue injury or difficult removal. |
| **Material** | Check latex allergy and use an appropriate alternative when needed.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup> |

Neither design guarantees freedom from infection.

## Open versus closed drainage

| Feature | Penrose (open) | JP or Blake (closed) |
|---|---|---|
| Drainage mechanism | Gravity and capillary action | Negative pressure |
| Collection | Dressings, with no reservoir | Sealed bulb or canister |
| Barrier to external bacteria | None | Closed system |
| Output measurement | Not reliable | Measured in the reservoir |
| Drain-fluid creatinine for urine leak | Difficult, because the fluid mixes with skin and dressings | Direct sampling |

### Bacterial migration

In a rabbit splenectomy model, the skin near the drain exit was inoculated with *Streptococcus*. At 72 hours, none of the 20 undrained controls had positive intraperitoneal cultures. Cultures of the splenic bed were positive in 18 of 20 animals (90%) with a Penrose drain and 4 of 20 (20%) with a JP drain (P &lt; 0.001).<sup>[[5]](#ref5)</sup> A two-center prospective observational study across surgical divisions associated open drains with surgical site infection compared with closed drains (OR 3.68, 95% CI 1.88 to 6.89). The association varied with the surgical division, wound class and duration of surgery, and the study was not restricted to urologic operations.<sup>[[6]](#ref6)</sup> These data explain the decline of open drainage. They do not give a Penrose infection rate for a particular urologic operation.

## Sampling and monitoring

Assess wound appearance, pain, swelling, fever, drainage character and the adequacy of ongoing drainage. A sudden reduction in discharge with worsening swelling or systemic symptoms requires reassessment.

An open drain does **not** make culture or fluid chemistry impossible, but contaminated dressing fluid is an unreliable specimen. If testing is needed, obtain fresh fluid aseptically using a collection method agreed with the laboratory. IDSA/ASM cautions that cultures from long-standing drains, particularly after more than three days, can reflect colonization rather than the cause of infection.<sup>[[2]](#ref2)</sup>

Give the patient or caregiver explicit instructions for dressings, skin care, protecting the drain, recognizing displacement and contacting the treating team. Simpler hardware does not eliminate the need for education or follow-up.

## Urologic use

**Partial nephrectomy.** Some surgeons prefer a Penrose after open partial nephrectomy because they expect suction to prolong a urine leak from the collecting-system repair or to promote delayed hemorrhage. In a retrospective series of 197 partial nephrectomies (184 patients) at one center, with the drain chosen by the surgeon, no significant difference in urine leak or hemorrhage was found.<sup>[[7]](#ref7)</sup>

| Parameter | Closed suction (n = 123) | Penrose (n = 74) |
|---|---|---|
| Mean drainage duration | 7.8 days | 7.1 days |
| Prolonged urinary drainage | 8.9% | 5.4% |
| Wound infection or perinephric abscess | 2.4% | 5.4% |
| Delayed hemorrhage | 2.4% | 0% |

None of the differences was statistically significant. Randomized and observational data on omitting the drain altogether are summarized on the [JP drain page](/docs/foundations/tools/biomaterials/surgical-drains/jackson-pratt).

**Other settings.** An open drain may be chosen for a contaminated or infected open wound, such as after Fournier debridement, where a wick or open-cavity route is wanted. After radical prostatectomy or cystectomy, closed suction is the usual choice when a drain is placed.

## Complications

- **Retrograde bacterial migration and surgical site infection.** See the comparison above.<sup>[[5]](#ref5)[[6]](#ref6)</sup>
- **Loss of output measurement.** Output flows into dressings, so drain-fluid creatinine cannot be sampled reliably to assess a urine leak.
- **Skin maceration.** Drainage contacts the skin at the exit site.
- **Unpredictable drainage.** Flow depends on patient position, and an open passive drain can obstruct.
- **Latex allergy.** The Cardinal Penrose line is latex.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup>
- **Retained drain.** A drain sutured to surrounding tissue can be retained. Secure it so it cannot migrate inward, and confirm removal is complete.

Closed-suction systems generated maximal negative pressures of 71 to 175 mm Hg in a bench study, and stripping raised pressure further.<sup>[[8]](#ref8)</sup> A Penrose applies no suction to the tissue. It also needs no evacuator teaching and is easy to remove at the bedside.

## Removal and antibiotics

Set a removal plan based on the purpose of drainage, wound condition and clinical progress. Do not apply a universal calendar or a closed-suction output threshold to an open drain. WHO recommends removing wound drains when clinically appropriate; its timing evidence was limited and does not establish a Penrose-specific cutoff.<sup>[[3]](#ref3)</sup>

Remove the securing material and drain with attention to unexpected resistance, then confirm that the device is intact. Continued swelling, discharge or infection may require further assessment even after removal.

Do not continue prophylactic antibiotics solely because a drain remains. Antibiotics for an established infection follow the diagnosis, source-control assessment and microbiology.<sup>[[3]](#ref3)</sup>
