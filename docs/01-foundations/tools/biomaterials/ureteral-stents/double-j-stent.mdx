# Double-J (DJ) Ureteral Stent

A **double-J (DJ) stent** provides internal drainage from the renal pelvis to the bladder across a ureteral obstruction or healing repair. Its two pigtail curls help retain position; migration, obstruction and encrustation can still occur. Before placement, decide why the stent is needed, how its function will be checked, and who will remove or exchange it.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[2]](#ref2)</sup> Finney described the double-J catheter stent in 1978.<sup>[[14]](#ref14)</sup>

For device-specific long-term options, see [Metal & Long-Term Stents](/docs/foundations/tools/biomaterials/ureteral-stents/metal-long-term-stents). [Nephrostomy](/docs/foundations/tools/biomaterials/ureteral-stents/nephrostomy-tube) provides external drainage, while a [nephroureteral catheter](/docs/foundations/tools/biomaterials/ureteral-stents/nephroureteral-stent) retains an external access port.

## History

- **1967.** Davis and Zimskind described indwelling silicone ureteral tubing for internal drainage.<sup>[[15]](#ref15)</sup>
- **1972.** Robert Gibbons developed the first commercially available ureteral stent. It had collapsible "wings" against distal migration, a barium-impregnated proximal tip for radiographic visualization, a distal flange that limited proximal migration and trigonal irritation, and a tail to aid removal. Its introduction established CPT code 52332, still in use.<sup>[[15]](#ref15)</sup>
- **1978.** Finney introduced the self-retaining double-J stent, with a J curl at each end.<sup>[[14]](#ref14)</sup>
- **1984.** Andriole and colleagues reported 87 patients with indwelling double-J stents for temporary and permanent drainage.<sup>[[16]](#ref16)</sup>
- **Since the 1990s.** Materials (polyurethane, silicone, copolymers), coatings (hydrophilic, antibacterial, anti-encrustation) and designs (metallic, biodegradable, drug-eluting) have evolved.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[47]](#ref47)</sup>

## Selection and Placement

Choose the length, caliber, material and dwell limit of the actual product, accounting for ureteral length, obstruction, transplant or reconstructed anatomy, expected debris and tolerance. A smaller diameter can improve symptoms in some settings, but drainage and insertion requirements also matter. Height formulas and brand names do not establish an ideal length or a universal exchange interval.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[2]](#ref2)</sup>

**Diameter.** Adult stents range from about 4.7 to 8.5 Fr, and 6 Fr is common. In a prospective randomized comparison of 4.7, 6 and 7 Fr stents after ureteroscopy (181 patients), USSQ scores favored the smaller stent. Differences were significant in all subdomains only for 4.7 versus 7 Fr, and success of the second ureteroscopy exceeded 82% in every group.<sup>[[17]](#ref17)</sup>

**Length.** Adult stents are usually 22–28 cm, with shorter lengths for children. Two approaches are compared here, and the evidence conflicts on which is more accurate.

- **Height formula.** With a height of less than 178 cm, a 22 cm stent is used; 178–193 cm, 24 cm; above 193 cm, 26 cm. In 41 ureters with 8 Fr Percuflex stents, this formula gave an acceptable length in 61%, and no stent was too short. Direct ureteral measurement oversized the stent in 83%, and the authors suggested that a redundant ureter shortens under the stent.<sup>[[18]](#ref18)</sup>
- **CT or direct endoscopic measurement.** In 59 ureters, ureteral length on CT correlated with directly measured length (rho 0.63–0.64), whereas height correlated poorly (rho 0.18). Ideal position (both curls fully coiled, bladder curl not crossing midline) was reached in 100% when stent length matched measured ureteral length versus 70.9% when mismatched (p = 0.006).<sup>[[19]](#ref19)</sup>

| Material | Characteristics | Typical use |
|---|---|---|
| Polyurethane | Tunable stiffness and good radiopacity; stiffer, with more symptoms and faster encrustation | Short to medium term |
| Silicone | Softer and better tolerated; higher friction and lower radial strength | Longer term or sensitive patients |
| Hybrid copolymers (for example Percuflex, C-Flex) | Balance flexibility and encrustation resistance | General use |
| Metallic (for example Resonance, Memokath, Allium) | Resist extrinsic compression; higher cost and specialized placement | Malignant or chronic extrinsic obstruction |
| Biodegradable | Degrade without removal; investigational | Not in routine clinical use |

The material characteristics above are general descriptions of device classes; individual products differ, and the product's instructions for use govern dwell limits.

Polymer and silicone products differ in stiffness, coatings and permitted duration. The tightly coiled metallic Resonance is not a nitinol self-expanding segmental stent; its delivery method and 12-month maximum indwell are addressed in the metal-stent hub. No material guarantees freedom from biofilm or encrustation.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[3]](#ref3)</sup>

Retrograde placement usually uses cystoscopy, a guidewire and imaging; antegrade or combined access is useful when retrograde access fails. Confirm the wire and both curls are in their intended locations. Resistance requires reassessment rather than force. Avoid assuming that an apparently well-positioned stent is functionally draining a compressed ureter or a high-pressure bladder.<sup>[[2]](#ref2)[[4]](#ref4)</sup>

## When a Stent Helps

| Situation | Practical role |
|---|---|
| Sepsis with an obstructing stone | Urgent decompression by a stent or PCN, with immediate antibiotics and cultures; defer definitive stone treatment until sepsis resolves. EAU strongly recommends urgent decompression by percutaneous drainage or ureteral stenting when an obstructing stone causes sepsis. Choose the approach most likely to achieve timely drainage.<sup>[[5]](#ref5)</sup> |
| Benign or malignant obstruction | Bypass an obstruction when internal drainage is feasible. Consider PCN, a PCNU, selected long-term devices or reconstruction if access, patency or tolerance is inadequate.<sup>[[2]](#ref2)</sup> |
| Ureteral injury or reconstruction | Support drainage across a repair or selected partial injury. Complete injury, devitalized tissue and persistent leaks may require reconstruction or additional diversion; a stent is not a substitute for a viable, tension-free repair.<sup>[[6]](#ref6)</sup> |
| Ureteroscopy | Selective postoperative drainage or staged access, as below.<sup>[[5]](#ref5)</sup> |
| Kidney transplantation | Prophylactic anastomotic stenting reduces major urological complications; technique and removal timing remain transplant-specific.<sup>[[7]](#ref7)</sup> |

A prophylactic catheter can assist ureteral identification in a difficult pelvic operation, but it does not eliminate injury risk or replace visual identification and careful dissection.<sup>[[6]](#ref6)</sup>

## Stenting Around Ureteroscopy

The EAU 2026 guideline gives a strong recommendation not to insert a stent in uncomplicated cases. A stent is placed when complications or uncertainty warrant drainage (for example ureteral trauma, perforation, concerning bleeding, residual fragments or infection risk). The EAU evidence summary states that pre-stenting improves outcomes for renal stones (level 2a) but not for ureteral stone procedures, and that patients have stent symptoms while waiting. A stent followed by another attempt after 7–14 days is an option when ureteral access cannot be obtained safely.<sup>[[5]](#ref5)</sup>

Comparative evidence supports this balance. A 2016 meta-analysis of 17 studies (1,943 participants) found that unstented patients had more unplanned medical visits (OR 1.63, 95% CI 1.15–2.30) but less dysuria (RR 0.39, 95% CI 0.25–0.62).<sup>[[48]](#ref48)</sup> The 2019 Cochrane review judged that stenting may slightly reduce unplanned return visits (very low certainty), may leave secondary interventions unchanged (RR 1.15, 95% CI 0.39–3.33; low certainty), and is probably associated with similar pain on the day of surgery.<sup>[[49]](#ref49)</sup>

There is no universal best postoperative dwell time. The reason for drainage, injury, residual burden and planned second procedure govern the plan.<sup>[[5]](#ref5)</sup>

- A 2026 systematic review (32 studies, 4,373 patients) found that dwell times most often ranged from 10 to 14 days in uncomplicated cases, with longer dwell associated with more encrustation and removal difficulty. The authors suggested 10–14 days as a window balancing healing against encrustation.<sup>[[25]](#ref25)</sup>
- In a cohort of 4,437 procedures, emergency department visits were more likely when a string-stent dwell was 4 days or less in non-prestented patients; the authors recommended at least 5 days.<sup>[[8]](#ref8)</sup>
- In a 3-day versus 7-day removal comparison, the 3-day group reported better urinary, pain and general-health scores, with no significant difference in complications.<sup>[[9]](#ref9)</sup>

These findings do not justify a fixed 3-day, 5-day or 10–14-day rule for every patient.

## Kidney Transplantation: What the Evidence Establishes

| Evidence | Finding and limit |
|---|---|
| Cochrane 2024 | Twelve studies, 1,960 recipients. Major urological complications were reduced in 11 studies/1,834 recipients: RR 0.30 (95% CI 0.16–0.55), moderate certainty, with the benefit also seen for urine leak and ureteral obstruction separately. The benefit held for stenting of 14 days or less (RR 0.39, 0.21–0.72) and for longer stenting (RR 0.22, 0.08–0.61). The UTI effect remained uncertain: RR 1.32 (0.97–1.80), very low certainty. Longer stents (20 cm or more) left in place beyond 6 weeks had more encrustation and migration. The review calls for further trials to define the optimal duration.<sup>[[7]](#ref7)</sup> |
| DUET trial, 2025 | Single-center randomized comparison of external Single-J removed on day 9 versus internal Double-J removed at 3 weeks. PCN within 6 months occurred in 21/145 (14.5%) versus 7/155 (4.5%), respectively (p = 0.003). This compares two care protocols, including different duration and discharge rules. Reconstructed tracts and bladder dysfunction requiring catheterization were excluded.<sup>[[10]](#ref10)</sup> |
| Yin 2026 meta-analysis | Sixteen RCTs (2,486 patients) reported lower urine leak (RR 0.25, 95% CI 0.13–0.47) and ureteral obstruction or stricture (RR 0.42, 0.25–0.71) and more UTI (RR 1.41, 1.08–1.84) with stenting. These are Yin estimates from a larger study set than the 12-study Cochrane review, and should not be quoted as Cochrane results.<sup>[[7]](#ref7)[[11]](#ref11)</sup> |
| Patel 2017 RCT | Multicenter randomized comparison of early (day 5) versus late stent removal (227 randomized, 205 analyzed). Stent-related complications occurred in 6/79 (7.6%) with early versus 36/126 (28.6%) with late removal (p < 0.001), mostly UTI (7.6% vs 24.6%).<sup>[[12]](#ref12)</sup> |
| Amara 2026 NSQIP cohort | 3,407 recipients, 75.6% stented. Major urological complications were similar (2.4% unstented vs 2.5% stented). UTI was higher with stenting (7.4% vs 3.2%; adjusted OR 2.22, 1.48–3.43). This registry analysis is observational and suggests that selected patients may not need universal stenting.<sup>[[39]](#ref39)</sup> |

Shorter transplant stenting can reduce the UTI burden in appropriate protocols, but one early-removal trial does not establish day 5 as safe for every anastomosis. Coordinate removal with the transplant team, particularly after a leak, difficult implantation or impaired drainage.<sup>[[7]](#ref7)[[12]](#ref12)</sup>

## Symptoms and Complications

Frequency, urgency, dysuria, hematuria and flank discomfort are common. Fever, rigors, worsening flank pain, reduced urine output or persistent or heavy bleeding warrant assessment for infection, obstruction, migration or another complication, rather than automatic attribution to stent irritation.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[2]](#ref2)</sup>

In a prospective observational study of 232 patients who completed the USSQ after ureteroscopy with stenting, 59.1% had daytime frequency of at least once per hour, 90.1% had nocturia, 86.6% urgency and 82.3% burning. Pain was reported by 83.2% (kidney 67.9%, bladder area 31.3%), and it interfered with daily life in 92.2%. Of the patients, 62% would be dissatisfied if another stent were proposed, and younger patients and women were more affected. The study lacked baseline evaluation.<sup>[[20]](#ref20)</sup>

Stent pain is multifactorial. Reviews attribute it to mucosal irritation (including trigonal irritation from the bladder curl) and retrograde reflux of urine through the stent during voiding, with ureteral spasm also proposed.<sup>[[21]](#ref21)</sup>

### Medical management

Check position and drainage, then individualize symptom treatment. An α-blocker can reduce stent-related symptoms (level 1a), and EAU strongly recommends offering one to patients with stent-related symptoms. In a meta-analysis of five trials (461 patients), α-blockers lowered the USSQ urinary symptom score by a mean of 8.4 points (95% CI 5.6–11.1) and the body pain score by 7.2 (2.5–11.8); pain episodes occurred in 45% versus 76% (RR 0.59, 0.47–0.71).<sup>[[5]](#ref5)[[23]](#ref23)</sup> Pharmacology, dosing and hypotension precautions belong in the [alpha-blocker hub](/docs/foundations/pharmacology/voiding-outlet/alpha-blockers).

A Bayesian network meta-analysis of 26 randomized studies found that the most effective regimen differed by symptom domain:<sup>[[22]](#ref22)</sup>

| Symptom domain | Highest-ranked regimen |
|---|---|
| Urinary symptoms | Silodosin 8 mg plus solifenacin 10 mg |
| Pain | Silodosin 8 mg plus solifenacin 10 mg |
| Sexual performance | Tadalafil 5 mg |
| General health | Silodosin 8 mg plus solifenacin 10 mg plus tadalafil 5 mg |
| Work performance | Solifenacin 10 mg |

These are indirect rankings, and they do not establish one best regimen for all patients. In a 2024 randomized comparison, solifenacin 10 mg plus tamsulosin 0.4 mg reduced pain more than control (p = 0.001), and no single drug was clearly superior.<sup>[[24]](#ref24)</sup>

Bacteriuria or colonization alone does not establish symptomatic UTI. Manage infection and procedure-specific antimicrobial needs through the [UTI treatment](/docs/foundations/pharmacology/infection-prophylaxis/uti-treatment-antibiotics) and [perioperative prophylaxis](/docs/foundations/pharmacology/infection-prophylaxis/perioperative-antibiotic-prophylaxis) hubs. Planned mucosa-traumatizing endourologic procedures and pregnancy have separate bacteriuria recommendations.<sup>[[13]](#ref13)</sup>

## Removal, Exchange and Follow-Up

- Record side, product, size, insertion date, indication, removal or exchange deadline and responsible clinician in a stent register; give the patient the plan and a route to contact the service.
- Follow the device's maximum dwell limit and shorten the interval for encrustation, infection, obstruction or other clinical concerns. A permitted maximum is not an assurance of patency until that date.
- Removal may be cystoscopic or, for an appropriate device and patient, by an extraction string or another specified retrieval system. Cystoscopy is not universally required.
- Do not force removal of a retained or encrusted stent. Image and plan endoscopic or staged removal according to the burden and location of encrustation.
- Confirm resolution of the underlying drainage problem when clinically required. Removing the stent does not itself establish that a stricture or leak has healed.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[2]](#ref2)</sup>

### Dwell time and encrustation

In a European multicenter retrospective study of 473 removed stents (indwelling 60–679 days), one-third had been in place more than 6 months and 9% more than 1 year. For non-stone indications, clinically significant encrustation was 1.3% before 4 months and 5.2% before 6 months, rising to about 10% long term. For stone indications, it was 8% before 4 months and nearly 17% after.<sup>[[26]](#ref26)</sup> In another retrospective study, indwelling time beyond 45–90 days and beyond 90 days was associated with a higher encrustation grade.<sup>[[27]](#ref27)</sup> Encrustation risk factors include prolonged dwell, stone history, infection, pregnancy and cystinuria.<sup>[[33]](#ref33)[[26]](#ref26)</sup>

### Extraction strings and removal methods

Across 32 studies, removal was by rigid cystoscopy in 48.7%, flexible cystoscopy in 19.9%, extraction string in 23.5% and device-assisted methods in 7.9%. Flexible cystoscopy and string removal were associated with lower pain scores, and strings carried a small risk of premature dislodgement.<sup>[[25]](#ref25)</sup> A systematic review of 22 studies (8,382 patients, mostly after ureteroscopy) reported shorter dwell, less pain and cost savings with strings, and more than 90% of patients willing to remove the stent at home.<sup>[[28]](#ref28)</sup> In a prospective series of 168 patients, self-removal succeeded in 79%, stents dislodged early in 3.0% and the string detached in 1.8%.<sup>[[29]](#ref29)</sup> In a controlled trial, string removal about 10 days after ureteroscopy was less painful than cystoscopic removal (mean VAS 2.73 vs 5.67, p < 0.001); the difference was significant in men and not in women.<sup>[[30]](#ref30)</sup>

### Retained and forgotten stents

A forgotten stent is preventable. A 45-year-old woman with a stent retained for 6 years after open ureterolithotomy presented with a 4.2 cm vesical stone that required pneumatic lithotripsy.<sup>[[34]](#ref34)</sup> Management scales with the encrustation burden, and computerized stent tracking has been advocated.<sup>[[33]](#ref33)</sup> In a prospective series of 51 patients, a complete retrograde ureteroscopic approach with holmium laser stent cutting removed the stent in 98%, and laser transection was useful in 71%; mean operating time was 110 minutes and 10% had nonobstructive pyelonephritis.<sup>[[31]](#ref31)</sup> In 58 patients with encrusted stents (indwelling under 6 months in 22%, 6–12 months in 57%, over 12 months in 21%), all stents were removed. Ureteroscopy was used in 90%, and 10% needed second-stage PCNL or combined intrarenal surgery; the stone-free rate was 84% at 1 month and complications occurred in 10.5%.<sup>[[32]](#ref32)</sup>

## Metallic, Biodegradable and Drug-Eluting Stents

Metallic stents were developed for malignant ureteral obstruction, where polymeric stents can fail from extrinsic compression. Device-specific detail is in [Metal & Long-Term Stents](/docs/foundations/tools/biomaterials/ureteral-stents/metal-long-term-stents).

- **Resonance.** In 50 ureteral units in 42 patients with malignant obstruction after polymeric stent failure, the Resonance stent had a mean 4-month longer functional duration (p < 0.0001), and hydronephrosis resolved or was stable in 90%.<sup>[[35]](#ref35)</sup> In a single-center chronic-obstruction cohort, metal stents stayed in longer (228.6 vs 146.1 days) and needed fewer changes per year (1.4 vs 6.3), with no significant cost-per-day difference.<sup>[[37]](#ref37)</sup> In a 23-patient series with yearly exchange, all 3 failures occurred in 2 patients with malignant obstruction, and the authors limited their conclusion to patients without urolithiasis.<sup>[[38]](#ref38)</sup>
- **Covered metallic stent.** In a randomized comparison of 10 covered metallic and 12 double-J ureters for malignant obstruction, median patency was 239 versus 80 days (overall patency p = 0.041). The trial was very small.<sup>[[36]](#ref36)</sup>
- **Biodegradable stents.** These stents degrade after a set period and remove the need for retrieval. Materials under study include PLGA, PCL, PLA and PDO. Degradation timing, mechanical strength and transient obstruction from degradation products remain limitations, and clinical data are limited. A modified biodegradable mesh stent has been described in animal models.<sup>[[41]](#ref41)[[40]](#ref40)</sup>
- **Surface-modified and drug-eluting stents.** Hydrophilic coatings aim to reduce friction, and antibacterial and anti-encrustation coatings aim to reduce biofilm.<sup>[[46]](#ref46)</sup> In a randomized trial of 20 patients, a triclosan-eluting stent reduced flank, abdominal and urethral pain scores but had no significant effect on culture, biofilm or encrustation in short-term stenting.<sup>[[42]](#ref42)</sup> Antibacterial coatings (a propolis-derived coating eliminated more than 90% of attached biofilm in vitro and reduced infection and encrustation in an animal model), rapamycin-eluting and dual-drug silk fibroin/PDO stents remain preclinical.<sup>[[43]](#ref43)[[44]](#ref44)[[45]](#ref45)</sup>

## Reconstructive Use

In reconstruction the stent acts as an intraoperative scaffold. Dwell times below are common practice and vary by surgeon and procedure, not tested standards.

| Setting | Commonly used dwell |
|---|---|
| Ureteroneocystostomy, Boari flap, psoas hitch | About 4–6 weeks |
| Pyeloplasty | About 4–6 weeks |
| Buccal mucosa graft ureteroplasty, appendiceal interposition | About 6–8 weeks or longer |
| Kidney transplant anastomosis | Early removal at about day 5 has been tested in one RCT (above) |

After ureteral injury during pelvic, colorectal or gynecologic surgery, see the EAU trauma guideline.<sup>[[6]](#ref6)</sup>
