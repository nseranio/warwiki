# Percutaneous Nephrostomy (PCN)

A **percutaneous nephrostomy (PCN)** drains the renal collecting system through a catheter placed under image guidance through the flank and renal parenchyma. It provides decompression, access for subsequent intervention or diversion from a urinary leak. Technical success exceeds 90% overall in published series (see below). It is distinct from a [PCNU](/docs/foundations/tools/biomaterials/ureteral-stents/nephroureteral-stent), which also traverses the ureter, and an internal [double-J stent](/docs/foundations/tools/biomaterials/ureteral-stents/double-j-stent).<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[2]](#ref2)[[10]](#ref10)</sup>

## Choosing Drainage

Sepsis with an obstructing stone requires urgent decompression and immediate antibiotics. EAU 2026 supports either PCN or a ureteral stent; choose according to anatomy, clinical stability, access and available expertise. Definitive stone treatment waits until sepsis is controlled. Resuscitation and correction of dangerous electrolyte or coagulation abnormalities proceed alongside the drainage plan rather than creating an automatic delay.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[3]](#ref3)</sup>

Other uses include benign or malignant obstruction, ureteral injury or leak (diversion away from the injury), antegrade pyelography, and access for antegrade stenting, balloon dilation of strictures or endourology.<sup>[[8]](#ref8)[[10]](#ref10)</sup> There is no absolute contraindication when drainage is urgent. Uncorrectable severe coagulopathy, terminal illness in which drainage would not change the outcome, and uncorrected severe metabolic derangement such as hyperkalemia are relative concerns.<sup>[[9]](#ref9)</sup> Hydronephrosis alone does not determine the need for drainage. In advanced malignancy, discuss symptom relief, renal recovery, possible cancer treatment, tube burden and the patient's goals.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[2]](#ref2)</sup>

A 2024 review of 18 comparative studies and 1,228 patients with malignant obstruction found less procedure or intervention failure with PCN, but less displacement, shorter procedure time and shorter stay with retrograde stents. It did not establish one universally superior option. Studies of antegrade versus retrograde stent placement answer a different question from PCN versus stent drainage.<sup>[[4]](#ref4)</sup>

| Setting | Finding |
|---|---|
| Malignant obstruction, meta-analysis of 18 studies (1,228 patients) | PCN had lower procedure and intervention failure rates; retrograde stenting had less displacement, shorter procedure time and shorter stay. Infection, blockage and unplanned exchange rates did not differ.<sup>[[4]](#ref4)</sup> |
| Malignant obstruction, antegrade versus retrograde stent placement (retrospective, 61 antegrade and 76 retrograde placements) | Technical success 98.4% antegrade and 47.4% retrograde. Retrograde failure was associated with age of 65 years or more, ureteral orifice invasion and extrinsic cancer.<sup>[[16]](#ref16)</sup> |
| Acute obstructive uropathy, meta-analysis of 7 trials (667 patients) | No difference in septic parameters, quality of life, failure rates, pain or analgesic use. PCN was associated with less hematuria and dysuria and a longer hospital stay.<sup>[[17]](#ref17)</sup> |
| Malignant obstruction, quality of life (74 PCN, 30 tandem stents) | Similar quality-of-life and tube-symptom scores. Of 13 patients who had used both, 11 preferred tandem ureteral stents.<sup>[[18]](#ref18)</sup> |

A PCN has a larger bore than a stent, which helps drain pus or debris, decompresses immediately in septic obstruction, allows exchange, irrigation and nephrostogram, and needs no cystoscopy, so it can be placed in a septic or bed-bound patient.

## Planning and Safe Access

| Check | Purpose |
|---|---|
| **Imaging and route** | Review collecting-system anatomy, kidney position, intervening bowel, pleura and vessels; altered anatomy or a nondilated system may require a different imaging approach. |
| **Bleeding risk** | Check blood count, coagulation and antithrombotics; agree correction/interruption with the procedural team, balancing urgency and thrombosis risk. |
| **Infection** | Obtain relevant cultures and distinguish treatment of an infected obstructed system from procedural prophylaxis. |
| **Catheter and future plan** | Choose caliber, retention mechanism and calyceal access for drainage contents and possible later antegrade intervention. |

These planning decisions are addressed in CIRSE's 2026 standards of practice; these are technical practice standards rather than a systematic clinical guideline.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup>

Use image-guided access through an appropriate calyx and verify intraluminal wire and catheter position. Posterior calyceal access is intended to reduce vascular injury; the exact calyx and angle are adapted to the kidney and the planned intervention (see Technique). Supracostal access has pleural risks. In an infected system, avoid unnecessary manipulation and pressurized distension.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup>

Drug choice depends on local susceptibility, prior cultures, allergy and renal function. Ceftriaxone does not provide reliable Enterococcus coverage; it should not be described as covering every listed uropathogen. Use the [UTI antibiotic](/docs/foundations/pharmacology/infection-prophylaxis/uti-treatment-antibiotics), [perioperative prophylaxis](/docs/foundations/pharmacology/infection-prophylaxis/perioperative-antibiotic-prophylaxis) and [antithrombotic workflow](/docs/foundations/perioperative-care/perioperative-protocols/antithrombotic-therapy) companions for agent and interruption decisions.<sup>[[5]](#ref5)</sup>

## Technique

Ultrasound guidance for initial access is combined with fluoroscopy for wire and catheter manipulation.<sup>[[8]](#ref8)[[10]](#ref10)</sup>

1. **Position.** The patient is prone or prone-oblique.
2. **Entry site.** Entry is ideally subcostal (below the 12th rib when feasible, and not above the 11th rib unless other routes are exhausted) and lateral to the paraspinous muscles.<sup>[[8]](#ref8)[[9]](#ref9)</sup>
3. **Calyx.** A posterior calyx is punctured through the relatively avascular zone between the anterior and posterior renal arterial territories (Brödel's line), at a 20° to 30° posterolateral oblique angle, with a single-wall puncture of the calyx.<sup>[[8]](#ref8)[[9]](#ref9)</sup>
4. **Seldinger sequence.** A needle, a guidewire coiled in the renal pelvis, serial dilation and a self-retaining catheter are used. Contrast placed over the wire confirms an intracollecting-system position. A safety wire is recommended for complex manipulations so that access to an obstructed kidney is not lost.<sup>[[8]](#ref8)[[9]](#ref9)</sup>
5. **Nondilated systems.** Access is more demanding. A double-puncture technique with intravenous contrast and air or carbon dioxide distension was successful in 25 of 26 kidneys in one series.<sup>[[11]](#ref11)</sup>
6. **Infected system.** Avoid overdistension and unnecessary prolonged manipulation.<sup>[[9]](#ref9)</sup>

Supracostal access reaches upper-pole calyces but carries pleural risk, including pneumothorax and [nephropleural fistula](/docs/clinical-conditions/03f-fistulas/all-patients/nephropleural).

**Reported technical success.**

| System | Success | Source |
|---|---|---|
| Dilated system | 96.4% (389 procedures); 98.2% primary (500 procedures) | <sup>[[13]](#ref13)[[12]](#ref12)</sup> |
| Nondilated system | 82.7% and 82% in the same two series; 96% with the double-puncture technique (26 kidneys) | <sup>[[13]](#ref13)[[12]](#ref12)[[11]](#ref11)</sup> |
| All comers | 94.6% primary (389 procedures); 97% (765 PCNs by urologists); more than 90% in a review | <sup>[[13]](#ref13)[[14]](#ref14)[[10]](#ref10)</sup> |

## Complications That Change Management

- **Obstruction, kinking or displacement.** New low output, flank pain, leakage or rising creatinine requires reassessment. Do not assume pericatheter leakage means the tube is draining adequately.
- **Infection or sepsis.** Assess the patient and tube function, culture appropriately, begin treatment and arrange source control when needed.
- **Bleeding.** Mild early hematuria can occur. Persistent, recurrent or heavy bleeding, hemodynamic change or falling hemoglobin warrants investigation for vascular injury; selective embolization may be needed. Balloon tamponade of the tract can temporize active bleeding.<sup>[[9]](#ref9)</sup>
- **Bowel or pleural injury.** Obtain urgent specialist assessment and imaging. Catheter removal, repositioning or retention depends on the injury and drainage needs; blind withdrawal is not a universal solution. One described approach for a transcolonic tract is to secure urinary drainage, treat with antibiotics and leave the catheter in the colon as a drain until the tract matures.
- **Dislodgement.** Contact the drainage service urgently. Tract age and anatomy determine whether image-guided recannulation is possible; a fixed “7–10 days means mature” rule is unsafe.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[6]](#ref6)</sup>

Published complication frequencies vary with indication and population. A review reports about 10% major and minor complications together and 4% to 5% major complications.<sup>[[10]](#ref10)</sup> Individual series report major complications in 0.45% (500 procedures), 3.89% (667 patients) and 9.6% (389 procedures, with more in nondilated systems), and minor complications in 9.1% to 14.2%.<sup>[[12]](#ref12)[[14]](#ref14)[[13]](#ref13)</sup> Quality-improvement action thresholds are not the same as an observed complication rate or an individual patient's risk.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup>

Dislodgement is the commonest long-term problem. In 147 patients with malignant obstruction and long-term PCN (229 renal units), the tube was replaced for dislodgement in 76% of renal units and pyelonephritis occurred at least once in 41.5% of patients. Follow-up time, BMI, diabetes, chemotherapy and catheter-related skin infection were associated with complications.<sup>[[15]](#ref15)</sup>

## Ongoing Care and Exchange

Secure the catheter without traction, keep the prescribed drainage arrangement functioning, and teach the patient to recognize leakage, reduced output, fever and displacement. Monitor fluid balance after relief of obstruction because post-obstructive diuresis may require replacement and electrolyte monitoring. Flushing should follow the prescribed device or service plan; resistance or pain should prompt reassessment rather than force.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[6]](#ref6)</sup>

Set an individualized exchange date. CIRSE 2026 describes an initial 6–8-week interval, extendable to 8–12 weeks if encrustation is not a problem; some patients need earlier changes. Pregnancy increases encrustation risk and commonly requires shorter intervals. Product instructions, catheter performance and clinical circumstances take precedence over a universal 3-month rule. Exchange does not sterilize a chronically colonized urinary tract.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[3]](#ref3)</sup>

Asymptomatic bacteriuria is common, not inevitable, and does not usually require antibiotics solely because a tube is present. Pregnancy and planned endourologic procedures involving mucosal trauma are important exceptions to a blanket “never treat” statement; device manipulation also requires its own procedural assessment.<sup>[[6]](#ref6)[[7]](#ref7)</sup>

A PCN may be temporarily capped in a planned trial when internal drainage is being assessed, including after ureteral stent placement. Give explicit instructions about symptoms that require reconnecting drainage and contacting the team. Removal requires evidence that drainage or diversion is no longer needed; do not equate a cap trial with proof of native ureteral healing.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup>

## Special Situations

- **Pregnancy.** Coordinate obstetric and urologic care and minimize radiation. Both PCN and stenting remain drainage options; ureteroscopy is another option in selected patients.<sup>[[3]](#ref3)</sup>
- **Children.** Use age-appropriate equipment and pediatric expertise; adult catheter sizes and outcome estimates do not transfer automatically.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup>
- **Long-term PCN in cancer patients.** Dislodgement, obstruction and pyelonephritis are frequent (see above), so plan tube changes and teach the patient to recognize failure.<sup>[[15]](#ref15)</sup>
- **Suspected upper-tract urothelial malignancy.** Include the tumor and access route in drainage planning; urgent decompression and subsequent oncologic management require coordinated decisions.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup>
