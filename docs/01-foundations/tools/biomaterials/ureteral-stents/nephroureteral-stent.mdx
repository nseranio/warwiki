# Percutaneous Nephroureteral Catheter (PCNU)

A **percutaneous nephroureteral catheter (PCNU)** — also called an **internal/external nephroureteral tube** — is a drainage catheter that extends from the renal collecting system, through the ureter, and into the bladder, with an external component exiting through the flank. Unlike a standard [PCN](/docs/foundations/tools/biomaterials/ureteral-stents/nephrostomy-tube) (drains only externally) or a fully internalized [double-J stent](/docs/foundations/tools/biomaterials/ureteral-stents/double-j-stent), the PCNU provides **dual drainage** — internally (antegrade through the ureter to the bladder) **and** externally (through the skin to a collection bag) — and uniquely allows a **capping trial** to assess obstruction resolution before tube removal.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup>

## Design

| Catheter | Tip | Drainage | External component |
|---|---|---|---|
| Standard PCN | Renal pelvis | External only | Always |
| **PCNU** | Bladder (via ureter) | **Internal + external** | When uncapped |
| Double-J stent | Renal pelvis and bladder coils | Internal only | Never |

The PCNU is a long catheter (often &gt; 60 cm) with sideports along the shaft, a retention pigtail in the bladder, and an external hub at the flank that can be capped or connected to a drainage bag.

## Indications

PCNU shares many indications with PCN, but is specifically preferred when:<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[2]](#ref2)</sup>

1. **Ureteral obstruction** with planned capping trial to assess resolution before definitive tube removal.
2. **Ureteral leaks and fistulas** — diverts urine away from the injury while splinting the ureter. Surgical ureteral repair failure rates are **13% with PCN / PCNU vs 87% without**.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup>
3. **Renal transplant ureteral complications** — leaks, fistulas, strictures, obstructions.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup>
4. **Intractable hemorrhagic cystitis** — Spradling case series (n = 6): PCNU diversion produced **stable hematocrit, no further transfusions within 30 days, and no clot obstruction of the tube** in all patients.<sup>[[2]](#ref2)</sup>
5. **Bridge to internalization** — PCNU followed by conversion to a fully internalized DJ stent or transileal retrograde nephroureteral catheter (e.g., in ileal-conduit patients).<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup>
6. **Access for endourologic procedures** — antegrade stenting, balloon dilation, stone treatment.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[3]](#ref3)</sup>

## Advantages Over Standard PCN

- **Internal drainage when capped** — patient can void normally; enables a capping trial.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup>
- **Dual-drainage safety** — if the internal pathway becomes obstructed (tumor progression, edema), the external limb is a safety valve.
- **Ureteral splinting** — the indwelling segment maintains ureteral patency across strictures, anastomoses, or sites of injury.
- **Bridge to fully internalized stenting** — existing access used for guidewire exchange.

## Disadvantages

- **Patient discomfort** — often not well tolerated long-term due to skin irritation and external-component burden; fully internalized DJ stents are preferred when feasible.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup>
- **Dislodgement risk** higher than simple PCN due to longer catheter.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[4]](#ref4)</sup>
- **Bladder irritation** — distal-tip irritative LUTS similar to a DJ stent.<sup>[[5]](#ref5)</sup>
- **External bag** when uncapped limits mobility; PCN / PCNU patients report more self-care difficulties than those with internalized stents.<sup>[[6]](#ref6)</sup>

## Placement Technique

Fluoroscopic guidance; two-stage is most common.<sup>[[3]](#ref3)[[7]](#ref7)</sup>

### Two-Stage (Standard)

1. **Stage 1** — standard PCN access via ultrasound + fluoroscopy / Seldinger technique.
2. **Stage 2** (1–2 weeks later) — through the existing tract, a guidewire is advanced antegrade across the renal pelvis, down the ureter, across the obstruction / injury, and into the bladder. The tract is dilated, and the PCNU is advanced over the wire until the distal pigtail coils in the bladder and the proximal end exits the flank.

### Single-Stage ("First-Hand")

Arslan 2024 comparison: first-hand single-session approach had equivalent technical success (93.1%) with **shorter fluoroscopy time (6.8 vs 8.2 min)** and a **3-fold lower complication rate** vs the nephrostomy-route approach.<sup>[[7]](#ref7)</sup>

### Technical Considerations

- **Stiff guidewire** (e.g., Amplatz super-stiff) to navigate the ureter and provide support.<sup>[[8]](#ref8)</sup>
- Torque-control catheters and hydrophilic wires help with ureteral tortuosity or kinking.
- Adult catheter size typically **8–12 Fr**.<sup>[[3]](#ref3)</sup>
- Confirmation of distal-tip position in the bladder via contrast injection.

## Capping Trial — Unique to PCNU

A signature use case:<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup>

1. External port is **capped**, forcing all urine to drain internally through the ureter.
2. Patient monitored **24–48 h** for flank pain, fever, ↓ urine output, or peri-site leakage.
3. **Tolerated** → obstruction considered resolved; catheter can be removed.
4. **Symptomatic** → uncap to restore external drainage; reassess.

## Complications

| Complication | Note |
|---|---|
| Catheter dislodgement / migration | Most common long-term issue; **higher than PCN** due to length; 3–17% range<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[4]](#ref4)</sup> |
| Encrustation / blockage | Risk rises with dwell; significant after 45–90 days; biofilm<sup>[[9]](#ref9)[[10]](#ref10)</sup> |
| UTI / bacteriuria | 4–17%; **asymptomatic bacteriuria is universal and should not be treated**<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[5]](#ref5)</sup> |
| Bladder irritation | Frequency / urgency / dysuria from the distal tip in the bladder<sup>[[6]](#ref6)</sup> |
| Skin site infection / irritation | ~2–3%; a major source of long-term intolerance<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[6]](#ref6)</sup> |
| Hemorrhage | Transient hematuria nearly universal; transfusion-requiring ~4%<sup>[[2]](#ref2)</sup> |
| Urine leak around catheter | 2–10%<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup> |
| Sepsis | ~4% overall; up to 10% in pyonephrosis<sup>[[6]](#ref6)</sup> |

## Maintenance &amp; Exchange

- **Recommended exchange interval ~3 months** to limit encrustation, biofilm, and infection.<sup>[[4]](#ref4)[[11]](#ref11)[[12]](#ref12)</sup>
- **Real-world data** (Moon 2024 MarketScan analysis): **41% of exchanges occur within 29 days, 67% within 59 days** — many patients require earlier exchange than the 90-day standard.<sup>[[12]](#ref12)</sup>
- Exchange performed under fluoroscopy; existing catheter removed over a wire and replaced.
- Monitor catheter function (output), creatinine, and US for hydronephrosis.<sup>[[13]](#ref13)</sup>

## Special Clinical Scenarios

### Ileal Conduit Patients

Fully internalized DJ stents tend to occlude rapidly from mucus production. The preferred approach is PCNU placement followed by fluoroscopically guided conversion to a **transileal retrograde nephroureteral catheter** — technical success **91.8%**, clinical success **89.8%** of renal units.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup> Chronic retrograde transileal ureteric stents need shorter routine maintenance intervals (median 8.5 wk) than conventional PCN (12.9 wk) but have **lower rates of non-routine interventions** due to fewer dislodgements (Dawson 2026).<sup>[[14]](#ref14)</sup>

### Hemorrhagic Cystitis

PCNU is particularly useful because the catheter traverses the **entire ureter**, ensuring complete urinary diversion from the bladder while draining both kidneys. Unlike PCN, it is **not susceptible to clot obstruction** in this setting (Spradling).<sup>[[2]](#ref2)</sup>

### Malignant Ureteral Obstruction

PCNU can serve as a bridge to antegrade DJ stent placement — antegrade technical success **98.4%** vs **47.4%** for retrograde in MUO.<sup>[[7]](#ref7)[[15]](#ref15)</sup> Long-term PCNU is often poorly tolerated, so conversion to a fully internalized stent is preferred when possible.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup>

## PCNU vs Standard PCN vs DJ Stent

| Feature | PCN | **PCNU** | DJ Stent |
|---|---|---|---|
| Drainage route | External only | **Internal + external** | Internal only |
| **Capping trial** | No | **Yes** | N/A |
| External bag required | Always | Only when uncapped | No |
| Patient comfort | Moderate | Lower (skin + bladder symptoms) | Higher |
| Dislodgement risk | Moderate | **Higher** (longer catheter) | Lower |
| Ureteral splinting | No | Yes | Yes |
| Exchange access | Percutaneous | Percutaneous | Cystoscopic |
| **Ideal for ureteral leak / fistula** | Partial (diverts but doesn't splint) | **Optimal** (diverts + splints) | Good if retrograde access possible |
