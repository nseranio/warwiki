# Percutaneous Nephroureteral Catheter (PCNU)

A **PCNU** connects the renal collecting system to the bladder or urinary diversion through the ureter, while retaining a catheter segment and hub outside the flank. The external port can be connected to a bag or capped for internal drainage. Capping removes the bag requirement in selected patients; it does not remove the external catheter.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[2]](#ref2)</sup>

## Distinguish the Devices

| Device | Course and access | Meaning of capping |
|---|---|---|
| [PCN](/docs/foundations/tools/biomaterials/ureteral-stents/nephrostomy-tube) | Flank to renal collecting system; does not itself bridge the ureter | A planned trial may assess drainage through the native ureter or a separate internal stent. |
| **PCNU** | Flank to collecting system and through the ureter to bladder/conduit | Urine can drain internally through the catheter; the external hub remains. |
| [Double-J stent](/docs/foundations/tools/biomaterials/ureteral-stents/double-j-stent) | Fully internal ureteral drainage | No external drainage hub to cap; removal method depends on the device. |
| **Retrograde transileal catheter** | From a conduit stoma retrograde to the kidney | External access/drainage is at the stoma, not the flank. |

Catheter length, side-hole locations and retention loops differ by product. In a ureteral leak, side holes must not direct urine into the injured segment or extraluminal space.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[2]](#ref2)</sup>

**Advantages over a standard PCN.**

- **Internal drainage when capped.** The patient can void, and a capping trial becomes possible.
- **A second route.** If the internal pathway obstructs (tumor progression, edema), the external limb can be reopened.
- **Ureteral splinting.** The indwelling segment supports the ureter across a stricture, anastomosis or injury.
- **Access for later steps.** The existing tract and wire route can be used for exchange or conversion to a fully internal stent.

**Disadvantages.**

- **External hub and skin care.** Skin irritation and the external component are a burden, and a fully internal double-J stent is preferred when feasible. In a meta-analysis of PCN versus double-J stents, PCN patients reported more difficulty with self-care (RR 3.07).<sup>[[12]](#ref12)</sup>
- **Bladder symptoms.** A distal tip in the bladder produces stent-type irritative symptoms.<sup>[[13]](#ref13)</sup>
- **Dislodgement and blockage.** Catheter displacement, occlusion and site inflammation account for most complications after nephrostomy and catheter changes (displacement 3.57%, occlusion 1.73%, hematuria 1.16%, UTI 0.87% and skin inflammation 5.3% of 1,036 procedures in one series that included PCN, stent placement and exchanges).<sup>[[11]](#ref11)</sup>

## When PCNU Is Useful

Consider PCNU when the ureter can be crossed and ongoing external access is valuable: obstruction, selected leaks or fistulas, transplant ureteral complications, planned antegrade interventions, or a trial before conversion to an internal stent. It is an option rather than the preferred device for every ureteral injury.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[2]](#ref2)</sup> Ureteral leaks and fistulas are drained while the ureter is splinted, and the device can serve as a bridge to a double-J stent or, in an ileal conduit, to a retrograde transileal catheter.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[2]](#ref2)</sup>

Evaluate the receiving bladder or conduit. A poorly emptying or high-pressure bladder may compromise internal drainage and requires its own management. “Neurogenic bladder” alone does not describe storage pressure or establish an absolute contraindication to every internal stent.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup>

For an infected obstructed system, prompt decompression is the priority. An initial PCN with later crossing and internalization may be appropriate when sepsis, bleeding, severe obstruction or tortuosity makes prolonged manipulation undesirable. Selected patients can undergo a single-session procedure; there is no universal mandatory 1–2-week staging interval.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[3]](#ref3)</sup>

## Placement and Exchange Principles

Plan access with imaging and the intended future intervention in mind. Establish and confirm intraluminal wire passage across the lesion and into the receiving bladder or conduit before advancing the catheter. Hydrophilic wires and stiff support wires have different roles; neither should be forced through an uncertain path. Confirm the drainage side holes and retention portions are correctly positioned, and secure the external segment.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[2]](#ref2)</sup>

Placement is usually staged. A standard PCN is placed first and, through the existing tract, a guidewire is advanced across the renal pelvis, the ureter and the lesion into the bladder; the tract is dilated and the PCNU is advanced over the wire until the distal pigtail forms in the bladder and the proximal end exits the flank. A stiff support wire and hydrophilic or torque-control wires help with tortuosity. Contrast confirms the distal tip in the bladder.<sup>[[2]](#ref2)</sup>

A single-session approach has been compared with the staged route for antegrade double-J stents (not PCNU). In that retrospective series, technical success was 93.1% in both groups, fluoroscopy time was 6.8 minutes with the first-hand approach and 8.2 minutes through a nephrostomy, and the complication risk was 3.08 times higher through the nephrostomy route (p = 0.047). Patients with urinary malignancy were more often stented through a nephrostomy. These data do not prove that one PCNU technique has fewer complications; patient selection and the reason for initial nephrostomy matter.<sup>[[4]](#ref4)</sup>

## Capping Trials: What They Can and Cannot Show

A trial should have an explicit purpose, monitoring plan and response to symptoms. Confirm that internal drainage is appropriate, then monitor pain, fever, pericatheter leakage, voiding or output and renal function as indicated. If symptoms occur, reconnect to drainage as instructed and contact the treating team; persistent symptoms need urgent reassessment.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[2]](#ref2)</sup>

:::warning[Capping is not proof that the ureteral obstruction has resolved]
A capped PCNU still bypasses the ureteral lesion. Tolerance may support conversion to an internal stent, but does not by itself show that the native ureter can drain without a catheter. Removal requires a separate assessment of the underlying obstruction or leak. The capped port is also not an automatic pressure-release valve; it must be deliberately reopened when external drainage is needed.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[2]](#ref2)</sup>
:::

Capping trials are not unique to PCNU: a PCN may also be capped when native or separately stented internal drainage is being assessed. Duration and testing depend on the clinical situation rather than a universal 24–48-hour removal rule.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[2]](#ref2)</sup>

## Maintenance and Problems

Arrange image-guided exchange according to the device and local service, shortening the interval for blockage, encrustation or infection. The historical PCNU technique review describes starting at 6–8 weeks, with extension toward 12 weeks in patients without encrustation. This is practice guidance, not a trial-established optimum.<sup>[[2]](#ref2)</sup> In a claims analysis of 19,689 nephrostomy exchanges (PCN, not PCNU specifically), 41% occurred within 29 days, 67% within 59 days and 81% within 89 days, so many patients need earlier exchange than a 3-month schedule.<sup>[[10]](#ref10)</sup>

Leakage, falling output, flank pain or rising creatinine can indicate blockage, migration or malposition. Fever or systemic illness requires infection assessment and attention to drainage. Persistent or substantial bleeding needs investigation. External securement and patient education remain important even while capped.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[5]](#ref5)</sup>

Bacteriuria alone is not synonymous with UTI. Follow the [infection treatment](/docs/foundations/pharmacology/infection-prophylaxis/uti-treatment-antibiotics) and [procedural prophylaxis](/docs/foundations/pharmacology/infection-prophylaxis/perioperative-antibiotic-prophylaxis) companions; pregnancy and mucosa-traumatizing endourologic procedures require specific assessment.<sup>[[6]](#ref6)</sup>

## Selected Scenarios and Evidence Limits

**Ileal conduit:** a retrograde transileal catheter can provide accessible drainage into the ostomy appliance and avoid a flank tube. Internal Double-J drainage is also used. Access anatomy, mucus, patency, symptoms and maintenance burden guide choice; neither route is universally superior.<sup>[[2]](#ref2)</sup> In a retrospective comparison of 30 patients with chronic retrograde transileal stents and 71 with PCN, renal function change did not differ. Retrograde stents needed fewer nonroutine interventions (5.9% vs 20.1%) but shorter routine exchange intervals (median 8.5 vs 12.9 weeks at 3 years, because of encrustation), and had more moderate to severe adverse events (30% vs 4.2%), mostly dislodgements that needed a new PCN.<sup>[[9]](#ref9)</sup>

**Refractory gross hematuria.** In six patients with hematuria refractory to irrigation and clot evacuation, hematocrit remained stable, no transfusion was needed over 30 days, no PCNU was obstructed by clot, and the bladder and both kidneys were adequately drained. This is limited salvage evidence from a small retrospective series and does not establish that PCNU is clot-proof.<sup>[[7]](#ref7)</sup>

**Malignant obstruction.** Assess drainage feasibility, prognosis, treatment goals and patient preference. The PCN-versus-retrograde-stent meta-analysis compares PCN, not PCNU.<sup>[[8]](#ref8)</sup>

## PCN, PCNU and Double-J Stent Compared

| Feature | PCN | **PCNU** | Double-J stent |
|---|---|---|---|
| Drainage route | External only | Internal and external | Internal only |
| Capping trial | Possible only with a separate internal stent or native drainage | Yes | Not applicable |
| External bag | Always | Only when uncapped | No |
| Ureteral splinting | No | Yes | Yes |
| Exchange access | Percutaneous | Percutaneous | Cystoscopic or device-specific |
| Main burden | External tube and dislodgement | External hub, skin care and bladder symptoms | Stent symptoms |
