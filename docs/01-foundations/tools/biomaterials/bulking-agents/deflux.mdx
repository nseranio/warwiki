# Deflux — Dextranomer / Hyaluronic Acid

**Deflux** is a dextranomer/hyaluronic-acid implant for endoscopic treatment of vesicoureteral reflux (VUR) grades II–IV in children under US labeling. It was first FDA approved in 2001.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup> The current US product information is maintained by Teleflex; the supplied implant's instructions remain the reference for eligibility, preparation and administration.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[2]](#ref2)</sup>

Its purpose is to improve ureteral coaptation. Radiographic reflux resolution, freedom from febrile infection and preservation of renal function are different outcomes and should be reported separately.

## Material and Mechanism

The sterile gel contains dextranomer 50 mg/mL in stabilized non-animal hyaluronic acid 15 mg/mL. Microspheres range from 80–250 μm. Injection near the ureteral orifice supports the distal ureter, and host connective tissue subsequently surrounds the microspheres. The implant is biodegradable, but visible deposits can persist and calcify.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup>

“Non-animal” does not mean allergy is impossible. The label warns against injection in patients with known allergy to hyaluronic-acid products, streptococcal proteins or dextran. Inflammation, migration and obstruction can occur despite particle size and biocompatibility.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup>

## Contraindications and Selection

:::danger[Current US label contraindications]
Do not use in patients with nonfunctional kidney(s), Hutch diverticulum, ureterocele, active voiding dysfunction, ongoing UTI, or primary refluxing megaureters with distal stenosis. Do not inject Deflux intravascularly; the label places this instruction under WARNINGS because vascular occlusion may occur.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup>
:::

The label separately identifies uncertainty in children younger than one year, treatment of duplex systems, and use of more than 6 mL in one session (3 mL at each ureteral orifice). Grossly dilated ureteral orifices may make a patient unsuitable. Observational reports of treatment in complex anatomy do not remove these restrictions or make grades I and V part of the US approved indication.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup>

The EAU 2026 guideline (limited update, March 2026) gives a weak recommendation to select management from renal scars, clinical course, reflux grade, ipsilateral renal function, bilaterality, bladder function, associated anomalies, age and sex, compliance and parental preference. It gives a strong recommendation to offer reimplantation for persistent high-grade reflux and endoscopic correction for lower grades, and a weak recommendation to offer either for frequent breakthrough infections. It states that the lack of robust prospective trials limits the strength of these recommendations. It strongly recommends investigating every child for lower urinary tract dysfunction and treating it first when found, and managing children presenting at age one to five years conservatively at first.<sup>[[3]](#ref3)</sup>

## Administration Principles

Deflux is injected under cystoscopic guidance by a trained surgeon. The US IFU describes STING, HIT and double HIT. It recommends a 3.7 Fr, 23-gauge, 350 mm needle through a pediatric cystoscope with at least a 4 Fr working channel and a semi-filled bladder. For HIT, the needle enters the submucosa of the mid to distal ureteral tunnel about 4 mm at the 6 o'clock position; 0.5 to 1.0 mL usually creates a sufficient bolus, the needle is held 15 to 30 seconds to prevent extrusion, and the endpoint is an orifice that no longer hydrodistends.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup>

- **STING.** Subureteric injection supporting the ureteral orifice.
- **HIT.** Intraureteric submucosal injection after hydrodistention identifies the ureteral tunnel.
- **Double HIT.** A second, more distal intraureteric injection when needed to complete coaptation.

Six observational comparisons pooled in a 2016 review reported radiographic resolution in 82.5% of HIT-treated versus 71.4% of STING-treated ureters.<sup>[[4]](#ref4)</sup> The frequently cited “92% use double HIT” figure came from 50 respondents to a 2012 survey, with an 18% response rate; it is not a contemporary census of US practice.<sup>[[5]](#ref5)</sup>

Keep the product sterile, do not mix it with other products or resterilize it, and document the agent and injection history. Provide the implant card: Deflux is radiographically opaque and can be mistaken for distal ureteral calculi.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup>

## Major Comparative Evidence

### Swedish Reflux Trial: Three Different Endpoints

This open randomized trial enrolled 203 children aged one to younger than two years with grade III–IV reflux: 69 assigned to antibiotic prophylaxis, 66 to endoscopic treatment and 68 to surveillance. Children in the injection arm continued prophylaxis until postoperative imaging showed absent or nondilating reflux; it was not an injection-without-antibiotics strategy.<sup>[[6]](#ref6)[[7]](#ref7)[[8]](#ref8)</sup>

| Outcome | Finding | Interpretation |
|---|---|---|
| Reflux after two years | Resolution or downgrading to grades I–II was reported in 71% after endoscopy, 39% with prophylaxis and 47% with surveillance. Only 52/66 children assigned to endoscopy completed the two-year VCUG. | The 71% is not complete reflux eradication or a fully observed all-randomized result. Recurrent dilating reflux after initial improvement was reported in 13/66 children assigned to endoscopy (20%) at two years.<sup>[[6]](#ref6)</sup> |
| Febrile UTI in girls | Recurrence affected 10/43 (23%) after endoscopic treatment, 8/43 (19%) with prophylaxis and 24/42 (57%) with surveillance. | Endoscopy and prophylaxis reduced infections compared with surveillance in girls. Their difference from each other was not significant; few boys had recurrent febrile UTI.<sup>[[7]](#ref7)</sup> |
| New renal damage | New damage occurred in 13 girls: five in the endoscopic arm, none with prophylaxis and eight under surveillance; two boys also developed new damage. | New renal damage was significantly more frequent with surveillance than prophylaxis.<sup>[[8]](#ref8)</sup> |

Across the wider evidence base, the 2019 Cochrane review found uncertain additional benefit of endoscopic injection, with or without antibiotics, over antibiotics alone for febrile UTI by one to two years (RR 0.74, 95% CI 0.31–1.78, low-certainty evidence).<sup>[[9]](#ref9)</sup> A 2026 meta-analysis of 15 mostly nonrandomized studies favored ureteral reimplantation over Dx/HA injection for radiographic resolution (risk ratio for nonresolution 4.30) and reoperation (risk ratio 6.42), with no difference in febrile or recurrent UTI; in its two-center cohort (179 injection, 130 reimplantation), postoperative UTI was 19.6% versus 3.8%, while injection had shorter operative time and stay.<sup>[[26]](#ref26)</sup>

### Comparisons with Other Bulking Materials

Two trials in the Cochrane review favored Macroplastique for persistent reflux, but patient-important infection outcomes were inconclusive and renal-parenchymal outcomes were not reported for that comparison. In the larger prospective study, reflux was fully corrected at three-month VCUG in 182/202 Macroplastique-treated ureters versus 159/197 Deflux-treated ureters.<sup>[[10]](#ref10)</sup> These ureter-level results do not establish equivalent advantages for children, febrile UTI or renal function; Macroplastique's US indication is different.<sup>[[9]](#ref9)[[10]](#ref10)</sup>

The often quoted grade-specific estimates from 5,527 patients/8,101 renal units combine endoscopic-injection literature across agents. The EAU also notes greater concern about delayed obstruction with polyacrylate-polyalcohol copolymer than with Dx/HA; a ranking of material durability alone is insufficient for product selection.<sup>[[3]](#ref3)</sup>

## Durability and Interpreting Follow-up

Large single-center Deflux cohorts report high anatomical resolution rates, often after repeated injections. A series of 1,551 children (2,341 ureters, grades II–V) reported resolution after the first, second and third injection in 87.1%, 11.3% and 1.6% of ureters, respectively. VCUG and ultrasound were performed at three months, then ultrasound annually; median overall follow-up was 5.6 years. Febrile UTI occurred in 69/1,551 children (4.4% by calculation; the abstract reports 4.6%), and no patient required reimplantation.<sup>[[20]](#ref20)</sup> A cohort of 851 children with 1,287 grade IV–V refluxing units reported first-injection resolution in 895/1,287 (69.5%).<sup>[[11]](#ref11)</sup> Subsequent clinical and ultrasound follow-up extended for a median 8.5 years, but routine VCUG was performed at three months rather than repeatedly in every child throughout that interval.<sup>[[11]](#ref11)</sup> Long clinical follow-up is not continuous proof of radiographic cure.<sup>[[11]](#ref11)</sup>

In another series, 111/150 ureters initially cured and reassessed at one year remained reflux-free. The authors' 46.1% combined one-year success estimate used a selected denominator of 241 ureters, including initial failures, rather than all 337 initially treated ureters. The one-year data show recurrence in some ureters, with incomplete follow-up and a selected denominator.<sup>[[12]](#ref12)</sup>

In the label's pivotal randomized study, VCUG grade 0 at 12 months was achieved in 27/39 (69%) children after Deflux versus 7/21 (33%) on antibiotic prophylaxis (*p* = 0.0041). In the 165-child post-approval study, VCUG grade 0 at 3 months was 66% (109/165); later success was defined as absence of febrile UTI, or grade 0 on a VCUG prompted by an event, and was 69% (96/139) at 12 months and 40% (32/80) at 5 years. Only 31/165 (18.8%) had complete five-year data, and 30% were retreated.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup>

## Complex Anatomy and Factors Associated with Success

Uncontrolled single-center series describe treatment in complete duplex systems. One series of 123 children with complete duplex systems (136 refluxing units, mostly grade III–IV) reported resolution after the first, second and third injection in 68.4%, 25.7% and 5.9% of units, with febrile UTI in 4.1% and no reimplantations. VCUG and ultrasound were performed at three months, then ultrasound every two years; mean overall follow-up was 6.7 years.<sup>[[21]](#ref21)</sup> The label's stated uncertainty about duplex systems, noted above, remains.

Reported factors associated with lower endoscopic success:

- **Reflux grade.** In the 851-child cohort, first-injection resolution was 70.4% for grade IV and 61.9% for grade V.<sup>[[11]](#ref11)</sup>
- **Sex.** In a single-surgeon retrospective series of 49 children with grade IV–V reflux (median follow-up 32 months), 84% needed no further intervention after one injection and 98% after further injections. Boys did better than girls on multivariable analysis.<sup>[[22]](#ref22)</sup>
- **Bladder and bowel dysfunction.** In the AUA guideline meta-analysis, initial reflux resolution after endoscopic surgery was 50% with bladder and bowel dysfunction and 89% without it, while open surgery resolved 97% in both groups. The guideline recommends treating the dysfunction first, preferably before reflux surgery.<sup>[[24]](#ref24)</sup>
- **Experience.** In the Lee series, one-injection radiographic success was 65.9% (112/170 ureters) in the first half and 80.2% (134/167) in the second half.<sup>[[12]](#ref12)</sup>

## Safety and Surveillance

Potential complications include infection, hematuria, dysuria, transient dilation, new contralateral reflux and ureteral obstruction with or without hydronephrosis. Delayed obstruction can require stenting or reimplantation and may affect renal function. The label and published case reports document late presentations, including occasional asymptomatic renal deterioration years after treatment.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[13]](#ref13)</sup>

Obstruction is not limited to high-volume injection. A four-center series found five patients requiring stenting among 745 treated, and all had received no more than 1 mL at the relevant injection; four had neurogenic bladder or dysfunctional voiding. The affected subgroup is too small for reliable risk prediction.<sup>[[14]](#ref14)</sup>

### Histology Is Not a Population Complication Rate

Giant-cell reactions were found in 94% of a selected 16-child surgical-excision series after failed injection.<sup>[[15]](#ref15)</sup> Calcification was seen in nine ureters among 13 treated children in a different failed-treatment or reimplantation series.<sup>[[16]](#ref16)</sup> These findings describe selected removed tissue, not the frequency of symptomatic granuloma or calcification in all children receiving Deflux.<sup>[[15]](#ref15)[[16]](#ref16)</sup>

A sonographic volume study had 296 ureters measured at two weeks but only 20 at 24–36 months. Its approximately 65% late volume retention does not validate avoiding reflux imaging solely because a mound remains visible.<sup>[[17]](#ref17)</sup>

Follow-up is arranged according to reflux severity, renal status and infection history. The IFU suggests postoperative VCUG to assess persistence, and the AUA guideline recommends voiding cystography after endoscopic injection and renal ultrasound after any reflux surgery to look for obstruction.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[24]](#ref24)</sup> An ultrasound-visible mound alone does not establish reflux resolution. In a series of 160 children (220 ureters, 30 with grade IV–V reflux), bladder ultrasound detected the mound in 89% of low-grade and 43.3% of high-grade ureters at last follow-up, which led the authors to propose ultrasound follow-up for low-grade reflux and VCUG for high-grade reflux.<sup>[[23]](#ref23)</sup> New febrile UTI, flank pain, hydronephrosis or deteriorating renal function warrants evaluation. In adults previously treated as children, distinguish a calcified extraluminal implant from a ureteral stone and assess for obstruction.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[3]](#ref3)</sup>

## Solesta Is a Separate Product and Indication

Related Dx/HA technology is marketed as Solesta, originally FDA approved in 2011 for fecal incontinence in adults aged at least 18 who have failed conservative therapy.<sup>[[18]](#ref18)</sup> That does not give Deflux a second interchangeable indication. ASCRS 2023 states that injection of biocompatible bulking agents into the anal canal is not routinely recommended for FI (conditional recommendation, low-quality evidence). See [Fecal Incontinence](/docs/clinical-conditions/03i-defecatory-disorders/fecal-incontinence) for treatment selection.<sup>[[18]](#ref18)[[19]](#ref19)</sup>
