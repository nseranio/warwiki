# Bulkamid — Polyacrylamide Hydrogel

**Bulkamid**, now part of Boston Scientific's product portfolio, is a permanent, nonparticulate hydrogel containing 2.5% cross-linked polyacrylamide and 97.5% water. It improves urethral coaptation through submucosal volume augmentation. The US indication is SUI due to intrinsic sphincter deficiency in adult women with SUI or stress-predominant mixed incontinence; FDA approval was January 28, 2020.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup><sup>[[2]](#ref2)</sup><sup>[[3]](#ref3)</sup>

This is the material and device hub. For evaluation, injection workflow and follow-up, see [Urethral Bulking Agents](/docs/surgical-techniques/04f-incontinence-procedures/procedures/urethral-bulking-agents).

## Composition and Mechanism

Bulkamid Hydrogel is a non-resorbable, transparent, hydrophilic gel. It consists of cross-linked polyacrylamide with loosely bound water, nominally 2.5% polyacrylamide and 97.5% non-pyrogenic water (w/w), supplied in pre-filled 1 mL syringes. The label describes it as biocompatible, non-biodegradable and non-migrational. Unlike the particulate agents [Macroplastique](/docs/foundations/tools/biomaterials/bulking-agents/macroplastique) and [Coaptite](/docs/foundations/tools/biomaterials/bulking-agents/coaptite), it is a homogeneous gel. It is injected into the submucosa of the proximal half of the urethra to improve coaptation, using a 23G, 12 cm needle through a rotatable sheath with a 2.7 mm, 0° cystoscope. It is not a mesh product.<sup>[[3]](#ref3)</sup>

## Role in Shared Decision-Making

Bulking can suit women who prioritize a less invasive procedure and shorter recovery, including selected women with persistent SUI after previous continence surgery. Counsel about lower cure rates than a sling and the frequent need for repeat treatment. The 2023 AUA/SUFU guideline does not establish one injectable as superior to another. Its report of PAHG benefit persisting to 96 months is evidence of possible durability, not a guaranteed duration or a head-to-head ranking.<sup>[[4]](#ref4)</sup>

Age, leakage severity and urethral mobility may influence outcomes, but exploratory predictors are not universal selection rules. The frequently quoted 90% response in older women with milder leakage (age 60 years or older with fewer than 2.5 daily stress episodes; 38% cured, versus 13% cured in younger women with 2.5 or more episodes) came from a post hoc analysis of 291 women combining PAHG and collagen recipients, not a validated Bulkamid-only prediction model.<sup>[[5]](#ref5)</sup> In a single-center sonographic cohort of 130 women, those with a normomobile urethra (linear dorsocaudal movement 6 to 24 mm) improved most on the ICIQ-UI SF (mean reduction 3.8 points). Women with hypomobile or hypermobile urethras also improved, to a lesser extent.<sup>[[14]](#ref14)</sup>

Future pregnancy is not an established reason to call someone an ideal candidate: the effect of treatment on pregnancy/delivery and the effect of pregnancy on treatment remain unknown. Discuss these uncertainties with women who have not completed childbearing.<sup>[[2]](#ref2)</sup>

## Labeling and Safety

**Acute UTI is a contraindication.** Assess for damaged urethral tissue and correct urethral or bladder-neck stricture before treatment. Overcorrection can cause obstruction. Do not inject intravascularly; vascular occlusion and embolism are possible. Stop injection if superficial mucosal capillaries fade, because this can indicate ischemia.<sup>[[2]](#ref2)</sup>

A homogeneous, nonparticulate composition does not eliminate every migration, erosion, granuloma or vascular complication. The FDA labeling distinguishes events observed in the pivotal study from complications reported with other bulking agents that may also occur with Bulkamid. Absence of an event in a study is not proof that its risk is zero.<sup>[[3]](#ref3)</sup>

Other considerations include bleeding risk, impaired emptying, infection elsewhere, immunosuppression and tissue quality. The US label identifies populations in which safety/effectiveness are not established, including neurogenic bladder, predominant urgency incontinence, detrusor overactivity, specified urethral hypermobility and higher-grade prolapse. These labeling limitations should not be silently converted either into evidence of safety or into a new universal guideline prohibition.<sup>[[2]](#ref2)</sup>

Do not mix the hydrogel with other substances or inject it into a site previously treated with a different bulking agent. The gel kit is single-patient use; the separate cystoscope has its own processing instructions. The current US cystoscope warning specifically prohibits contact of patients with a bladder-cancer history with devices disinfected using Cidex OPA.<sup>[[2]](#ref2)</sup>

## Administration Boundaries

Injection uses endoscopic visualization and a dedicated sheath and needle system. Local anesthesia and an outpatient setting are feasible, but are not requirements for every patient. Confirm infection status, the antimicrobial plan and the ability to empty the bladder after treatment.<sup>[[3]](#ref3)</sup>

The 2020 FDA-posted instructions describe the following sequence.<sup>[[3]](#ref3)</sup>

1. Test the urine and do not proceed if a UTI is present. Position the patient in lithotomy. Place anesthetic gel in the urethra, or inject local anesthetic bilaterally along the urethral mucosa (3 and 9 o'clock) 5 to 10 minutes beforehand. Prophylactic antibiotics are advised per local protocol.
2. Advance the sheath under vision, inspect the bladder and let it empty. Place the needle tip adjacent to the bladder neck, then retract the system to about 2 cm from the bladder neck.
3. Press the sheath parallel to the urethral wall so the mucosa lies in front of the needle tip. Do not angle the system, which risks injecting too deep or too superficially.
4. Insert the needle submucosally until the 1 cm marking on the needle is level with the mucosa.
5. Inject at each site until the gel reaches the midline of the lumen, keeping the superficial mucosal vessels visible to avoid ischemia. Run water inflow to dilate the urethra.
6. Repeat at two further sites for even circumferential bulking (for example 6, 2 and 10 o'clock, or 3 and 9 o'clock). Rotate only the rotatable part of the sheath so that all sites lie in one plane. Limit the number of punctures to reduce extravasation, and do not reinject the same site.
7. Open the outflow between injections to avoid urgency. Empty the bladder afterward with a disposable 10 to 12 Fr catheter, not the sheath.
8. Measure the residual volume after the first void. Discharge is permitted at 100 mL or less. Patients who cannot void may perform intermittent catheterization with a 10 to 12 Fr catheter.

In a single-blinded randomized trial of in-office injection, adding a periurethral block to topical EMLA and intraurethral lidocaine gel lowered mean procedural pain (VAS 4.5 versus 7.4; *p* &lt; 0.01) and reduced postprocedure catheterization (0% versus 33%). The trial stopped at 23 of 52 planned women after three topical-only patients needed an unplanned block.<sup>[[23]](#ref23)</sup>

The label gives 1.5 to 2.0 mL as the typical total volume per session. In the pivotal trial the Bulkamid arm received a mean 1.6 mL initially and 3.3 mL across all sessions, and 52 of 228 (22.8%), 95 (41.7%) and 81 (35.5%) had one, two and three sessions.<sup>[[3]](#ref3)</sup>

The FDA-posted 2020 physician instructions specify a maximum 2 mL per session, top-up no earlier than four weeks, and evidence for up to three treatment sessions. These are not instructions to administer three sessions automatically. Use the current supplied IFU for exact positioning, injection plane, equipment and discharge criteria; avoid a universal per-site volume that could exceed the total limit.<sup>[[3]](#ref3)</sup>

## Main Comparative Evidence

### North American Pivotal Trial — Comparison With Collagen

The FDA analysis included 228 Bulkamid and 117 collagen recipients. At 12 months, its composite endpoint of at least 50% reduction in both pad-test leakage and daily incontinence episodes was met by 107/228 (46.9%) versus 50/117 (42.7%), using the specified intention-to-treat analysis with last observation carried forward. Bulkamid met the trial's noninferiority criterion. This comparison was against Contigen collagen, not a sling; the original journal report used somewhat different reported analysis figures.<sup>[[3]](#ref3)</sup><sup>[[6]](#ref6)</sup>

In the journal report of the same trial, 53.2% of hydrogel and 55.4% of collagen recipients had a 50% or greater decrease in leakage and incontinence episodes at 12 months, 47.2% versus 50% reported zero stress incontinence episodes, and 77.1% versus 70% considered themselves cured or improved. Major adverse events were rare in each group. In the FDA complete-case analysis, cured, dry, much improved or improved responders were 144/187 (77.0%) versus 71/101 (70.3%).<sup>[[3]](#ref3)[[6]](#ref6)</sup>

Repeat treatment was common: 176/228 received two or three injection sessions. In the FDA clinical data, 60/228 (26.3%) had at least one treatment-related adverse event, 64.2% of events were mild, and 93.8% had resolved at study closure. Treatment-related acute retention occurred in 13/228 (5.7%), implant-site pain in 28/228 (12.3%) and UTI in 8/228 (3.5%). One serious hematuria event was considered probably procedure-related. These are study-specific rates, not ceilings on complications in practice.<sup>[[3]](#ref3)</sup>

### Short- and Medium-Term Series

| Study | Population and follow-up | Findings |
|---|---|---|
| Lose 2010 (open multicenter) | 135 women (67 stress, 68 mixed); 12 months | Subjective response 66%. Episodes fell from 3.0 to 0.7 per 24 hours and leakage from 29 to 4 g. Reinjection in 47 (35%).<sup>[[15]](#ref15)</sup> |
| Toozs-Hobson 2012 | Two-year follow-up of the same open-label study | Subjective responder rate 64%, compared with 67% at 12 months. No safety issues.<sup>[[16]](#ref16)</sup> |
| Lamblin 2025 (French multicenter cohort) | 320 women; median follow-up 24 months | At last follow-up 204 (63.7%) cured, 70 (21.9%) improved and 46 (14.4%) reported failure. Acute retention 3.1%, urinary infection 2.2%, transient hematuria 7.2%.<sup>[[17]](#ref17)</sup> |
| Sze 2026 (retrospective) | 284 women; median follow-up 6.7 months | Median pad use fell from 2.0 to 1.0 per day and UDI-6 from 45.8 to 16.7. Repeat injection in 16.5% at a median 7.6 months, and 17 women (6.0%) progressed to a sling. Procedures without general anesthesia or sedation were associated with repeat injection (OR 2.82, 95% CI 1.33 to 5.79).<sup>[[18]](#ref18)</sup> |

A systematic review of eight studies (767 women) reported reinjection in 186 (24.3%, range 12 to 35%), injection-site pain in 4 to 14% and UTI in 3 to 7%.<sup>[[19]](#ref19)</sup> A separate review of 26 Bulkamid and Macroplastique studies reported subjective success of 66 to 89.7% at 12 months and objective success of 25.4 to 73.3%.<sup>[[20]](#ref20)</sup> The EAU 2026 female LUTS guideline summarizes long-term effectiveness of 42 to 70% for Bulkamid, with minimal erosion or migration reported.<sup>[[21]](#ref21)</sup>

### Helsinki Randomized Trial — Five-Year TVT Comparison

The 2025 follow-up provides a long-term randomized comparison with a retropubic tension-free vaginal tape (TVT). 223 women were randomized, 212 received treatment as assigned and 195 attended at five years—92% of those treated, rather than 92% of everyone randomized.<sup>[[7]](#ref7)</sup>

| Five-year outcome | PAHG | TVT |
|---|---|---|
| Satisfaction score at least 80/100 | 74/99 (74.7%) | 89/96 (92.7%) |
| Median satisfaction score | 90 | 98 |
| Peri-/postoperative complication before crossover | 22/99 (22.2%) | 42/96 (43.8%) |

PAHG did not meet the prespecified 20-percentage-point noninferiority margin for satisfaction: the between-group difference was 18.0 points, with a 95% CI of 7.7–28.0. The complication outcome included events before crossover and is not interchangeable with serious complications, mesh exposure or reoperation. High satisfaction in many PAHG recipients can coexist with better average efficacy after TVT.<sup>[[7]](#ref7)</sup>

Stress-test negativity, combined objective cure and patient-reported improvement are different endpoints. In the three-year publication, the cough stress test alone was negative in 78.1% after PAHG versus 95.7% after TVT.<sup>[[8]](#ref8)</sup>

### Retreatment and Previous Sling Surgery

In a Finnish observational cohort of 391 primary procedures, two-year retreatment was 27.0% after PAHG versus 0.9% after TVT. PAHG complications in that cohort were grades I–II, but this does not establish that higher-grade complications can never occur. Patient choice, selection and local practice limit causal comparison.<sup>[[9]](#ref9)</sup>

A systematic review after failed midurethral sling reported a pooled 80% cure/improvement estimate for the Bulkamid subgroup (95% CI 74 to 85%). The estimate for all agents was 75%, with a pooled failure rate of 32% and a reoperation rate of 25%. “Cure or improvement” is broader than dryness, and heterogeneous small studies do not establish the optimal salvage procedure.<sup>[[10]](#ref10)</sup> A 2025 meta-analysis of 20 studies of bulking agents for recurrent SUI reported a pooled objective cure or improvement of 56% and a pooled subjective satisfaction of 77%, with re-intervention after salvage bulking ranging from 9.8 to 42%.<sup>[[24]](#ref24)</sup> In the Norwegian registry, 57 women received Bulkamid after retropubic TVT with insufficient improvement or recurrence. The stress test was negative in 72.9%, 73.7% were satisfied, and there was 1 complication in 67 injections. De novo urgency incontinence occurred in five women (8.8% of the group, 26.3% of those without preoperative urgency incontinence).<sup>[[22]](#ref22)</sup>

Office-based delivery can improve cost-effectiveness, but the often-cited 58% office-use threshold comes from a particular economic model. It is not a universal local cost threshold or evidence that every patient should prefer bulking.<sup>[[11]](#ref11)</sup>

## Guideline Position

The AUA/SUFU 2023 female SUI guideline states that bulking agents are viable treatments, that retreatment is common, and that there are inadequate data to recommend one injectable over another. Calcium hydroxyapatite, polydimethylsiloxane and PAHG have longer-term data showing persistence of effect at 73.2, 83 and 96 months, respectively. The guideline notes that erosion was not present in PAHG patients versus other agents in multiple studies. It considers bulking for women who wish to avoid more invasive surgery, are concerned about a longer recovery, or have insufficient improvement after a previous anti-incontinence procedure.<sup>[[4]](#ref4)</sup>

## Male Use and Pediatric Reflux

The current US Bulkamid label warns not to use it in male patients. Older reports about other bulking materials after prostatectomy do not establish a Bulkamid indication or override that warning. More broadly, the AUA/GURS/SUFU guideline recommends that clinicians counsel patients that efficacy is low and cure is rare with urethral bulking agents for incontinence after prostate treatment (Strong Recommendation; Evidence Level Grade B).<sup>[[2]](#ref2)</sup><sup>[[12]](#ref12)</sup>

PAHG has been studied for pediatric vesicoureteral reflux. A prospective study of 76 children (123 refluxing units) reported 71% success 3 months after a single injection, a median injected volume of 1.0 mL per renal unit, and no upper-tract deterioration or bulking-agent calcification at a median follow-up of 36 months. Urinary infections occurred in 9 (12%) nonfebrile and 2 (3%) febrile cases. This is outside the US Bulkamid indication; comparison with historical dextranomer/HA results does not establish equivalence or routine pediatric eligibility.<sup>[[13]](#ref13)</sup><sup>[[2]](#ref2)</sup>
