# Autologous Chondrocyte Injection — Historical Bulking Research

**Autologous chondrocyte injection** is a tissue-engineered bulking approach developed by Atala and colleagues in the early 1990s. Auricular cartilage is harvested, its chondrocytes are expanded in culture and suspended in calcium alginate, and the gel is injected endoscopically to create a living cartilage nidus. It was studied for pediatric vesicoureteral reflux (VUR) and for female stress urinary incontinence (SUI) from intrinsic sphincter deficiency (ISD), and it remained investigational. The clinical studies were uncontrolled and small, and they showed relapse and late mound calcification. Simpler off-the-shelf agents replaced it, but adults who were injected as children may still present with its late effects.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[2]](#ref2)[[3]](#ref3)[[4]](#ref4)</sup>

## Historical Timeline

| Year | Report | Finding |
|---|---|---|
| 1993 | Atala, athymic mouse study | Cartilage formed at 34/36 experimental injection sites; no migration or granuloma in distant organs.<sup>[[5]](#ref5)</sup> |
| 1994 | Atala, mini-pig reflux model (n = 4) | Reflux resolved on the treated side and persisted on the untreated side in every animal; cartilage was present at the injection site.<sup>[[6]](#ref6)</sup> |
| 1999 | Diamond and Caldamone | First clinical VUR series: 29 children, 46 ureters.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup> |
| 2001 | Caldamone and Diamond | One-year follow-up of the same program; correction fell to 70% of ureters.<sup>[[2]](#ref2)</sup> |
| 2001 | Bent | Only clinical SUI study: 32 women with ISD.<sup>[[3]](#ref3)</sup> |
| 2004 | Paltiel | Sonographic mound analysis; mean mound volume decreased.<sup>[[7]](#ref7)</sup> |
| 2009 | Gargollo | Mound calcification in 10/27 patients.<sup>[[4]](#ref4)</sup> |

## Composition and Mechanism

- **Cell source.** Posterior auricular cartilage, harvested through a small incision at the time of diagnostic cystoscopy in the VUR studies.<sup>[[2]](#ref2)</sup>
- **Cells.** Chondrocytes were expanded in culture for about six weeks. Surplus cells were stored for retreatment.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup> The mini-pig study used 40 million cells/mL.<sup>[[6]](#ref6)</sup>
- **Carrier.** A biodegradable alginate gel served as the three-dimensional scaffold.<sup>[[3]](#ref3)[[5]](#ref5)</sup>
- **Intended behavior.** The gel supplies immediate bulk. The chondrocytes then form cartilage as the alginate degrades. In the mouse study the polymer was progressively replaced by cartilage, and the final cartilage size was related to the seeded cell concentration.<sup>[[5]](#ref5)</sup>

The theoretical advantage was a permanent, nonantigenic implant without foreign-body material. The clinical volume-loss data (below) indicate that stable cartilage was not reliably achieved.

## Biological Concept and Early Experiments

The proposed mechanism combined immediate gel bulk with subsequent cartilage formation. The original 1993 experiment used bovine articular chondrocytes in athymic mice, not human auricular cartilage: cartilage formed at 34/36 experimental injection sites, with no migration or granuloma found during the experiment. A subsequent study used autologous ear chondrocytes in four mini-pigs with surgically created reflux; treated ureters showed reflux resolution while contralateral controls remained refluxing.<sup>[[5]](#ref5)[[6]](#ref6)</sup>

These are preclinical observations. Autologous origin does not remove the risks of the scaffold, processing, injection or donor-site procedure.

## How the Clinical Studies Were Performed

The pediatric studies used two stages.

1. **Harvest.** Posterior auricular cartilage was biopsied at the time of cystoscopy, and the chondrocytes were expanded in culture for about six weeks.
2. **Injection.** Chondrocytes were injected endoscopically at the ureterovesical junction of each refluxing ureter. Ultrasound was obtained at one month and cystography at three months. Persistent reflux was retreated with stored cells.

The female SUI study used one outpatient injection just distal to the bladder neck, again after cartilage harvest and culture. These are research protocols, not preparation instructions for current clinical use.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[2]](#ref2)[[3]](#ref3)</sup>

## Clinical Evidence

| Study | Population and assessment | Results and interpretation |
|---|---|---|
| Diamond and Caldamone 1999 | 29 children, 46 grade II–IV refluxing ureters; cystography at three months | First injection corrected 26/46 ureters (57%). A second injection corrected 12/19 retreated ureters (63%), bringing total correction to 38/46 ureters and 24/29 children (83%). The 63% figure is success among retreated ureters, not cumulative success.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup> |
| Caldamone and Diamond 2001 | Follow-up report from the pediatric program | At one year, reflux correction was reported in 32/46 ureters (70%) and 19/29 children (65%). Follow-up after retreatment of three ureters was unavailable. Cystoscopy in failures showed mound volume loss and displacement; the early response did not ensure sustained reflux control.<sup>[[2]](#ref2)</sup> |
| Bent 2001 | Uncontrolled multicenter study; 32 women with ISD; single treatment | Incontinence grading at 12 months found 16 dry and ten improved, giving 50% dry and 81.3% combined dry or improved. All 26 who were dry or improved at three months maintained that status at 12 months, and only four women had a 12-month pad weight above 2.2 g. Quality-of-life scores improved. Without a comparator, these results cannot be ranked against a marketed bulking agent or sling.<sup>[[3]](#ref3)</sup> |

The studies concern different sites, diseases and endpoints, so their rates are not combined. Cell-based results also should not be compared with modern cohorts without accounting for selection, repeat treatment and follow-up.

## Mound Volume and Imaging

Paltiel's sonographic study included 32 children and 56 treated ureters. Early and late cystograms and sonograms were available for 18 children (30 ureters), and the paired volume analysis was limited to 14 children with both early and late scans. Mean mound volume fell from 0.56 to 0.37 cm³ (approximately 34%; *p* = 0.004) over mean imaging intervals of 1.4 and 12 months.<sup>[[7]](#ref7)</sup>

Mound morphology tracked the reflux result on early imaging. Of 16 ureters with persistent reflux, the mound was absent in six, unilobed in seven and multilobed in three. Of 29 ureters without reflux, the mound was unilobed in 28 and multilobed in one. Absent or multilobed mounds were therefore associated with persistent reflux. Volume loss is one mechanism of relapse, but the study does not show it is the only one.<sup>[[7]](#ref7)</sup>

Mild hydronephrosis occurred in three patients and distal hydroureter in one on early imaging. These findings resolved. Treatment-induced hydroureteronephrosis was uncommon and self-limited, but a small series cannot exclude obstruction in individual patients.<sup>[[7]](#ref7)</sup>

## Late Calcification and Prior-Implant Assessment

In Gargollo's single-center cohort, 10/27 patients (37%) developed mound calcification. Overall median follow-up was nine years; calcification was first identified at a median 2.1 years after injection, not necessarily at year nine. Seven of the ten had hematuria with or without flank pain, and three were initially thought to have ureterovesical-junction stones. No hydroureteronephrosis was found in the patients with calcified mounds.<sup>[[4]](#ref4)</sup>

The mechanism remained uncertain, and this cohort's event rate should not be applied to all patients or other injectables. A calcified mound can mimic a stone, but prior injection does not justify dismissing new hematuria, pain, infection, or obstruction. Retrieve earlier operative and imaging records and assess the current presentation.<sup>[[4]](#ref4)</sup>

Three children in the 2001 follow-up underwent successful open reimplantation after failed injection. This supports feasibility in those cases, not a guarantee that all subsequent reconstruction is unaffected.<sup>[[2]](#ref2)</sup>

## Why It Was Not Adopted

- **Logistics.** Two stages with a six-week culture interval require cell-culture infrastructure.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup>
- **Relapse.** One-year correction fell to 65% of children, and mound volume decreased by about a third over 12 months.<sup>[[2]](#ref2)[[7]](#ref7)</sup>
- **Calcification.** Mound calcification occurred in 37% of one cohort, and calcified mounds mimic stones.<sup>[[4]](#ref4)</sup>
- **Evidence.** The clinical base is one uncontrolled SUI study (n = 32) and one VUR program (n = 29 children) with no randomized trial.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[3]](#ref3)</sup>
- **Regulation.** Cell-based products are regulated by the FDA as biologics, which adds a separate regulatory pathway to the device route used by synthetic agents.<sup>[[10]](#ref10)</sup>
- **Alternatives.** Off-the-shelf agents such as [Deflux](/docs/foundations/tools/biomaterials/bulking-agents/deflux) for VUR and [Bulkamid](/docs/foundations/tools/biomaterials/bulking-agents/bulkamid) for SUI avoid the two-stage process.

## Auricular Chondrocytes in Other Applications

- **Vocal-fold medialization.** In an ex vivo study, drilled human auricular or nasal septal cartilage passed through an 18-gauge needle as an injectable slurry. About 10% of auricular lacunae contained chondrocytes.<sup>[[8]](#ref8)</sup>
- **Tissue-engineered urethral stents.** Bovine chondrocytes seeded on polymer cylinders formed cartilaginous tubes in nude mice and in bioreactors, with adequate elasticity and pressure tolerance. The study proposed their use for stricture disease.<sup>[[9]](#ref9)</sup>

These are preclinical or ex vivo reports. Other cell approaches, such as muscle-derived cell injection, are different interventions and do not validate auricular chondrocyte bulking.
