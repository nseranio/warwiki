# Contigen — Glutaraldehyde Cross-Linked Bovine Collagen

**Contigen** is a historical urethral bulking agent. FDA records give an original approval date of September 30, 1993 and a withdrawal date of October 25, 2011. The original indication was urinary incontinence due to intrinsic sphincter deficiency (ISD), after at least 12 months without improvement; it was not restricted to women. It served as the comparator in the pivotal trials of Macroplastique, Durasphere, Coaptite and Bulkamid, so its results anchor much of the bulking-agent literature. This page helps interpret older studies and assess patients with previous collagen injections.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[2]](#ref2)</sup>

## Material and Tissue Response

Contigen was glutaraldehyde cross-linked bovine dermal collagen. Injection produced local bulk to improve urethral coaptation; cross-linking was intended to improve persistence. It was a biological implant capable of eliciting an immune response.<sup>[[3]](#ref3)[[4]](#ref4)</sup>

A histological study examined specimens from 29 patients undergoing reimplantation after failed subureteral injection. Host fibroblast ingrowth and new human types I and III collagen were found, with occasional inflammatory reactions. Those selected specimens support tissue remodeling, not guaranteed complete resorption, absence of granulomas, or uncomplicated subsequent reconstruction in every patient.<sup>[[5]](#ref5)</sup> An earlier series of seven patients reimplanted 3 to 19 months after failed subureteral injection found a minimal localized inflammatory reaction without granuloma formation, and the implant did not hinder reimplantation.<sup>[[18]](#ref18)</sup>

Remodeling is also the limitation. As the bovine collagen is resorbed and replaced, the bulking effect diminishes and incontinence recurs, which is why repeat injections were the rule.

## Historical Indications and Treatment Burden

Contigen was studied in female and male ISD, and separately for pediatric vesicoureteral reflux. The latter literature should not be treated as an extension of its FDA urinary-incontinence indication. Current reflux treatment is discussed in [Deflux](/docs/foundations/tools/biomaterials/bulking-agents/deflux).<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[5]](#ref5)</sup>

Older protocols used transurethral or periurethral injections, often with repeated treatments. Volumes varied substantially with population, response, and number of sessions: the collagen arm of the Coaptite trial averaged 6.6 mL over the study course, whereas a separate series of 42 women averaged 28.3 mL per patient. Neither is a universal initial dose, and the older product's technique should not be transferred to another agent.<sup>[[6]](#ref6)[[7]](#ref7)</sup>

## What the Outcome Studies Actually Showed

| Study | Population and follow-up | Findings and limits |
|---|---|---|
| Smith 1997 | 96 women treated; 94 included in the reported outcome; median 14 months | 38.3% dry and 28.7% socially continent, giving 67% combined success. The 82.9% one-year maintenance estimate applied to **35 initially successful women with sufficient follow-up**, not all treated women.<sup>[[8]](#ref8)</sup> |
| Richardson 1995 | 42 women with ISD; mean follow-up 46 months | 35 (83%) were cured (17), greatly improved (5) or improved (13). Median 2 treatments and mean 28.3 cc per patient. The 22 women who were cured or greatly improved needed a mean 2.4 treatments and 17.5 cc, compared with 4.1 treatments and 39.5 cc in the others.<sup>[[7]](#ref7)</sup> |
| Cross 1998 | 139 women | 103 (74%) were substantially improved, 29 (20%) improved and 7 not improved. Of the substantially improved group, 72% were continent after two or fewer injections, and 11% needed a booster more than 6 months after initial treatment.<sup>[[19]](#ref19)</sup> |
| Gorton 1999 | 61 women enrolled; 53 known failures or with information beyond five years | 14/53 (26%) reported improvement; only **one was completely dry**, and seven of the 14 still leaked daily. Improvement is not equivalent to continence.<sup>[[9]](#ref9)</sup> |
| Chrouser 2004 (retrospective comparison with Durasphere) | Patients with ISD; median follow-up 62 months for Contigen | Initial effectiveness was 63% for both agents. Contigen remained effective in 19% at 24 months and 9% at 36 months, and in 2 patients (5%) at last follow-up. A third of patients in each group considered the treatment a success.<sup>[[20]](#ref20)</sup> |
| Bent 2001 (urethral hypermobility) | 90 women with 1 to 3 injections | Of the 58 who reached 12 months, 19 (33%) were dry and 19 (33%) improved. Among all 90 entered, 21% were dry and 21% improved. The probability of maintaining initial improvement for 12 months was 44%.<sup>[[21]](#ref21)</sup> |
| Faerber 1997 (men) | 68 men after prostate treatment; mean follow-up 38 months | Mean 36 cc over 5 sessions. 10% were cured, 10% greatly improved, 67% had minimal or no improvement and 13% worsened. Men with incontinence after TURP did best.<sup>[[22]](#ref22)</sup> |
| Westney 2005 | Retrospective review of 322 men after prostate-cancer or BPH treatment; mean follow-up 40.1 months | Mean 4.37 injections, pad use 5.15 to 2.98/day, and mean response duration **6.3 months**. The 17% who achieved complete continence had a mean response duration of **11.1 months**. These are separate outcomes from an uncontrolled series.<sup>[[10]](#ref10)</sup> |

Contigen was the comparator in pivotal trials of newer agents.

| Comparator | Trial | Findings at 12 months |
|---|---|---|
| Macroplastique | Ghoniem 2009; 122 versus 125 women | Improvement by at least one Stamey grade in 61.5% versus 48%, and dryness in 36.9% versus 24.8% (*p* &lt; 0.05). It was the only one of these trials in which the comparator was statistically superior.<sup>[[11]](#ref11)</sup> |
| Durasphere | Lightner 2001; 61 versus 68 women | Improvement by at least one grade in 80.3% versus 69.1% (*p* = 0.162). Durasphere used less material (4.83 versus 6.23 mL) and had a higher short-term risk of urgency and retention.<sup>[[23]](#ref23)</sup> |
| Coaptite | Mayer 2007; 131 versus 100 women analyzed | Improvement by at least one Stamey grade in 63.4% versus 57.0% (*p* = 0.34). One injection sufficed in 38.0% versus 26.1%.<sup>[[6]](#ref6)</sup> |
| Bulkamid | Sokol 2014; 229 versus 116 women | At least 50% decrease in leakage and episodes in 53.2% versus 55.4%; zero stress episodes in 47.2% versus 50%. Bulkamid met the noninferiority criterion.<sup>[[24]](#ref24)</sup> |

Those outcomes should not be combined into one “cure” estimate. Agent-specific trial populations, analysis denominators, and current formulations are maintained on [Macroplastique](/docs/foundations/tools/biomaterials/bulking-agents/macroplastique), [Coaptite](/docs/foundations/tools/biomaterials/bulking-agents/coaptite), [Durasphere](/docs/foundations/tools/biomaterials/bulking-agents/durasphere), and [Bulkamid](/docs/foundations/tools/biomaterials/bulking-agents/bulkamid).

## Male Post-Prostatectomy Incontinence and Pediatric Reflux

In men, collagen gave modest and short-lived benefit (Westney and Faerber rows above). Westney's authors called it a good option for short-term therapy.<sup>[[10]](#ref10)</sup>

In pediatric reflux, an investigational US study treated 57 patients (92 ureters) with one to three injections. Cure at 1 month after the last treatment was 75% of ureters and cure at 1 year was 65% of all ureters evaluated. Of ureters cured at 1 month, 79% remained cured at 1 year. No adverse reactions to the implant were reported.<sup>[[25]](#ref25)</sup> Deflux replaced collagen for this indication.

## Durability

Long-term results were consistently poor. A 2020 review concluded that positive outcomes with bulking agents in general were poorly sustained beyond 6 months.<sup>[[26]](#ref26)</sup> Gorton reported 26% improvement beyond five years, Chrouser 5% still effective at a median 62 months, and Westney a mean response of 6.3 months in men.

## Immune and Local Complications

Historical treatment required skin testing. About 3% of candidates were ineligible because of an immediate hypersensitivity reaction to the skin test. A negative test did not exclude a delayed reaction: a published case described delayed hypersensitivity with urinary retention lasting approximately a year, and the author estimated delayed reactions in up to 2.5% of patients despite a negative skin test. This case establishes possibility, not a reliable incidence estimate.<sup>[[12]](#ref12)</sup>

The urethral Contigen immunology study reported anti-bovine type I collagen antibodies in approximately 28% of treated patients and characterized serum from 27 antibody responders. IgG predominated; the study did not find a correlation between immunoglobulin class and clinical adverse events. Antibody formation should not be equated with symptomatic allergy.<sup>[[4]](#ref4)</sup>

Some frequently quoted collagen safety figures come from different exposures: the 3.8% positive skin-test figure was reported with Atelocollagen, while the dermatomyositis or polymyositis signal concerned cosmetic bovine collagen dermal implants. In that cohort of about 345,000 recipients, the standardized incidence ratio was 18.8 (95% CI 8.1 to 37.0) for dermatomyositis alone and 5.05 (95% CI 2.31 to 9.59) for dermatomyositis or polymyositis combined. These are not product-specific urethral Contigen event rates.<sup>[[13]](#ref13)[[14]](#ref14)</sup>

FDA subsequently strengthened connective-tissue disease and adverse-event information. Its uncontrolled long-term Cohort C enrolled 62 young male and female patients, with only 21% follow-up reported. FDA recommended including a possible higher incidence of autoimmune responses in women in labeling; the severe attrition and absence of a control group limit risk quantification and causal interpretation.<sup>[[15]](#ref15)[[2]](#ref2)</sup>

In a prospective cohort of 337 women, Stothers reported complications in approximately 20%, including new urgency with incontinence in 12.6%, transient hematuria in 5%, and retention in 1.9%. Persistent symptoms were reported in some patients.<sup>[[16]](#ref16)</sup> Sterile abscess at the injection site is a documented rare complication: three collagen-treated patients had abscesses, and two needed drainage for obstructive and irritative symptoms.<sup>[[27]](#ref27)</sup> Late periurethral masses and obstruction are also documented: a collagenoma case required excision, followed by treatment of recurrent SUI with a rectus fascial sling. These reports refute a blanket guarantee that previous collagen injection cannot complicate later care.<sup>[[17]](#ref17)</sup>

## Assessment of a Patient With Previous Contigen

Retrieve the injection history where possible, including agent, sites, dates, number of sessions, and previous reactions. Distinguish recurrent stress leakage from urgency, infection, retention, or a periurethral mass before selecting another treatment. Subsequent continence surgery may be feasible, but requires assessment of the urethra and the patient's present condition; a historical injection history alone does not determine the next operation.<sup>[[16]](#ref16)[[17]](#ref17)</sup>

The FDA database documents withdrawal in 2011; it does not establish commercial competition, bovine spongiform encephalopathy, or a particular safety event as the reason. Avoid attributing a cause without a source.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup>
