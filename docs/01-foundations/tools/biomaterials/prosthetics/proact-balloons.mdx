# ProACT — Adjustable Periurethral Balloon System

**ProACT** (Adjustable Continence Therapy), manufactured by Uromedica and listed in Coloplast's US portfolio, is a paired periurethral balloon system for male stress urinary incontinence. Two silicone balloons lie on either side of the bladder neck. Silicone tubing tunneled subcutaneously connects each balloon to a titanium port in the dependent scrotum. The clinician adjusts balloon volume by percutaneous needle access to the ports; the patient does not operate a pump to void.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup><sup>[[2]](#ref2)</sup>

For implantation, outcomes and comparison with other continence procedures, see [ProACT (procedure)](/docs/surgical-techniques/04f-incontinence-procedures/procedures/proact-balloons).

## Mechanism and Comparison

ProACT compresses the bladder neck focally from both sides and does not encircle the urethra. Volume can be changed in clinic over weeks to months, so continence can be titrated without revision surgery. Each patient receives two complete balloon, tubing and port units in one procedure. Contrast in the balloons allows fluoroscopic volume checks.<sup>[[2]](#ref2)</sup>

| Device | Site of action | Principal mechanism | Postoperative adjustment |
|---|---|---|---|
| AMS 800 AUS | Bulbar urethra | Circumferential cuff occlusion | Patient-operated pump |
| AdVance / AdVance XP, Virtue | Bulbar urethra | Suburethral repositioning or compression | None |
| ATOMS, Remeex | Bulbar urethra | Adjustable suburethral compression or tape tension | Clinician |
| Argus | Bulbar urethra | Foam-pad compression with washer tensioning | Clinician, through hardware access |
| ProACT | Bladder neck | Paired periurethral balloon compression | Clinician, percutaneous |

The distinguishing features of ProACT are its bladder-neck location, office-based percutaneous titration and less invasive implantation. The trade-off is a substantial explantation and revision rate.<sup>[[5]](#ref5)</sup>

## Eligibility

FDA approval dated November 24, 2015 covers adult men with intrinsic-sphincter-deficiency SUI persisting at least 12 months after radical prostatectomy or TURP, despite conservative therapy.<sup>[[3]](#ref3)</sup>

AUA/GURS/SUFU 2024 permits offering adjustable balloons to non-radiated men with mild through severe SUI (conditional recommendation, Grade C). This is a shared-decision option with substantial revision or explantation risk, and the guideline does not treat it as equivalent to an AUS. For SUI after pelvic radiotherapy, the guideline favors AUS over balloons or male slings.<sup>[[4]](#ref4)</sup>

## Handling and safety

The FDA-posted physician IFU lists the following contraindications: active systemic or urinary infection; incontinence from detrusor overactivity; poor compliance; PVR greater than 100 mL; primary urgency incontinence; suspected bladder cancer; untreated bladder stones; bleeding disorders; and radiotherapy within the preceding or coming six months, or ongoing treatment. The guideline avoids prior radiotherapy more broadly than the IFU's six-month window.<sup>[[2]](#ref2)[[4]](#ref4)</sup>

- Fill with isotonic saline or contrast correctly diluted according to the IFU. Iohexol is not inherently iso-osmolar. Maintain the same inflation medium during adjustments.
- Maximum fill is 8 mL per balloon, and adjustment uses a 23-gauge needle. Excess volume or the wrong needle can damage the device.
- The implant is MR Conditional. MRI staff check the model-specific IFU and all of its scanning conditions.
- Implantation requires device-specific training. Use the IFU supplied with the actual implant; the FDA reference below is the original approval document.<sup>[[2]](#ref2)</sup>

## Failure and subsequent treatment

Counsel about mechanical leakage or failure, migration, erosion, infection, retention and possible reoperation. In a 2025 systematic review, mechanical failure, migration and erosion accounted for 30%, 21% and 16% of 752 reported adverse events, respectively. These are shares of events, not individual patient risks. In the same review of 1,607 patients, devices were explanted in 24% and revised or reimplanted in 28%. In the MAUDE database, erosion accounted for 49% of reported events, surgical site infection 19% and urinary retention 11%. The [procedure page](/docs/surgical-techniques/04f-incontinence-procedures/procedures/proact-balloons#complications-and-durability) discusses denominators and limitations.<sup>[[5]](#ref5)</sup>

Migration is usually displacement away from the bladder neck, erosion can involve the urethra or bladder, and port-pocket or deep device infection requires explantation. In a long-term series of 134 men (median follow-up 118 months), 112 implantations (82.6%) were successful. Of 63 men who were revised, 49 (77.8%) had a successful outcome, with high satisfaction despite the revision rate.<sup>[[6]](#ref6)</sup>

## Outcomes at Device Level

In a meta-analysis of 19 studies (1,264 patients, mean follow-up 3.6 years), pad use fell from 4.0 to 1.1 per day, 60.2% were dry and 81.9% were dry or improved by more than 50%.<sup>[[7]](#ref7)</sup> In a 68-patient prospective cohort, mean 24-hour pad weight fell from 293 g to 73 g at 4 years, with one procedure-related serious adverse event (retention).<sup>[[8]](#ref8)</sup> In an early series of ProACT implantations (mean follow-up 13 months, mean 3 adjustments), 67% of men were dry (at most one security pad) and 92% improved.<sup>[[9]](#ref9)</sup> Dry rates vary with baseline severity, and these estimates come from device-development and uncontrolled series.
