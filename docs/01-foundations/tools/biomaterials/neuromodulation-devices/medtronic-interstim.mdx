# Medtronic InterStim — Sacral Neuromodulation

**InterStim** is a family of sacral neuromodulation systems for bladder and bowel dysfunction. A lead near a sacral nerve connects to an implanted pulse generator; treatment modulates neural control and does not mechanically close an outlet. This is the device hub. For assessment, test placement and operative technique, see [Sacral Neuromodulation](/docs/surgical-techniques/04f-incontinence-procedures/procedures/sacral-neuromodulation).<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup>

## History

Sacral nerve stimulation grew out of NIH-funded research at UCSF in the 1970s, which progressed from direct bladder-wall stimulation to spinal cord stimulation and finally sacral root stimulation.<sup>[[22]](#ref22)</sup> The early procedure was an open operation under general anesthesia. The tined lead, introduced in the early 2000s, made placement percutaneous under local anesthesia in two stages, allowed a longer test period (at least 14 days recommended) and reduced lead migration. One review reported that the test-phase response rate almost doubled to about 80%.<sup>[[23]](#ref23)</sup> The FDA approved InterStim for urinary incontinence in 1997.<sup>[[24]](#ref24)</sup> Indications later expanded to urgency-frequency and non-obstructive retention,<sup>[[4]](#ref4)</sup> and in 2011 to chronic fecal incontinence.<sup>[[13]](#ref13)</sup> The second-generation InterStim II is smaller than the original generator.<sup>[[23]](#ref23)</sup> In a single French center, median observed generator lifespan was 7.29 years for the original InterStim and 5.9 years for InterStim II.<sup>[[25]](#ref25)</sup>

## Current Hardware and MRI Eligibility

| Generator | Power | MRI-relevant lead combination |
|---|---|---|
| **InterStim II — 3058** | Recharge-free | 978B1 SureScan can support conditional full-body 1.5 T/3 T scanning |
| **InterStim X — 97800** | Recharge-free; extended battery generation | 978B1 SureScan |
| **InterStim Micro — 97810** | Rechargeable | 978A1 SureScan |

MRI eligibility depends on the complete system, and neither the generator's name nor whether its battery recharges settles it. Older generators or leads and retained or damaged components require their own labeling review. It is incorrect to call every InterStim II head-only or to pair Micro's full-body eligibility with an older 3889 lead.<sup>[[2]](#ref2)</sup>

Before scanning, verify model numbers, lead status, MRI mode and all scanner, coil and energy limits in the current instructions. External evaluation equipment is distinct from an eligible fully implanted system.<sup>[[2]](#ref2)</sup> A retrospective Swiss series of 55 patients with an InterStim II (191 MRIs, 92% at 1.5 T, 58% of the 1.5 T scans imaging regions other than the head, device turned off) found possibly related complications in 2 scans (1%), both transient pain or heat at the implant site. A prospective series of 11 patients reported no significant adverse events with lumbosacral MRI. These observational series do not override labeling requirements.<sup>[[26]](#ref26)</sup><sup>[[27]](#ref27)</sup>

InterStim X has a manufacturer-projected battery life exceeding a decade, potentially up to 15 years at low-energy settings. Micro requires periodic recharging; its advertised 20-minute weekly schedule and 15-year battery performance depend on specified settings and charger placement. These are product specifications, not promises of surgery-free treatment.<sup>[[3]](#ref3)</sup>

## Mechanism

The mechanism is incompletely understood. A 2025 narrative review proposes that stimulation modulates afferent signaling, possibly through sub-sensory activation of pelvic floor muscles, with transmission via the spinal cord to supraspinal structures. It also proposes effects on sympathetic-parasympathetic balance and on neural plasticity at peripheral, spinal and supraspinal levels. These are hypotheses.<sup>[[28]](#ref28)</sup>

## Indications and Test Response

Current US labeling covers urinary retention and OAB symptoms after conservative-treatment failure or intolerance, and chronic fecal incontinence after failure of or unsuitability for conservative treatment. Urinary mechanical obstruction must be excluded. The label contraindicates implantation without an appropriate response to test stimulation, inability to operate the system and diathermy.<sup>[[4]](#ref4)</sup>

PNE and staged tined-lead evaluation are alternative ways to document response. Duration and programming depend on the clinical plan and system instructions. A favorable single-stage cost model or small case series does not establish that the test-response requirement can routinely be omitted. The initial test also does not guarantee long-term success.<sup>[[5]](#ref5)</sup><sup>[[6]](#ref6)</sup>

| Approach | Description | Reported data |
|---|---|---|
| PNE | Office test with a temporary wire near S3 | In one center, 35 of 76 (46%) screened by PNE met criteria for permanent implant<sup>[[5]](#ref5)</sup> |
| Staged tined lead | Permanent tined lead placed in the operating room, external stimulator during a test period of at least 14 days | In the same center, 18 of 41 (44%) who failed PNE were implanted after a successful tined-lead test; long-term success was independent of screening method<sup>[[5]](#ref5)</sup> |
| Single stage | Lead and generator implanted in one procedure | A 2019 cost-minimization model using 2017 Medicare reimbursement found single-stage placement less costly when the success rate exceeded 61.3% to 65.4%; a 15-patient single-institution series reported 93.3% success<sup>[[6]](#ref6)</sup><sup>[[29]](#ref29)</sup> |

In Medicare data for older women, staged implantation was associated with a 17% lower likelihood of any device-related reoperation than PNE or full implant.<sup>[[19]](#ref19)</sup>

The 2024 AUA/SUFU OAB guideline supports shared decisions about minimally invasive treatment, including offering it without mandatory prior behavioral or drug trials. That treatment-sequencing recommendation is separate from the manufacturer's device eligibility and test-response requirements.<sup>[[7]](#ref7)</sup>

## High-Yield Clinical Evidence

### OAB and Non-Obstructive Retention

InSite's prospective study tested 340 people and implanted 272. At five years, success was 67% in a modified-completers analysis versus 82% among completers. Those percentages use different assumptions about missing follow-up. Neither is the success rate of all people initially considered for therapy. Among completers, mean reduction was 2.0 leaks per day in participants with UUI and 5.4 voids per day in those with urgency-frequency. The most common device-related adverse events were an undesirable change in stimulation (22%), implant-site pain (15%) and ineffectiveness of therapy (13%).<sup>[[8]](#ref8)</sup>

A separate older worldwide study implanted 152 patients after testing, including 31 with retention. At five years, reported success was 68% for urge incontinence, 56% for urgency-frequency and 71% for retention. In the retention cohort, catheterization frequency fell from 5.3 to 1.9/day on average. Mean volume per catheterization fell from 379.9 to 109.2 mL. These selected-cohort results support benefit but do not prove retention is universally the best indication or predict catheter freedom for an individual.<sup>[[9]](#ref9)</sup> A single-center series of 217 patients (mean follow-up 46.9 months) reported success of about 70% for urgency incontinence, 68% for urgency-frequency and 73% for idiopathic retention, with cure rates of 20%, 33% and 58%. Forty-one percent had at least one device- or treatment-related surgical reintervention.<sup>[[30]](#ref30)</sup>

### ROSETTA — SNM Versus Botulinum Toxin

ROSETTA compared SNM with 200 U onabotulinumtoxinA in women with refractory UUI. At six months, toxin reduced daily UUI slightly more (3.9 versus 3.3 episodes; between-group difference 0.63), with more UTIs and temporary catheterization. The two-year extension evaluated clinical responders: data were available for 260/298, and mean UUI reduction did not differ significantly. Recurrent UTI was more frequent after toxin (24% versus 10%).<sup>[[10]](#ref10)</sup><sup>[[11]](#ref11)</sup>

The toxin dose was higher than the usual 100 U labeled idiopathic-OAB starting dose; see the [botulinum toxin hub](/docs/foundations/pharmacology/storage-oab/botulinum-toxin) for indication-specific dosing. ROSETTA should not be presented as a direct comparison against every current 100 U regimen or every newer SNM generator. At six months, treatment satisfaction and endorsement were higher with toxin, and the trial found no difference in perceived convenience, adverse effects or treatment preference. At two years, satisfaction and endorsement remained higher with toxin.<sup>[[10]](#ref10)</sup><sup>[[11]](#ref11)</sup>

The economic study compared two-stage SNM with 200 U onabotulinumtoxinA in 2018 US dollars. Two-year costs were $35,680 for SNM and $7,460 for toxin, and five-year modeled costs were $36,550 and $12,020. There was no difference in mean UUI reduction or QALYs at two years, and the authors concluded that SNM was not good value compared with toxin. The analysis does not establish today's local lifetime cost ranking.<sup>[[12]](#ref12)</sup>

### Fecal Incontinence

ASCRS 2023 states that SNM may be considered a first-line surgical option for incontinent patients with and without sphincter defects (conditional recommendation; low-quality evidence). It does not mean every patient should proceed directly to surgery or that a structural defect is irrelevant to the broader evaluation.<sup>[[13]](#ref13)</sup>

In the North American multicenter InterStim study, 120 patients were implanted after test stimulation (133 tested, 90% success). At 12 months, 83% had therapeutic success (at least 50% fewer incontinent episodes per week) and 41% had complete continence. Weekly episodes fell from 9.4 to 1.9.<sup>[[24]](#ref24)</sup> At five years, 76 patients (63%) were followed. Of 72 with data, 89% had at least a 50% improvement and 36% were fully continent, and 27 of 76 (35.5%) had a revision, replacement or explant.<sup>[[31]](#ref31)</sup> In a French retrospective cohort of 360 implanted patients, 162 (45%) had a favorable outcome at ten years, with 23% lost to follow-up; the Kaplan–Meier estimate was 64%. These are different estimands. A 2025 review of 36 long-term studies found benefits but judged all included studies at serious risk of bias and emphasized heterogeneous definitions.<sup>[[14]](#ref14)</sup><sup>[[15]](#ref15)</sup>

## Neurological Disease and Other Off-Label Uses

AUA/SUFU 2021 states that clinicians may offer SNM to select NLUTD patients with urgency, frequency and/or UUI (Conditional Recommendation; Grade C). It also states that clinicians should not offer SNM to NLUTD patients with spinal cord injury or spina bifida (Moderate Recommendation; Grade C). Neither statement addresses stress incontinence. Manufacturer safety and effectiveness remain unestablished for urinary dysfunction of neurological origin, so selection requires explicit consideration of diagnosis, progression, MRI needs and alternative bladder management.<sup>[[16]](#ref16)</sup><sup>[[4]](#ref4)</sup>

A 2021 meta-analysis of neurogenic lower urinary tract dysfunction reported a pooled test-stimulation success of 66.2% (21 studies, 887 patients) and a pooled permanent-implant success of 84.2% (24 studies, 428 patients). Included studies were mostly retrospective case series at substantial risk of bias. A 2025 meta-analysis of 15 studies (573 patients with neurogenic bladder or bowel dysfunction) reported improvements in voiding frequency, leakage episodes, bladder compliance and maximum detrusor pressure.<sup>[[17]](#ref17)</sup><sup>[[33]](#ref33)</sup>

High pooled success after permanent implantation can reflect selection of prior test responders. It should not be generalized to all neurological patients.<sup>[[17]](#ref17)</sup>

IC/BPS, chronic pain, constipation and sexual symptoms are outside labeled indications and have separate evidence. A 2017 meta-analysis of 17 studies (583 patients) with refractory IC/BPS reported reduced pelvic pain, frequency and nocturia and a pooled success rate of 84% (95% CI 76–91%), largely from small observational series of test responders.<sup>[[34]](#ref34)</sup> For constipation, the Cochrane review found limited evidence.<sup>[[18]](#ref18)</sup> An observational pooled response does not show that SNM treats every pelvic-floor condition.

## Reintervention and Device Choice

Pain, altered stimulation, infection, lead problems, loss of benefit and battery depletion may require reprogramming or surgery. In 32,454 older Medicare women, the estimated cumulative eight-year device-related reoperation incidence was 43%; this included generator replacement as well as revision or removal. It is not an eight-year revision rate for current Micro or X alone.<sup>[[19]](#ref19)</sup>

| Study | Population and follow-up | Reintervention |
|---|---|---|
| Medicare women 65 and older<sup>[[19]](#ref19)</sup> | 32,454; cumulative incidence at 1, 3 and 8 years | 9.4%, 20% and 43% for any device-related reoperation, including generator replacement |
| Single-center urinary series<sup>[[32]](#ref32)</sup> | 96 devices, median follow-up 50.7 months | 39% revision; 20.8% explantation; tined lead reduced revision from 50% to 31% versus the earlier lead model |
| Single-center urinary series<sup>[[30]](#ref30)</sup> | 217 patients, mean follow-up 46.9 months | 41% at least one surgical reintervention |
| Single-center series<sup>[[35]](#ref35)</sup> | 219 patients | 16% revision or explantation |
| Fecal incontinence systematic review<sup>[[15]](#ref15)</sup> | 36 studies, 3,770 patients, at least 36 months | 35.2% revision; 19.7% explantation |
| French fecal incontinence cohort<sup>[[14]](#ref14)</sup> | 360 patients, 10 years | 64.7% surgical revision; 26.1% explantation |

These rates come from different populations, indications, generations of devices and definitions, so they are not directly comparable. In one single-center series of 127 patients (135 operations), the revision rate was 22% of operations, with 6.7% explantation. Reasons included no efficacy, lead migration, infection, pain, generator malposition and the wish for an MRI-compatible device. Revisions for complications were associated with lower BMI at revision and older age, and antimicrobial envelope use did not change complication rates.<sup>[[36]](#ref36)</sup>

A single-center retrospective study of 246 OAB patients found more revisions with rechargeable devices (34% of 150 versus 13.5% of 96); charging difficulty accounted for 35.3% of rechargeable revisions. This association does not establish a universal rechargeable “class effect,” nor does lack of a significant manufacturer difference prove devices equivalent. Current charging intervals, training, patient dexterity and ability to obtain support all matter.<sup>[[20]](#ref20)</sup> In a single-center observational cohort of 37 patients with fecal incontinence who received an InterStim Micro (28 as replacements for nonrechargeable devices), 62% expressed regret, 96% reported charging challenges and 86% reported connectivity problems, and continence improvement was modest.<sup>[[37]](#ref37)</sup>

## Accessory Safety Notice

An April 2025 FDA recall concerned specified Enhanced Verify evaluation handsets, CFN HH90130FA, that could fail to communicate and interrupt evaluation therapy. The affected serial list and replacement instructions should be checked. This was an evaluation-handset action and not an instruction to explant functioning implanted InterStim systems.<sup>[[21]](#ref21)</sup>
