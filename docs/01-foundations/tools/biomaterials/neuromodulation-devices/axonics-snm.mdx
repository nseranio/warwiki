# Axonics Sacral Neuromodulation Systems

**Axonics**, now part of Boston Scientific, makes rechargeable and recharge-free sacral neuromodulation (SNM) systems. This page describes the hardware and device-specific evidence. The [InterStim/SNM hub](/docs/foundations/tools/biomaterials/neuromodulation-devices/medtronic-interstim) covers broader trial comparisons, neurogenic selection and long-term reintervention.

## History

The original Axonics r-SNM was the first FDA-approved rechargeable SNM system, designed to deliver therapy for at least 15 years.<sup>[[5]](#ref5)</sup> Its design targeted two limitations of earlier non-rechargeable generators. Battery depletion required replacement surgery, and average battery life was about 4.4 years in the historical device.<sup>[[4]](#ref4)</sup> Early European experience (RELAX-OAB) preceded the US pivotal study (ARTISAN-SNM), and the FDA approved the system in 2019.<sup>[[5]](#ref5)[[10]](#ref10)</sup> Boston Scientific acquired Axonics in 2024. The current F15 and R20 are successors to that first rechargeable generator.

## Current Models

| System | Power and model | Manufacturer specification |
|---|---|---|
| **F15** | Recharge-free, model 4101; 10 cc | Projected 17.6-year battery life at 1 mA, 14 Hz, 210 µs and 1200 Ω; life varies with energy requirements |
| **R20** | Rechargeable, model 5101; 5 cc | Designed for 20+ years; current typical recharge interval six to ten months |

R20 is not recharge-free. Older R15/ARTISAN charging schedules do not apply to R20. Neither projected battery life nor a small generator guarantees freedom from revision. Both current systems use constant-current stimulation. The generator, lead and accessories are selected together.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup>

The rationale for a long-lived generator is that a nonrechargeable device requires replacement surgery when the battery is depleted. A rechargeable device is expected to reduce replacement surgery, but revisions unrelated to the battery still occur, so a rechargeable system does not eliminate ongoing maintenance.<sup>[[4]](#ref4)[[11]](#ref11)</sup>

## Labeling, MRI and Patient Tasks

US labeling covers OAB symptoms and urinary retention after inadequate response or intolerance to conservative therapy, plus chronic fecal incontinence after failure of or unsuitability for conservative therapy. It excludes mechanical urinary obstruction. An appropriate test-stimulation response is required, and inability to operate the system is a contraindication.<sup>[[2]](#ref2)</sup>

The fully implanted systems are MR Conditional. Eligibility and scanner limits depend on model, lead configuration and current MRI instructions, and external trial equipment has different restrictions. Full-body 1.5 T and 3 T labeling applies under those conditions and is not unrestricted MRI access. Diathermy is prohibited, and electrosurgery, ablation and other energy procedures need specific precautions.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[2]](#ref2)[[3]](#ref3)</sup> In one single-center revision series, the wish for an MRI-compatible device was the reason for 7 of 30 revisions.<sup>[[12]](#ref12)</sup>

The patient needs access to the remote and follow-up support, and rechargeable models also require a sustainable charging plan. Neurological-origin urinary dysfunction, pregnancy and pediatric use carry labeling limitations. Device selection accounts for dexterity, cognition, caregiver support, anticipated MRI and the patient's preferences.<sup>[[2]](#ref2)</sup>

## ARTISAN-SNM — Clinical Benefit and Denominators

ARTISAN was a single-arm prospective study of 129 people with UUI, 98% women, using an earlier rechargeable Axonics system. It used single-stage research implantation rather than a required new external test phase. Nine participants had already passed an earlier external trial without receiving a full implant. This design does not override current labeling and does not establish routine single-stage implantation as the best approach.<sup>[[6]](#ref6)[[7]](#ref7)</sup>

| Follow-up | Reported response: at least 50% UUI reduction | Other reported data |
|---|---|---|
| Six months | Approximately 90% in the all-implanted analysis | UUI episodes per day 5.6 to 1.3; 34-point ICIQ-OABqol improvement; no serious device-related adverse events |
| One year | 89% across 129 participants | UUI episodes per day 5.6 to 1.4; all participants able to recharge; 96% found recharging acceptable |
| Two years | 93% of 121 completers, versus 88% of all 129 when missing or exited participants counted as failures | UUI episodes per day 5.6 to 1.0; 94% found recharging acceptable; no unanticipated or serious device-related adverse events |

At two years, 82% achieving at least 75% reduction and 37% being dry were percentages of the 113 responders, not all implanted participants. Recharging was acceptable to 94% of the 121 follow-up participants. The study reported 14 surgical interventions in 11 of 129 participants, despite no unanticipated or serious device- or procedure-related adverse events.<sup>[[5]](#ref5)[[6]](#ref6)[[7]](#ref7)</sup>

In a secondary analysis of 124 participants with complete one-year data, 68.5% were very satisfied, 25.8% moderately satisfied and 2.4% slightly satisfied. Satisfaction correlated with the degree of symptom improvement. Nine of 14 participants who were nonresponders at one month were responders at one year.<sup>[[8]](#ref8)</sup>

These results support meaningful benefit for many patients. The uncontrolled design, predominance of women and manufacturer sponsorship limit comparisons and generalization. They are not 20-year R20 outcomes.

RELAX-OAB was a European post-market study in which 51 participants were implanted in a single stage. At three months, 31 of 34 (91%) who had responded during an initial trial period continued to benefit. One participant was explanted for infection, and 19.6% had device-related adverse events, most often stimulation discomfort resolved with reprogramming.<sup>[[9]](#ref9)</sup> At one year, 94% of initial test responders continued to respond. The one-year QoL gain was 21.1 points on ICIQ-OABqol, 84% of subjects were satisfied and 98% found charging acceptable. Device-related adverse events occurred in 21%, with stimulation discomfort in 20%.<sup>[[10]](#ref10)</sup> The 94% figure applies to initial test responders and not to all 51 implanted. Early and selected-responder studies should not be pooled informally into a universal success percentage.

## Fecal Incontinence

In a prospective series of 15 patients with fecal incontinence implanted in a single stage with the Axonics system, 13 (87%) were test responders at four weeks. Median weekly incontinence episodes fell from 8 to 1.5 at four weeks and 1.5 at six months. Of the 13 four-week responders, 12 were therapy responders at six months. Mean recharge time was 37 minutes once or twice a week, and all patients were moderately or very satisfied. There were no unanticipated device- or procedure-related adverse events.<sup>[[13]](#ref13)</sup>

## Rechargeable Versus Recharge-Free

One retrospective single-center comparison of 246 patients found revisions in 34% of 150 rechargeable recipients versus 13.5% of 96 nonrechargeable recipients (p &lt; 0.001). Difficulty charging accounted for 35.3% of rechargeable revisions. The comparison raises a counseling issue. It does not show an inherent class-wide disadvantage across models, and it does not show that the newest R20 carries the same burden.<sup>[[11]](#ref11)</sup>

Trial acceptability of charging (94% to 98% across the Axonics studies) contrasts with a single-center observational cohort of 37 patients who received a rechargeable InterStim Micro for fecal incontinence, most as replacements for nonrechargeable devices. In that cohort, 62% expressed regret, 96% reported charging challenges and 86% reported connectivity problems. That cohort used a different manufacturer's device and a different indication. It shows that ARTISAN satisfaction does not guarantee acceptability in an individual patient.<sup>[[6]](#ref6)[[7]](#ref7)[[14]](#ref14)</sup> Device choice should connect expected treatment benefit with the patient's ability to manage the specific system.

The 2025 SNM-versus-implantable-tibial meta-analysis (20 studies, 1,416 SNM and 350 implantable tibial patients) used separate single-arm cohorts and no randomized head-to-head comparison. Weighted UUI responder rates were 71.8% for SNM and 71.3% for implantable tibial neuromodulation, and OAB responder rates were 73.9% and 79.4%. Device-related adverse event rates were similar. The implantable tibial studies did not use a trial phase. Similar pooled rates do not establish equivalence or cheaper lifetime care.<sup>[[15]](#ref15)</sup>

## Guideline Position

The AUA/SUFU 2024 OAB guideline does not distinguish between manufacturers. It states that clinicians should offer sacral neuromodulation, percutaneous tibial nerve stimulation and/or intradetrusor botulinum toxin injection to patients with an inadequate response to, or intolerable side effects from, pharmacotherapy or behavioral therapy (Moderate Recommendation; Evidence Level Grade A). Clinicians may offer minimally invasive therapies without requiring prior trials of behavioral, non-invasive or pharmacologic management (Expert Opinion), and may offer them to patients unable or unwilling to undergo those therapies (Clinical Principle).<sup>[[16]](#ref16)</sup>

## Tined-Lead Insertion Safety Update

October 2025 notices from Health Canada and the Australian TGA described difficulty advancing some model 1201 tined leads through the introducer sheath, associated with adhesive affecting tine deflection. The notices recommend backup kits and exchange of affected devices. Implanted-lead performance was not affected. The Australian correction was closed in January 2026. If resistance is encountered, check current local manufacturer instructions. Do not force advancement or assume an implanted lead needs removal.<sup>[[17]](#ref17)</sup>
