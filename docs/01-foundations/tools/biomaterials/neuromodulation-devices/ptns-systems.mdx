# Percutaneous Tibial Nerve Stimulation — Office-Based Devices

PTNS is an office-based tibial nerve stimulation therapy delivered by external devices, and the treatment burden is the main practical difference from implanted systems. For patient assessment, insertion technique and clinical alternatives, see [PTNS procedure](/docs/surgical-techniques/04f-incontinence-procedures/procedures/percutaneous-tibial-nerve-stimulation).

## Available Systems

| System | Hardware |
|---|---|
| **Urgent PC — Laborie** | External stimulator (9 V alkaline battery) with a single-use lead set: lead wire with a fuse that limits it to one session, two 34-gauge needle electrodes and a surface electrode. The international IFU lists urgency, frequency, urge incontinence and fecal incontinence<sup>[[7]](#ref7)</sup> |
| **NURO — Medtronic** | External stimulator with a rechargeable lithium battery and a permanently attached lead wire, run on purchased therapy credits (one per session) with single-use needle, holder and electrode pad. The 2013 FDA 510(k) (K132561, Advanced Uro-Solutions) covers OAB symptoms and cites Urgent PC (K101847) as the equivalent predicate<sup>[[2]](#ref2)[[8]](#ref8)</sup> |

Both deliver tibial stimulation without an implant. Urgent PC, originally marketed by Uroplasty and now by Laborie, received FDA 510(k) clearance in 2005 and was the first widely used PTNS system.<sup>[[6]](#ref6)</sup> Electrode placement, connections and programming follow each system's instructions. The [PTNS procedure page](/docs/surgical-techniques/04f-incontinence-procedures/procedures/percutaneous-tibial-nerve-stimulation) lists the needle position and electrical parameters from the Urgent PC instructions (20 Hz, 200 μs, current 0 to 9 mA, 30-minute sessions).<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup><sup>[[2]](#ref2)</sup><sup>[[7]](#ref7)</sup> The NURO 510(k) summary states the same waveform, pulse width and frequency for both devices.<sup>[[8]](#ref8)</sup>

## Course and Evidence

A commonly studied induction course is 30 minutes weekly for 12 weeks. In the 220-participant SUmiT sham-controlled trial, 54.5% versus 20.9% reported moderately or markedly improved overall bladder symptoms at 13 weeks. This is a global-improvement outcome and not a percentage of patients made dry.<sup>[[3]](#ref3)</sup>

Maintenance is individualized. STEP enrolled 50 initial SUmiT responders, of whom 29 completed three years; after a taper, the median maintenance frequency was 1.1 treatments per month. This selected-responder study does not establish a fixed interval or durable response for everyone starting PTNS. Across the 50 enrolled initial responders, 29 of whom completed the protocol, a Bayesian model accounting for early withdrawal estimated that 77% (95% CI 64–90) maintained moderate or marked improvement in overall bladder symptoms at three years, and the burden of ongoing visits and possible symptom recurrence is part of counseling.<sup>[[4]](#ref4)</sup> The visit burden of induction and maintenance was one driver for implantable tibial systems.

## Eligibility and Precautions

US manufacturer summaries describe OAB-associated urgency, frequency and urge incontinence. They list contraindications including pacemakers or implantable defibrillators, excessive bleeding risk, relevant nerve damage, and pregnancy or planned pregnancy.<sup>[[2]](#ref2)</sup> Treatment through compromised skin is avoided, and the full device instructions govern use. Minor insertion-site bleeding, discomfort and inflammation can occur.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup><sup>[[2]](#ref2)</sup>

The 2024 AUA/SUFU guideline allows shared decisions about minimally invasive therapies without mandatory preceding treatment trials. Statement 25 specifically recommends offering percutaneous tibial stimulation, SNM and/or intradetrusor botulinum toxin after inadequate response or intolerance to behavioral therapy or medication. The older “third-line only” description does not reflect that framework.<sup>[[5]](#ref5)</sup>

## Implants and Surface-Electrode Therapy

[eCoin](/docs/foundations/tools/biomaterials/neuromodulation-devices/ecoin), [Revi](/docs/foundations/tools/biomaterials/neuromodulation-devices/revi-system) and [Altaviva](/docs/foundations/tools/biomaterials/neuromodulation-devices/altaviva) reduce repeat office stimulation visits but introduce implantation, maintenance and device-specific safety requirements. The AUA 2024 implantable-device section does not transfer the PTNS recommendation to these systems.<sup>[[5]](#ref5)</sup>

Transcutaneous tibial nerve stimulation (TTNS) uses surface electrodes and can support a home protocol. Its device, regimen, population and evidence differ from percutaneous and implanted stimulation; a shared target nerve does not establish interchangeable efficacy. See the [procedure hub](/docs/surgical-techniques/04f-incontinence-procedures/procedures/percutaneous-tibial-nerve-stimulation) for that comparison.
