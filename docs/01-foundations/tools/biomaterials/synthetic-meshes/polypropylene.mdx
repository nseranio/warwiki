# Polypropylene Mesh

**Polypropylene (PP)** is a permanent synthetic mesh used in midurethral slings for stress urinary incontinence (SUI) and abdominal sacrocolpopexy for apical pelvic organ prolapse (POP). A sling, an abdominal prolapse mesh and a transvaginal POP mesh are different devices and procedures. Their evidence and regulatory status differ and are discussed separately here.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup>

This is the material hub. For selection and operative management, see [Female SUI](/docs/clinical-conditions/03a-storage-incontinence/sui-female), [Prolapse Repair](/docs/surgical-techniques/04g-prolapse-repair) and [Mesh Complications](/docs/surgical-techniques/04g-prolapse-repair/mesh-complications).

## Material Properties

PP is a semi-crystalline synthetic thermoplastic. Modern pelvic-floor PP devices commonly use macroporous monofilament mesh (Amid type I); NICE specifies type 1 macroporous PP for retropubic midurethral slings.<sup>[[2]](#ref2)[[15]](#ref15)</sup> Porosity, filament structure, weight, stiffness and deformation all affect the tissue response. A pore-size threshold does not guarantee against bacterial colonization, exposure or pain.<sup>[[2]](#ref2)[[3]](#ref3)</sup>

| Amid type | Structure | Example | Use in pelvic-floor surgery |
|---|---|---|---|
| I | Macroporous, monofilament | Polypropylene | Current standard for slings and sacrocolpopexy |
| II | Microporous | ePTFE (Gore-Tex) | Historical |
| III | Macroporous with multifilament or microporous components | Polyester (Mersilene) | Historical |
| IV | Submicronic | Silastic | Not used |

Amid's classification comes from hernia surgery, and the "use" column is a summary of current practice, not part of the original scheme.<sup>[[15]](#ref15)</sup>

In animal models, PP evoked a host response that was less inflammatory than, or similar to, that of other synthetic and biologic meshes, and a lightweight, large-pore structure fared best.<sup>[[17]](#ref17)</sup> The vaginal niche has a distinct hormonal, immune and microbial environment, which has been proposed to amplify the inflammatory response to transvaginal mesh.<sup>[[3]](#ref3)</sup>

Mesh deformation after tensioning has been proposed as a mechanism of complications. In a rhesus macaque model, meshes implanted with collapsed pores or wrinkles produced vaginal exposure and myofibroblast proliferation with fibrosis at 12 weeks, whereas flat, open-pore meshes did not. Uniform, open-pore geometry after tensioning is therefore a design goal ("effective porosity").<sup>[[23]](#ref23)[[5]](#ref5)</sup>

PP is not biologically inert: implantation produces a tissue response, and the vaginal environment differs from the abdominal wall. Claims that PP degradation has been definitively disproved are too strong. One explant study attributed apparent surface cracking to removable protein or fixation artifacts, whereas subsequent materials literature continues to describe possible in-vivo degradation. Neither position establishes an individual patient's cause of pain or predicts failure from an explant image alone.<sup>[[3]](#ref3)[[4]](#ref4)[[5]](#ref5)</sup>

Lighter mesh is not automatically a safer interchangeable device. Page's 2023 sacrocolpopexy study compared consecutive prospective cohorts, including a lightweight partially absorbable PP/poliglecaprone product. At a median follow-up of about 8 years, graft-related complications were 13/178 (7.3%) with lightweight (28 g/m²) versus 23/101 (22.8%) with heavier-weight (more than 44 g/m²) PP; graft-related reinterventions were 5/238 (2.1%) versus 19/101 (18.8%). Subjective improvement (PGIC 4 or higher) and anatomical failure did not differ between groups. Different outcome denominators and nonrandom allocation limit causal attribution to weight alone, and the audit was supported by Ethicon.<sup>[[6]](#ref6)</sup>

## Evidence by Operation

| Application | Clinically useful interpretation |
|---|---|
| **Midurethral sling for SUI** | AUA/SUFU includes retropubic, transobturator and single-incision slings among options for appropriately evaluated index patients, alongside autologous fascia, Burch colposuspension and bulking. Discuss permanent-mesh risks and alternatives before selection.<sup>[[7]](#ref7)</sup> |
| **Retropubic versus transobturator sling** | The 2017 Cochrane review found broadly similar short-term subjective cure. Reported subjective cure at up to 1 year ranged from 71% to 97% with retropubic and 62% to 98% with transobturator slings; beyond 5 years the ranges were 51% to 88% and 43% to 92%. Retropubic placement had more bladder perforation (4.5% vs 0.6%) and voiding dysfunction; transobturator placement had more groin pain (6.4% vs 1.3%). Repeat incontinence surgery was more likely after transobturator placement (4 trials, low-certainty evidence). A single route-wide cure percentage should not be quoted.<sup>[[8]](#ref8)</sup> |
| **Single-incision sling** | FDA's April 2024 assessment found comparable effectiveness and adverse-event types/rates to traditional MUS through 36 months. The 2023 Cochrane review supports similar 12-month efficacy versus transobturator slings, while retropubic comparisons and longer-term outcomes remain less certain.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[9]](#ref9)</sup> |
| **Abdominal sacrocolpopexy** | Remains a distinct mesh application outside the FDA transvaginal POP order. In the CARE trial, the Kaplan-Meier estimate of mesh erosion at 7 years was 10.5% (95% CI 6.8–16.1%), with 59% of participants completing 7-year follow-up. Product, concomitant procedures and length of follow-up affect exposure risk. Vaginal POP-kit complication rates cannot simply be transferred to sacrocolpopexy.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[6]](#ref6)[[16]](#ref16)</sup> |
| **Transvaginal permanent mesh for POP** | In the 2024 Cochrane review, lower prolapse awareness and repeat prolapse surgery were offset by more total repeat surgery for prolapse, SUI or exposure (RR 1.56, 95% CI 1.07–2.26), bladder injury and de novo SUI. Exposure was 11.8%, with 6.1% undergoing exposure surgery. These are POP-mesh data, not MUS estimates.<sup>[[10]](#ref10)</sup> |

A large English administrative cohort of 95,057 women found estimated 9-year risks of 3.3% for sling removal, 4.5% for further SUI surgery and 6.9% for either. These overlapping outcomes are not additive, and reoperation does not capture every symptomatic complication. Nine-year removal risk was 2.7% after transobturator and 3.6% after retropubic insertion.<sup>[[11]](#ref11)</sup> In a New York State cohort of 36,195 women, estimated 7-year risks were 3.7% for erosion and 6.7% for reoperation.<sup>[[19]](#ref19)</sup>

## Safety and Complication Assessment

AUA/SUFU states that clinicians should not place a mesh sling after inadvertent urethral injury (Clinical Principle) or concurrently with urethral diverticulectomy, urethrovaginal fistula repair or urethral mesh excision (Clinical Principle). Clinicians should strongly consider avoiding mesh with radiation injury, substantial scarring or poor tissue quality (Expert Opinion).<sup>[[7]](#ref7)</sup>

Potential problems include vaginal exposure, urinary-tract perforation, infection, voiding dysfunction, recurrent incontinence, urgency, pain and dyspareunia. A percentage from a specialist mesh-complication referral series is not the incidence among all implant recipients. Mesh removal may itself cause injury or recurrent prolapse or incontinence and may not resolve pain.<sup>[[2]](#ref2)[[8]](#ref8)</sup>

**Exposure.** Reported exposure rates depend on the operation. After transvaginal POP mesh, the Cochrane review found exposure in 11.8% and surgery for exposure in 6.1%.<sup>[[10]](#ref10)</sup> After retropubic and transobturator slings, Cochrane reported tape exposure in 21 and 24 per 1000 respectively.<sup>[[8]](#ref8)</sup> A 2026 meta-analysis of six studies (n = 3068; fixed-effects pooling) reported exposure prevalence from 1.3% to 13.7% after MUS, and associated prior pelvic surgery (OR 10.37, 95% CI 6.70–16.07), diabetes (OR 4.63), smoking (OR 3.38), obesity (OR 2.79) and postmenopausal status (OR 2.34) with exposure.<sup>[[20]](#ref20)</sup> In the New York cohort, prior hysterectomy was associated with higher and high-volume facilities with lower erosion risk.<sup>[[19]](#ref19)</sup>

**Pain and dyspareunia.** In a retrospective multicenter series of 347 women referred to four tertiary centers for mesh complications, 42.7% had exposure, 34.6% pelvic pain and 30% dyspareunia; 77% had a grade 3 or 4 complication and 60% needed two or more interventions. These are referral-center data. Pain associated with mesh may not resolve after removal. Peripheral nerve neuromodulation has been described in a single case report of refractory retropubic sling pain.<sup>[[18]](#ref18)[[22]](#ref22)</sup>

NICE's management framework includes:<sup>[[2]](#ref2)</sup>

- For a single vaginal exposure smaller than 1 cm², discuss topical vaginal estrogen and review within 3 months.
- Consider vaginal mesh removal for larger exposure, extrusion, failure of conservative treatment or the patient's informed preference; the extent of removal requires individualized counseling.
- Refer urinary-tract perforation or complex symptoms to a specialist mesh service. Persistent pain without an identified mesh abnormality can require pelvic-floor, pain and other multidisciplinary treatment rather than automatic complete excision.

ACOG Committee Opinion 694 advises examination for exposure or erosion in women with persistent vaginal bleeding, discharge or recurrent urinary tract infection after mesh placement, and states that asymptomatic women have no role for intervention. It lists observation, physical therapy, medication and surgery as management options.<sup>[[21]](#ref21)</sup>

An asymptomatic recipient does not need preventive removal solely because a mesh product was withdrawn.<sup>[[12]](#ref12)</sup> New bleeding, discharge, pain, dyspareunia, infection or voiding symptoms warrants evaluation.<sup>[[12]](#ref12)[[21]](#ref21)</sup>

## Regulatory Distinctions

| Jurisdiction | Scope |
|---|---|
| United States | FDA's April 2019 order stopped sale/distribution of the remaining transvaginal POP products. It did not remove SUI slings or abdominal POP mesh.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup> |
| Australia | TGA published its decision in December 2017 and cancelled transvaginal POP meshes and single-incision mini-slings from the ARTG in January 2018. Its current information distinguishes standard midurethral slings and abdominally placed prolapse mesh, which remain available. Australia did not ban every MUS.<sup>[[13]](#ref13)</sup> |
| England / NICE guidance | The July 2018 high-vigilance restriction, which covered vaginally inserted mesh for both SUI and POP, allowed specified exceptions; it was not a permanent, uniform UK-wide ban on all mesh. NICE recommendations differ from AUA's in route selection and use of single-incision slings; current local restrictions also apply.<sup>[[2]](#ref2)[[14]](#ref14)</sup> |
| **FDA milestones** | October 2008 Public Health Notification and July 2011 Safety Communication on transvaginal mesh; January 2016 reclassification of transvaginal POP mesh to class III (premarket approval); July 2018 order to stop selling the last posterior-compartment POP mesh; April 2019 order covering the remaining transvaginal POP products.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup> |

Record the exact implant and route. Brand family, material name or a generic hernia indication does not establish suitability for a different pelvic-floor operation.

## Future Directions

- Lightweight, large-pore designs that keep their pore structure after tensioning are intended to reduce pore collapse and downstream complications.<sup>[[5]](#ref5)[[23]](#ref23)</sup>
- Three-dimensionally printed patient-specific meshes based on pelvic imaging, electrospun scaffolds and bioabsorbable composites have been proposed.<sup>[[24]](#ref24)</sup>
- Mandatory mesh registries and longitudinal surveillance have been proposed.<sup>[[3]](#ref3)</sup>
