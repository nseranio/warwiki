# Surgical Energy Devices in Urology

This page compares energy mechanisms, model-specific limits and the evidence most relevant to functional and reconstructive urology. The linked instrument pages hold detailed device information; the operative hubs hold patient selection and procedural technique. A brand name, small electrode or low displayed wattage does not establish a safe thermal distance.

## Electrosurgery: Circuit and Tissue Effect

Electrosurgery heats tissue using high-frequency current. In **monopolar** electrosurgery, current travels from the active electrode through the patient to the return circuit. **Bipolar** instruments localize the circuit between working electrodes, usually without a separate return pad; current still passes through patient tissue. Conventional bipolar coagulation and an advanced vessel-sealing system are not interchangeable.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[2]](#ref2)</sup>

| Concept | Practical meaning |
|---|---|
| Cutting | Rapid heating and vaporization can divide tissue. A continuous lower-voltage waveform is common, but the mode is generator-specific. |
| Coagulation/desiccation | Heating denatures protein and removes water. Several different waveforms and feedback systems are called “coag.” |
| Fulguration | Arcing across a gap in an approved application; the visible surface effect does not prove shallow injury. |
| Tissue effect | Depends on waveform, voltage, contact area, tissue impedance, compression, duration and repeated activation. Equal displayed watts do not imply equal effects. |
| Thermal spread | Depends on the specific device and test conditions. An animal histologic distance or temperature threshold is not a validated boundary protecting a human nerve, ureter or bowel. |

Monopolar hazards include insulation failure, direct coupling to another instrument, capacitive coupling and return-electrode injury. Bipolar instruments reduce some circuit-related risks but still produce local and residual heat. There is no universal “coag is ten times the voltage,” fixed duty cycle or safe number of millimeters for every generator and task. See [Electrosurgical Pencil](/docs/foundations/tools/instruments/cautery/electrosurgical-pencil), [Bovie Tips](/docs/foundations/tools/instruments/cautery/bovie-tip) and the robotic [Monopolar Curved Scissors](/docs/foundations/tools/instruments/robotic/monopolar-curved-scissors).<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[3]](#ref3)</sup>

### Monopolar Hazards and Bipolar Advantages

- **Insulation failure:** a break in electrode insulation discharges current to unintended tissue.
- **Capacitive coupling:** current is induced through intact insulation into adjacent conductors, mainly in laparoscopy.
- **Direct coupling:** the active electrode touches another instrument.
- **Return-electrode burns:** poor pad contact concentrates current.
- **TUR syndrome:** monopolar resection requires hypotonic, nonconductive irrigation (glycine, sorbitol), and absorption causes hyponatremia and volume overload.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[4]](#ref4)[[18]](#ref18)</sup>

Bipolar resection allows saline irrigation and needs no return pad. In a 12-dog canine model, monopolar TURP raised intraprostatic temperature by 24.2 °C versus 6.8 to 8.1 °C with two bipolar systems, and thermal damage was 0.59 mm versus 0.07 to 0.15 mm. No temperature rise was measured near the neurovascular bundles with any device. These are animal data and do not define a safe human distance.<sup>[[19]](#ref19)</sup>

## Bipolar Versus Monopolar TURP

The 2019 Cochrane review of 59 randomized trials and 8,924 participants found little or no clinically important difference in symptom improvement. Bipolar TURP probably reduces classic TUR syndrome and transfusion; certainty was moderate for these outcomes and erectile function, but low for incontinence and repeat TURP. Low-certainty similarity is not proof of equivalence.<sup>[[4]](#ref4)</sup>

Bipolar resection permits saline irrigation and substantially reduces the problem of absorbing hypotonic irrigation. It does not prevent fluid absorption or make prolonged resection unlimited: saline absorption can cause volume overload and hyperchloremic metabolic acidosis. Monitor fluid balance and the patient's physiology according to the procedure and anesthetic plan. Reports of this complication establish its possibility, not a universal incidence or a replacement monitoring protocol.<sup>[[5]](#ref5)</sup>

Patient selection, technique and postoperative care belong in the [TURP operative hub](/docs/surgical-techniques/bph-male-luts/turp).

## Laser Platforms

Laser-tissue interaction is temperature-dependent. Above 60°C, protein denatures and tissue coagulates, producing delayed tissue sloughing and prolonged postoperative dysuria; near the boiling point of water, about 100°C, tissue vaporizes, which is the goal of prostate ablative surgery because tissue is removed from the field immediately; above 150°C, tissue chars (carbonization).<sup>[[31]](#ref31)</sup>

| Platform | Typical wavelength and operation | Main role in this scope | Important distinction |
|---|---|---|---|
| Holmium:YAG | Approximately 2,100 nm; pulsed | Lithotripsy and HoLEP | Settings, pulse modulation, fibers and thermal effects vary by system. |
| Thulium fiber laser (TFL) | Approximately 1,940 nm; lithotripsy uses pulses | Lithotripsy; selected soft-tissue/enucleation platforms | Not the same laser as thulium:YAG. The SOLTIVE specification is 1,920–1,960 nm. |
| Thulium:YAG | Approximately 2,010 nm; continuous or pulsed depending on platform | Prostatic vaporization/enucleation | Do not transfer a TFL lithotripsy result to all thulium tissue lasers. |
| GreenLight | 532 nm; hemoglobin-targeted photovaporization | PVP for benign prostatic obstruction | Device generation, patient selection and the antithrombotic plan matter. |
| Diode systems | Wavelength and absorption vary | Selected soft-tissue/prostatic applications | “Diode” alone does not specify tissue penetration, technique or evidence. |
| Nd:YAG | Approximately 1,064 nm; absorbed by oxyhemoglobin; continuous or pulsed | Rarely used in contemporary urologic practice; historically for a large hemorrhagic prostate | Deepest tissue penetration and least predictable coagulation zone of the lasers on this table; carries the largest reported share of urologic laser adverse events (see below). |

"Penetration depth" for a given laser and chromophore is conventionally the distance over which about 90% of the laser energy is deposited; the remaining energy can still travel farther depending on the chromophore, so it is not an absolute cutoff.

Wavelength-related optical absorption is not the same as lateral thermal injury or a safe working distance. Follow the exact laser, fiber, endoscope and eyewear instructions; a wavelength table is not an operating protocol.<sup>[[6]](#ref6)[[7]](#ref7)[[8]](#ref8)[[17]](#ref17)</sup>

Ho:YAG is the most widely used platform across lithotripsy, HoLEP and urethral stricture incision. Its effect is water-absorbed and shallow, and fiber size and settings vary by system. In stone surgery it was the only laser technology and the gold standard until TFL and pulsed Tm:YAG arrived (EAU 2026).<sup>[[6]](#ref6)</sup>

| Clinical scenario | Reasonable platform |
|---|---|
| Ureteroscopic lithotripsy | Ho:YAG or TFL |
| HoLEP | Ho:YAG (largest evidence base and training infrastructure) |
| Thulium enucleation (ThuLEP) | Thulium:YAG or TFL |
| Vaporization in a patient on antithrombotics | GreenLight, with an antithrombotic plan (see below) |
| Urethral stricture incision | Ho:YAG |
| One-platform budget | Ho:YAG covers lithotripsy, HoLEP and stricture incision |

This is a practical summary, not a guideline recommendation.

### Lithotripsy

The EAU 2026 guideline strongly recommends either Ho:YAG or TFL for (flexible) ureteroscopic lithotripsy, and rates them the most effective systems (evidence summary LE 2a). Comparative findings vary between studies and meta-analyses. EAU cites one meta-analysis that could not show TFL superiority in stone-free rate, though operative time was shorter, and another that found higher stone-free rate, shorter operative time and shorter stay with TFL but a higher sepsis rate. TFL is not uniformly superior across stone-free rate, operative time and complications. In the 2024 meta-analysis of 11 studies, TFL had a higher stone-free rate for renal stones (OR 3.14, 95% CI 1.69 to 5.86) but not for ureteral stones, and a lower intraoperative complication rate (OR 0.34); operative time (p = 0.051) and major complications did not differ. It also has advantages in retropulsion and fiber size, which come from bench and clinical technique reports rather than from these pooled endpoints.<sup>[[20]](#ref20)</sup> Its pulsed lithotripsy mode should not be described as “continuous-wave dusting.”

Both systems can heat irrigation fluid. EAU 2026 states that laser lithotripsy converts most energy to heat, that intraluminal temperature can pass the cytotoxic threshold (about 43 °C) within seconds when irrigation is inadequate, and that both Ho:YAG and TFL can generate injurious temperatures. Power, activation duration, irrigation and effective outflow influence temperature; raising irrigation pressure to cool the field can introduce a different risk. Intrarenal pressure and infection control require attention alongside fragmentation efficiency. The guideline's intrarenal pressure and temperature sections provide the relevant framework. A pooled adverse-event signal does not establish a specific device-caused mechanism.<sup>[[6]](#ref6)</sup>

### Prostate surgery

Energy choice does not replace anatomical enucleation skills or patient counseling. Use the [HoLEP/enucleation](/docs/surgical-techniques/bph-male-luts/prostate-enucleation-holep) and [GreenLight PVP](/docs/surgical-techniques/bph-male-luts/greenlight-pvp) hubs for technique and outcome comparisons. EAU 2026 strongly recommends GreenLight PVP as an alternative to TURP for 30–80 mL prostates; its recommendation in patients receiving antiplatelets or anticoagulants with prostates below 80 mL is weak. Hemostatic capability does not make PVP risk-free or automatically permit continuation of every antithrombotic. In a 13-study meta-analysis, GreenLight and thulium vaporization had comparable symptom, flow, complication and reoperation outcomes; thulium vaporization was about 8.6 minutes shorter.<sup>[[9]](#ref9)[[21]](#ref21)</sup>

### Fiber safety update

:::warning[SOLTIVE fiber correction]
Olympus's May 6, 2025 US device correction addresses damaged SOLTIVE fibers and possible thermal events. It calls for checking the aiming beam for leakage along the fiber or strain relief before use, placing the laser in standby when damage is suspected and replacing a fiber that fails inspection or testing. A distal aiming beam does not exclude damage elsewhere. The notice permits continued use with the specified precautions; it is not a blanket withdrawal of the platform. Use the current local notice and IFU for the exact system and fiber.<sup>[[10]](#ref10)</sup>
:::

## Ultrasonic Instruments and Advanced Vessel Sealers

Ultrasonic devices such as Harmonic ACE act by high-frequency blade vibration that denatures protein through friction. Uses in this field include hilar and perirenal dissection, pelvic lymph node dissection and fine dissection near the neurovascular bundle. Older laparoscopic series reported it for nephrectomy and partial nephrectomy, with limited sealing of vessels above about 3 mm in the porcine work below.<sup>[[22]](#ref22)</sup>

Ultrasonic instruments convert mechanical vibration into tissue effects, including heat; they do not pass electrosurgical current through the patient. This does not make the jaws cool, the plume harmless or the device automatically safe beside a nerve. Bench work documents very hot active blades and residual heat after activation, but does not establish one cooling interval for all instruments and tissues.<sup>[[11]](#ref11)</sup>

| Device example | Model-specific distinction | Detailed page |
|---|---|---|
| Harmonic family | Sealing capacity depends on the exact product and mode; ACE+7 has a separate Advanced Hemostasis indication up to 7 mm. This does not extend that indication to every Harmonic or historical robotic instrument. | [Harmonic ACE](/docs/foundations/tools/instruments/robotic/harmonic-ace) |
| LigaSure | Many models are indicated for vessels up to 7 mm; jaw shape, generator, rotation and tissue restrictions vary. Rotation is not wrist articulation. | [LigaSure](/docs/foundations/tools/instruments/cautery/ligasure) |
| da Vinci Vessel Sealer Extend | Up to 7 mm; mechanical cutting component; sterile single use. | [Robotic Vessel Sealers](/docs/foundations/tools/instruments/robotic/vessel-sealers) |
| da Vinci SynchroSeal | Up to 5 mm; RF cutting rather than VSE's mechanical blade; sterile single use. | [Robotic Vessel Sealers](/docs/foundations/tools/instruments/robotic/vessel-sealers) |
| Standard robotic bipolar graspers | Grasping/coagulation instruments are not automatically validated vessel sealers with a generic 3–5 mm capacity. | [Maryland](/docs/foundations/tools/instruments/robotic/maryland-bipolar), [Fenestrated](/docs/foundations/tools/instruments/robotic/fenestrated-bipolar), [Force Bipolar](/docs/foundations/tools/instruments/robotic/force-bipolar) |
| Thunderbeat | Combines ultrasonic frictional heat with bipolar energy in one instrument; a porcine comparison found higher versatility and shorter dissection time than Harmonic ACE, LigaSure V and EnSeal, with similar burst pressure and thermal spread.<sup>[[33]](#ref33)</sup> | — |

In a porcine comparison of energy devices (in vivo burst pressures and full-thickness peripheral energy spread), the results were:<sup>[[23]](#ref23)</sup>

| Device | Mean artery burst pressure | Peripheral spread, artery |
|---|---|---|
| LigaSure V | 536 mmHg | 4.5 mm |
| Harmonic ACE | 436 mmHg | 0.6 mm |
| Harmonic LCS-C5 | 363 mmHg | 0.3 mm |

The authors concluded that LigaSure sealed vessels up to 7 mm and ACE up to 5 mm. An earlier porcine study found the 5-mm LigaSure sealed arteries to 6 mm and veins to 12 mm, and that the Harmonic scalpel did not reliably seal vessels above 3 mm but had the least collateral injury (0 to 1 mm).<sup>[[24]](#ref24)</sup> These generations of device differ from current models, so use the current label.

Clinical reports in urology are limited. In a 32-patient open series, LigaSure use was associated with shorter operative time (113 vs 135.5 min) and less blood loss (529 vs 642 mL) in radical prostatectomy compared with the surgeon's prior technique.<sup>[[25]](#ref25)</sup> In an 80-patient randomized trial at cystectomy, LigaSure and a stapler had similar blood loss (687 vs 708 mL), and device cost was lower with LigaSure ($625 vs $1,490).<sup>[[26]](#ref26)</sup>

These are label and product distinctions, not a ranking of clinical superiority. Follow vessel-size, tissue-bundle, jaw-loading, activation and contraindication instructions for the actual model. Animal burst-pressure results do not authorize sealing larger human vessels, and a small study in one operation does not establish the best device for all reconstruction.<sup>[[2]](#ref2)[[12]](#ref12)[[13]](#ref13)</sup>

## Aquablation

Aquablation removes tissue with an image-guided waterjet. The waterjet is nonthermal, but hemostasis may include cautery or laser, and the complete procedure still carries bleeding, continence and sexual-function risks. EAU 2026 strongly recommends it as an alternative to TURP for 30–80 mL prostates, particularly when ejaculation preservation matters. The 2019 Cochrane review found one randomized trial (184 men, prostates up to 80 mL, 12-month follow-up): symptom improvement was probably similar to TURP (moderate certainty), and ejaculatory dysfunction may be slightly less (very low certainty).<sup>[[27]](#ref27)</sup> Larger-gland evidence includes the single-arm WATER II cohort; it should not be described as randomized proof of unlimited size independence. Waterjet treatment time is also not total operating time.<sup>[[9]](#ref9)</sup>

See [Aquablation](/docs/surgical-techniques/bph-male-luts/aquablation) for selection, functional outcomes and hemostasis.

## Morcellation After Enucleation

Morcellation mechanically fragments and retrieves enucleated adenoma. Systems differ: the Piranha uses an oscillating blade and suction. Manufacturer descriptions of tissue capture do not eliminate bladder-injury risk. Published technical experience supports training and careful technique and do not support a universal grams-per-minute target or an assumption that serious injury cannot occur.<sup>[[14]](#ref14)[[15]](#ref15)</sup>

Two designs exist. Oscillating systems (Piranha) rotate and reciprocate a blade; reciprocating systems move it linearly. A video-based report from 436 GreenLEP cases by three surgeons describes technique and troubleshooting for the oscillating type; ultrasound has been described as an aid to completing morcellation in difficult HoLEP cases.<sup>[[14]](#ref14)[[15]](#ref15)[[28]](#ref28)</sup>

Maintain a clear view of the working blade and adequate bladder distension; control bleeding and irrigation and outflow before proceeding. Stop cutting when visualization is lost and re-establish the field. Do not import laparoscopic renal or uterine specimen-containment advice into routine intravesical prostate morcellation as an unvalidated “use an entrapment bag” instruction. Keep the morcellator in the bladder lumen and do not advance it against resistant tissue. Avoid morcellating near the trigone and ureteral orifices. The operative [enucleation hub](/docs/surgical-techniques/bph-male-luts/prostate-enucleation-holep) holds the procedural workflow.

## Safety Across Modalities

- **Identify the exact system.** Check compatible instruments, fibers, cannulas, generator modes and single-use or reprocessing requirements. A general “all-metal trocar” rule cannot replace platform instructions.
- **Inspect and visualize.** Check insulation, tip covers and fiber integrity. Activate only with the intended target and working end visible; avoid unintended metal contact or energizing another instrument.
- **Account for residual heat.** Stopping activation does not instantly cool a blade or jaw. A cold mechanical maneuver immediately after activation can still transfer heat.
- **Protect critical structures.** Consider cold dissection, pressure, clips or sutures at delicate planes; changing to bipolar or ultrasonic energy alone does not guarantee protection.
- **Prevent fire and plume exposure.** Coordinate oxidizer delivery and ignition sources, allow preparation to dry according to its label, holster idle instruments and evacuate smoke at its source. Laser eyewear must match the system's wavelength and required optical density.
- **Plan for implanted electronic devices.** Use a device-specific perioperative plan, monitoring and restoration of altered therapies; see the [electrosurgical pencil](/docs/foundations/tools/instruments/cautery/electrosurgical-pencil) for the AHA-based framework.

- **Laser-specific adverse events** are dominated by equipment failure rather than tissue effect. A review of the FDA MAUDE database (1992–2012, clinically related events) and the Rockwell Laser Industries accident database (1964–2005, mostly research-related events) found 433 urologic laser adverse events; 46% resulted from generator failure or fiber-tip breakage. Eye injuries, ranging from corneal abrasion to permanent vision loss, were reported in 164 events with Nd:YAG, KTP and diode lasers and in none with holmium. Patient harm accounted for 8.3% of events, including 7 deaths: 4 from air embolism with Nd:YAG and 3 from ureteral perforation with Ho:YAG causing retroperitoneal bleeding. No adverse events were reported with Tm:YAG.<sup>[[32]](#ref32)</sup>
- **Active electrode monitoring (AEM)** detects insulation failure and capacitive coupling and shunts stray current. It has been described for laparoscopic monopolar surgery, but adoption is limited and it is not a substitute for inspection and technique.<sup>[[29]](#ref29)</sup>
- **Use the lowest effective power,** avoid prolonged activation without tissue contact, and prefer bipolar or cold dissection near the nerve bundle, ureter and bowel.<sup>[[3]](#ref3)[[30]](#ref30)</sup>

These principles complement the actual IFU and team training. They do not supply universal settings or a fixed thermal safety margin.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[3]](#ref3)[[10]](#ref10)[[16]](#ref16)</sup>
