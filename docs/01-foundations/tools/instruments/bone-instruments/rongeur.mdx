# Rongeur

A **bone rongeur** removes bone with cutting jaws operated by a manual handle. Jaw size, curvature and mechanical linkage differ. Leksell-type instruments commonly use a double-action mechanism; the exact device's intended tissue and dimensions should guide selection.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup><sup>[[2]](#ref2)</sup>

## Instrument Families

Leksell, Stille and smaller bone-biting patterns provide different access and bite profiles. A Kerrison-type punch has a footplate and cutting arrangement suited to particular exposures. Fine [pituitary rongeurs](/docs/foundations/tools/instruments/bone-instruments/pituitary-rongeur) often have a different intended use and are not automatically substitutes for cortical-bone cutting.

## Use in Reconstructive Urology

A rongeur may be used during selected pubic-bone debridement or to shape a planned bony exposure in complex posterior reconstruction. The indication and extent of removal must follow the clinical and operative plan.

For [urethropubic fistula with osteomyelitis](/docs/clinical-conditions/03f-fistulas/in-males/urethropubic), removal of infected or nonviable bone is part of a broader source-control and reconstruction strategy. “Debride to viable tissue” should not be converted into a rule to remove all cortex until cancellous bone bleeds. Bleeding is one assessment cue, affected by tissue condition and prior treatment; it does not independently define a safe resection boundary.

## Handling Principles

- Obtain a visible purchase suited to the jaw capacity; avoid oversized bites that can fracture bone or damage the instrument.
- Protect adjacent soft tissue and keep it out of the jaws. Reassess a deep or poorly seen bite before closing.
- Clear fragments to maintain visibility. Do not use the jaws as a lever against unexposed structures.
- Elevate periosteum only where needed for the planned exposure, and avoid stripping it routinely.
- Inspect the jaws and linkage before and after use, and follow model-specific reprocessing instructions.

No comparative evidence cited here establishes a universal speed or precision ranking over an osteotome or powered drill. Tool selection depends on the task, access, bone quality and operator experience.
