# Osteotome and Mallet

An **osteotome** is a chisel-like manual cutting instrument used to divide or shape bone, driven by controlled strikes of a surgical mallet. It is the oldest bone-cutting method in orthopedic surgery and gives a defined, straight cut. Blade width, curvature, bevel and handle design vary, and the name does not imply a standard blade size. One catalogued lateral osteotome, for example, has a 10 mm tip on a 33 cm instrument.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup>

## Design

- **Blade.** Flat or curved, with a bevel on one or both sides. A single-bevel (chisel) blade cuts preferentially in one direction. A double-bevel blade is symmetrical and cuts in line with the handle axis.
- **Handle.** A straight metal handle with a striking surface at the proximal end.
- **Named patterns** include Lambotte, Stille and Smith-Petersen osteotomes.
- **Mallet.** Metal or nylon-faced. A nylon face reduces noise and rebound. The Mead mallet is a common pattern. Use a compatible striking surface that does not mar the handle.

## Use in Reconstructive Urology

- **Partial pubectomy** for [urethropubic fistula](/docs/clinical-conditions/03f-fistulas/in-males/urethropubic) with osteomyelitis: the osteotome makes the initial defined cut and a rongeur completes the removal.
- **[PFUI](/docs/special-populations/05a-trauma-emergencies/pfui) reconstruction** with inferior pubectomy: the osteotome defines the cut line for controlled bone removal.
- **Inferior pubectomy** to expose the membranous urethra in deep posterior urethroplasty: a straight cortical cut with the osteotome, finished with a rongeur.

The need for bone removal comes from the reconstructive or infection-control plan, not from the instrument.

## Technique

- Expose the target bone with a [periosteal elevator](/docs/foundations/tools/instruments/bone-instruments/periosteal-elevator), stripping only as much periosteum as the cut requires.
- Position the blade at the intended cut line before applying force, with the angle set by the desired direction. Keep control of the cutting edge and anticipate where a fragment could move.
- Strike in controlled increments suited to the bone. A sudden heavy strike can propagate an unintended fracture or drive the blade past the planned plane.
- Deepen progressively. If the blade binds or the cut deviates, stop and reassess the exposure, blade angle and bone before continuing. A harder strike is not the answer.
- Account for fragments and inspect the resulting edge and nearby tissue before continuing reconstruction.
- Inspect the blade and handle before use, and follow the instrument's reprocessing instructions.

## Comparison with a Powered Drill

- **Edge quality.** Osteotome cuts are generally taught to leave a cleaner cortical edge than a burr, which can heat and damage bone. Comparative data in pubectomy are not available, so this is teaching rather than measured outcome.
- **Heat and dust.** An osteotome generates no heat and no aerosolized bone dust. Bone comes off in fragments.
- **Cost and availability.** It is inexpensive, simple and widely available.
- **Speed.** It is slower than a drill for bulk removal, and cut quality depends on mallet technique.
- **Percussion.** Vibration and noise are transmitted to the patient and the room, and percussion can still cause unintended fracture or deep penetration under general anesthesia.

A drill may suit progressive contouring, and an osteotome may suit a defined cut.
