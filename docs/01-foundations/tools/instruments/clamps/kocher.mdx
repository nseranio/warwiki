# Kocher Clamp

<img src="/img/instruments/kocher-clamp.jpg" alt="Close-up of Kocher jaws showing transverse serrations and the interlocking tip tooth" className="instrument-photo" />
*Kocher jaw detail: serrations and an interlocking tip tooth. (Image: [Wikimedia Commons](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Kocher_clamp_01.JPG), public domain.)*

The common **Kocher tissue or hemostatic clamp** has ring handles, a ratchet, serrated jaws and 1×2 interlocking teeth at the tip. It is commonly taught as the strongest-grip, most traumatic tissue clamp on the standard open tray, used for tough or fibrous tissue and large pedicles that need a firm hold. The tooth can penetrate and the jaws can crush; this is not a clamp for delicate vessel, bowel, ureteral or other tissue that must remain in situ.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup><sup>[[2]](#ref2)</sup>

## Identify the pattern

Kocher, Ochsner and Rochester-Ochsner names overlap in instrument catalogs, but size, weight and jaw pattern vary. Check the complete product name. A separate Kocher intestinal pattern or a laparoscopic instrument should not be assumed to have the same jaws as the open toothed clamp described here.

| Feature | What to inspect |
|---|---|
| **Tip teeth** | The single tooth fits between two opposing teeth; verify alignment and integrity. |
| **Serrated jaws** | Standard examples have transverse serrations along the jaw, unlike a merely toothed tip. |
| **Straight or curved shaft/jaws** | Select for the access and intended tissue bite. |
| **Ratchet and joint** | Confirm engagement and release; a fixed notch number does not specify a safe tissue pressure. |

The tip tooth is the anti-slip feature. On dense fascia or a thick pedicle, serrations alone allow rotation under traction, and the interlocking tooth holds the tissue at the tip. The same tooth is why the clamp injures any layer that must keep its wall or serosa. Sklar's Rochester-Ochsner 17-2862 is a straight 6¼-inch example. Longer Kocher models and more delicate patterns exist; the name does not establish that every version is heavier or stronger than every other clamp.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup><sup>[[2]](#ref2)</sup>

## Reconstructive use and limits

- **Fascial traction and closure:** selected edges may tolerate a toothed grasp, for example rectus fascia and external oblique aponeurosis during open bladder neck, augmentation, diversion and re-do laparotomy closures, and the cut end of a rectus fascia strip during pubovaginal sling harvest. Preserve tissue needed for closure; use of a Kocher on fascia does not imply the fascia must be discarded.
- **Specimens or tissue planned for excision:** firm purchase is useful for urachal remnants, partial cystectomy or nephrectomy specimens, excised lichen sclerosus vulvectomy skin and the spermatic cord before orchiectomy for an infected prosthesis, provided adjacent viable structures are protected.
- **Pedicle work:** broad ligament, ovarian and infundibulopelvic pedicles during open hysterectomy and oophorectomy are clamped before ligation. Choose a clamp and ligation method appropriate to the identified structure; a generic tissue-clamp description is not a vascular-control protocol.
- **Reoperative or irradiated tissue:** a Kocher can hold dense scar during re-do diversion, augmentation, AUS or post-radiation pelvic re-entry. Fibrosis does not prove tissue strength, so avoid forceful clamp traction to create an unconfirmed plane.
- **Drape and towel work** when a strong locking clamp is needed.

Use a tissue-preserving strategy for anastomotic edges, grafts and fragile viscera: a [Babcock](/docs/foundations/tools/instruments/clamps/babcock) or [DeBakey](/docs/foundations/tools/instruments/forceps/debakey) for bowel and ureter in situ, and an [Allis](/docs/foundations/tools/instruments/clamps/allis) for vaginal cuff and mesh edges. An Allis is also toothed, and a Babcock can still compress; switching names alone does not make a grasp safe. Use the first or second ratchet for traction and reserve a full crush for pedicles about to be ligated and divided. Remove the clamp as the operative step permits, because prolonged crush with rotation can twist and tear tissue, and assess the tissue before proceeding.

Choose by the actual jaw geometry and task. The usual teaching order of grip, from the least to the most traumatic, is Babcock, Allis, then Kocher; catalog patterns overlap, so it is not a validated ranking.

| Clamp | Tip and serrations | Typical fit |
|---|---|---|
| **Kocher** | 1×2 tip tooth, transverse serrations | Fascia, dense scar, pedicles, discarded tissue |
| [**Allis**](/docs/foundations/tools/instruments/clamps/allis) | Short interlocking teeth | Skin, fascia, vaginal cuff, resected tissue |
| [**Babcock**](/docs/foundations/tools/instruments/clamps/babcock) | Smooth, fenestrated | Bowel, ureter, hollow viscera in situ |
| [**Kelly**](/docs/foundations/tools/instruments/clamps/kelly) | Distal-half serrations, no teeth | Small vessel hemostasis, dissection |
| **Rochester-Carmalt** | Longitudinal and transverse cross-hatch, no tip tooth | Large pedicles, tissue bundles |

The tip tooth separates the Kocher from the otherwise similar Rochester-Carmalt.

## Historical context

The clamp is named for **Emil Theodor Kocher** (1841–1917) of Bern, who received the 1909 Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine for thyroid surgery and was the first surgeon to win it. He introduced many instruments, of which the Kocher clamp is still in use.<sup>[[3]](#ref3)</sup>
