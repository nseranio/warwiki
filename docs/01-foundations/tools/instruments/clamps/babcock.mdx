# Babcock Clamp

<img src="/img/instruments/babcock.jpg" alt="Two Babcock forceps showing broad fenestrated jaws and ratcheted ring handles" className="instrument-photo" />
*Babcock forceps with broad, fenestrated jaws. The design does not eliminate compression injury. (Image: [Wikimedia Commons](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Babcock.JPG), public domain.)*

A **Babcock forceps** is a ratcheted, ring-handled tissue clamp with rounded, fenestrated jaws, used for selected bowel and tubular-tissue handling. It lacks the projecting interlocking tip teeth of an Allis or Kocher. **Toothless does not mean smooth:** the Sklar 36-1662 pattern has horizontal serrations on its grasping surface.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup>

## What the design does

The broad contact surface is intended to distribute the grasp over more tissue than a sharp tip. “Atraumatic” or “non-crushing” describes the intended handling pattern, **not immunity from ischemia, perforation or tearing**. Closing the jaws still loads the tissue; the window does not create a pressure-free enclosure.

| Feature | Practical implication |
|---|---|
| **Fenestrated jaws** | Useful for selected tubular or soft-tissue handling; inspect what is actually caught between the surfaces. |
| **Ratchet** | Holds closure without continuous hand pressure. No notch guarantees an injury-free force. |
| **Model variation** | Length, jaw dimensions and surface finish differ. A standard Sklar example is 6¼ inches (longer patterns exist for deep exposure); inspect the actual instrument.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup> |
| **Delicate or compromised tissue** | Reduce direct grasping and traction; choose another exposure method when safe purchase cannot be maintained. |

## Traditional ranking of tissue clamps

Textbook teaching places the Babcock at the gentle end of a series that runs Babcock, [Allis](/docs/foundations/tools/instruments/clamps/allis), [Kocher](/docs/foundations/tools/instruments/clamps/kocher) from least to most traumatic. The Babcock is used for tissue that stays in situ, Allis for tissue to be excised or that tolerates focal injury, and Kocher for fascia and discarded tissue that needs strong purchase. The ranking is convention, not comparative injury data.

The gentler grasp costs purchase. Tissue can slip under traction, and squeezing harder to compensate adds trauma. When stronger purchase is needed on tougher tissue, change to an Allis or a thumb forceps rather than closing the Babcock further.

## Reconstructive use

Babcock forceps are used to handle selected bowel, appendix and other tubular tissue. The viable wall and its blood supply must be protected throughout the maneuver. The instrument name alone does not justify routinely locking a clamp across a ureter, vas, vascular pedicle or thin repair edge.

- **Urinary diversion.** Handling of the bowel segment during ileal conduit, orthotopic neobladder and continent cutaneous reservoir construction: segment isolation, mesenteric window development, antimesenteric opening, detubularization and pouch assembly.
- **Augmentation cystoplasty.** Mobilization and reconfiguration of an ileal, ileocecal or sigmoid patch.
- **Catheterizable channels.** Handling of the appendix or a Yang-Monti ileal tube without crushing the channel wall.
- **Ureteral reconstruction.** Mobilization, reimplant setup, ureteroureterostomy and ileal ureter interposition.
- **Bladder.** Grasp during cystotomy, bladder flap mobilization and partial cystectomy.
- **Vas, cord and sacs.** Handling of the vas, spermatic cord, or a hernia or hydrocele sac when a clamp gives more stable presentation than thumb forceps.

If tissue slips, reassess exposure, the bite and the instrument. Increasing compression or automatically changing to a toothed clamp can trade slipping for tissue injury. Avoid pulling against fixed resistance and repeated grasping at one fragile point.

Do not use a Babcock for skin, fascia, vaginal cuff, mesh or specimens (use an Allis), or for vessels (use a vascular clamp or [DeBakey](/docs/foundations/tools/instruments/forceps/debakey) forceps).

Laparoscopic and robotic graspers have their own jaw geometries, closing forces and instructions. Atraumatic bowel graspers, including the robotic ProGrasp and fenestrated designs, follow the same idea, but a fenestrated appearance does not make them interchangeable with an open Babcock.

## Technique

- **Grip.** Thumb and ring finger through the rings, index finger along the shank.
- **Encircle.** Surround the tubular structure with the fenestration and lock at the lowest ratchet position that holds. No notch guarantees an injury-free force.
- **Duration.** Release as soon as the step allows repositioning. A gentle clamp still injures if held long enough.
- **Pairing.** A [Singley](/docs/foundations/tools/instruments/forceps/singley) fenestrated thumb forceps serves handheld work on bowel and peritoneum. The Babcock is used when the segment must stay locked in position.

## Handling and care

Inspect the jaws, alignment, box lock and ratchet. Use only the closure and traction required for the task, release when feasible, and check the tissue after the grasp. Follow the model's cleaning and sterilization instructions; a reusable instrument supplied nonsterile needs validated processing before use.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup>

## Historical context

The clamp is named for **William Wayne Babcock** (1872–1963), professor of surgery at Temple University. His other eponyms include the Babcock operation for varicose veins, the Babcock-Bacon operation for rectal and sigmoid cancer, the Babcock probe and a sump drain.<sup>[[2]](#ref2)</sup>
