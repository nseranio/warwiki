# Gemini Dissecting Forceps

**Gemini** denotes a slender dissecting-forceps pattern in the right-angle/curved-clamp family. Teleflex describes it as a more delicate modification of Mixter, with **acutely curved, serrated jaws**. A catalog name does not establish a precise 90° bend, a universal size or proven tissue-safety superiority.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[2]](#ref2)</sup>

## Match the Model to the Field

The current Teleflex family lists **18.5, 23 and 28.5 cm** lengths. Gemini instruments are therefore not invariably short or limited to 14–20 cm. Check the catalog number, jaw profile, tip width, working length and handling of the actual instrument.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup>

| Feature | Practical consequence |
|---|---|
| Slender curved jaws | May fit a prepared passage that is too narrow for a heavier clamp; this does not justify forcing an unexposed plane. |
| Serrated grasping surface | Can hold a tie or loop but can also injure delicate tissue. “Fine” is not equivalent to atraumatic. |
| Long variants | Provide reach; they do not guarantee the same tactile control as a shorter instrument. |
| Ratcheted handle | Allows locking, but the target structure does not necessarily need to be compressed. |

## Gemini and Mixter

| Feature | Gemini | [Mixter](/docs/foundations/tools/instruments/clamps/mixter) |
|---|---|---|
| Jaw profile | Slender, acutely curved, serrated | Right-angle jaw, fine to medium |
| Reach | 18.5, 23 and 28.5 cm in the Teleflex family | Length varies by model |
| Usual fit | Small structures in a tight plane | General pass-around and tie |

Larger structures or deeper fields call for a standard Mixter or a sturdier right-angle clamp. Many teams keep several sizes available and change as the dissection requires.

## Uses in Reconstruction

Use a suitable Gemini to pass a ligature or loose identification loop around a structure that has been exposed and separated safely. Follow the [Mixter pass-around principles](/docs/foundations/tools/instruments/clamps/mixter#controlled-pass-around-technique): identify the target, follow the tip, avoid blind spreading, and confirm what the loop surrounds before applying tension.

Situations where a slender right-angle pattern is commonly chosen include:

- **Vas and cord.** Fine ties around the vas, vasal vessels or spermatic cord branches during vasovasostomy, varicocelectomy and orchidopexy.
- **Penile dissection.** Fine dissection near the dorsal neurovascular bundle during plication, penile disassembly or penectomy.
- **Dorsal venous complex.** Small tributaries during open retropubic prostatectomy and cystectomy. A modified right-angle clamp has been described for dividing the puboprostatic ligaments and dorsal vein complex as a single unit.<sup>[[3]](#ref3)</sup>
- **Hypospadias and pediatric work.** Glanular vessels and the urethral plate, and scaled-down structures in orchidopexy, reimplantation and pyeloplasty.
- **Vulvar and introital reconstruction.** Labial and clitoral vessels during labiaplasty, vestibulectomy and clitoral reconstruction.
- **Lymphatic and microvascular preparation.** Small recipient veins and lymphatics.

These are common uses of a fine right-angle pass-around clamp, not indications established for the Gemini by comparative data. Nerves, urethral plates, lymphatics and microvascular anastomoses require handling and instruments suited to those tissues, and the dedicated procedure's technique governs.

## Inspection and Selection

Check tip alignment, serrations, hinge and locking action. Do not force a resistant plane or use delicate jaws to lever dense tissue. If exposure or reach is inadequate, improve the exposure or change instruments. Follow the particular model's cleaning and sterilization instructions.

Delicate jaws can spring or splay if forced, and small misalignments cost the precision the design is meant to provide. Use the lightest touch. No comparative clinical evidence establishes Gemini as the least traumatic member of the family, so select by anatomy and intended maneuver, with the actual model's design in view.

## Naming

Gemini is a catalog name rather than the eponym of a single surgeon-inventor. Institutions may call the instrument a fine right angle or a small Mixter, and ordering by that description can communicate more reliably than "Gemini" alone.
