# Adson Tissue Forceps

**Fine, short, spring-action thumb forceps** often used for skin and superficial-tissue handling in reconstructive-urology and urogynecology closure. Catalog variants include toothed (1×2), smooth-serrated, and Brown-Adson (7×7 fine teeth) tips; select the actual tip for the tissue and exposure rather than treating one pattern as the default for every listed incision.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[4]](#ref4)</sup>

## Design

- **Length**: about 12 cm (4.75 in). Short enough for fine bimanual skin work, too short for deep pelvic exposure.
- **Profile**: wide flat thumb grip tapering to narrow, finely tapered tips.
- **Mechanism**: spring-action thumb forceps; the two arms are joined proximally and held open by the metal's spring.
- **Material**: surgical-grade stainless steel, autoclavable; disposable variants also widely available.

### Working-Surface Variants

| Variant | Working surface | Best tissue |
|---|---|---|
| **Adson-toothed (1×2)** | Single tooth interlocking between two opposing teeth | Skin edges, dartos, fascia during closure |
| **Adson smooth-serrated** | Fine transverse serrations, no teeth | Selected delicate superficial tissue; finer dedicated instruments may be preferable for vessels, nerves or mucosa |
| **Adson-Brown (7×7)** | Multiple fine interlocking teeth across a wider platform | Plastic / reconstructive skin handling where 1×2 teeth slip |
| **Double-ended Adson** | Toothed jaws on one end, smooth on the other | Hand and head-and-neck cases requiring frequent switching<sup>[[2]](#ref2)</sup> |

## The Toothed-vs-Smooth Trade-Off

Toothed Adson tips can secure dermis with less squeezing than a smooth tip, but tooth puncture and traction still cause injury. Smooth/serrated tips avoid tooth punctures yet can slip and tempt excessive compression. In an in-vitro sheep-stomach jaw experiment (not an Adson clinical trial), larger teeth improved grip but increased measured tissue trauma relative to wave-pattern jaws.<sup>[[5]](#ref5)</sup> Match the jaw to the tissue, use the least force that controls it, and avoid implying that smooth tips are inherently safe on nerves, small vessels or vasal/ureteral mucosa.

The Brown-Adson variant has a broader 7×7 fine-tooth platform used in some plastic-surgery skin handling.<sup>[[4]](#ref4)</sup> Whether that pattern reduces injury in thin or sun-damaged skin compared with a specific Adson tip has not been established by the cited evidence.

## Technique

- **Grip**: pencil grip between thumb and index, stabilized by the middle finger.
- **Match the variant to the tissue layer and exposure**: fine teeth may help on skin; use appropriately delicate, often dedicated instruments for vessels, nerves and urethral or ureteral mucosa rather than assuming a smooth Adson is ideal.
- **Brief, focal grasp**: the goal is to evert and present the wound edge for needle passage, not to crush it. Repeated regripping at the same point compounds focal trauma.
- For dermal skin closure, a toothed Adson may be paired with a fine needle driver and appropriate suture. The Custis 2015 split-wound trial tested adhesive strips added to buried sutures after dermatologic excision, not Adson versus another forceps or routine reconstructive-urology closure; it should not be used to claim an Adson-specific cosmetic benefit.<sup>[[3]](#ref3)</sup>

## Reconstructive-Urology and Urogyn Uses

- **Scrotal and penile-shaft-skin closure** after hydrocelectomy, varicocelectomy, vasectomy / vasovasostomy, IPP and AUS scrotal incisions, penile-prosthesis exposure, and skin-bridge revision.
- **Suprapubic skin closure** for open BNR, augmentation, diversion, and AUS abdominal pouch incisions.
- **Vaginal introital and perineal closure** for prolapse repair, perineorrhaphy, fistula repair, episiotomy-defect revision, and post-defibulation closure.
- **Inguinal incisions** for groin-flap harvest, lymph-node dissection, and orchiopexy.
- **Microsurgical setup**: a smooth Adson may help with superficial setup or adventitial exposure, but very fine vasal or epididymal layers generally call for dedicated microsurgical forceps under magnification.

## Distinctions from Adjacent Forceps

| Forceps | Profile vs Adson | Best layer |
|---|---|---|
| **Adson** (this page) | Short, fine, 1×2 teeth or smooth-serrated | Skin, dartos, subcutaneous tissue |
| [**Gerald**](/docs/foundations/tools/instruments/forceps/gerald) | Longer, narrower, finer; microsurgical | Vasal / epididymal / ureteral mucosa, tunica |
| [**DeBakey**](/docs/foundations/tools/instruments/forceps/debakey) | Longer, atraumatic, parallel ridged platform | Vessels, ureter, bowel serosa |
| [**Bonney**](/docs/foundations/tools/instruments/forceps/bonney) | Heavier, larger, 1×2 deep teeth | Rectus fascia, abdominal wall, dense scar |
| [**Russian**](/docs/foundations/tools/instruments/forceps/russian) | Concentric serrated cup tips, no teeth | Atraumatic handling of bulkier tissues |
| [**Singley**](/docs/foundations/tools/instruments/forceps/singley) | Long, fenestrated, ring-handled | Bowel, packing |

## Historical Context

Named for Alfred Washington Adson (1887–1951), the first chair of the Section of Neurological Surgery at the Mayo Clinic and one of the founders of American neurosurgery. Adson described operations across spinal-cord tumors, peripheral nerve, and cerebrovascular disease, designed multiple instruments still in daily use, and lent his name to both the Adson test for thoracic outlet syndrome and the Adson-Brown forceps.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup>
