# Raz-Pereyra Trocar

The **Raz-Pereyra trocar** denotes a long retropubic suture carrier associated with the Pereyra and Raz needle-suspension family. Long-needle carriers can also deliver the suture arms of an autologous pubovaginal sling (PVS); the fascial graft itself remains suburethral. Instrument geometry, suture capture and reprocessing depend on the specific model. The related procedures have distinct historical and contemporary roles:

- **Historical:** transvaginal needle bladder-neck suspension, including Pereyra, Stamey and Raz variants. A Cochrane review found worse continence than open retropubic colposuspension in small trials; it did not establish a direct comparative claim against every sling technique.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[2]](#ref2)[[3]](#ref3)</sup>
- **Contemporary carrier role:** autologous PVS for selected SUI patients, including some with prior failed surgery or reasons to avoid synthetic mesh. A Raz-type carrier, Stamey needle or suitable clamp can convey the graft's sutures retropubically; do not infer that the Raz instrument is required or preferred for every PVS.<sup>[[7]](#ref7)</sup>

## Design

- **Long rigid carrier** suited to finger-guided retropubic passage. Length, curvature, tip and suture-loading mechanism must be checked against the actual model; a universal 25–30 cm/forked-tip specification is not established by the sources below.
- **Processing is model-specific.** Confirm the manufacturer's cleaning, sterilization and inspection instructions rather than assuming every carrier is reusable or autoclavable.

## Contemporary Use — Autologous Rectus-Fascia Pubovaginal Sling

One contemporary application for a long suture carrier is rectus-fascia PVS. The following is a technical outline, not a device IFU or a single mandatory PVS method; graft site, dimensions, level and tensioning vary with anatomy and surgeon technique.<sup>[[7]](#ref7)</sup>

1. **Harvest and prepare** autologous fascia according to the chosen PVS technique; attach suture arms to the graft ends.
2. Create the vaginal exposure and bilateral retropubic paths at the selected suburethral level.
3. Pass the chosen long needle or carrier under continuous vaginal-finger guidance from the suprapubic incision through the retropubic space to the vaginal incision, one side at a time.
4. Use the carrier's eyelet or other model-specific suture-loading feature to draw each graft suture arm, not the entire fascial strip, into the suprapubic field.<sup>[[7]](#ref7)</sup>
5. After both passes, perform cystoscopy to detect urinary-tract injury before final graft placement and tensioning.<sup>[[7]](#ref7)</sup>
6. Position and secure the graft without obstructive tension using the selected operative technique; the sling level and fixation are not universal.

The carrier creates a route for the suture arms; a Stamey needle or clamp is an explicitly described alternative.<sup>[[7]](#ref7)</sup>

### Why Autologous PVS Persists

- It is a non-mesh surgical option for selected patients after failed synthetic sling or with mesh complications, complex anatomy or a preference to avoid mesh; it is not universally the best salvage procedure.<sup>[[7]](#ref7)</sup>
- It may be considered for intrinsic sphincter deficiency or a fixed or immobile urethra, alongside other guideline-listed options; counsel about voiding dysfunction and other harms.
- In the SISTEr randomized comparison, five-year follow-up was published in 2012, not 2017. Its strict composite continence estimate was 30.8% after fascial sling versus 24.1% after Burch, while satisfaction remained higher in the sling group; these endpoint-specific results do not prove PVS is the "highest-durability" treatment against all alternatives.<sup>[[8]](#ref8)</sup>

See [Female Stress Urinary Incontinence](/docs/clinical-conditions) and the female-SUI surgical-techniques database for the broader treatment algorithm.

## Historical Use — Transvaginal Needle Bladder-Neck Suspension

The trocar's original 20th-century role. Three named operations defined the family:

| Operation | Year | Modifier | Anchoring strategy |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Pereyra** | 1959 | Armand J. Pereyra | Original transvaginal needle suspension; suture from periurethral tissue to anterior abdominal fascia<sup>[[4]](#ref4)</sup> |
| **Stamey** | 1973 | Thomas A. Stamey | Cystoscopy-guided suture placement with [Stamey needle](/docs/foundations/tools/instruments/urethral-specialty/stamey-needle) modification |
| **Raz** | 1981 | Shlomo Raz | Blunt endopelvic-fascia dissection; anchorage of **periurethral fascia and pubourethral ligament**; fixation over rectus muscle<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup> |

**Mechanism.** Indirect urethral support delivered through the vaginal walls (not directly to the urethra itself).<sup>[[2]](#ref2)</sup> The needle pass through the retropubic space was blind; cystoscopy was used to verify no bladder injury.<sup>[[2]](#ref2)</sup>

### Historical Outcomes

- Overall subjective success approximately 85% (range 40–100%) across approximately 2,000 procedures in the early-literature pool, limited by subjective endpoint definitions and short follow-up.<sup>[[3]](#ref3)</sup>
- **Recurrent SUI**: one small, nonrandom series of 36 women reported 83% cure after Raz and 78% after Stamey; it does not establish comparative superiority.<sup>[[5]](#ref5)</sup>
- **Postoperative voiding dysfunction in 20–50%** across studies.<sup>[[3]](#ref3)</sup>

### Why the Needle Suspensions Fell Out of Favor

- **Cochrane review (Glazener 2017).** Concluded that bladder-neck needle suspension is inferior to open retropubic colposuspension for SUI.<sup>[[2]](#ref2)</sup>
- **JAMA scientific review (Holroyd-Leduc 2004).** Reached the same inferiority conclusion at the population level.<sup>[[6]](#ref6)</sup>
- **Modern MUS and other options.** Replaced routine needle suspension, but this Cochrane comparison does not directly quantify needle suspension versus all MUS routes. UK NICE now explicitly advises not offering needle suspension for female SUI.<sup>[[2]](#ref2)[[9]](#ref9)</sup>
- **Durability is limited in some long-follow-up series.** Suture or tissue support failure is one proposed mechanism, not a demonstrated explanation for every recurrence.

Needle bladder-neck suspension is now of primarily historical importance. Long carriers remain relevant to some autologous PVS techniques; the actual instrument choice is surgeon- and model-specific.<sup>[[7]](#ref7)[[9]](#ref9)</sup>

## Lineage — Pereyra, Raz, and the URPS Family

- **Armand J. Pereyra** (Long Beach) described the original 1959 needle bladder-neck suspension, the operation that gave the family its name and the trocar its original purpose.<sup>[[4]](#ref4)</sup>
- **Shlomo Raz** (UCLA) modified the needle-suspension technique; the Raz name also identifies a surgical lineage (see [Surgical Genealogy](/docs/roots/surgical-lineage)). The available source does not establish that one named trocar became the exclusive contemporary PVS carrier.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[7]](#ref7)</sup>

## Contemporary Trocar vs Adjacent Instruments

| Instrument | Best fit | Status |
|---|---|---|
| **Raz-Pereyra trocar** | Historical Raz/Pereyra suspension; possible long-needle PVS carrier | Confirm exact model, loading and IFU before use |
| **[Stamey needle](/docs/foundations/tools/instruments/urethral-specialty/stamey-needle)** | Eyelet-bearing suture carrier for historical Stamey suspension and described PVS techniques | Instrument remains applicable even though the named suspension is historical<sup>[[7]](#ref7)</sup> |
| **[Midurethral sling trocars](/docs/foundations/tools/instruments/urethral-specialty/midurethral-sling-trocars)** (TVT / TOT family) | Mesh midurethral sling placement | Current dominant SUI operation by volume |
| **[Capio suture-capturing device](/docs/foundations/tools/instruments/urethral-specialty/capio-device)** | Sacrospinous-ligament fixation and apical POP suspension | Different operative role |
