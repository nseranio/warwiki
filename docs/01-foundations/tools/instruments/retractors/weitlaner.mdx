# Weitlaner Retractor

**Self-retaining hinged scissor-style retractor with two opposing pronged arms and a ratchet lock**, one of the most widely used superficial-to-intermediate-depth self-retaining retractors in surgery. Named for Austrian physician Franz Weitlaner (1872–1944), who published a description of the retractor in 1905.<sup>[[8]](#ref8)</sup> Squeezing the handles opens the prongs (the opposite of scissors), and the ratchet at the rings holds the retractor open at the desired width without an assistant.

## Design

- **Two opposing arms** that cross over each other scissor-style; squeezing the handles opens the prongs.
- **Ratcheted box-lock** at the ring handles, which clicks open in graded increments and holds without sustained hand pressure.
- **Pronged tips.** Typically 2×3 or 3×4 configuration (asymmetric prong counts on each side).
- **Prong variants**:
  - **Sharp prongs.** Firm grip on skin and superficial fascia.
  - **Blunt prongs.** Blunt tips avoid sharp-tip puncture. Nabatoff described minimal trauma with a different blunt-prong self-retaining retractor, and Pearl et al. described tissue damage from sharp tips on traditional self-retainers; neither study compared Weitlaner variants.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[2]](#ref2)</sup>
- **Length.** Typically 10–20 cm overall; multiple sizes for different wound depths.
- **Material**: surgical-grade stainless steel, autoclavable.
- **Low-profile design.** Keeps the instrument out of the surgical field while maintaining exposure.

## Mechanism — Self-Retaining via Ratchet

1. **Prong placement**: prongs inserted into wound edges (skin / subcutaneous tissue / superficial fascia).
2. **Hand-squeeze opens the prongs** (scissor-style mechanism in reverse).
3. **Ratchet lock** holds the prongs at the chosen separation, graded by ratchet click.
4. **Hands-free retraction** maintained throughout the procedure; no assistant required at the retractor. Self-retaining retractors in general free personnel and keep the assistant's view of the field open.<sup>[[2]](#ref2)[[5]](#ref5)</sup>
5. **Adjustable tension** by advancing or releasing ratchet clicks during the case.

## Reconstructive-Urology and Urogyn Uses

The Weitlaner fills the self-retaining superficial-to-intermediate-depth niche on RU and urogyn trays and is useful whenever sustained hands-free retraction is needed at the skin, subcutaneous or superficial-fascia level:

- **Scrotal incisions.** IPP, AUS, hydrocelectomy, varicocelectomy, vasovasostomy or orchiectomy when the dissection requires sustained hands-free exposure beyond what a handheld Senn or Adson-Beckman delivers.
- **Inguinal incisions.** Open inguinal orchidopexy, hernia repair, groin-flap harvest (Singapore or pudendal-thigh), inguinal LND.
- **Perineal incisions.** Perineal urethrostomy, transperineal RUF repair (superficial layers), perineoplasty.
- **Vulvar or introital wound exposure.** Labiaplasty, vestibulectomy, Foldès clitoral reconstruction when sustained exposure outlasts an assistant's hand.
- **Suprapubic skin and subcutaneous.** SPC placement, AUS reservoir, small open BNR exposures.
- **Lymph-node-biopsy and minor procedure exposure.** Penile-cancer sentinel-node, inguinal-LND.
- **Office and ED genital-laceration repair.** When hands-free sustained retraction matters.
- **Closure phase.** Redeploy at the subcutaneous or skin closure layer after deeper retractors are removed.

## Weitlaner vs Adjacent Self-Retaining Retractors

| Retractor | Mechanism | Best fit |
|---|---|---|
| **Weitlaner** | Hinged scissor-style, ratchet, multi-prong | Superficial / intermediate-depth wound retraction |
| [**Adson-Beckman**](/docs/foundations/tools/instruments/retractors/adson-beckman) | Hinged ratchet with two opposing pronged blades | Shallow wedge-style superficial retraction |
| **Gelpi** | Single-point sharp tips per arm | Tight wounds where multi-prong distribution doesn't fit |
| [**Lone Star**](/docs/foundations/tools/instruments/retractors/lone-star) | Disposable plastic ring + elastic stays | Soft-tissue perineal / vaginal traction without metal-blade pressure |
| [**Bookwalter**](/docs/foundations/tools/instruments/retractors/bookwalter) | Table-fixed ring + interchangeable blades | Major open abdominal / pelvic |

Weitlaner and Adson-Beckman are used interchangeably in some institutions, but prong count alone does not distinguish them: Weitlaner models include 2×3 and 3×4 prongs, while Adson-Beckman models include 4×4 prongs. Compare the actual blade depth, length and hinge geometry before substituting one for the other.

## Comparison to Handheld Alternatives

For the same depth band, the handheld equivalents are the [Senn rake](/docs/foundations/tools/instruments/retractors/senn) and the [Volkmann rake](/docs/foundations/tools/instruments/retractors/volkmann). Switch to the Weitlaner when:

- The procedure is long enough that assistant hand-fatigue compromises exposure.
- The team is short on assistants.
- The exposure needs to be stable for fine fine-motor work (suturing, microsurgery-adjacent steps).

## Limitations

- **Sharp-prong risk.** Skin or subcutaneous puncture, glove perforation, sharps-injury risk to the surgical team. Blunt-prong variant mitigates.
- **Shallow or intermediate depth only.** Switch to [Richardson](/docs/foundations/tools/instruments/retractors/richardson) or [Deaver](/docs/foundations/tools/instruments/retractors/deaver) for deep layers; [Bookwalter](/docs/foundations/tools/instruments/retractors/bookwalter) for major abdominal or pelvic.
- **Wound-edge ischemia under prolonged tension.** Reassess tissue pressure and release or reposition the retractor periodically during long cases.
- **Bilateral retraction along one axis only.** For circumferential or multi-vector exposure, switch to [Lone Star](/docs/foundations/tools/instruments/retractors/lone-star) or a ring system.

## Cross-Specialty Context

Out of WARWIKI's primary scope but relevant to multidisciplinary practice, the Weitlaner is commonly used in spine and other neurosurgical exposures, tendon and fracture work (orthopedics), carotid endarterectomy (vascular), and tracheostomy, thyroidectomy or lymph-node biopsy (general surgery and ENT). Other self-retaining multi-blade and malleable systems have been described for neurosurgery and head-and-neck plastic surgery.<sup>[[3]](#ref3)[[4]](#ref4)</sup>

## Technique

1. **Insert prongs at the wound edge.** Sharp for skin or dense subcutaneous tissue; blunt for fascia, muscle or near delicate structures.
2. **Squeeze the handles** to open the prongs; advance the ratchet to the desired tension click.
3. **Reassess and release tension periodically** in long cases; reposition earlier if tissue pressure or exposure warrants it.
4. **Match the size** to the wound: 10–14 cm for smaller incisions (scrotal, perineal); 16–20 cm for larger (inguinal, suprapubic, lymph-node biopsy).
5. **Keep sharp and blunt prongs clear of neurovascular structures.** A blunt tip does not prevent pressure injury from sustained retraction.<sup>[[7]](#ref7)</sup>

## Historical Context

Named for Franz Weitlaner, a late-19th or early-20th century surgeon whose hinged-scissor-style ratcheted retractor became one of the most widely adopted self-retaining instruments across surgical specialties. The Weitlaner belongs to the broader genealogy of self-retaining retractors that revolutionized hands-free wound exposure and reduced operative-assistant demand, alongside the [Adson-Beckman](/docs/foundations/tools/instruments/retractors/adson-beckman) and contemporaries.<sup>[[5]](#ref5)[[6]](#ref6)</sup>
