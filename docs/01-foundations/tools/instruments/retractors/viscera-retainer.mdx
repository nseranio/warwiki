# Viscera Retainer

The **Glassman Viscera Retainer**, commonly called the **FISH**, is a sterile, single-use flexible synthetic-polymer paddle temporarily placed over abdominal viscera while an internal wound layer is sutured. It is not a metal alternative to a separate FISH device: Glassman and FISH refer to the same current product family.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[2]](#ref2)</sup>

## Current Device Identity

Current FDA device records list pediatric, small, medium, large, extra-large and jumbo models in commercial distribution. The active device definition is a thin flexible synthetic-polymer paddle that may have an external removal aid; the device is supplied sterile and is single use.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup>

The manufacturer describes a pliable paddle with an integral ring used to signal and facilitate removal. Exact size, radiopacity and handling instructions must come from the label and instructions for the selected model.<sup>[[2]](#ref2)</sup>

Do not apply the FISH label to a metal malleable retractor. A [malleable retractor](/docs/foundations/tools/instruments/retractors/malleable) is a separate reusable instrument that can also provide a temporary barrier during closure.

## Practical Role in Abdominal Closure

The retainer is placed between the viscera and the abdominal-wall layer being closed. It can help keep bowel away from the needle and suture path while fascial bites are placed. Relevant settings include:

- open urinary diversion, cystectomy and augmentation cystoplasty, where mobilized or distended bowel crowds the closure plane;
- open sacrocolpopexy and large prolapse repairs closed through an extended Pfannenstiel or low midline incision;
- reoperative abdomens with adhesions, prior radiation, prior mesh or prior diversion;
- obese patients with a deep wound, where the needle tip is hardest to see;
- pediatric augmentation, Mitrofanoff and exstrophy closure, where the abdominal wall is thin and the working space small.

## Safe-Use Principles

1. Confirm the product, size, package integrity and label instructions before use.
2. Place the paddle so the suture path is separated from the underlying viscera without trapping bowel against the wound edge.
3. Keep the removal ring or other removal aid visible and include the device in the operative count.
4. Withdraw the device before completing external wound closure, then visually confirm removal and inspect the protected field as exposure permits.
5. Do not reuse a single-use FISH or substitute undocumented reprocessing instructions.

The device must come out before the last sutures are placed, so the final fascial bites are made without a shield.<sup>[[6]](#ref6)</sup> Common practice is to withdraw it in steps as bites are tied, keep the bowel edge in view with a handheld retractor for the last one or two bites, and have an assistant hold the bowel back. This is a technique commonly taught rather than a tested protocol.

The need for removal is clinically important. A single FDA adverse-event report documents a retained model 3204 detected radiographically after surgery and removed at reoperation; that report demonstrates a possible failure mode but cannot estimate its incidence.<sup>[[3]](#ref3)</sup>

## Evidence Boundaries

No reviewed clinical comparison establishes FISH-versus-no-FISH outcome effects. Published enterotomy studies instead describe the background risk during ventral-hernia repair or adhesiolysis:

- In an Americas Hernia Society Quality Collaborative cohort of 5,916 ventral-hernia repairs, recognized inadvertent enterotomy occurred in 1.9% and was associated with increased surgical-site infection, reoperation, readmission and mortality.<sup>[[4]](#ref4)</sup>
- In a prospective cohort of 133 abdominal-wall repairs, 17 patients (12.8%) sustained enterotomy during adhesiolysis; adhesiolysis duration was the independent predictor.<sup>[[5]](#ref5)</sup>

Retrospective and review data put the risk higher in reoperative abdomens. Inadvertent enterotomy occurred in 52 of 270 reoperations (19%) in a single-center retrospective review, most often when dividing lower abdominal and pelvic adhesions, and a review cites rates as high as 20% in open surgery.<sup>[[7]](#ref7)[[8]](#ref8)</sup>

None of these studies tested a viscera retainer or isolated needle injury during final closure, so none of the rates should be presented as device efficacy.

The 2023 WSES emergency-laparotomy guideline found very limited evidence comparing blunt-tapered with sharp needles for closure and made no recommendation. It did not evaluate the FISH.<sup>[[9]](#ref9)</sup>

## Investigational Biodegradable Barrier

Kaymakcalan and colleagues described a puncture-resistant degradable CC-DHA elastomer intended to remain in situ during closure. Testing included mechanical work and a murine laparotomy model showing dissolution without observed toxicity or intra-abdominal scarring.<sup>[[6]](#ref6)</sup> This is preclinical biomaterial evidence, not a clinically validated or routinely available replacement for a current viscera retainer.
