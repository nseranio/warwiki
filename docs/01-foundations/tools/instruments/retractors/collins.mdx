# Collin (Collins) Abdominal Retractor

The **Collin**, also called Collins in clinical literature, is a **self-retaining abdominal retractor** that holds wound edges apart without continuous manual traction. It is encountered in lower-abdominal gynecologic and cesarean surgery and can be useful when a transverse abdominal incision gives the required reconstructive access.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup>

## Design and configurations

The exact frame, spreading mechanism and blade set depend on the model. KLS Martin lists complete Collin retractors with paired lateral blades and a central blade. Two examples illustrate the differences:<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup>

| Catalog model | Lateral blades | Central blade | Listed spread |
|---|---|---|---|
| **15-824-00-07** | 45 × 80 mm | 50 × 75 mm | 100 mm |
| **15-826-00-07** | 60 × 80 mm | 60 × 75 mm | 140 mm |

The catalog lists compatible replacement blades and a spare screw for the larger model. Identify the actual locking mechanism before use; “Collins” does not mean every model has the same ratchet or interchangeable blades.

This is an abdominal instrument, distinct from similarly named **Collin vaginal specula** and **Collin-Hartmann handheld retractors**. Specify the pattern and blade configuration when preparing a tray.

## Practical applications and setup

For a low abdominal incision, the lateral blades maintain the wound opening while an additional blade or handheld instrument supplies directed deeper exposure. The frame does not replace the need to mobilize the operative plane. In a narrow, deep or scarred pelvis, a table-mounted [Bookwalter](/docs/foundations/tools/instruments/retractors/bookwalter), [Omni-Tract](/docs/foundations/tools/instruments/retractors/omni-tract) or [Thompson](/docs/foundations/tools/instruments/retractors/thompson) may offer more independently adjustable directions of traction.

A practical setup sequence is:

1. Check the assembled frame, locks and matching blade attachments before placement.
2. Select blades that support the abdominal wall without extending unnecessarily into the pelvis.
3. Place blades under direct vision, excluding bowel and other structures from the contact and locking surfaces.
4. Open incrementally to obtain the required view. Add directed exposure where needed rather than relying on maximal lateral spread.
5. Reassess blade depth and tissue pressure after changes in positioning, packing or the operative target.
6. Release traction and remove blades in a controlled sequence, accounting for removable components.

Avoid concentrated pressure on deep lateral pelvic structures. There is no single “over the psoas” or “medial to the psoas” instruction that makes all blade placements safe. The tissue in contact with the blade must be identified and reassessed.

## Relationship to a wound protector

A Collin holds the wound with metal blades. A dual-ring [wound protector/retractor](/docs/foundations/tools/instruments/retractors/o-ring-wound-protector) combines circumferential retraction with a sheath lining the incision. These are mechanically different tools; the appropriate choice depends on access and the operation.

Cesarean studies comparing plastic sheath retractors with conventional retraction are relevant to that setting. They should not be converted into a universal infection-risk ranking for all reconstructive operations, or a rule that benefit appears or disappears at a particular BMI. The original trial and systematic review remain linked below for this separate clinical-outcome question.
