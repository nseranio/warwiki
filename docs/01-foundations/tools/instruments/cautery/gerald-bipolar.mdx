# Gerald Bipolar Forceps

**Broad-flat-tip bipolar electrosurgical forceps** — the bipolar variant of the [Gerald thumb forceps](/docs/foundations/tools/instruments/forceps/gerald) lineage adapted for **hemostasis on slightly larger vessels and broader tissue surfaces** than fine-tip jeweler-style bipolars can comfortably grip. Sits between the **jeweler / microbipolar** (fine pointed tips) and the **[Malis](/docs/foundations/tools/instruments/cautery/malis-bipolar)** (mirror-finish anti-stick) on the bipolar-forceps spectrum.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[2]](#ref2)</sup>

## Design

- **Tip profile**: **broad, flat, smooth** — often called a **platform** or **paddle** tip. The tip width is the defining feature that separates the Gerald bipolar from jeweler-style bipolars; tip widths typically **1–2 mm** (broader variants exist).
- **Shaft**: straight, bayonet, or angled depending on manufacturer; the **bayonet** configuration keeps the surgeon's hand out of the line of sight during deep pelvic or microsurgical work.
- **Insulation**: shanks fully insulated; only the tips serve as active and return electrodes.
- **Material**: tips in stainless steel, titanium, or **gold-plated metal** — gold-plated and non-stick-coated tips significantly reduce tissue adherence and produce less structural damage to coagulated vessels.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[3]](#ref3)</sup>

## How Bipolar Differs from Monopolar

Bipolar current passes **only between the two forceps tips**, so:

- **Thermal spread is limited to the tissue grasped between the tips** — no current path through the patient's body.
- **No dispersive return pad required** — eliminates the dispersive-pad-burn class of complications listed on the [electrosurgical-pencil page](/docs/foundations/tools/instruments/cautery/electrosurgical-pencil).
- **Safer in patients with pacemakers, ICDs, and cochlear implants** — no significant EMI through the patient.
- **Coagulation only** — bipolar forceps cannot cut tissue; use [monopolar](/docs/foundations/tools/instruments/cautery/electrosurgical-pencil) for division.

## Reconstructive-Urology and Urogyn Uses

The broad-tip Gerald bipolar is the right instrument **whenever bipolar is preferred for safety but a fine-tip / jeweler bipolar would slip off or pierce the vessel rather than seal it**:

- **Peri-urethral and peri-corporal vessel control** during urethroplasty (anastomotic, BMG ventral / dorsal onlay, posterior, Mathieu / TIP for hypospadias), Peyronie's plication, and penile-disassembly procedures.
- **Penile dorsal NVB-adjacent dissection** — bipolar control of small dorsal-venous tributaries without conducting current into the dorsal nerve / artery bundle.
- **AUS and IPP scrotal exposure** — small dartos and tunica-vaginalis bleeders where monopolar near the device could damage the cylinder, cuff, or pump.
- **Vasovasostomy / microsurgical varicocelectomy** — coagulation of small parallel veins and adventitial vessels; for the finest vasal-wall vessels switch to a jeweler-style microbipolar.
- **Vulvar and introital fine work** — labial / vestibular vessels during labiaplasty, vestibulectomy, Foldès clitoral reconstruction, post-defibulation closure.
- **Peri-ureteral bleeder control** during ureteral mobilization, reimplantation, ureterolysis — monopolar near the ureter risks delayed thermal stricture; bipolar Gerald is the safer choice.
- **Pediatric urology** — hypospadias, orchidopexy, pyeloplasty, where smaller-field bleeders benefit from low-collateral-spread coagulation.
- **CIED patients** — the default cautery instrument for any open RU/urogyn case in a pacemaker / ICD patient, replacing as much monopolar as feasible.
- **Microsurgical flap-pedicle skeletonization** — gracilis / omental / SCIP / IGAP / PAP / DFAP pedicle vessels during reconstructive flap work.

## Gerald Bipolar vs Adjacent Bipolar Forceps

| Forceps | Tip | Best fit |
|---|---|---|
| **Gerald bipolar** | Broad flat 1–2 mm platform | Slightly larger vessels, peri-urethral / peri-corporal, dartos, peri-ureteral bleeders |
| **Jeweler / microbipolar** | Fine pointed, < 0.5 mm | Microsurgical perforator and microvascular work |
| [**Malis mirror-finish**](/docs/foundations/tools/instruments/cautery/malis-bipolar) | Polished, non-stick coating | Reduced tissue sticking; long microsurgical / neurosurgical cases |
| **Heat-pipe modern bipolar** | Engineered heat-dissipating tips | Reduced thermal collateral injury<sup>[[5]](#ref5)</sup> |

## Technique

- **Grasp once, activate once**: hold the vessel between the tips, activate, observe the coagulation, release. Repeated regrasping at the same point produces inconsistent coagulation and tissue charring.
- **Lower-power-first**: start at the lowest effective generator setting; increase only if coagulation is incomplete. Lower power preserves the vessel architecture and reduces tissue adherence.<sup>[[2]](#ref2)</sup>
- **Irrigation**: in-line or external saline irrigation between coagulations reduces tip sticking and improves consistency.<sup>[[3]](#ref3)[[4]](#ref4)</sup>
- **Match the variant**: drop down to a jeweler / microbipolar for microvascular layers; switch to a Malis or gold-plated Gerald for cases where adherence is the rate-limiting issue.
- **Activation discipline**: short bursts; prolonged activation produces collateral thermal injury that the bipolar's localized-current advantage was supposed to prevent.<sup>[[4]](#ref4)[[5]](#ref5)</sup>

## Limitations

- **No cutting** — bipolar forceps coagulate only; pair with [Mayo](/docs/foundations/tools/instruments/scissors/mayo) / [Metzenbaum](/docs/foundations/tools/instruments/scissors/metzenbaum) / [Potts](/docs/foundations/tools/instruments/scissors/potts) scissors or a monopolar tip for division.
- **Tissue adherence** still occurs even with gold-plated and non-stick coatings, particularly at higher power settings or on partially desiccated tissue.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup>
- **Less precise than a jeweler / microbipolar** for the very smallest perforating arteries and microvascular tributaries — choose tip width by target vessel caliber.
- **Slow** for high-bleeding fields where monopolar fulguration would clear the field faster — bipolar is precision-not-speed.

## Bipolar Coagulation Physics

All bipolar forceps share the same fundamental physics:

- Current flows only between the two tips, **driven by the tissue resistance between them**.
- Heat production is concentrated **at the tip–tissue interface** rather than along a current path through the patient.
- **Tip material** drives coagulation performance: gold-plated tips reduce adherence and structural vessel damage at equivalent settings compared to stainless steel or titanium.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup>
- **Modern heat-pipe bipolar designs** further reduce collateral thermal injury by engineering heat dissipation through the shaft.<sup>[[5]](#ref5)</sup>

## Historical Context

The bipolar coagulation paradigm was popularized in modern neurosurgery by **Leonard Malis** in the 1960s and has since become the safety-default cautery instrument for neurosurgical, microsurgical, plastic, and reconstructive work.<sup>[[2]](#ref2)</sup> The **Gerald bipolar** descends from the Gerald [thumb-forceps lineage](/docs/foundations/tools/instruments/forceps/gerald) — the broad-flat platform tip that defines the bipolar variant inherits the same precision-without-sharpness profile that made Gerald thumb forceps the microsurgery-adjacent default.
