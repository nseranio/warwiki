# Gerald Bipolar Forceps

**Gerald bipolar forceps** are a family of open bipolar coagulating forceps, with straight or bayonet handles and manufacturer-specific tip widths. They are not defined by a broad 1–2 mm paddle: for example, Novo lists bayonet versions with 0.5 or 1.0 mm tips, including irrigating options. Select the exact catalog item, and do not infer dimensions from the instrument name or a representative image.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup> The instrument is the bipolar counterpart of the [Gerald thumb forceps](/docs/foundations/tools/instruments/forceps/gerald) and is used where a fine jeweler-style bipolar tip would slip off or pierce a vessel rather than seal it.

## Design and Selection

- **Tip geometry:** match width and configuration to the vessel or bleeding point. A bayonet handle can keep the hand away from the line of sight during deep exposure.
- **Insulation and connections:** verify the intended generator, cable, intact insulation and tip alignment. Product families include different insulation and irrigation configurations.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup>
- **Tip surface:** in a laboratory assay, gold-plated tips collected significantly less adherent coagulum than titanium or stainless steel, and an artery coagulated with a gold-plated tip collapsed without destruction of its layers, whereas the other materials produced severely damaged structures. Performance still depends on the surface finish and generator, and these results are not evidence of better reconstructive outcomes.<sup>[[2]](#ref2)</sup> Irrigating tips and nonstick or heat-dissipating designs address sticking and heat spread in neurosurgical use.<sup>[[6]](#ref6)</sup>
- **Function:** conventional Gerald forceps coagulate; they do not incorporate a cutting blade. Pair with appropriate scissors when division is required. Advanced bipolar sealing-and-cutting instruments are a different device class.

## Bipolar Current and Thermal Injury

The current circuit is concentrated between the two tips through the grasped patient tissue. A separate dispersive return pad is generally unnecessary. Heat is not confined to the tissue between the jaws: lateral conduction, residual hot tips and prolonged activation can injure adjacent structures. There is no universal nerve- or ureter-safe clearance established for every forceps and generator combination.<sup>[[3]](#ref3)[[4]](#ref4)</sup>

Compared with monopolar electrosurgery, true bipolar use usually reduces electromagnetic interference with a pacemaker or defibrillator. The AHA nevertheless advises a procedure- and device-specific plan; bipolar application directly to a cardiac device can cause interference. Other implants, including cochlear or neurostimulation devices, require their own manufacturer instructions, and a blanket “bipolar is safe” rule does not apply.<sup>[[5]](#ref5)</sup>

## Reconstructive Applications

These forceps are used to coagulate visible small bleeders during open genital, urethral, perineal and flap surgery. The intended target must be distinguished from the blood supply that reconstruction depends on. Fine tips do not make activation harmless beside a dorsal neurovascular bundle, ureter, urethral plate, microsurgical anastomosis or prosthetic component.

Uses that have been described include:

- **Peri-urethral and peri-corporal vessel control** during urethroplasty, hypospadias repair and Peyronie's plication.
- **Small dorsal venous tributaries** near the penile neurovascular bundle, where bipolar energy avoids conducting current through the bundle.
- **Scrotal exposure for AUS and IPP placement.** Small dartos and tunica vaginalis bleeders, where monopolar energy near a cylinder, cuff or pump could damage the device.
- **Small veins and adventitial vessels** in vasovasostomy and microsurgical varicocelectomy. A jeweler-style microbipolar suits the finest vasal-wall vessels.
- **Vulvar and introital work**, including labiaplasty, vestibulectomy, clitoral reconstruction and post-defibulation closure.
- **Peri-ureteral bleeders** during mobilization, reimplantation and ureterolysis, where monopolar energy risks delayed thermal injury.
- **Pediatric urology**, including hypospadias, orchidopexy and pyeloplasty.
- **Flap pedicle skeletonization** for gracilis, omental and perforator flaps.
- **Patients with cardiac implantable devices**, in whom bipolar energy replaces monopolar energy where feasible.

Use cold dissection, pressure, clips or sutures when these better preserve nearby structures. Do not use broad jaws to obtain indiscriminate hemostasis across a flap pedicle, and do not assume that changing from monopolar to bipolar energy eliminates delayed thermal injury.

## Comparison With Other Bipolar Forceps

| Forceps | Tip | Typical fit |
|---|---|---|
| Gerald bipolar | Flat bayonet or straight tip, manufacturer-specific width (for example 0.5 or 1.0 mm)<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup> | Small vessels, peri-urethral and peri-corporal work, dartos, peri-ureteral bleeders |
| Jeweler or microbipolar | Fine pointed tip | Microsurgical perforator and microvascular work |
| Nonstick or mirror-finish bipolar | Polished or coated surface | Long microsurgical or neurosurgical cases where sticking limits work |
| Heat-pipe bipolar | Engineered heat-dissipating tips | Reduced thermal spread in the tested system<sup>[[4]](#ref4)</sup> |

## Technique and Care

1. Expose the bleeding point, identify adjacent structures and grasp only the intended tissue. Grasp once and activate once; repeated regrasping at the same point gives inconsistent coagulation and charring.
2. Use a compatible generator and the lowest effective setting for that instrument and task, with brief controlled activation.
3. Reassess hemostasis and tissue condition before further activation; repeated heating can increase injury or adherence.
4. Clean and, where specified, irrigate according to the device instructions. Irrigation between activations reduces tip sticking in neurosurgical use.<sup>[[6]](#ref6)</sup> Use short bursts, because prolonged activation produces the collateral thermal injury that localized current is meant to avoid. Do not mix a forceps and generator combination solely because an experimental paper reported a favorable thermal profile.
5. Account for residual heat before touching other tissue. Nonstick coatings and heat-dissipating designs reduce particular problems without making the tip nonthermal.

## Evidence Limits

Mikami's experimental assay found less adherent coagulum with the tested gold-plated forceps than titanium or steel. Keshavarzi's infrared study showed that both the forceps and generator changed heat dissipation. Elliott-Lewis compared heat-pipe and conventional forceps in calf liver and rat brain, with less injury in the tested heat-pipe system. These studies support attention to instrument design and activation conditions; they do not establish a universally superior Gerald model or a clinical safe distance in reconstructive urology.<sup>[[2]](#ref2)[[3]](#ref3)[[4]](#ref4)</sup>

## Limitations

- Conventional Gerald forceps cannot cut. Pair them with scissors or a monopolar tip when division is needed.
- Tissue adherence still occurs with gold-plated and nonstick tips, particularly at higher power or on partly desiccated tissue.<sup>[[2]](#ref2)</sup>
- Broader tips are less precise than a jeweler-style bipolar for the smallest perforating and microvascular vessels. Choose tip width by target vessel caliber.
- Bipolar coagulation is slower than monopolar fulguration for high-volume oozing.

## Historical Context

Bipolar electrocautery was developed to reduce the burn and fire risks that monopolar electrosurgery brought into the operating room.<sup>[[7]](#ref7)</sup> Leonard Malis popularized bipolar coagulation in neurosurgery in the 1960s. Bipolar forceps have since become a standard cautery instrument in neurosurgical, microsurgical, plastic and reconstructive work.<sup>[[6]](#ref6)</sup>
