# Resectoscope

Specialized endoscopic system combining a sheath, working element, compatible electrode and telescope. It is an operative platform for transurethral resection in the lower urinary tract (TURP, TURBT, bladder-neck incision) and transcervical or intrauterine resection in gynecology (hysteroscopic myomectomy, polypectomy, septum incision, endometrial ablation). Continuous-flow sheaths are common but model-specific; a monopolar electrode cannot simply be exchanged for a bipolar one without matching the working element, generator and irrigant.<sup>[[18]](#ref18)[[19]](#ref19)</sup> TURP remains an important comparator for newer BPH technologies and bladder-tumor resection techniques.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[2]](#ref2)</sup>

## Components

| Component | Detail |
|---|---|
| **Outer sheath** | Irrigation inflow/outflow according to the model; continuous-flow designs are common. **24–28 Fr** is a common urologic range, not a universal size. |
| **Inner sheath / working element** | Houses the cutting loop; spring-loaded thumb-driven mechanism converts hand squeeze into in/out loop motion (newer designs add lateral rotation) |
| **Cutting loop / electrode** | Wire loop carrying **monopolar or bipolar RF energy**; vaporization-roller / bar variants for ablation |
| **Telescope** | Rigid rod-lens (**12° or 30°**); couples to a video camera |
| **Irrigation system** | **Continuous-flow (Iglesias 1975)** — simultaneous inflow + outflow keeps the field clear at low intracavitary pressure |

### Monopolar vs Bipolar

| Feature | **Monopolar** | **Bipolar** |
|---|---|---|
| **Irrigant** | **Electrolyte-free** (1.5% glycine, 3% sorbitol) | **Normal saline** |
| **Classic hyponatremic TUR syndrome** | Risk with absorption of electrolyte-free fluid | **Substantially reduced**, not zero in pooled trials: Cochrane estimated 20 fewer events per 1,000 with bipolar TURP.<sup>[[3]](#ref3)</sup> |
| **Transfusion** | Risk varies by cohort and technique | Cochrane estimated 28 fewer transfusions per 1,000 with bipolar TURP.<sup>[[3]](#ref3)</sup> |
| **Catheterization / irrigation duration** | Reference | Shorter (~ 22 / 9 h less) |
| **Symptom / QoL outcome** | Equivalent | Equivalent |

Both monopolar and bipolar TURP remain guideline-supported in appropriately selected patients. Bipolar systems permit saline irrigation and probably reduce classic TUR-syndrome and transfusion events, but saline absorption can still cause volume overload or hyperchloremic acidosis; monitor distension or irrigation fluid according to the procedure and local protocol.<sup>[[3]](#ref3)[[4]](#ref4)[[20]](#ref20)[[21]](#ref21)</sup>

## Reconstructive-Urology and Functional-Urology Uses

The resectoscope is encountered in WARWIKI scope across three pathways:

### TURP — reference standard for BPH

- **Indications.** Moderate-to-severe LUTS refractory to medical therapy, refractory retention, recurrent UTI from BPO, bladder stones, gross hematuria of BPH origin, renal insufficiency from BPO.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[2]](#ref2)</sup>
- **Outcomes.** Mean IPSS improvement 12–15 points, mean Qmax increase of approximately 162%; retreatment rate 3–14.5% at 5 years.<sup>[[2]](#ref2)[[5]](#ref5)</sup>
- **Position in the BPH algorithm.** EAU 30–80 mL prostate; AUA 2026 reference standard against which water vapor, PUL, aquablation, PAE, and HoLEP / ThuLEP are benchmarked.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[2]](#ref2)</sup>

### TURBT — out-of-scope as a primary oncology topic, included briefly because the instrument is the same

- **Quality determinants** that drive downstream decisions: complete resection, clear operative documentation and detrusor muscle in the specimen where required.<sup>[[7]](#ref7)[[18]](#ref18)</sup> The current EAU NMIBC guideline recommends repeat TURBT after incomplete or doubtfully complete resection, absent detrusor muscle except Ta low-grade/G1 and primary CIS, or any T1 tumour, generally within 2–6 weeks. Size or multifocality alone is not listed as a universal repeat-TURBT trigger. An older high-grade Ta/T1 synthesis found 51% persistence and 8% T1 understaging; a more contemporary 81-study synthesis reported 31.4% residual disease and 2.8% upstaging in T1, so neither pair is an all-comer risk.<sup>[[18]](#ref18)</sup>
- **En-bloc resection (ERBT).** Teoh 2024 phase-3 RCT (≤ 3 cm tumors) reported 1-yr recurrence 29% versus 38% with conventional TURBT (p = 0.007); Xu 2025 meta of 12 RCTs (n = 2,097) found higher detrusor sampling (OR 1.90), lower perforation (OR 0.30) and lower obturator-reflex (OR 0.18). EAU accepts en-bloc or fractioned resection but notes that ERBT's overall superiority remains debated because other RCTs and reviews did not reproduce a recurrence advantage.<sup>[[8]](#ref8)[[9]](#ref9)[[10]](#ref10)[[18]](#ref18)</sup>

### Hysteroscopic resectoscope — urogyn-shared use

- **Submucosal myoma**, **endometrial polyp**, **uterine septum**, **intrauterine adhesions**, **endometrial ablation.** ACOG endorses direct hysteroscopic removal over blind procedures for polypectomy; the mini-resectoscope extends feasibility to the office setting.<sup>[[11]](#ref11)[[12]](#ref12)</sup>
- **Loop resectoscope versus hysteroscopic tissue-removal systems (morcellators).** Morcellators are faster and more complete for polyps and type 0–I myomas; the loop resectoscope retains the advantage for type II (deeply embedded) myomas where loop dissection is needed.<sup>[[11]](#ref11)</sup>

## Complications of TURP — the RU-relevant Long-Tail

Modern TURP complication rates vary with patient, platform and follow-up. The events most relevant to reconstructive practice downstream are urethral stricture, bladder-neck contracture and post-TURP incontinence:<sup>[[2]](#ref2)[[3]](#ref3)[[13]](#ref13)[[14]](#ref14)</sup>

| Complication | Contemporary rate | Reconstructive implication |
|---|---|---|
| **Mortality** | 0.1% | — |
| **TUR syndrome** | 0–1.4% (near-zero bipolar) | — |
| **Transfusion** | 0.4–2.9% | — |
| **Clot retention** | 2–5% | May need [3-way irrigation catheter](/docs/foundations/tools/biomaterials/urinary-catheters/three-way-catheter) |
| **Urinary retention after catheter removal** | 3–5.8% | Often detrusor failure, not incomplete resection |
| **Urethral stricture** | **2.2–9.8%** | Drives downstream [DVIU](/docs/surgical-techniques/04a-urethral-reconstruction/dviu) or urethroplasty; see [Sachse urethrotome](/docs/foundations/tools/instruments/sounds-bougies/sachse-urethrotome) |
| **Bladder-neck contracture** | **0.3–9.2%** | Drives transurethral incision / resection of BNC; see [Balloon Dilator](/docs/foundations/tools/instruments/sounds-bougies/balloon-dilator); risk factors include small prostate and prior prostatitis<sup>[[14]](#ref14)</sup> |
| **Retrograde ejaculation** | ~ 65% | Expected; counsel preoperatively |
| **Erectile dysfunction** | ~ 6.5–10% | — |
| **Stress incontinence (late)** | 0.5% | Late SUI may need AUS, sling, or PAUS; **early urge incontinence ~ 30–40% but transient** |
| **Retreatment** | 3–14.5% at 5 yr | — |

For the reconstructive surgeon, post-TURP urethral stricture, BNC, and post-prostatectomy SUI are the operative downstream consequences that bring these patients to urethroplasty, transurethral BNC incision, AUS, and sling.

## TURBT-Specific Technical Considerations

- **Obturator-nerve reflex** during lateral-wall resection causes sudden adductor contraction and risks bladder perforation. Consider an appropriate obturator block or anesthetic plan; bipolar energy alone does not reliably prevent the reflex or perforation in randomized comparisons.<sup>[[15]](#ref15)[[22]](#ref22)</sup>
- **Enhanced visualization.** Blue-light PDD and NBI improve detection of CIS and flat lesions missed under white light; see the [rigid](/docs/foundations/tools/instruments/endoscopy/rigid-cystoscope) and [flexible cystoscope](/docs/foundations/tools/instruments/endoscopy/flexible-cystoscope) pages for the BLC and NBI data.<sup>[[6]](#ref6)[[16]](#ref16)</sup>
- **En-bloc resection energy sources.** Bipolar, Ho:YAG, thulium, KTP or GreenLight, or hybrid knife; laser and hybrid-knife platforms show superior outcomes in network meta.<sup>[[17]](#ref17)</sup>

## Technique — TURP

1. **Spinal or general anesthesia**; dorsal lithotomy; cystoscopic survey.
2. **Continuous-flow video resectoscope** (24–28 Fr) with bipolar working element (preferred) and 12° / 30° telescope.
3. **Systematic resection** from bladder neck to verumontanum, leaving the verumontanum and external sphincter intact.
4. **Chips evacuated** with [Ellik evacuator](/docs/foundations/tools/instruments) or [Toomey syringe](/docs/foundations/tools/instruments); average resected mass approximately 28 g; incidental prostate carcinoma in approximately 10% of specimens.<sup>[[13]](#ref13)</sup>
5. **24–26 Fr [3-way Foley](/docs/foundations/tools/biomaterials/urinary-catheters/three-way-catheter)** with 30 mL balloon for fossa tamponade and CBI for 24–72 h.

## Limitations

- **TUR syndrome risk persists with monopolar.** Bipolar is the safer modern default.
- **Prostate size ceiling.** Large glands (typically &gt; 80–100 g) increasingly handled by enucleation (HoLEP / ThuLEP) or open simple prostatectomy.
- **Reconstructive sequelae.** Urethral stricture and bladder-neck contracture are the late events that bring TURP patients to RU clinic.
- **Hysteroscopic mini-resectoscope** has expanded office feasibility but still requires distending media monitoring.
