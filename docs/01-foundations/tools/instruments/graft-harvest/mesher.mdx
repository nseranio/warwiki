# Skin Mesher

The **skin mesher** is a mechanical device that cuts parallel rows of staggered slits into a split-thickness skin graft (STSG), allowing it to be expanded to cover a larger wound surface than the original harvested graft. Developed by James C. Tanner and colleagues in the 1960s, the Tanner mesh-graft technique enabled coverage of large wounds with limited donor skin. Meshing is also used selectively in genital, perineal and scrotal reconstruction when drainage, conformity or donor-area economy matters.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[2]](#ref2)[[3]](#ref3)[[19]](#ref19)</sup>

## Design and Mechanism

Modern meshers share a cutting element, graft carrier and feed mechanism, but carrier orientation and what sets the expansion ratio differ by model:<sup>[[15]](#ref15)[[16]](#ref16)</sup>

| Zimmer Biomet model | Graft-bearing carrier face | Ratio selection |
|---|---|---|
| **Zimmer Skin Graft Mesher** | **Smooth side** | Interchangeable cutter selects 1:1, 1.5:1, 2:1, 3:1 or 4:1 |
| **MeshGraft II Tissue Expansion System** | **Grooved side** | Model-compatible Dermacarrier II selects 1.5:1, 3:1, 6:1 or 9:1 |

The blades create staggered slits; spreading the graft opens a diamond/lattice pattern.<sup>[[5]](#ref5)</sup> On MeshGraft II, the manufacturer-authored instruction manual requires the grooved side up and a compatible single-use Dermacarrier II: placing graft on the smooth side can shred it. It allows epidermal-side-up placement or dermal-side-up placement for direct transfer; dermal-side-down is not a universal mesher rule. Do not autoclave the disposable carrier or heat it in solution. Consult the current IFU for the exact model and carrier before use.<sup>[[15]](#ref15)[[16]](#ref16)</sup>

Herbert's 1990 Tanner-carrier method applied petroleum jelly to a grooved carrier and laid the carrier onto a graft still on the donor site. That technique should not be transferred automatically to another mesher or substituted for its IFU.<sup>[[4]](#ref4)</sup>

## Historical Lineage

Tanner et al. evolved the instrument through several iterations:<sup>[[3]](#ref3)</sup>

1. **Flat block mesher.** Earliest prototype, flat block cutting staggered slits
2. **Mesh Dermatome Type I.** Roller device with staggered cuts
3. **Mesh Dermatome Type II.** Roller with continuous cuts and grooved dermacarrier
4. **Later commercial meshers.** Including distinct Zimmer Skin Graft Mesher and MeshGraft II mechanisms described above

## Expansion Ratios — Nominal vs Actual

Practical pearl: actual expansion and recipient-bed coverage can be substantially less than the nominal ratio. The figures below are from Kan's 51-patient/57-operation series, not a device- or GU-site guarantee.<sup>[[8]](#ref8)</sup>

| Nominal ratio | Actual area expansion | Clinical coverage rate | Donor size needed (% of recipient) |
|---|---|---|---|
| **1.5:1** | ~ 1.16× | ~ 1.02× | ~ 85% |
| **3:1** | ~ 1.61× | ~ 1.29× | ~ 60% |
| **6:1** | ~ 2.32× | ~ 2.18× | ~ 45% |

In a separate ex-vivo porcine-skin experiment, Henderson 2012 measured 1.36× actual expansion from a 1.5:1 carrier and 1.80× from a 3:1 apparatus. Kan found lower clinical coverage on concave recipient sites; whether these estimates apply to a particular scrotal or perineal defect requires local planning rather than direct extrapolation.<sup>[[6]](#ref6)[[8]](#ref8)</sup>

**Practical implication for reconstructive planning.** Do not size a donor solely from the nominal ratio. Kan's percentages are study-derived starting estimates; adjust for recipient-bed shape and the actual device and graft behavior.

## Over-Meshing vs Cross-Meshing

Henderson 2012 also investigated re-meshing ex-vivo porcine split-skin samples:<sup>[[6]](#ref6)</sup>

- **Over-meshing** (re-mesh in the same axis as the initial mesh): some tested combinations maintained graft integrity and reached up to 2.3× expansion (1.5:1 followed by 3:1).
- **Cross-meshing** (re-mesh orthogonal to the initial mesh): cut the samples into small pieces; avoid transferring this maneuver into a clinical technique without device- and graft-specific assessment.

## Indications and Ratio Selection

Choice of meshing ratio depends on donor availability, wound size, recipient contour and cosmetic and functional priorities. The burn cohort and surgeon survey below describe burn practice, not a GU default.<sup>[[9]](#ref9)[[19]](#ref19)</sup>

| Ratio | Best fit | Reconstructive notes |
|---|---|---|
| **Sheet (unmeshed)** | Cosmetically sensitive areas — **face, hands, neck, fingers** | Best cosmetic outcome; routinely chosen for visible recon |
| **1:1 / 1.5:1** | Drainage with little to moderate expansion | Ratios available depend on the mesher model; the cited unexpanded-mesh reference is **veterinary**, not a human cosmetic-equivalence study<sup>[[11]](#ref11)</sup> |
| **2:1** | Intermediate expansion where a compatible device offers this cutter/carrier | Not an established default for every genital or perineal site<sup>[[15]](#ref15)</sup> |
| **3:1 or higher** | Large burns/defects when donor supply is limiting | Pripotnev 2017: ≥3:1 burn patients had higher mean total and full-thickness burn area; **all surveyed surgeons with ≥3:1 experience who answered that item** cited burn size, not all Canadian surgeons<sup>[[9]](#ref9)</sup> |
| **High expansion / Meek** | Major donor limitation in selected burn cases | Meek micrografting is a distinct method; evidence cannot be generalized to GU defects from burn cohorts alone<sup>[[7]](#ref7)[[13]](#ref13)[[18]](#ref18)</sup> |

## Reconstructive / Urogyn Uses

- **Scrotal reconstruction after Fournier's gangrene.** One reconstructive review describes its team's 1.5:1 scrotal/abdominal meshing practice; this is a reported technique, not a universal protocol<sup>[[19]](#ref19)</sup>
- **Large perineal debridement wounds.** Meshed STSGs expand donor and drain serous fluid
- **Buried-penis reconstruction.** Shaft resurfacing with meshed or sheet STSG depending on cosmetic priority (penile shaft often left unmeshed for cosmesis when donor area allows)
- **Post-radical skin excision** for hidradenitis suppurativa / chronic dermatitis / severe lichen sclerosus
- **Phalloplasty donor-site coverage.** Meshing can provide drainage and expansion where a grafted donor site needs coverage; ratio and dressing are donor-/patient-specific
- **Lower-extremity wound coverage.** In mixed reconstructive practice, Shin 2024 found meshed grafts in the lower extremity showed lower rates of graft loss and ulcer recurrence versus nonmeshed<sup>[[12]](#ref12)</sup>

## Advantages of Meshing

- **Expanded coverage.** Smaller donor for a larger wound, which matters in extensive burns or large debridement<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[9]](#ref9)</sup>
- **Fluid drainage.** Slits let blood or serum escape from beneath the graft, reducing hematoma or seroma lift-off<sup>[[11]](#ref11)</sup>
- **Conformability.** Mesh pattern allows the graft to drape over irregular or contoured recipient beds, which matters for the scrotum and perineum<sup>[[11]](#ref11)</sup>
- **Potential donor-area reduction.** Achieved expansion may reduce the area harvested, but a clinical morbidity advantage is not established by lower-extremity meshed-versus-sheet data alone<sup>[[8]](#ref8)[[12]](#ref12)</sup>

## Limitations and Complications

- **Permanent mesh pattern.** Visible "fishnet" / "crocodile-skin" lattice persists for life<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[10]](#ref10)</sup>
- **Hypertrophic scarring.** Higher expansion ratios prolong re-epithelialization, increasing infection and hypertrophic-scar risk<sup>[[10]](#ref10)</sup>
- **Heterogeneous pigmentation.** Wide-mesh grafts produce uneven scar pigmentation<sup>[[10]](#ref10)</sup>
- **Inferior cosmesis versus sheet grafts.** This is the reason sheet grafts are still chosen for visible areas<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup>

## Mesh Grafting vs Meek Micrografting

The **Meek technique** cuts skin into small islands rather than creating a lattice, a different expansion paradigm. The following comparison is from Noureldin 2022, a randomized 40-child burn study with 3-month scar assessment, not adult or GU reconstruction:<sup>[[13]](#ref13)</sup>

| Outcome | **Meek** | **Mesh** | p |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Graft take** | **84.25%** | 71.5% | **0.006** |
| Epithelialization time (days) | 27.1 | 33.5 | 0.176 |
| Infection rate | 25% | 40% | 0.311 |
| **POSAS scar (patient)** | **3.17** | 4.2 | **0.048** |
| **POSAS scar (observer)** | **2.89** | 4.1 | **0.003** |
| Operative time | Longer | Shorter | 0.001 |
| Maximum expansion | **Up to 9:1** | Up to 6:1 | — |

Kreis 1994 measured actual expansion: approximately 1:9 Meek versus approximately 1:4 mesh at nominally-equivalent dial settings. Max distance between graft elements: 9 mm Meek versus 12 mm mesh; Meek delivers more uniform coverage at high expansion.<sup>[[7]](#ref7)</sup>

The protocol's intra-patient RCT has reported 3-month results: 70 patients at two burn centers had greater day-8 graft take with mesh than Meek (87 ± 19% versus 79 ± 25%; p = 0.003) and better scores on most short-term scar items with mesh. The mesh arm used more donor skin and achieved less actual expansion. These adult/mixed-wound results differ from the pediatric between-patient trial above; neither establishes a universal winner or a GU-site preference. The 12-month primary scar outcome is not established by the short-term report. A later correction changed a cited scar-color reference and discussion wording but stated the conclusions were unaffected.<sup>[[14]](#ref14)[[18]](#ref18)[[20]](#ref20)</sup>

## Technique

1. **Harvest STSG** with a dermatome ([Zimmer](/docs/foundations/tools/instruments/graft-harvest/zimmer-air-dermatome), [Padgett](/docs/foundations/tools/instruments/graft-harvest/padgett-dermatome), [Humby](/docs/foundations/tools/instruments/graft-harvest/humby-dermatome), [Goulian](/docs/foundations/tools/instruments/graft-harvest/goulian-dermatome))
2. **Identify the exact mesher and its compatible carrier/cutter**; verify the selected ratio and the manufacturer's carrier face and graft-orientation instructions. Zimmer Skin Graft Mesher uses the smooth carrier face; MeshGraft II uses the grooved face. Herbert's petroleum-jelly method applies to a Tanner carrier, not automatically to either current device.<sup>[[4]](#ref4)[[15]](#ref15)[[16]](#ref16)</sup>
3. **Place and smooth the graft on the correct carrier face** according to that model's IFU. For MeshGraft II, either epidermal-up or dermal-up placement is described for different transfer workflows; the single-use carrier must remain at room temperature and must not be autoclaved.<sup>[[16]](#ref16)</sup>
4. **Feed through the mesher** according to its model-specific setup and inspect the cut pattern before transfer
5. **Lift the meshed graft and gently spread** on the recipient bed, pulling apart the lattice
6. **Secure and immobilize according to the recipient bed** (staples, sutures and/or selected [quilting stitches](/docs/foundations/surgical-skills/quilting-stitch))
7. **Choose an appropriate bolster or other dressing** and reassessment schedule for the graft, site and local protocol rather than assuming one universal duration

:::warning[Lot-specific carrier recall]
The FDA records a 3:1 Dermacarrier recall for model 00219501300, lots 65292843 and 65390419: the labeled 3:1 carriers had a 1.5:1 ridge pattern. Identify and quarantine affected stock according to the manufacturer notice; this is not a recall of every mesher or carrier. Check current recall/lot status before use.<sup>[[17]](#ref17)</sup>
:::

## Practical Donor-Size Guidance (Kan 2020)

For real-world surgical planning, donor area should be approximately:<sup>[[8]](#ref8)</sup>

- **85% of recipient** for 1.5:1 meshing
- **60% of recipient** for 3:1 meshing
- **45% of recipient** for 6:1 meshing
- **Adjust for local geometry**: concave sites had lower coverage in Kan's series, but the quoted percentages have not been validated specifically for scrotal/perineal beds
