# Cavernotomes

**Cavernotomes are specialized cutting dilators used to create a channel through dense corporal fibrosis during penile-prosthesis implantation when blunt dilation is inadequate.** They act within the **corpus cavernosum**, not the urethra, and are grouped here with other cutting-dilation instruments. In a retrospective 42-patient multicenter referral cohort, cavernotomes were used in 25 cases (59.5%); that figure describes the selected cohort, not routine implant practice.<sup>[[4]](#ref4)</sup>

## When They Are Needed

Cavernotomes may be considered after an initial corporal tract has been established but dense scar prevents adequate blunt dilation.<sup>[[11]](#ref11)</sup> Severe fibrosis is particularly encountered after:

- **Prior prosthesis explant** — for infection (~40%) or erosion (~17%)<sup>[[4]](#ref4)</sup>
- **Ischemic priapism** (~33%), especially prolonged episodes or those treated with distal shunts<sup>[[4]](#ref4)[[5]](#ref5)</sup>
- **Other causes** — Peyronie disease, intracavernosal injections, trauma and prior pelvic radiation are also reported, although severity and distribution vary.<sup>[[6]](#ref6)[[11]](#ref11)</sup>

The full intraoperative escalation ladder (VED prehab → Hegar dilation → cavernotomes → counter incisions → corporeal excavation → grafting) is laid out on the [penile-prosthesis revision-scenarios page](/docs/surgical-techniques/04j-sexual-dysfunction/penile-implants/revision-scenarios#corporal-scarring--fibrosis).

## Designs

### Carrion-Rossello cavernotomes

The Carrion-Rossello set uses a backward-cutting rasp: the instrument is advanced and then withdrawn repeatedly so its rasp files fibrotic tissue. A contemporary review describes sequential 8–12 mm sizes and emphasizes keeping the smooth surface toward the septum and urethra.<sup>[[11]](#ref11)</sup> This is a distinct design from the forward-cutting Mooreville instruments; neither name should be used as a generic specification for every cavernotome.

### Mooreville cavernotomes (Uramix)

The original Mooreville cavernotome was reported in 1999 as a five-instrument, 6–13 mm set making controlled 1 mm cuts with oscillating advancement and rotational shaving.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup> The current manufacturer page describes a different **Mooreville II double-bladed** configuration; do not transfer the original set count or blade geometry to the current product.<sup>[[10]](#ref10)</sup>

| Current Mooreville II feature | Manufacturer-listed specification |
|---|---|
| **Main set** | Six instruments: 6, 7, 8, 9, 10 and 11 mm |
| **Extra-large set** | 12, 13 and 14 mm |
| **Working element** | Two parallel 5 cm blades, extending to within 0.5 cm of the rounded tip; blade height remains within the dilator circumference |
| **Instrument length** | 23.5 cm, with centimeter markings |
| **Handles** | Fixed- and removable-handle versions; an additional handle is listed for torque and blade orientation |
| **Movement** | Longitudinal cutting or rotational shaving; instruments are used in succession |

These are manufacturer specifications for the currently marketed product, not comparative clinical claims. The public product page says the instrument and handle are autoclavable, but the current full instructions for use and validated reprocessing parameters were not available in this review.<sup>[[10]](#ref10)</sup>

## Technique

1. **Expose and open the corpus sufficiently for instrument entry.** The current Mooreville II instructions describe scalpel access extended proximally and distally; the original clinical series used a high transverse scrotal approach.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[10]](#ref10)</sup>
2. **Establish an initial tract.** Reviews describe blunt dilation first and note that a cavernotome requires a channel in which to work.<sup>[[5]](#ref5)[[11]](#ref11)</sup>
3. **Start small and progress sequentially.** Match the sequence to the specific system and the intended cylinder; millimeters, not French, are used for these instruments.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[10]](#ref10)</sup>
4. **Orient the working element laterally at entry.** Maintain awareness of the urethra, septum and tunica; orientation details differ between backward- and forward-cutting systems.<sup>[[10]](#ref10)[[11]](#ref11)</sup>
5. **Advance with controlled motion.** The Mooreville design uses oscillating longitudinal movement and, when needed, rotational shaving rather than forceful thrusting.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[10]](#ref10)</sup>

Where no developable plane exists, the [Wilson backward-cutting scissors](/docs/foundations/tools/instruments/scissors/wilson-backward-cutting) are used first to carve the **initial channel** so a cavernotome can be seated.

## Outcomes

In the **original Mooreville series (n = 16)**, bilateral three-piece device implantation was completed in all patients; 14 received downsized cylinders, no urethral laceration caused termination, and no graft was needed to close the corporotomy. These are uncontrolled single-series findings with follow-up only to publication.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup>

In the **Krughoff multicenter retrospective cohort (n = 42)**, 25 patients had cavernotome dilation, 15 had standard sequential dilation, and one had limited sharp excision plus cavernotomes; one major complication was reported. The study evaluated an overall fibrosis cohort, not a randomized comparison of instruments.<sup>[[4]](#ref4)</sup>

## Complications and Limitations

The defining limitation is cutting within the corpus without direct visualization of the entire instrument path. In the original 16-patient series:<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup>

- **Proximal crural perforation — 5/16 (31%)**, managed by suturing the rear-tip extender to the tunica with nonabsorbable suture<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup>
- **Distal tunical perforation — 4/16 (25%)**, repaired with native tissue<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup>
- **No urethral laceration terminated a case**, but this small series does not eliminate urethral-injury risk<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup>

## Direct-Vision Adjuncts and Alternatives

Several techniques address the "out of sight" problem or substitute for cavernotomes entirely:

- **Ultrasound-guided cavernotomy (Shaeer 2007)** — real-time sonography keeps the instrument in the mid-corpus, away from the tunica and urethra; reported operative time 50–60 min with no complications.<sup>[[7]](#ref7)</sup>
- **Corporoscopic excavation (Shaeer's technique)** — optical urethrotomy plus transurethral resection instruments perform force-free, **directly visualized** excavation of scar through a ~1.5 cm tunical incision.<sup>[[8]](#ref8)</sup>
- **Corporeal excavation (Montague-Angermeier)** — extended ventral corporotomies via an **inverted-T penoscrotal incision** develop a plane between scar and inner tunica for core removal of nearly all fibrotic tissue; successful in all 9 patients in the original series without grafting.<sup>[[9]](#ref9)</sup>
- **Downsized / narrow-base cylinders** — narrow-diameter devices may allow implantation when full dilation is unsafe or not achievable.<sup>[[4]](#ref4)[[11]](#ref11)</sup>

## Place in Practice

Evidence is dominated by technical reports and retrospective series. Choice among blunt dilation, a named cavernotome, narrow cylinders, excavation, additional corporotomies or graft reconstruction depends on the fibrosis pattern, equipment and surgeon experience.<sup>[[2]](#ref2)[[3]](#ref3)[[11]](#ref11)</sup> Current EAU guidance lists Carrion-Rossello, Uramix and Mooreville cavernotomes among options for severe post-priapism corporal fibrosis; it does not endorse one design as superior.<sup>[[12]](#ref12)</sup> Complex cases are generally directed to experienced, high-volume prosthetic practices.<sup>[[11]](#ref11)</sup>
