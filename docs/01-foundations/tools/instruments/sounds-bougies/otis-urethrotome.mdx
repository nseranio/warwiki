# Otis Urethrotome

The **Otis urethrotome** is a blind internal-urethrotomy instrument combining calibrated expansion with a dorsal cold-knife incision; cutting motion differs between the original conical design and later parallel-expanding models.<sup>[[14]](#ref14)</sup> It preceded direct-vision internal urethrotomy (DVIU) and has largely been supplanted by the Sachse urethrotome. Historical distal use and equipment-limited use remain relevant to understanding the instrument, but contemporary EAU guidance weakly recommends open meatoplasty or distal urethroplasty for meatal and fossa-navicularis strictures.<sup>[[10]](#ref10)</sup> Named for Fessenden Nott Otis (1825–1900), the American urologist who is also the namesake of the [Otis Bougie-à-Boule](/docs/foundations/tools/instruments/sounds-bougies/otis-bougie). The ICS male LUTS-surgery terminology document explicitly defines blind urethrotomy as "opening of the stricture with the use of a special instrument (Otis urethrotome) to perform the incision without direct visualization."<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup> The technique below describes male urethral use; EAU found no outcomes specific to urethrotomy for female stricture, for which incision sites differ.<sup>[[20]](#ref20)</sup>

## Design

- **Long slender metal shaft** introduced per urethra with the blade retracted and the expandable segment collapsed.
- **Calibrated expandable distal segment.** Turning the handle screw widens the segment to a selected French setting shown on the graduated scale, allowing dilation before the cut. The original conical model does not expand uniformly, and its cut depth does not always correspond to the setting; the Otis–Mauermayer model expands evenly along its length.<sup>[[13]](#ref13)</sup>
- **Concealed retractable cold-knife blade** housed within the shaft; when deployed, the blade extends from a longitudinal slot for a dorsal 12 o'clock incision. In the original conical Otis, the extended blade cuts as the instrument is withdrawn; in the parallel-expanding Otis–Mauermayer, the shaft remains stationary while the blade moves through the stricture.<sup>[[13]](#ref13)</sup>
- **Handle** carries the expansion screw, blade-deployment mechanism, and the French-gauge scale.
- **Stainless steel**, fully autoclavable.

Calibrated expansion and a cold-knife incision in one instrument distinguish the Otis from a plain sound (no cut) and the Sachse / Collins-knife systems (incision under vision, no calibrated dilation).<sup>[[13]](#ref13)</sup>

## Reconstructive-Urology Uses

### Stricture incision (historical and niche)

- **Meatal and fossa-navicularis strictures.** The short straight distal segment does not establish that blind dorsal incision is the safest approach. EAU weakly recommends offering open meatoplasty or distal urethroplasty for these strictures.<sup>[[11]](#ref11)</sup>
- **Resource-limited settings.** Lack of an endoscopic urethrotome and irrigation system does not by itself establish an indication for blind Otis incision; stricture site, length, and safe lumen access govern treatment selection.<sup>[[10]](#ref10)</sup>
- **Bulbar strictures.** Otis urethrotomy has historical use. For a primary, single, nonobliterative bulbar stricture &lt;2 cm, EAU 2026 weakly recommends [DVIU or dilation](/docs/surgical-techniques/04a-urethral-reconstruction/dviu).<sup>[[10]](#ref10)</sup> DVIU is incision under direct vision; this recommendation does not establish an indication for blind Otis incision. The guideline strongly advises against DVIU or dilation alone for a segment &gt;2 cm.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[10]](#ref10)</sup>

### Prophylactic urethrotomy before TURP (historical)

The Otis was widely used as a preliminary cut at the meatus and distal urethra before TURP to accommodate the 24–28 Fr resectoscope sheath and reduce post-TURP stricture rates.

- **Schultz 1989 RCT (n = 185).** Otis urethrotomy before TURP yielded 4% stricture versus 16% after a two-day urethral catheter-dilation protocol (significant); the trial also randomized resectoscope sheath type.<sup>[[5]](#ref5)</sup>
- **Nielsen 1989 RCT (n = 200).** No significant difference: urethrotomy plus TURP 14% versus TURP alone 21%; the authors concluded that "urethrotomy before TURP cannot prevent postoperative urethral strictures."<sup>[[6]](#ref6)</sup>
- EAU 2026 strongly recommends against routine urethrotomy when no pre-existing stricture is present and notes that only one of the historical comparative studies had at least 12 months of follow-up.<sup>[[17]](#ref17)</sup> In Nielsen's randomized 200-patient trial, the three-month stricture rates were 21% after TURP alone and 14% after preliminary urethrotomy (not statistically significant); eight versus five additional patients, respectively, developed strictures at late follow-up (median 54 months).<sup>[[6]](#ref6)</sup> Vanthoor et al. suggested that stricture incidence may be lower after enucleation than TURP.<sup>[[7]](#ref7)</sup> The EAU 2026 guideline cites a systematic review finding no significant difference after 12 months and no strong evidence of a clinically important long-term difference among modalities.<sup>[[17]](#ref17)</sup> A separate 2025 meta-analysis of randomized trials found fewer urethral strictures after enucleation than TURP overall (RR 0.55, 95% CI 0.34–0.90), with a significant difference only at 6–24 months; risk of bias and longer-term uncertainty limit that inference.<sup>[[21]](#ref21)</sup>

For procedural context, see [DVIU and Urethral Dilation](/docs/surgical-techniques/04a-urethral-reconstruction/dviu).

## Technique — Otis Method

1. **Preoperative preparation and anesthesia.** Screen for bacteriuria, usually with a urine culture, and treat bacteriuria or UTI before mucosa-breaching surgery; administer intraoperative antibiotic prophylaxis according to local resistance patterns.<sup>[[19]](#ref19)</sup> Use general or regional anesthesia. The blind dilation component is more painful than a visual urethrotomy under local.<sup>[[3]](#ref3)</sup>
2. **Insertion** with blade retracted and segment collapsed.
3. **Advancement past the stricture.** Do not force the Otis through a tight or uncertain lumen. If preliminary [filiform-and-follower](/docs/foundations/tools/instruments/sounds-bougies/filiforms-followers) or sound dilation is considered, confirm true-lumen guidewire access endoscopically or fluoroscopically first; stop and reassess if passage cannot be confirmed.<sup>[[12]](#ref12)</sup>
4. **Expansion.** Set the caliber for the treated segment and instrument model. Mauermayer described opening his parallel model to contact the lumen, then increasing the setting only if a deeper cut was needed; his 30 Ch setting was for distal access before a 24 Ch TURP resectoscope, not a general stricture target.<sup>[[13]](#ref13)</sup>
5. **Blade deployment and incision.** The intended longitudinal incision is dorsal at 12 o'clock, but cutting motion depends on the model: the original conical Otis was withdrawn with its knife extended, whereas the parallel-expanding Otis–Mauermayer holds the dilator in place and retracts the knife through the stricture. Verify the local model before use.<sup>[[13]](#ref13)</sup>
6. **Postprocedure drainage after Otis urethrotomy**<sup>[[2]](#ref2)[[3]](#ref3)</sup>. If a urethral catheter is needed, choose caliber and duration for the operative indication; EAU weakly recommends against catheters &gt;18 Fr when urinary drainage is the sole purpose.<sup>[[17]](#ref17)</sup>

## Otis vs Sachse (DVIU)

| Feature | **Otis (blind)** | **Sachse (DVIU)** |
|---|---|---|
| **Visualization** | None — blind | **Direct endoscopic vision** |
| **Mechanism** | Original conical model: withdraw shaft with blade extended; Otis–Mauermayer model: move blade with shaft stationary<sup>[[13]](#ref13)</sup> | Cold-knife incision under vision |
| **Incision control** | Cuts at 12 o'clock through whatever tissue is there | **Surgeon selects site / depth** under vision |
| **Stricture traversal** | Must pass instrument through / past stricture first | Can incise progressively from distal to proximal after a guidewire crosses the stricture<sup>[[15]](#ref15)</sup> |
| **Anesthesia** | Generally GA / regional | Usually general or spinal in well-resourced settings; local feasible in selected cases<sup>[[10]](#ref10)</sup> |
| **Era of predominance** | Late 1800s – 1970s | **1974 – present** |
| **False-passage risk** | Possible during blind instrument passage<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[10]](#ref10)</sup> | Reported with DVIU despite direct vision<sup>[[10]](#ref10)</sup> |
| **Calibrated dilation built in** | **Yes** | No |

## Outcomes

- **Frohmüller / Bülow 1975.** Otis urethrotomy in 23 patients; approximately 60% required no further dilations; the authors reported a low complication rate.<sup>[[2]](#ref2)</sup>
- **Hjortrup 1983.** 72 consecutive patients over 5 years; success 82% (95% CI 71–90%) at mean 29-month follow-up.<sup>[[3]](#ref3)</sup>
- **Cochrane (Wong 2012).** The only RCT directly comparing dilation to urethrotomy used the Sachse technique, not the Otis; head-to-head data between Otis and Sachse remain sparse.<sup>[[4]](#ref4)</sup>
- **AUA 2023.** For initial bulbar strictures &lt;2 cm, the guideline lists dilation, DVIU, and urethroplasty as Conditional, Grade C options; if endoscopic treatment is chosen, dilation and DVIU are interchangeable (Conditional, Grade C). Endoscopic success is highest for bulbar strictures &lt;1 cm and is low for strictures &gt;2 cm or after prior endoscopic failure; penile strictures have high endoscopic recurrence. For recurrent anterior stricture after failed dilation or DVIU, AUA recommends urethroplasty over repeat endoscopy (Moderate, Grade C), while permitting DVIU or dilation plus a drug-coated balloon for recurrent bulbar strictures &lt;3 cm (Conditional, Grade B).<sup>[[8]](#ref8)</sup>
- **Verla 2019 review.** DVIU success reaches up to approximately 80% for primary strictures &lt; 1 cm, with the best results in primary strictures &lt; 1.5 cm.<sup>[[9]](#ref9)</sup> These figures describe cold-knife DVIU, not the Otis, and are given for comparison.

## Safety Profile

- **False passage.** The Otis performs urethrotomy without direct visualization.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup> Hjortrup's 72-patient Otis series reported few complications.<sup>[[3]](#ref3)</sup> EAU 2026 identifies false passage as a risk of blind dilation and weakly recommends visually controlled dilation, while noting that direct comparisons are lacking.<sup>[[10]](#ref10)</sup>
- **Uncontrolled incision depth.** A deep blind 12 o’clock cut in the penile urethra risks corporal-tunica injury. Severe ventral curvature with spongiosal and tunical fibrosis was reported six months after cold-knife DVIU plus a deep (30 Fr) Otis incision with immediate marked urethrorrhagia; this single case cannot isolate the Otis contribution.<sup>[[22]](#ref22)</sup>
- **Urethral hemorrhage, UTI, bacteremia.** Standard urethral-instrumentation risks.
- **Stricture recurrence.** The underlying biology (re-fibrosis) is not changed by switching from blind to visual incision; recurrence remains the principal limitation of any endoscopic stricture management.

## Limitations and Modern Positioning

The Otis remained in use in a 72-patient series published in 1983.<sup>[[3]](#ref3)</sup> Mauermayer described using direct-vision Sachse urethrotomy for strictures obstructing resectoscope passage, and optical urethrotomy became standard care where endoscopic equipment was available.<sup>[[13]](#ref13)[[4]](#ref4)</sup> Practical differences include:

- **Direct vision** lets the surgeon see and select the incision plane; evidence is insufficient to determine whether it lowers false-passage risk compared with Otis urethrotomy.<sup>[[4]](#ref4)[[10]](#ref10)</sup>
- **Progressive incision** from the distal face toward the proximal end follows guidewire placement and avoids passing the urethrotome through the full stricture before cutting.<sup>[[16]](#ref16)</sup>
- Office or local-anesthesia DVIU is possible in selected cases.
- Distal Otis incision has historical use, but equipment limitations alone do not establish it as the preferred treatment; EAU weakly recommends offering open meatoplasty or distal urethroplasty for meatal and fossa-navicularis strictures.<sup>[[18]](#ref18)</sup>

## Historical Significance

The Otis urethrotome was one of the first instruments to move stricture management beyond simple dilation toward controlled incision. Otis's principle, that a measured, calibrated longitudinal cut heals across to a wider lumen rather than tearing scar, is the conceptual ancestor of modern DVIU and remains the foundation of endoscopic stricture management today.
