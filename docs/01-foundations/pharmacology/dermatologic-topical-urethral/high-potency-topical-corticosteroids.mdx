# High-Potency Topical Corticosteroids

**Topical corticosteroids** are first-line conservative treatment for symptomatic phimosis and for genital lichen sclerosus (LS/BXO), and are used for several other genital dermatoses. **Potency is condition-specific:** potent / ultrapotent treatment is appropriate for LS and selected genital lichen planus; genital psoriasis and uncomplicated dermatitis generally start with lower-potency agents. Intralesional triamcinolone is a separate route discussed in its companion page.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[2]](#ref2)[[3]](#ref3)[[4]](#ref4)</sup>

For related agents, see [Topical calcineurin inhibitors](/docs/foundations/pharmacology/dermatologic-topical-urethral/topical-calcineurin-inhibitors), [Intralesional corticosteroids](/docs/foundations/pharmacology/dermatologic-topical-urethral/intralesional-corticosteroids), [Platelet-rich plasma](/docs/foundations/pharmacology/dermatologic-topical-urethral/platelet-rich-plasma), and the clinical [Lichen sclerosus](/docs/clinical-conditions/03g-genital-scrotal/lichen-sclerosus) article.

## Potency classification and genital prescribing principles

The US 7-class potency system governs appropriate genital prescribing. Genital skin is thin and highly absorptive; potency selection must balance efficacy against atrophy risk.<sup>[[5]](#ref5)[[6]](#ref6)</sup>

| US class | Potency | Agents used in urology |
|---|---|---|
| **I — Super-high** | Very high | **Clobetasol propionate 0.05%**, halobetasol propionate 0.05% |
| **II — High** | High | **Betamethasone dipropionate 0.05%**, fluocinonide 0.05%, desoximetasone 0.25% |
| **III–V — Medium** | Medium | Betamethasone valerate 0.1%, mometasone furoate 0.1%, triamcinolone acetonide 0.1%, fluticasone propionate 0.05% |
| **VI–VII — Low** | Low | Hydrocortisone 1–2.5%, desonide 0.05%, alclometasone 0.05% |

**Core genital-use principles:**

- AAFP and AAD classify the genitalia as thin-skin sites with enhanced percutaneous absorption<sup>[[5]](#ref5)</sup>
- **Low-potency** agents (VI–VII) are appropriate for routine genital dermatoses (contact dermatitis, genital psoriasis)
- **High-potency** agents (I–II) are reserved for specific conditions, lichen sclerosus and lichen planus, where benefit clearly outweighs atrophy risk
- **Ointments are often preferred for LS** because of barrier support and fewer potential irritants; check the actual vehicle, since formulations differ<sup>[[5]](#ref5)[[7]](#ref7)</sup>

## Phimosis — the primary urologic application

### Guideline positioning

Current EAU guidance supports topical corticosteroids for symptomatic phimosis. Asymptomatic physiological non-retractability and adhesions usually need no treatment; avoid forced retraction. Selected infants at increased UTI risk are an exception.<sup>[[35]](#ref35)</sup>

### Dual mechanism

1. **Anti-inflammatory:** phospholipase-A2 inhibition leads to reduced prostaglandins and leukotrienes and to decreased edema, fibrin, capillary dilation and fibroblast proliferation
2. **Tissue remodeling:** inhibition of epidermal proliferation and collagen synthesis leads to skin thinning and increased elasticity, allowing the stenotic preputial ring to stretch<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup>

### Cochrane 2024 — 14 RCTs, n = 1,459<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup>

| Outcome | Effect vs placebo | RR (95% CI) |
|---|---|---|
| **Complete resolution (4–8 wk)** | **+436 per 1,000** | **RR 2.73** (1.79–4.16) |
| Partial resolution (4–8 wk) | +202 per 1,000 | RR 1.68 (1.17–2.40) |
| **Long-term complete resolution (≥6 mo)** | **+528 per 1,000** | **RR 4.09** (2.80–5.97) |
| Adverse effects | Too imprecise to establish a difference | RR 0.28 (0.03–2.62) |

Subgroup analysis suggested topical corticosteroids may be more effective in younger boys (&lt;7 y); certainty of evidence was low across outcomes.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup>

### Which corticosteroid is best — Sridharan 2021 network meta-analysis

The network meta-analysis ranked several agents favorably and its authors favored hydrocortisone. Indirect rankings do not establish equivalence between potencies or a proven safety advantage. Consider the named formulation, indication and direct trial evidence rather than choosing solely by rank.<sup>[[8]](#ref8)</sup>

**Yang 2005 head-to-head RCT.** Betamethasone valerate 0.06% versus clobetasone butyrate 0.05%: response rates 81.3% versus 77.4% (p = 0.63). Failure to show a difference does not establish equivalence.<sup>[[9]](#ref9)</sup>

### Campos 2026 multicenter cohort — n = 235

Across 12 hospitals, betamethasone 0.05% BID × 8 weeks achieved 68% success, with no differences across phimosis severity grades or age groups. The only significant predictor of failure was altered preputial skin appearance (success 72% healthy skin versus 29% altered skin; p = 0.007) , a surrogate for underlying BXO / LS. Adherence, age, symptoms, and prior balanitis were not predictive.<sup>[[10]](#ref10)</sup>

### Adult phimosis — thinner literature

**Lygas 2022 systematic review** found significant heterogeneity and limited trial data. Topical steroids appear probably safe with some potential to reduce LS-associated phimosis signs / symptoms, but good-quality RCT data with patient-reported outcomes are needed.<sup>[[11]](#ref11)</sup>

### Standard phimosis protocol<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[9]](#ref9)[[10]](#ref10)</sup>

| Parameter | Recommendation |
|---|---|
| **Agent** | Betamethasone 0.05% ointment (most studied); choose the named preparation and concentration rather than assuming all steroid products are interchangeable |
| **Application** | Thin layer to the stenotic distal prepuce |
| **Technique** | Gentle retraction of foreskin as far as possible **without pain** before application |
| **Frequency** | Twice daily |
| **Duration** | **4–8 weeks** (most protocols use 8 weeks) |
| **Post-treatment** | Continue gentle retraction exercises and hygiene after course completion |
| **Partial response** | May repeat a second 4–8-week course |
| **Failure / refractory** | Refer for circumcision or preputioplasty |
| **Expected success** | **65–96%** depending on definition and severity |

## Lichen sclerosus / BXO — the most important chronic indication

### Overview

LS is a chronic inflammatory dermatosis predominantly affecting the anogenital region. In males it is called **balanitis xerotica obliterans (BXO)** and typically involves the foreskin and glans, causing phimosis, meatal stenosis, and potentially urethral stricture. Mean age in boys is 8 years; BXO is the most common cause of pathologic phimosis in boys &gt;9 y.<sup>[[12]](#ref12)[[7]](#ref7)</sup>

### First-line — ultrapotent / potent topical corticosteroids

The German S3 guideline (2025; published 2026), ACOG Practice Bulletin 224, British Association of Dermatologists, and Cochrane review all recommend ultrapotent or potent topical corticosteroids as first-line for genital LS regardless of age or sex.<sup>[[13]](#ref13)[[3]](#ref3)[[14]](#ref14)</sup> Clobetasol propionate 0.05% ointment is the most studied and widely used agent.

### Cochrane 2011 review — Chi et al., 7 RCTs, n = 249<sup>[[14]](#ref14)[[15]](#ref15)</sup>

- **Clobetasol 0.05%** versus placebo: participant-rated improvement RR 2.85 (95% CI 1.45–5.61); investigator-rated SMD 5.74 (95% CI 4.26–7.23)
- **Mometasone** versus placebo: improved clinical grade of phimosis (SMD −1.04; 95% CI −1.77 to −0.31)
- **Topical testosterone, DHT, and progesterone** showed no benefit; topical testosterone worsened symptoms when used as maintenance after clobetasol
- **Pimecrolimus** was comparable to clobetasol for symptom relief but less effective for gross appearance (SMD −1.64; 95% CI −2.40 to −0.87)

### Male-genital LS conservative management — Shieh 2024 systematic review

Across 17 studies of histologically confirmed penile / urethral LS:<sup>[[2]](#ref2)</sup>

- Topical corticosteroids remain the mainstay of conservative management
- **Tacrolimus** is a selected second-line option when corticosteroids are insufficient or contraindicated
- **PRP and laser remain uncertain:** they are not established substitutes for anti-inflammatory care. See the [clinical LS evidence comparison](/docs/clinical-conditions/03g-genital-scrotal/lichen-sclerosus#laser-and-prp-symptoms-are-not-the-same-as-disease-control), including the large 2026 laser trial and sham-controlled studies<sup>[[13]](#ref13)</sup>

### Treatment framework<sup>[[13]](#ref13)[[36]](#ref36)</sup>

| Phase | Regimen | Duration |
|---|---|---|
| **Induction** | Clobetasol propionate 0.05% ointment, commonly once daily | Usually 1–3 months, adjusted to response and phenotype |
| **Taper** | Reduce application frequency after disease control | Individualized |
| **Maintenance** | Often once/twice weekly proactive treatment or treatment of recurrence | Long-term follow-up; not automatic lifelong identical dosing for every patient |
| **Monitoring** | Follow-up at 3 months to assess clinical signs, symptoms, application technique and adverse effects | Then every 3–6 months while unstable; at least annually when stable |
| **Refractory** | Reassess adherence/diagnosis, exclude neoplasia, then specialist-directed escalation | Surgery may be required for fixed scarring |

**Amount and application.** Apply the ointment only to skin involved by LS, in a fingertip-unit amount (one fingertip unit covers the whole vulva; the guideline notes a 30 g tube usually lasts about a year of long-term use). Applying it to unaffected skin adds atrophy and irritation without benefit. Mometasone furoate 0.1% once daily performed similarly to clobetasol in a 12-week head-to-head trial in women, and the guideline prefers a milder potent agent in children and in pregnancy. Add emollients. Ask every LS patient about voiding; a reduced flow rate or meatal change warrants urology referral and a retrograde urethrogram to look for a distal stricture.<sup>[[36]](#ref36)</sup>

**Adherence changes outcomes.** In a prospective cohort of 507 women with vulvar LS on titrated preventive corticosteroid (mean follow-up 4.7 years), none of the 357 adherent women developed VIN or SCC, versus 4.7% of the 150 partially adherent women; scarring or adhesions occurred in 3.4% versus 40%, and steroid-induced atrophy was rare and reversible. The design is observational and single-center, so it shows association rather than proof that treatment prevents cancer.<sup>[[37]](#ref37)</sup>

### BXO-specific considerations in males

- **Early or intermediate BXO** responds to topical corticosteroids; advanced or irreversibly scarred phimosis does not respond and requires circumcision<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[12]](#ref12)</sup>
- **German S3 guideline 2026.** In LS-associated phimosis unresponsive to standard therapy, circumcision with complete foreskin removal is indicated<sup>[[13]](#ref13)</sup>
- **Meatal stenosis** may require meatotomy or meatoplasty; topical steroids are adjunctive post-procedure<sup>[[12]](#ref12)</sup>
- **Long-term surveillance** is important; LS carries an increased SCC risk in affected tissue<sup>[[12]](#ref12)[[15]](#ref15)</sup>

## Lichen planus

Chronic autoimmune inflammatory condition. In uncircumcised men, genital LP can cause adhesions and phimosis; in women, erosive vulvovaginal LP can cause scarring, synechiae, and vaginal stenosis.<sup>[[16]](#ref16)[[17]](#ref17)</sup>

### Treatment

| Line | Agent |
|---|---|
| **First-line** | **Clobetasol 0.05% ointment** — cutaneous, genital, and mucosal erosive LP<sup>[[3]](#ref3)[[16]](#ref16)[[17]](#ref17)</sup> |
| Erosive vulvar LP | Clobetasol BID attenuated symptoms in **71%** in a prospective cohort; complete resolution (except scarring) uncommon<sup>[[16]](#ref16)</sup> |
| **Synechiae prevention** | Vaginal dilators (women); foreskin retraction (men) to prevent adhesions<sup>[[16]](#ref16)</sup> |
| **Second-line** | Tacrolimus 0.1% — particularly for vulvovaginal LP<sup>[[3]](#ref3)[[17]](#ref17)</sup> |
| **Refractory** | Intralesional corticosteroids; systemic corticosteroids; acitretin; mycophenolate<sup>[[3]](#ref3)[[17]](#ref17)</sup> |
| **Uncircumcised men** | **Circumcision usually recommended** — LP occurs less frequently in circumcised men<sup>[[16]](#ref16)</sup> |

## Genital psoriasis

Affects up to 63% of psoriasis patients at some point; frequently underdiagnosed because patients are neither questioned nor examined for this manifestation.<sup>[[18]](#ref18)</sup>

**Treatment principles unique to genital skin:**<sup>[[19]](#ref19)[[18]](#ref18)</sup>

- Warm moist environment enhances drug penetration and irritation / atrophy risk
- **Low-potency corticosteroids** (classes VI–VII) are preferred
- **Calcineurin inhibitors** (tacrolimus 0.1%, pimecrolimus 1%) are off-label steroid-sparing options with short-term evidence in facial, genital and inverse psoriasis; most studies do not isolate genital outcomes<sup>[[20]](#ref20)[[18]](#ref18)</sup>
- **Vitamin D analogues** (calcipotriene) may cause genital irritation; calcitriol better tolerated<sup>[[18]](#ref18)</sup>
- **Proactive maintenance** after clearing: twice-weekly low-potency corticosteroid or calcineurin inhibitor to prevent relapse<sup>[[19]](#ref19)</sup>

## Zoon balanitis (plasma cell balanitis)

Idiopathic chronic inflammatory condition of the glans and prepuce — "cayenne-pepper" speckled plaques, almost exclusively in uncircumcised men.<sup>[[21]](#ref21)[[22]](#ref22)</sup>

**Treatment hierarchy:**

1. **Circumcision.** Considered curative<sup>[[22]](#ref22)[[23]](#ref23)</sup>
2. **Topical corticosteroids.** Trimovate cream (clobetasone butyrate, oxytetracycline and nystatin): clinical resolution in 10 of 10 cases in one series; intralesional or topical steroids yielded satisfactory improvement in another<sup>[[24]](#ref24)[[23]](#ref23)</sup>
3. **Topical calcineurin inhibitors.** Tacrolimus 0.1% BID with good results in 4 weeks in a 9-patient series; option for recalcitrant cases<sup>[[21]](#ref21)[[25]](#ref25)</sup>
4. **Topical mupirocin.** Case report of resolution with monotherapy over 3 months<sup>[[22]](#ref22)</sup>

## Labial adhesions

Common in prepubertal girls; topical corticosteroids are an alternative to topical estrogen for symptomatic cases.<sup>[[26]](#ref26)[[27]](#ref27)</sup>

- **Myers 2006.** Betamethasone 0.05% cream achieved complete separation in 68% (13/19); 14 of the 19 girls had previously failed conjugated estrogen, and 85% of the responders separated after a single 4–6-week course<sup>[[27]](#ref27)</sup>
- **Huseynov 2020 classification.** Betamethasone valerate 0.1% achieved 100% in type I (2 wk) and 80% in type II (3 wk); ineffective in type III–IV (thick dense adhesions)<sup>[[28]](#ref28)</sup>
- **Mayoglou 2009.** Betamethasone separated adhesions faster (1.3 versus 2.2 mo) with fewer recurrences and fewer side effects than topical estrogen<sup>[[29]](#ref29)</sup>
- **Dowlut-McElroy 2019 RCT.** Lateral traction plus topical estrogen was better than emollient for adhesion severity, but complete resolution rates not different (36% versus 19%; p = 0.21)<sup>[[30]](#ref30)</sup>

**NASPAG 2015:** for symptomatic labial adhesions, topical estrogen and/or steroid cream is often curative; surgical separation for failures; recurrence common until puberty.<sup>[[26]](#ref26)</sup>

## Intralesional and intravesical triamcinolone in urology

### IC/BPS with Hunner lesions

**AUA IC/BPS guideline 2022 (Recommendation; Grade C):** if Hunner lesions are present, fulguration with electrocautery and/or injection of triamcinolone should be performed. One of the few IC/BPS therapies producing months-long improvement after a single procedure.<sup>[[31]](#ref31)</sup>

### Bladder instillation — negative and comparative data

- **Cardenas-Trowers 2021 RCT** (n = 90): adding triamcinolone to a standard BTH instillation did not improve symptoms versus standard (OLS change −6.7 vs −5.8; p = 0.31)<sup>[[32]](#ref32)</sup>
- **Moss 2023 RCT** (n = 83 newly diagnosed IC/PBS): DMSO plus triamcinolone and BTH both improved symptoms; the primary >30% ICSI responder comparison was 63% versus 43%, p = 0.15, so the trial did not establish a superior overall regimen<sup>[[33]](#ref33)</sup>

See [Intralesional corticosteroids](/docs/foundations/pharmacology/dermatologic-topical-urethral/intralesional-corticosteroids) and [Intravesical IC/BPS agents](/docs/foundations/pharmacology/bladder-pain-ic-bps) for the broader instillation framework.

## Adverse effects on genital skin

Thin skin and occlusion increase absorption, but risk depends on the formulation, amount, site and duration. Correctly supervised LS therapy is generally well tolerated; do not equate disease-related atrophy with steroid injury. The table lists possible adverse effects and their risk factors, not measured complication rates for LS.<sup>[[5]](#ref5)[[6]](#ref6)[[13]](#ref13)</sup>

| Adverse effect | Main consideration | Clinical significance |
|---|---|---|
| **Skin atrophy** | Potency, amount, duration and occlusion | Most concerning; worse with higher potency, longer duration, and occlusion |
| Striae | Excessive/prolonged exposure | **Irreversible** once formed |
| Telangiectasia | Excessive/prolonged exposure | Cosmetically concerning |
| Hypopigmentation | Local effect | More apparent in darker skin tones |
| Red-scrotum syndrome | Reassess prolonged exposure and diagnosis | Persistent redness after prolonged TCS use |
| Secondary infection | Consider when symptoms worsen | Candida or bacterial superinfection |
| Contact sensitization | Consider when treatment irritates | Usually vehicle components |
| **HPA-axis suppression** | Depends on potency, area, duration and occlusion | Reported at doses as low as **2 g/day** in eczema; **50 g/week is a product ceiling, not a safe genital dose**<sup>[[34]](#ref34)</sup> |
| Folliculitis / acneiform eruption | Local effect | More common with occlusion |

### Safety and label boundaries

The cited US clobetasol ointment label limits treatment to two consecutive weeks and ≤50 g/week and advises against groin application. Guideline-directed genital LS treatment is a supervised off-label use, with small site-specific amounts and follow-up; the package ceiling is not a target genital regimen. Potency depends on vehicle and formulation, so the class table is only an orientation.<sup>[[34]](#ref34)[[36]](#ref36)</sup>

Children are more susceptible to systemic absorption. Assess application technique, skin changes and cumulative exposure rather than using a universal genital duration limit.<sup>[[34]](#ref34)</sup>

## Second-line — topical calcineurin inhibitors

For LS, consider a TCI when appropriate corticosteroid therapy is ineffective, contraindicated or not tolerated; controlled disease alone is not a reason to switch:<sup>[[3]](#ref3)[[14]](#ref14)[[21]](#ref21)</sup>

| Agent | Concentration | Key advantages | Key disadvantages | Best indications |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Tacrolimus ointment** | 0.03%, 0.1% | No atrophy; effective for LS, LP, Zoon | Burning / stinging; boxed warning: long-term safety unestablished; causality of reported malignancies unproven | Steroid-refractory LS; vulvovaginal LP; Zoon balanitis; psoriasis maintenance |
| **Pimecrolimus cream** | 1% | No atrophy; less burning than tacrolimus | Less effective than clobetasol for LS (investigator-rated); variable Zoon results | Selected second-line LS; psoriasis; steroid-sparing |

See [Topical calcineurin inhibitors](/docs/foundations/pharmacology/dermatologic-topical-urethral/topical-calcineurin-inhibitors) for the dedicated deep-dive.

## Clinical Positioning

- **Topical corticosteroids are first-line for symptomatic phimosis.** Cochrane found improved resolution with low-certainty evidence; comparative studies do not establish one universally best potency. Specify the product and concentration, and avoid forced retraction.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[8]](#ref8)[[9]](#ref9)</sup>
- **Altered preputial skin appearance predicts failure** (29% success versus 72% with healthy skin); it signals underlying BXO, and those patients often need circumcision.<sup>[[10]](#ref10)</sup>
- **LS needs long-term follow-up and individualized disease control.** See the induction/maintenance framework above; reassess fixed scarring and suspicious lesions rather than simply increasing steroid exposure.<sup>[[13]](#ref13)[[36]](#ref36)</sup>
- **Ointment is better than cream on genital skin.** Better barrier, fewer irritants, better delivery.<sup>[[5]](#ref5)[[7]](#ref7)</sup>
- **Do not use topical testosterone, DHT, or progesterone for LS.** Cochrane shows no benefit, and topical testosterone actually worsened symptoms as maintenance after clobetasol.<sup>[[14]](#ref14)</sup>
- **Always biopsy atypical or refractory lesions.** SCC risk in LS is real, and ACOG recommends vulvar biopsy for any atypical, refractory, or worsening lesion.<sup>[[3]](#ref3)</sup>
- **Zoon balanitis is curative with circumcision.** Medical therapy (Trimovate, tacrolimus) is appropriate if circumcision is declined.<sup>[[23]](#ref23)[[24]](#ref24)</sup>
- **Symptomatic labial adhesions may be treated with topical estrogen or steroid.** The reported faster separation with betamethasone came from retrospective data and does not establish universal superiority; asymptomatic adhesions usually need observation.<sup>[[26]](#ref26)[[27]](#ref27)[[29]](#ref29)</sup>
- **Genital psoriasis uses low-potency steroids, not high-potency steroids.** Use calcineurin inhibitors for maintenance.<sup>[[18]](#ref18)</sup>
- **Use site-specific amounts under supervision.** The clobetasol package maximum is not a genital dosing recommendation.<sup>[[34]](#ref34)</sup>
- **Intralesional triamcinolone** is the instillation-adjacent indication (AUA Grade C for Hunner lesions); adding it to standard BTH does not improve outcomes beyond it (Cardenas-Trowers RCT).<sup>[[31]](#ref31)[[32]](#ref32)</sup>
