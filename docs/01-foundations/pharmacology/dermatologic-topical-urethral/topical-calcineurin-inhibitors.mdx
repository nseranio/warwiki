# Topical Calcineurin Inhibitors

**Topical calcineurin inhibitors (TCIs)** — **tacrolimus** (0.03% and 0.1% ointment) and **pimecrolimus** (1% cream) — serve primarily as **second-line, steroid-sparing anti-inflammatory agents** in urology and urogynecology. Their **off-label genital uses** include **genital lichen sclerosus, vulvar / penile lichen planus, Zoon balanitis, and other anogenital inflammatory dermatoses**.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[2]](#ref2)[[3]](#ref3)</sup> A novel investigational application is **intravesical tacrolimus for refractory IC/BPS**.

The defining advantage over topical corticosteroids is **no risk of skin atrophy** — a critical property in the thin, sensitive anogenital skin, when individualized long-term treatment is needed.<sup>[[3]](#ref3)[[4]](#ref4)</sup>

For related agents, see [High-potency topical corticosteroids](/docs/foundations/pharmacology/dermatologic-topical-urethral/high-potency-topical-corticosteroids), [Intralesional corticosteroids](/docs/foundations/pharmacology/dermatologic-topical-urethral/intralesional-corticosteroids), [Platelet-rich plasma](/docs/foundations/pharmacology/dermatologic-topical-urethral/platelet-rich-plasma), [Intravesical IC/BPS agents](/docs/foundations/pharmacology/bladder-pain-ic-bps), and the clinical [Lichen sclerosus](/docs/clinical-conditions/03g-genital-scrotal/lichen-sclerosus) article.

---

**US labeling:** these products are second-line atopic-dermatitis therapies, not FDA-approved genital LS treatments. Tacrolimus ointment is labeled for moderate to severe atopic dermatitis: adults may use 0.03% or 0.1%, and children aged 2–15 use 0.03% only. Pimecrolimus 1% cream is labeled for mild to moderate atopic dermatitis from age 2. Neither is labeled for children under two, and both labels call for short-term, non-continuous use and stopping when signs resolve. Genital use requires deliberate off-label selection.<sup>[[7]](#ref7)[[35]](#ref35)</sup>

## Mechanism

TCIs inhibit **calcineurin**, a calcium-dependent phosphatase required for NFAT-mediated T-cell activation. The resulting block suppresses transcription of **IL-2, IL-3, IL-4, IFN-γ, and TNF**.<sup>[[5]](#ref5)[[6]](#ref6)</sup> Additional mast-cell stabilization and suppression of histamine release contribute to the **antipruritic effect**.<sup>[[7]](#ref7)[[8]](#ref8)</sup>

Critically, TCIs **do not affect collagen synthesis** and therefore **do not cause skin atrophy, striae, telangiectasia, or the rebound effects** seen with long-term corticosteroid use — the pharmacologic basis for their role as steroid-sparing maintenance agents in chronic genital dermatoses.<sup>[[3]](#ref3)[[4]](#ref4)</sup>

## Vulvar and anogenital lichen sclerosus — primary indication

The most extensively studied urogynecologic application.

**Second-line positioning:** the EuroGuiDerm guideline and its German S3 adaptation recommend ultrapotent or potent topical corticosteroids as standard therapy for genital LS at any age, with calcineurin inhibitors as second-line therapy when corticosteroids are insufficient or contraindicated. TCIs remain off-label for genital disease.<sup>[[9]](#ref9)[[36]](#ref36)[[38]](#ref38)</sup>

### Evidence

| Study | Design | Finding |
|---|---|---|
| **Hengge 2006** | Phase II multicenter (n = 84) — tacrolimus 0.1% BID × 16 wk | **43% clearance** of active LS + **34% partial resolution**; significant reduction in lesional area and symptom scores; **only 9% recurred** during 18-mo follow-up<sup>[[10]](#ref10)</sup> |
| **Funaro 2014 RCT** | Double-blind clobetasol 0.05% vs tacrolimus 0.1% | Both reduced symptoms; **clobetasol significantly more effective** for complete absence of signs/symptoms (p = 0.002)<sup>[[11]](#ref11)</sup> |
| **Chi 2011 Cochrane** | Systematic review | Pimecrolimus comparable to clobetasol for symptom relief; **less effective for gross clinical improvement**<sup>[[12]](#ref12)[[13]](#ref13)</sup> |
| **Mazzilli 2018** | Open study, 10 girls aged 4–9 with vulvar LS — tacrolimus 0.03% BID × 6 wk | Well-tolerated; significant improvement in itching, pain, and constipation<sup>[[14]](#ref14)</sup> |

### Clinical position

Consider TCIs after reassessing the diagnosis, application technique, adherence and possible neoplasia in disease that is not responding. Appropriate uses include corticosteroid intolerance or contraindication, and selected inadequate response. **Do not routinely replace effective corticosteroid maintenance**, or interpret LS-related atrophy as proof of steroid injury. Pediatric use requires specialist selection rather than substitution based on steroid fear alone.<sup>[[15]](#ref15)[[16]](#ref16)[[36]](#ref36)</sup>

**Clobetasol 0.05% ointment remains first-line for induction**; TCIs are second-line and steroid-sparing. See [High-potency topical corticosteroids](/docs/foundations/pharmacology/dermatologic-topical-urethral/high-potency-topical-corticosteroids) for the induction framework.

## Penile lichen sclerosus / BXO

**Shieh 2024** *J Urol* systematic review — topical corticosteroids remain the mainstay of conservative management for penile / urethral LS, with **tacrolimus supported as an alternative or adjuvant** when escalation is needed.<sup>[[17]](#ref17)</sup>

Circumcision can resolve foreskin-limited pediatric BXO, but meatal or urethral disease may persist. Evidence for topical immunomodulators preventing recurrent meatal stenosis is limited; they do not replace follow-up or management of functional obstruction.<sup>[[18]](#ref18)</sup>

**Kim 2012** — tacrolimus in anogenital LS (n = 10; extragenital LS responded poorly): **90% objective response** (50% complete, 40% partial). However, **relapse was common (6/9)** during long-term follow-up, underscoring the need for ongoing maintenance therapy.<sup>[[19]](#ref19)</sup>

---

## Vulvar and genital lichen planus

**ACOG Level B:** TCIs for LP resistant to topical corticosteroid therapy.<sup>[[9]](#ref9)</sup>

- **Byrd 2004** — retrospective data on tacrolimus for recalcitrant vulvar LP showed effective symptom control, but benefits were **not sustained after discontinuation** — individualized ongoing treatment may be needed<sup>[[20]](#ref20)</sup>
- **Goldstein 2009** — both tacrolimus and pimecrolimus supported as effective and well-tolerated second-line options for genital LP<sup>[[3]](#ref3)</sup>

## Other anogenital dermatoses

| Condition | TCI role | Evidence |
|---|---|---|
| **Plasma cell (Zoon) balanitis** | Tacrolimus 0.1% — good results within 4 weeks | Kyriakou 2014 series (n = 9)<sup>[[21]](#ref21)[[22]](#ref22)</sup> |
| **Genital psoriasis** | Off-label steroid-sparing treatment; short-term benefit in studies mixing facial, inverse and genital sites | Amiri 2023 SR (24 studies, five RCTs); no single pooled genital clearance rate<sup>[[23]](#ref23)[[24]](#ref24)</sup> |
| **Perianal eczema** | Tacrolimus 0.1% BID × 2 weeks | Schauber 2009 (n = 24) — clinical improvement in all returning patients<sup>[[25]](#ref25)</sup> |
| **Resistant anogenital pruritus** | Tacrolimus — likely via TRPV1 activation | Yosipovitch 2013<sup>[[26]](#ref26)</sup> |

## Intravesical tacrolimus for IC/BPS — investigational

A novel off-label application under active investigation.

- **Mishra 2019 pilot** (n = 24 intractable IC/BPS) — tacrolimus dissolved in DMSO/sterile water instilled intravesically; **improvement in 54% (13/24)** over 6–63-month follow-up. Serum tacrolimus levels remained safe; no significant systemic AEs<sup>[[27]](#ref27)</sup>
- **2025 global consensus** lists tacrolimus among immunotherapies for IC/BPS, alongside cyclosporine<sup>[[28]](#ref28)</sup>

**Cyclosporine A has stronger evidence** in IC/BPS based on Di 2021 network meta-analysis and Giannantoni 2012 European Urology systematic review — intravesical tacrolimus remains **early-stage off-label** pending controlled trials.<sup>[[29]](#ref29)[[30]](#ref30)</sup>

See [Intravesical IC/BPS agents](/docs/foundations/pharmacology/bladder-pain-ic-bps) for the complete instillation armamentarium.

## Genital graft-versus-host disease — caution

- **Choi 2001** case series (n = 18 steroid-refractory chronic cutaneous GVHD) — tacrolimus 0.1% improved pruritus or erythema in 13 (72%), often as a bridge to systemic therapy<sup>[[4]](#ref4)</sup>
- **Elad 2003** — 7 of 10 patients with steroid-dependent chronic cutaneous GVHD responded to tacrolimus 0.03–0.1%<sup>[[31]](#ref31)</sup>
- **Rostagno 2022 scoping review** concluded no topical intervention has strong evidence in GVHD — topical therapies serve as adjuncts to systemic treatment<sup>[[32]](#ref32)</sup>

**FDA label caution:** avoid topical tacrolimus in cutaneous GVHD due to skin-barrier defects that can drive systemic absorption. **Toxic tacrolimus levels have been reported** when occlusive dressings are used concurrently.<sup>[[33]](#ref33)[[34]](#ref34)</sup>

## Safety

### Adverse effects

| Concern | Details |
|---|---|
| **Local irritation** | Burning / stinging / pruritus — most common in first days; **subsides with continued use**<sup>[[5]](#ref5)[[6]](#ref6)[[7]](#ref7)</sup> |
| **FDA boxed warning** | "Long-term safety of topical calcineurin inhibitors has not been established." Rare cases of malignancy (skin and lymphoma) have been reported, and a causal relationship has not been established. The label directs avoiding continuous long-term use and, for pimecrolimus, use in children under 2<sup>[[7]](#ref7)[[35]](#ref35)</sup> |
| **No skin atrophy** | Key advantage over corticosteroids for chronic anogenital use<sup>[[3]](#ref3)</sup> |
| **Systemic absorption** | Minimal through intact skin; caution with skin-barrier defects (GVHD, Netherton's), occlusive dressings, or large BSA application<sup>[[7]](#ref7)[[33]](#ref33)</sup> |
| **Avoid on premalignant / malignant lesions** | Per FDA label (pimecrolimus label states these can present as dermatitis); avoid in immunocompromised patients<sup>[[7]](#ref7)[[35]](#ref35)</sup> |
| **Sun protection** | The tacrolimus label advises minimizing natural or artificial sunlight exposure during treatment<sup>[[7]](#ref7)</sup> |

**Malignancy evidence:** in the Adams 2026 TriNetX cohort (6,227 TCI-exposed LS patients), TCI exposure was not associated with increased vulvar dysplasia or malignancy at any time point, and risk did not differ significantly from clobetasol; clobetasol alone showed a lower 2-year malignancy risk (RR 0.62).<sup>[[37]](#ref37)</sup> Adults in the European JOELLE cohort showed little evidence linking TCIs to skin cancer or lymphoma, though a cutaneous T-cell lymphoma signal for tacrolimus (IRR 1.80) and modest excesses for pimecrolimus persisted and confounding by indication is likely; results in children were inconclusive.<sup>[[39]](#ref39)</sup> These are observational data and do not prove long-term safety or equivalence to clobetasol. See [clinical LS malignancy risk](/docs/clinical-conditions/03g-genital-scrotal/lichen-sclerosus#malignancy-risk) for the study and its limits.

### Tacrolimus vs pimecrolimus

| Agent | Strength | Practical differentiators |
|---|---|---|
| **Tacrolimus ointment 0.03% / 0.1%** | 0.1% is the standard adult dose; 0.03% used in pediatrics | Most LS, LP and Zoon balanitis data are for tacrolimus; **more burning / stinging on application** |
| **Pimecrolimus cream 1%** | Single strength | One RCT in LS found symptom relief similar to clobetasol but **less gross clinical improvement**; selected second-line LS use when appropriate corticosteroid treatment cannot be used<sup>[[12]](#ref12)[[13]](#ref13)</sup> |

## Clinical Positioning

- **TCIs are second-line, steroid-sparing agents** for chronic anogenital inflammatory dermatoses — their defining feature is **no skin atrophy**, which can support selected intermittent use in chronic disease.<sup>[[3]](#ref3)[[9]](#ref9)</sup>
- **Clobetasol remains first-line in LS.** Do not automatically switch every controlled patient to tacrolimus; assess disease activity, steroid tolerance and the limited long-term genital TCI evidence.<sup>[[11]](#ref11)[[12]](#ref12)</sup>
- **In pediatric LS**, corticosteroids remain first line. Small TCI series support selected specialist use, but do not establish superior long-term safety or efficacy over appropriately supervised corticosteroid treatment.<sup>[[9]](#ref9)[[14]](#ref14)</sup>
- **Counsel about application-site burning** — the dominant early AE; reassure that it **subsides with continued use** over 1–2 weeks to improve adherence.<sup>[[5]](#ref5)[[6]](#ref6)</sup>
- **Explain the boxed warning accurately:** malignancy causality is unestablished, but the label does not certify long-term safety. Avoid continuous indefinite application, suspicious lesions and immunocompromised use without specialist assessment.<sup>[[7]](#ref7)</sup>
- **Pimecrolimus is less effective than clobetasol for LS gross improvement** — consider it only as a selected second-line option, with response monitoring.<sup>[[12]](#ref12)[[13]](#ref13)</sup>
- **In Zoon balanitis, circumcision remains curative**; tacrolimus is the best-supported non-surgical option for patients declining or unable to undergo circumcision.<sup>[[21]](#ref21)</sup>
- **Intravesical tacrolimus for IC/BPS is investigational** (Mishra 2019 — 54% response). Cyclosporine has stronger evidence in this setting; do not default to intravesical tacrolimus as routine care outside a properly evaluated specialist research protocol.<sup>[[27]](#ref27)[[29]](#ref29)</sup>
- **Avoid topical tacrolimus on broken skin or under occlusion** — systemic absorption can reach toxic levels (GVHD case reports). In cutaneous GVHD specifically, the FDA cautions against its use.<sup>[[33]](#ref33)[[34]](#ref34)</sup>
- **Avoid application on premalignant or malignant lesions.** Evaluate unresponsive or atypical vulvar lesions with biopsy before escalating immunomodulatory therapy — CTCL and early vulvar SCC can masquerade as dermatitis or LS.<sup>[[7]](#ref7)[[9]](#ref9)</sup>
- **Sun protection during treatment** is reasonable given photocarcinogenicity signals.<sup>[[4]](#ref4)</sup>
