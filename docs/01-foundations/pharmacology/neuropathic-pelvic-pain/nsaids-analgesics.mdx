# NSAIDs and Analgesics

**NSAIDs and analgesics** play several roles in urology: (1) acute renal colic (the most evidence-based application, where NSAIDs are first-line over opioids); (2) perioperative pain management in urologic surgery; (3) ureteral stent symptoms; (4) analgesia during observation / medical expulsive therapy (MET) for ureteral stones; (5) chronic prostatitis / chronic pelvic pain syndrome (CP/CPPS); (6) interstitial cystitis / bladder pain syndrome (IC/BPS); (7) bladder cancer chemoprevention (investigational); and (8) urinary tract analgesia (phenazopyridine). NSAIDs carry significant nephrotoxicity risk that is amplified in urologic patients (obstructive uropathy, solitary kidney, CKD, RAAS-inhibitor use), and the opioid-stewardship movement has fundamentally reshaped analgesic prescribing in the field.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[2]](#ref2)[[3]](#ref3)[[4]](#ref4)</sup>

For the analgesia comparator classes (broadly framed as nonnarcotic methods of pain management<sup>[[9]](#ref9)</sup>), see [Gabapentinoids](/docs/foundations/pharmacology/neuropathic-pelvic-pain/gabapentinoids), [SNRIs](/docs/foundations/pharmacology/neuropathic-pelvic-pain/snris), and [Tricyclic antidepressants](/docs/foundations/pharmacology/neuropathic-pelvic-pain/tricyclic-antidepressants).

## Mechanism — Why NSAIDs Work in the LUT

NSAIDs inhibit COX, which reduces prostaglandin synthesis. The class effects are urologic at four levels:<sup>[[2]](#ref2)</sup>

1. **Renal pelvis and ureter.** Prostaglandins drive ureteral spasm, mucosal inflammation, and renal vasodilation around an obstructing stone. NSAIDs blunt all three, lowering urine output and intra-pelvic pressure, a mechanism different from opioids, which only mask central pain
2. **Ureteral smooth muscle.** Reduced peristaltic spasm; a proposed, not established, stone-expulsion effect<sup>[[5]](#ref5)</sup>
3. **Bladder and prostate.** COX-2 is upregulated in inflamed prostate and urothelium; these mechanisms do not establish disease modification in CP/CPPS or IC/BPS<sup>[[6]](#ref6)</sup>
4. **Bladder cancer biology.** COX-2 is overexpressed in transitional cell carcinoma; COX-2 inhibition induces apoptosis, blocks EMT, and reduces tumor growth in preclinical models<sup>[[7]](#ref7)[[8]](#ref8)</sup>

## Acute Renal Colic — the primary urologic application

The most evidence-based use of NSAIDs in urology, supported by multiple Cochrane reviews and large meta-analyses.

### Headline evidence

- **Cochrane 2025 (29 RCTs, n = 3,593; search through August 2023):** the placebo comparison favored NSAIDs at 30 minutes (MD −3.84 cm on a 10-cm VAS; 95% CI −6.41 to −1.27), but this came from only three studies / 250 participants, with high heterogeneity and low certainty. Agent comparisons, including a possible IV ibuprofen advantage over ketorolac, do not establish a universally best NSAID. Evidence about harms was uncertain; IV and IM routes probably have similar analgesic effects.<sup>[[2]](#ref2)</sup>
- **Pathan 2018 meta (36 RCTs, n = 4,887):** NSAIDs marginally outperform opioids at 30 min (small mean difference with substantial heterogeneity); fewer rescue treatments (NNT 11) and lower vomiting (NNT 5) versus opioids; fewer rescue treatments than paracetamol (RR 0.56; p &lt; 0.05)<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup>
- **Network comparisons:** indirect rankings of drug / route combinations should not override renal, gastrointestinal and cardiovascular risk, contraindications, or local availability.<sup>[[10]](#ref10)</sup> EAU 2026 recommends an NSAID as first choice when appropriate, and decompression or stone removal for analgesia-refractory colic.<sup>[[49]](#ref49)</sup>
- **Pathan 2016 *Lancet* RCT (1,645 randomized; 1,644 intention-to-treat):** at least 50% pain reduction by 30 minutes occurred in 68% with IM diclofenac, 66% with IV paracetamol and 61% with IV morphine. Diclofenac was superior to morphine in the primary analysis (p = 0.0187); paracetamol was not (p = 0.0629). Both comparisons favored the nonopioid drug in the smaller imaging-confirmed, per-protocol population; that does not replace the primary analysis.<sup>[[18]](#ref18)</sup>
- **CDC Guideline 2022:** opioids are not recommended as first-line therapy for kidney-stone pain<sup>[[19]](#ref19)</sup>

### Agent selection and dosing boundaries

The following are adult examples, not interchangeable prescriptions. Select the agent, route and dose for the patient and the local product label.

| Agent | Example / boundary | Key limitation |
|---|---|---|
| **Diclofenac** | 75 mg IM was used in the large renal-colic trial | Check cardiovascular, GI and renal risk; formulation availability differs by country<sup>[[18]](#ref18)[[49]](#ref49)</sup> |
| **Ketorolac** | US single IV dose: 30 mg if under 65; **15 mg** if ≥65, renally impaired or under 50 kg | Use lower effective doses when appropriate. **All IV/IM and oral treatment combined must stay within 5 days**; US oral dosing is continuation of parenteral therapy, not initial therapy<sup>[[47]](#ref47)</sup> |
| **Ibuprofen** | Oral or IV according to clinical need and the product label | Comparative studies do not justify routinely choosing the highest IV dose<sup>[[2]](#ref2)</sup> |
| **Acetaminophen / paracetamol IV** | ≥50 kg: 1,000 mg every 6 h or 650 mg every 4 h; under 50 kg requires weight-based dosing | Infuse over 15 minutes; include every route and combination product in the total dose (see below)<sup>[[48]](#ref48)</sup> |

:::warning[NSAID safety before prescribing]
Ketorolac is contraindicated with active or prior peptic ulcer / GI bleeding, advanced renal impairment or risk of renal failure from volume depletion, concurrent aspirin / other NSAIDs, and CABG surgery. Because it inhibits platelet function, it is also contraindicated with suspected or confirmed cerebrovascular bleeding, hemorrhagic diathesis, incomplete hemostasis or high bleeding risk, and as prophylactic analgesia before any major surgery (give it after hemostasis is secure). Other labeled contraindications include labor and delivery, neuraxial (epidural / intrathecal) administration because of the alcohol content, and concurrent probenecid or pentoxifylline. Injectable daily totals are capped at 120 mg, or 60 mg for patients ≥65 years, renally impaired or under 50 kg. NSAIDs carry serious GI bleeding and cardiovascular thrombotic risks. Check bleeding risk and anticoagulants as well as renal function. Do not assume a COX-2-selective drug removes these risks.<sup>[[47]](#ref47)</sup>

FDA recommends limiting NSAIDs at 20–30 weeks of pregnancy because of fetal renal dysfunction / oligohydramnios, and avoiding them from about 30 weeks because of premature ductus arteriosus closure. If use at 20–30 weeks is necessary, use the lowest dose for the shortest duration; consider amniotic-fluid monitoring if treatment exceeds 48 hours.<sup>[[50]](#ref50)</sup>
:::

## Perioperative Pain Management

NSAIDs are the foundation of opioid-sparing multimodal analgesia in urologic surgery.

### Opioid-sparing magnitude (literature review of 32 trials)<sup>[[21]](#ref21)</sup>

These ranges come mainly from patient-controlled-analgesia studies across surgical fields, not urology-specific trials.

- **Diclofenac** reduces opioid consumption 17–50%
- **Ketorolac** 9–66%
- **Ibuprofen** 22–46%
- **Ketoprofen** 34–66%

Several included trials reported less nausea, vomiting, sedation and pruritus with NSAIDs. Absence of a bleeding signal in these trials does not establish safety for every operation, patient, NSAID or dose; assess the specific procedure and contraindications.

### SKOPE RCT — ketorolac vs oxycodone post-URS<sup>[[11]](#ref11)</sup>

This double-blind noninferiority trial included 81 patients and compared as-needed ketorolac 10 mg with oxycodone 5 mg over postoperative days 1–5. Both groups also received three oxycodone rescue tablets. Pain outcomes supported ketorolac as an alternative for suitable patients, with fewer days confined to bed (1.3 versus 2.3; p = 0.02). It supports reducing routine opioid prescribing, not withholding rescue analgesia or ignoring ketorolac contraindications / the US oral-continuation restriction.<sup>[[47]](#ref47)</sup>

### Opioid-stewardship results in urology

#### NOPIOIDS protocol (Mian 2023)<sup>[[17]](#ref17)</sup>

This single-center, nonrandomized cohort study followed 647 opioid-naive patients through sequential prescribing pathways after radical cystectomy, radical / partial nephrectomy, or radical prostatectomy:

- Discharge opioid prescription rate: from 80.9% (control) to 57.9% (lead-in) to 2.2% (NOPIOIDS)
- Median tablets prescribed: from 14 to 4 to 0 (p &lt; 0.001)
- The pathway recorded low pain scores and high satisfaction, with no detected increase in complications. Selection, temporal confounding and incomplete patient-reported follow-up limit causal or universal safety claims; individualized rescue access remains necessary.

#### ORIOLES — post-prostatectomy<sup>[[22]](#ref22)[[46]](#ref46)</sup>

In 205 RP patients with 100% follow-up, median prescribed 225 mg OMEQ but median used 22.5 mg OMEQ; 77% of prescribed opioids unused; 84% required ≤ 112.5 mg OMEQ; only 9% appropriately disposed of leftover medication. Prescribing more was independently associated with greater use.

#### Prolapse and incontinence surgery<sup>[[54]](#ref54)[[55]](#ref55)[[56]](#ref56)</sup>

- **AUGS-IUGA 2022 ERAS consensus:** multimodal analgesia with restrictive opioid use is among the components graded as high-level evidence for urogynecologic surgery.<sup>[[56]](#ref56)</sup>
- **Consumption versus prescribing (Dahl 2022, 261 women, 9 centers):** median 28 MME consumed (about three to four oxycodone 5-mg tablets) against 75 MME prescribed. Higher use tracked with BMI above 30, chronic pain, high baseline pain, greater blood loss and longer operations, which helps identify who needs more.<sup>[[55]](#ref55)</sup>
- **Restrictive prescribing RCT (Yuan 2024, 133 randomized, single center, unblinded):** in opioid-naive women having minor or major prolapse / incontinence surgery, all receiving multimodal analgesia, no routine opioid prescription (opioid only on request) was noninferior to 3–10 oxycodone 5-mg tablets for satisfaction at 6 weeks (92.1% versus 92.2%). No opioid use in the first week was reported by 70.8% versus 48.4%; refills (6.7% versus 9.4%) and pain calls were similar.<sup>[[54]](#ref54)</sup>

#### Expert-panel prescribing ranges<sup>[[4]](#ref4)</sup>

Koo and colleagues’ multidisciplinary Delphi consensus concerned opioid-naive patients without chronic pain undergoing 16 endourological / minimally invasive procedures. It is an expert consensus, not a universal AUA drug limit. The minimum suggested quantity was zero; procedure-specific upper ranges varied from zero to 15 oxycodone 5-mg-equivalent tablets. Match prescribing to expected need, contraindications to nonopioid drugs, prior opioid exposure and access to reassessment.<sup>[[4]](#ref4)[[16]](#ref16)</sup>

## Ureteral Stent–Related Symptoms

NSAIDs are part of multimodal stent-symptom pharmacotherapy.<sup>[[15]](#ref15)</sup>

- **Alpha-blockers** carry the strongest monotherapy evidence
- **NSAIDs** target the inflammatory and pain components
- **Antimuscarinics** target the urinary symptom components
- Add treatments selectively rather than stacking drugs according to indirect rankings. Sedation and respiratory risks matter if considering [Gabapentinoids](/docs/foundations/pharmacology/neuropathic-pelvic-pain/gabapentinoids); the cited 2016 stent review does not establish a preferred pregabalin combination.<sup>[[15]](#ref15)</sup>

---

## Medical Expulsive Therapy

NSAIDs treat pain during conservative stone management; they are not established stone-expulsive treatment. EAU 2026 recommends offering off-label α-blockers as an option for distal ureteral stones 5–10 mm in patients suitable for observation.<sup>[[49]](#ref49)</sup>

- **Lv & Tang 2014 (RCT, 105 consented, 103 completed):** distal-stone expulsion was 94.3% with naftopidil plus celecoxib (200 mg every 12 h after a 400-mg load), 82.9% with naftopidil alone and 60.6% with celecoxib alone. Celecoxib alone was significantly worse than either naftopidil arm; the combination was not significantly better than naftopidil alone in the reported comparisons<sup>[[12]](#ref12)</sup>
- Small adjunct trials and mechanistic reviews are insufficient to recommend adding celecoxib specifically for stone passage.<sup>[[5]](#ref5)</sup>

## CP/CPPS

AUA 2025 allows anti-inflammatory agents as one component of multimodal CP/CPPS pain management, and discourages long-term use because of harms. Average improvements in reviews are small and uncertain; individual response should be reassessed.<sup>[[6]](#ref6)[[23]](#ref23)[[24]](#ref24)[[51]](#ref51)</sup>

- **Cochrane 2019 (7 RCTs, n = 585 men):** anti-inflammatories versus placebo: NIH-CPSI MD −2.5 (95% CI −3.74 to −1.26), a small average difference with low-certainty evidence; a within-person 6-point response threshold is not identical to a between-group mean difference<sup>[[23]](#ref23)</sup>
- **Network meta-analysis 2022 (25 RCTs, n = 3,514; 26 treatments):** few oral drugs beat placebo. The only ibuprofen-containing signal was a single-study doxazosin plus ibuprofen plus thiocolchicoside combination, with low- to very-low-quality evidence and a possible excess of adverse events; it does not isolate an NSAID effect<sup>[[24]](#ref24)</sup>
- Use an individualized short trial within multimodal treatment; the guideline does not prescribe a universal 4–6-week high-dose ibuprofen course. Routine opioid treatment is inappropriate for this chronic condition.<sup>[[6]](#ref6)[[51]](#ref51)</sup>
- Reasonable as part of multimodal therapy; limit to short courses to minimize nephrotoxicity and GI risk.

## IC/BPS

The 2022 AUA IC/BPS Guideline addresses pain pharmacology as a Clinical Principle: pain management for IC/BPS should mirror other chronic-pain conditions; non-opioid agents should be used preferentially; pain management alone is not sufficient; multimodal therapy is necessary.<sup>[[3]](#ref3)[[25]](#ref25)</sup>

Evidence does not establish NSAIDs as disease-specific IC/BPS treatment. Use for selected symptoms within the broader bladder-directed and pain-management plan; reassess benefit and toxicity.

---

## Phenazopyridine — the urinary-tract analgesic

Phenazopyridine offers short-term symptom relief and does not treat infection or its cause. The cited US prescription product is marketed as unapproved drug other; a DailyMed listing does not establish FDA approval. Follow the [phenazopyridine hub](/docs/foundations/pharmacology/legacy-low-evidence/phenazopyridine) for dosing, the two-day limit with antibacterials, renal contraindication, discoloration and methemoglobinemia / G6PD precautions.<sup>[[26]](#ref26)</sup> TRPM8 inhibition is a proposed mechanism from laboratory work, not proof of clinical benefit for chronic bladder pain.<sup>[[27]](#ref27)</sup>

## Celecoxib and Bladder Cancer Chemoprevention

Strong preclinical rationale; clinical trials are negative, and CV risk precludes routine use.

### Preclinical (strong)<sup>[[7]](#ref7)[[8]](#ref8)[[29]](#ref29)[[30]](#ref30)[[31]](#ref31)</sup>

- Celecoxib reduced bladder-cancer incidence 65–95% in rat models when given preventively
- Mechanisms include COX-2-dependent and -independent pathways: apoptosis, anti-EMT (miR-145 / TGFBR2 / Smad3), anti-inflammatory, anti-proliferative, antioxidant
- **Preventive but not curative** efficacy; curative dosing may aggravate tumor malignancy

### Clinical (negative)

| Trial | Design | Result |
|---|---|---|
| **BOXIT (Kelly 2019)** | Phase III RCT, n = 472, intermediate / high-risk NMIBC | **No significant benefit:** 3-year **recurrence-free rates** 68% vs 64% (HR 0.82, 95% CI 0.60–1.12; p = 0.2). Exploratory pT1 subgroup findings do not establish an indication. Serious CV events were numerically higher (5.2% vs 1.7%; p = 0.07)<sup>[[13]](#ref13)</sup> |
| **Sabichi 2011** | RCT, n = 146, high-risk NMIBC | Did not significantly prolong time to recurrence (p = 0.17; median follow-up 2.49 years). **12-month recurrence-free rates** were 88% vs 78%; adjusted HR 0.69 (95% CI 0.37–1.29). Exploratory recurrent-event analysis did not establish benefit<sup>[[14]](#ref14)</sup> |

Celecoxib is not recommended for bladder-cancer chemoprevention.

## NSAIDs and Erectile / Gonadal Function

The relationship between NSAIDs and ED is controversial and likely confounded by indication.

| Study | Direction |
|---|---|
| **Gleason 2011 (n = 80,966)** | Regular NSAID use associated with ED (aOR 1.38)<sup>[[32]](#ref32)</sup> |
| **Shiri 2006 (n = 1,126)** | NSAID use raised ED risk (IDR 2.0)<sup>[[33]](#ref33)</sup> |
| **Senbel preclinical** | Indomethacin reduced rat erectile responses; diclofenac less; celecoxib unaffected<sup>[[34]](#ref34)</sup> |
| **Patel PCPT (n = 4,726)** | After controlling for indication, **NSAID use not associated with ED** — apparent association attributable to confounding<sup>[[35]](#ref35)</sup> |
| **Halpern NHANES (n = 3,749)** | Regular NSAID use **not associated** with testosterone, AMH, or compensated hypogonadism<sup>[[36]](#ref36)</sup> |
| **Li 2018 systematic review** | Mixed; methodologically heterogeneous<sup>[[37]](#ref37)</sup> |

**Practical message:** current evidence does not support counseling patients that NSAIDs cause ED, although non-selective COX inhibitors (especially indomethacin) may have direct effects on erectile physiology in preclinical models.

## Nephrotoxicity — the critical urologic concern

NSAID nephrotoxicity is amplified in urologic patients.<sup>[[38]](#ref38)[[39]](#ref39)[[40]](#ref40)[[41]](#ref41)</sup>

### Mechanisms

1. **Hemodynamic AKI** (most common, usually reversible). NSAIDs blunt prostaglandin-mediated compensatory vasodilation in low-perfusion states, reducing GFR
2. **Acute interstitial nephritis** (rare, idiosyncratic). Any NSAID
3. **Renal papillary necrosis.** Chronic use
4. **Electrolyte disturbance.** Sodium / water retention, hyperkalemia, hyponatremia
5. **CKD.** Regular use may raise CKD incidence; relationship with progression less clear

### Risk factors that matter for the urologist

- Pre-existing CKD (CrCl &lt; 60)
- Volume depletion / dehydration
- Concurrent RAAS inhibitors and diuretics (the "triple whammy")
- Advanced age (&gt; 65)
- **Solitary kidney.** Common after nephrectomy
- **Bilateral or high-grade obstruction**
- Heart failure, cirrhosis

### Practical implications

- **Renal colic.** NSAIDs remain first-line in patients with adequate renal function; check baseline function and avoid in significant CKD, dehydration, or bilateral obstruction<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[41]](#ref41)</sup>
- **Solitary-kidney patients.** Favor acetaminophen as primary analgesic
- **Stone formers on thiazides.** Combination raises nephrotoxicity risk
- **Lowest effective dose for the shortest possible time** is the universal rule<sup>[[39]](#ref39)[[40]](#ref40)</sup>

## Acetaminophen / Paracetamol in Urology

A first-line analgesic when NSAIDs are contraindicated.

- **Renal colic.** IV paracetamol is an alternative when NSAIDs are unsuitable; the Pathan intention-to-treat analysis did not establish superiority over morphine<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[18]](#ref18)[[20]](#ref20)</sup>
- **Perioperative.** Core component of multimodal analgesia in ERAS protocols; reduces opioid consumption when paired with NSAIDs; the cited emergency-general-surgery guideline supports its use but does not establish a universal order of administration for urologic procedures<sup>[[42]](#ref42)</sup>
- **Renal safety.** Often preferable to NSAIDs when renal risk is high, but dose must reflect body weight, liver health and total exposure; little evidence that chronic paracetamol monotherapy causes CKD or analgesic nephropathy; a suitable first-line choice in CKD with dose individualization in advanced renal failure<sup>[[43]](#ref43)[[44]](#ref44)[[45]](#ref45)</sup>
- **CP/CPPS.** Recommended alongside short-term NSAIDs; opioids should be avoided<sup>[[6]](#ref6)</sup>

---

### Acetaminophen dose safeguards

The cited IV label limits total exposure from all routes and all combination products to 4 g/24 h for adults ≥50 kg. Under 50 kg, use 15 mg/kg every 6 h or 12.5 mg/kg every 4 h, maximum 75 mg/kg/day. These are ceilings, not goals; lower limits may be needed with hepatic risk, malnutrition or alcohol use. Severe hepatic impairment / severe active liver disease is a contraindication. Avoid accidental duplication with opioid–acetaminophen products.<sup>[[48]](#ref48)</sup>

## Opioid and Tramadol Label Safety

When an opioid is needed, the US immediate-release oxycodone label starts at 5–15 mg every 4–6 hours as needed, at the lowest dose that works, and titrates to response.<sup>[[57]](#ref57)</sup> The class boxed warning covers addiction, abuse and misuse; life-threatening respiratory depression (highest at initiation or dose increase); accidental ingestion; neonatal opioid withdrawal syndrome; interaction with CYP3A4 inhibitors; and profound sedation, respiratory depression and death with benzodiazepines or other CNS depressants (reserve co-prescribing for patients without alternatives). The label also directs prescribers to a REMS-compliant education program.<sup>[[57]](#ref57)[[58]](#ref58)</sup>

**Tramadol** is not a safer opioid for pelvic-pain or postoperative patients. Its label carries the same boxed warnings, plus:<sup>[[58]](#ref58)</sup>

- **Ultra-rapid CYP2D6 metabolism:** deaths from respiratory depression in children, some after tonsillectomy / adenoidectomy. It is contraindicated under 12 years and under 18 years after tonsillectomy / adenoidectomy.
- **CYP interactions:** CYP3A4 inducers or inhibitors and 2D6 inhibitors change exposure in complex ways; review the full medication list.
- **Seizure:** can occur at recommended doses; risk rises with epilepsy, prior seizures and other seizure-risk drugs.
- **Serotonin syndrome:** possible with tramadol alone and more likely with serotonergic drugs. Check for SNRIs, SSRIs and tricyclics, which are common in chronic pelvic pain regimens (see [SNRIs](/docs/foundations/pharmacology/neuropathic-pelvic-pain/snris)).
- **Dosing limits:** 50–100 mg every 4–6 hours as needed, maximum 400 mg/day. Above 75 years, maximum 300 mg/day. With creatinine clearance below 30 mL/min, dose every 12 hours with a 200 mg/day maximum.

For chronic pelvic pain, AUA 2025 does not include opioids among its treatments and advises that urologists not act as the long-term opioid prescriber for chronic pain syndromes; refer such patients to a pain specialist.<sup>[[51]](#ref51)</sup>

## New nonopioid option — suzetrigine

**Suzetrigine (Journavx)**, a peripheral NaV1.8 inhibitor, received FDA approval in January 2025 for moderate-to-severe acute pain in adults. Two phase-3 trials after abdominoplasty (n = 1,118) and bunionectomy (n = 1,073) showed benefit over placebo over 48 hours, but neither established superiority to hydrocodone / acetaminophen. These were not urologic or chronic pelvic-pain trials.<sup>[[52]](#ref52)[[53]](#ref53)</sup>

The July 2026 label includes postoperative pain and notes that acute-pain use beyond 14 days has not been studied. Strong CYP3A inhibitors are contraindicated; moderate inhibitors require dose reduction, strong / moderate inducers should be avoided, and severe hepatic impairment is a reason to avoid treatment. Check its effects on CYP3A substrates and hormonal contraception. This is an additional acute-pain option, not an established replacement for GU-specific multimodal pathways.<sup>[[28]](#ref28)</sup>

## Opioid Stewardship in Urology — Key Principles

- **Opioids are not first-line** for kidney-stone pain<sup>[[19]](#ref19)</sup>
- When opioids are warranted, prescribe immediate-release at the lowest effective dose for the shortest expected duration<sup>[[19]](#ref19)</sup>
- Maximize NSAIDs and acetaminophen; explore non-pharmacologic options
- Expert consensus offers procedure-specific ranges, for opioid-naive patients without chronic pain; do not apply those ranges automatically to other populations<sup>[[4]](#ref4)</sup>
- **NOPIOIDS-style protocols** support reducing routine discharge opioid prescribing after major cancer surgery, with patient education and reassessment; the evidence is observational<sup>[[17]](#ref17)</sup>
- **77% of opioids prescribed after RP are unused.** Calibrate prescribing accordingly using initiatives like ORIOLES<sup>[[22]](#ref22)[[46]](#ref46)</sup>
- **Screen for benzodiazepines, other sedatives, serotonergic drugs and CYP interactions** before any opioid or tramadol prescription<sup>[[57]](#ref57)[[58]](#ref58)</sup>

## Clinical Positioning

- **Renal colic:** NSAID first-line in patients with adequate renal function (IM diclofenac, IV ibuprofen, IV ketorolac); IV paracetamol is the alternative when NSAIDs are contraindicated; opioids may be needed when nonopioid treatment is contraindicated or inadequate<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[2]](#ref2)[[10]](#ref10)[[19]](#ref19)</sup>
- **Post-URS, post-cystectomy, post-RP.** Multimodal NSAID plus acetaminophen is the standard; SKOPE and NOPIOIDS show 0–minimal opioid prescribing is feasible<sup>[[11]](#ref11)[[17]](#ref17)</sup>
- **Stent symptoms.** NSAIDs as adjunct to α-blockers ± antimuscarinics; select adjuncts by symptoms and adverse-effect risk<sup>[[15]](#ref15)</sup>
- **MET.** Α-blockers are an option for selected distal stones; NSAIDs provide analgesia and are not established expulsive agents<sup>[[12]](#ref12)</sup>
- **CP/CPPS.** An individualized short NSAID trial may fit multimodal care; average benefit is small and evidence uncertain<sup>[[6]](#ref6)</sup>
- **IC/BPS.** NSAIDs as part of general non-opioid pain management; multimodal therapy required<sup>[[3]](#ref3)</sup>
- **Phenazopyridine** provides short-term urinary symptom relief; 2-day limit with antibacterials, contraindicated in renal insufficiency<sup>[[26]](#ref26)</sup>
- **Bladder cancer chemoprevention.** Celecoxib is not recommended despite preclinical signal<sup>[[13]](#ref13)[[14]](#ref14)</sup>
- **Nephrotoxicity** is the dominant safety concern in urologic patients; assess renal function, assess volume depletion, interacting drugs, CKD and obstruction rather than assuming safety from a normal single creatinine, use the lowest effective dose for the shortest possible time<sup>[[38]](#ref38)[[39]](#ref39)[[40]](#ref40)[[41]](#ref41)</sup>
- **Don't counsel patients that NSAIDs cause ED.** The apparent association is confounded by indication<sup>[[35]](#ref35)[[37]](#ref37)</sup>
