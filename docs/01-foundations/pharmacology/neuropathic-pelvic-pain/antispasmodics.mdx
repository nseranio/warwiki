# Antispasmodics

> **Category:** Pharmacology > Neuropathic & Pelvic Pain

**Antispasmodics** are drugs used to relax smooth muscle of the bladder, ureter and bowel, and, in pelvic-pain practice, to settle painful pelvic-floor and bladder spasm. Two groups matter to the reconstructive and functional surgeon. Antimuscarinic antispasmodics (hyoscyamine, dicyclomine, hyoscine butylbromide, oxybutynin, tolterodine) and direct smooth-muscle relaxants (flavoxate, drotaverine) act on visceral smooth muscle. Skeletal-muscle relaxants (baclofen, diazepam, tizanidine, cyclobenzaprine, methocarbamol) act on the striated pelvic floor and sphincter. The evidence is strongest for antimuscarinics in overactive bladder and catheter-related bladder discomfort, and weakest for most agents used for pelvic pain or renal colic. Nearly all pelvic-pain uses are off-label.

For skeletal-muscle relaxants, including vaginal diazepam and the trial evidence for it, see [Skeletal Muscle Relaxants](/docs/foundations/pharmacology/voiding-outlet/skeletal-muscle-relaxants). For the antimuscarinic OAB drug class in full, see [Anticholinergic / Antimuscarinic Agents](/docs/foundations/pharmacology/storage-oab/anticholinergics).

---

## Mechanism of Action

- **Antimuscarinics** block M2/M3 receptors on detrusor and other visceral smooth muscle, reducing involuntary contraction. Their anticholinergic effects (dry mouth, constipation, retention, blurred vision, confusion) are class effects.
- **Flavoxate** is a synthetic urinary-tract spasmolytic that acts directly on smooth muscle.<sup>[[3]](#ref3)</sup>
- **Belladonna and opium (B&O)** combines belladonna alkaloids (antimuscarinic; hyoscyamine and scopolamine) with opium (opioid). The belladonna component targets bladder and ureteral spasm, while the opioid component adds analgesia.
- **Skeletal-muscle relaxants** act centrally (baclofen at GABA-B receptors, tizanidine at alpha-2 receptors) or through sedation (diazepam, cyclobenzaprine, methocarbamol).

## Ureteral Stent Pain and Post-Procedure Bladder Spasm

Stent symptoms combine bladder irritation, flank pain on voiding and urgency. A 2023 Bayesian network meta-analysis of 26 randomized studies found the best drug class differed by symptom domain. Combined alpha-blocker plus anticholinergic ranked best for urinary symptoms, and combined anticholinergic plus pregabalin ranked best for pain. The ranking rests largely on indirect comparisons.<sup>[[12]](#ref12)</sup> See [NSAIDs & Analgesics](/docs/foundations/pharmacology/neuropathic-pelvic-pain/nsaids-analgesics) for the first-line analgesic layer.

### Belladonna and opium suppositories

Trials of B&O across three settings gave divergent results, so its benefit is setting-specific.

| Setting | Design | Regimen | Result |
|---|---|---|---|
| Ureteral stent after ureteroscopy | RCT, n = 71<sup>[[4]](#ref4)</sup> | Suppository (15 mg belladonna / 30 mg opium) after induction, before stent placement | Better quality-of-life and work scores and less pain with urination by postoperative day 3; no difference in analgesic use; no retention<sup>[[4]](#ref4)</sup> |
| Vaginal prolapse surgery | RCT, n = 90<sup>[[5]](#ref5)</sup> | B&O 16A (16.2 mg / 60 mg) after surgery and every 8 h for 3 doses | Pain scores and morphine use equivalent to placebo; adverse effects (constipation, retention) similar<sup>[[5]](#ref5)</sup> |
| In-office intravesical onabotulinumtoxinA | RCT, n = 26<sup>[[6]](#ref6)</sup> | Suppository with lidocaine instillation | No reduction in injection pain; retention rates did not differ<sup>[[6]](#ref6)</sup> |

**Practical position:** consider B&O for stent-related discomfort. Do not add it routinely after vaginal surgery or for office bladder injections, where the RCTs were negative. Confirm current formulation and opioid-stewardship requirements locally, because it is an opioid-containing product.

## Catheter-Related Bladder Discomfort

Catheter-related bladder discomfort (CRBD) is an urgency-like burning and pressure sensation from the indwelling catheter, and it is common on emergence from anesthesia. Antimuscarinics are the most direct pharmacologic answer.

- In an RCT of 234 patients undergoing percutaneous nephrolithotomy, discomfort occurred in 58% with placebo, 35% with oxybutynin 5 mg and 33% with tolterodine 2 mg given 1 h preoperatively, with lower severity in both drug groups.<sup>[[7]](#ref7)</sup>
- A 2022 systematic review and meta-analysis of 45 randomized trials found that tolterodine, oxybutynin, gabapentin, pregabalin, dexmedetomidine, tramadol, ketamine, nefopam and pudendal nerve block were effective. Dexmedetomidine and gabapentin ranked best when efficacy and adverse effects were considered together. Evidence for butylscopolamine was insufficient.<sup>[[8]](#ref8)</sup>

Anticholinergic exposure is short in this setting, which matters for the cognitive-risk discussion below. Retention after catheter removal, sedation and dry mouth are the main adverse effects to anticipate.

## Renal Colic

NSAIDs are first-line for renal colic. Antispasmodics have been tested as adjuncts with disappointing results for the antimuscarinic agents.

- **Hyoscine butylbromide:** in a 192-patient RCT (178 analyzed), adding hyoscine butylbromide to morphine did not reduce opioid requirement (median 0.12 vs 0.11 mg/kg) or the need for further morphine (33% vs 38%).<sup>[[9]](#ref9)</sup> In a 128-patient trial in colic not responding to oral NSAIDs, continuous IV butylscopolamine 100 mg/24 h was no better than placebo, and the authors concluded there is no role for continuous infusion.<sup>[[10]](#ref10)</sup>
- **Drotaverine:** a placebo-controlled RCT of IV 80 mg reported pain relief in 79% versus 46% with placebo (102 analyzed patients), with transient hypotension, vertigo and nausea as the commoner adverse effects.<sup>[[11]](#ref11)</sup> It is not an FDA-labeled US product.

This section addresses analgesia only. Whether antispasmodics improve stone passage is a separate question, and alpha-blocker medical expulsive therapy is covered on its own page.

## Bladder and Pelvic-Floor Spasm in Pain Syndromes

- **Hypertonic pelvic floor and levator spasm:** pelvic-floor physical therapy is the foundation. Systemic skeletal-muscle relaxants and vaginal diazepam are off-label adjuncts with limited trial support; the trials and safety details are on the [Skeletal Muscle Relaxants](/docs/foundations/pharmacology/voiding-outlet/skeletal-muscle-relaxants) page. Within that evidence, a triple-blinded RCT found no improvement with nightly 10 mg vaginal diazepam over 4 weeks,<sup>[[13]](#ref13)</sup> and a 2020 systematic review found that the randomized trials showed no significant benefit in pain scores.<sup>[[14]](#ref14)</sup>
- **IC/BPS:** smooth-muscle antispasmodics are not a defined treatment tier. See [Oral IC/BPS Agents](/docs/foundations/pharmacology/bladder-pain-ic-bps/oral-ic-bps-agents) and [Intravesical Agents](/docs/foundations/pharmacology/bladder-pain-ic-bps/intravesical-agents).
- **Flavoxate** carries a label indication for suprapubic pain and dysuria in cystitis and prostatitis, but the supporting literature is older, and a review of the drug in urogynecology noted that no randomized trials had been done in the preceding decade.<sup>[[3]](#ref3)[[15]](#ref15)</sup>

## Antimuscarinics and Beta-3 Agonists for OAB

For idiopathic OAB, the 2024 AUA/SUFU guideline states that clinicians should offer an antimuscarinic or a beta-3 agonist to improve urgency, frequency and urgency incontinence (Strong Recommendation; Evidence Level Grade A). Treatment is chosen by side-effect profile through shared decision-making.<sup>[[16]](#ref16)</sup> In the 2023 Cochrane review of 104 trials, oral anticholinergics were probably better than placebo for perceived cure or improvement (RR 1.38, 95% CI 1.15 to 1.66), with more dry mouth (RR 3.50) and probably more urinary retention (RR 3.52, low-certainty evidence).<sup>[[17]](#ref17)</sup> Drug selection, dosing and the beta-3 agonists are covered in [Anticholinergic / Antimuscarinic Agents](/docs/foundations/pharmacology/storage-oab/anticholinergics) and [Beta-3 Agonists](/docs/foundations/pharmacology/storage-oab/beta3-agonists).

## Cognitive Risk and Anticholinergic Burden

Chronic antimuscarinic use is associated with incident dementia in observational data; the association does not establish causation, and confounding by indication is possible.

- The AUA/SUFU guideline states that clinicians should discuss the potential risk of dementia and cognitive impairment with patients taking or prescribed antimuscarinics (Clinical Principle), and should use them with extreme caution in narrow-angle glaucoma, impaired gastric emptying or a history of urinary retention (Clinical Principle).<sup>[[16]](#ref16)</sup> The guideline cites a meta-analysis of 14 studies (1,564,181 subjects) reporting an association with all-cause dementia (RR 1.20, 95% CI 1.15 to 1.26).<sup>[[16]](#ref16)[[18]](#ref18)</sup>
- The 2022 SUFU white paper (expert opinion) concluded that chronic use (longer than 3 months) is likely associated with new-onset dementia and short-term use (under 4 weeks) is likely safe in most people.<sup>[[19]](#ref19)</sup>
- In a French nested case-control study, OAB anticholinergic use was associated with dementia (adjusted OR 1.23, 95% CI 1.10 to 1.37), with a dose-response pattern: 1.07 (0.91 to 1.25) for 1 to 90 daily doses, 1.29 for 91 to 365 and 1.48 for more than 365. Risk was marked for oxybutynin and solifenacin and not increased for trospium.<sup>[[20]](#ref20)</sup> A large English nested case-control study reported an adjusted OR of 1.18 overall, with oxybutynin, solifenacin and tolterodine most strongly associated and no significant increase for darifenacin, fesoterodine, flavoxate, propiverine or trospium.<sup>[[21]](#ref21)</sup>

Each additional anticholinergic (hyoscyamine, dicyclomine, tricyclics, cyclobenzaprine, diphenhydramine) adds to total burden. Before adding an antispasmodic in an older patient, review the whole medication list.

## Contraindications and Precautions

- Antimuscarinics and antispasmodics: narrow-angle glaucoma, obstructive uropathy (including bladder outlet obstruction), gastrointestinal obstruction, ileus and myasthenia gravis appear in hyoscyamine, dicyclomine and flavoxate labeling.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[2]](#ref2)[[3]](#ref3)</sup> Check post-void residual before starting in patients at risk of retention.<sup>[[16]](#ref16)</sup>
- **Oral baclofen:** hallucinations and seizures have occurred with abrupt withdrawal, so taper slowly.<sup>[[22]](#ref22)</sup> Renal impairment causes accumulation. The FDA boxed warning is for **intrathecal** baclofen (Lioresal, Gablofen): abrupt discontinuation, from any cause, has led to high fever, altered mental status, rebound spasticity, muscle rigidity, and in rare cases rhabdomyolysis, multiple organ failure and death.<sup>[[23]](#ref23)</sup>
- **Diazepam:** the boxed warning covers concomitant opioid use (profound sedation, respiratory depression, coma, death), abuse, misuse and addiction, and dependence and withdrawal reactions.<sup>[[24]](#ref24)</sup> This applies to vaginal use when absorption is systemic, and to co-prescription with B&O or postoperative opioids.
- **Tizanidine:** strong CYP1A2 inhibitors are contraindicated. It can cause hepatocellular liver injury (aminotransferases at baseline and 1 month after reaching maximum dose) and hypotension, especially with antihypertensives.<sup>[[25]](#ref25)</sup>
- **Cyclobenzaprine:** contraindicated with MAO inhibitors and in the acute recovery phase of myocardial infarction, arrhythmias, heart block and heart failure. Serotonin syndrome has been reported with serotonergic drugs. The label does not recommend use beyond two or three weeks.<sup>[[26]](#ref26)</sup>
- **Methocarbamol:** labeled for acute painful musculoskeletal conditions, with sedation and additive CNS depression.<sup>[[27]](#ref27)</sup> Its pelvic-floor use has no trial support.

## Practical Pearls

- Match the drug to the muscle. Smooth-muscle spasm (bladder, ureter) responds to antimuscarinics; striated pelvic-floor spasm does not, and needs physical therapy first.
- A bladder-spasm plan after reconstruction should say how long the antimuscarinic runs and when to check for retention, particularly after urethral or bladder-neck surgery.
- Avoid stacking anticholinergics. Hyoscyamine plus oxybutynin plus a tricyclic multiplies dry mouth, constipation, retention and cognitive effects.
- Do not pair a benzodiazepine with an opioid-containing suppository without a clear reason.
- Never stop intrathecal baclofen abruptly, including during pump-related or reconstructive surgery. Confirm pump function and refill dates.
