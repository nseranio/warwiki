# Androgen Adjuncts

hCG, selective estrogen receptor modulators (SERMs) and aromatase inhibitors can raise endogenous testosterone in selected men. A higher serum testosterone or sperm concentration is not proof of improved pregnancy or live-birth outcomes. Treatment should follow the cause of the endocrine disorder and the patient's reproductive goals.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup>

The AUA/ASRM 2024 guideline conditionally allows hCG, SERMs, aromatase inhibitors or combinations for infertile men with low testosterone (Grade C). It advises against prescribing exogenous testosterone when current or future fertility is a priority. Adding hCG to testosterone has insufficient evidence to guarantee preserved fertility or support routine use as a workaround.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup>

For diagnosis, indications and monitoring of conventional treatment, see [testosterone replacement](/docs/foundations/pharmacology/sexual-medicine-andrology/testosterone-replacement). Confirm symptoms and repeated appropriate testosterone measurements; assess LH/FSH and the cause of deficiency before selecting an adjunct. Markedly elevated LH from testicular failure predicts limited response to stimulation; primary pituitary dysfunction can also prevent a SERM response. Neither situation should be reduced to “testosterone is the only option” when fertility evaluation may still be needed.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[2]](#ref2)</sup>

## hCG and gonadotropin treatment

hCG can support intratesticular testosterone through direct LH-receptor stimulation. It does not preserve normal hypothalamic-pituitary feedback: rising testosterone/estradiol can suppress endogenous gonadotropins. Spermatogenesis also depends on Sertoli-cell stimulation by FSH; serum testosterone alone is an inadequate fertility-monitoring endpoint.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[3]](#ref3)</sup>

### Dosing and response

The Pregnyl US label lists two regimens for selected male HH: 500–1,000 units IM three times weekly for three weeks, then the same dose twice weekly for three weeks; or 4,000 units IM three times weekly for 6–9 months, then 2,000 units three times weekly for another three months. These are label examples, not one universal modern induction protocol. Specialist regimens are individualized to testosterone, adverse effects, testicular development and serial semen results; FSH may be added or used in a combined induction strategy. The AUA testosterone table lists hCG 500–4,000 IU subcutaneously or intramuscularly two to three times weekly, and 250–500 IU every other day when combined with exogenous testosterone.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[2]](#ref2)[[3]](#ref3)</sup>

The label retains a cryptorchidism indication, but this should not be read as a recommendation for routine hormonal induction of descent: AUA guidance favors timely surgical management and recommends against hormones for that purpose.<sup>[[3]](#ref3)[[9]](#ref9)</sup>

### Fertility evidence

Muir 2025 pooled 41 studies/1,673 men with pathological gonadotropin deficiency. After median 18 months of treatment, 78% achieved some sperm in the ejaculate, but only 36% exceeded 5 million/mL and 15% exceeded 20 million/mL. Combined hCG/FSH was associated with better sperm outcomes than hCG alone. These heterogeneous treatment cohorts do not establish an 80% natural-conception rate or a universal 50% pregnancy expectation.<sup>[[10]](#ref10)</sup>

Response depends on etiology, testicular development and prior cryptorchidism; some patients need prolonged treatment or assisted reproduction. Favorable predictors of spermatogenic response in hypogonadotropic hypogonadism are post-pubertal onset, no history of undescended testes, larger baseline testicular volume and higher baseline inhibin B.<sup>[[22]](#ref22)</sup> Combined hCG and FSH for 12–24 months is the conventional gonadotropin regimen in these patients; the review of Boeri and colleagues describes testicular growth in almost all, spermatogenesis in roughly 80% and pregnancy in about half, figures that come from selected hypogonadotropic cohorts (see Muir above for the pooled sperm-concentration data).<sup>[[22]](#ref22)</sup>

hCG alone or with a SERM can help recover testosterone production and spermatogenesis after prior exogenous testosterone or anabolic-steroid use. Return of sperm to the ejaculate is highly variable: it can take up to two years after stopping exogenous testosterone, and some men never recover sperm. Do not promise recovery by six months or two years.<sup>[[2]](#ref2)</sup> In a randomized trial in older men with androgen deficiency (mean age 67), hCG raised total testosterone by about 150%, showing that the aging testis can still respond to gonadotropin.<sup>[[2]](#ref2)</sup>

The often-cited Hsieh series involved only 26 men receiving testosterone plus hCG 500 units every other day. It reported no azoospermia and nine partner pregnancies, but was retrospective without a control group. It supports further study, not a guarantee that concomitant hCG protects a patient who continues testosterone.<sup>[[11]](#ref11)[[1]](#ref1)</sup>

### Safety and access

Gynecomastia is the most common adverse effect of gonadotropin therapy; hCG stimulates aromatase and raises estradiol.<sup>[[22]](#ref22)</sup> The AUA table also lists headache, irritability, depression, fatigue, edema and injection-site pain.<sup>[[2]](#ref2)</sup> Monitor gynecomastia/breast symptoms, edema, headache, mood symptoms and injection reactions; anaphylaxis is possible. The reviewed label lists hormone-sensitive tumors, including male breast/prostate tumors, and uncontrolled endocrine disease among relevant contraindications. A rise in estradiol alone does not mandate adding an aromatase inhibitor.<sup>[[3]](#ref3)</sup>

Urinary and recombinant products are not automatically interchangeable. A 2024 pharmacology study (crossover volunteers plus 52 gonadotropin-deficient men) found similar testosterone responses at the tested doses, despite lack of formal pharmacokinetic bioequivalence, and proposed a recombinant starting dose of 62.5 µg (6 pen clicks). It does not establish a universal conversion or equivalence of every dispensing device.<sup>[[12]](#ref12)</sup>

Following the March 2020 biologic transition, hCG products are not eligible for the usual 503A/503B compounded-drug exemptions. Confirm the specific authorized product and pharmacy supply; old published dollar estimates are not current price guidance.<sup>[[13]](#ref13)</sup>

## Clomiphene citrate

Clomiphene contains enclomiphene and zuclomiphene, with different estrogenic effects and persistence. Male use is off-label. The AUA testosterone-deficiency table describes 25–50 mg orally every one to two days; titration requires reassessment of symptoms, testosterone and the endocrine response rather than chasing a high laboratory value. This is not the female ovulation-induction schedule.<sup>[[2]](#ref2)[[5]](#ref5)</sup>

### What the studies can and cannot tell us

- **Habous 2018** randomized 282 men who wished to preserve fertility to clomiphene 50 mg, hCG 5,000 IU twice weekly or both for three months. Testosterone rose in all groups without a significant difference between them; the combination had greater questionnaire improvement. It did not demonstrate equivalent pregnancy/live-birth outcomes or establish a universal percentage testosterone rise.<sup>[[14]](#ref14)</sup>
- **Krzastek 2019** was a retrospective series of 400 men with mean treatment 25.5 months, range up to 84 months. Only 120 received treatment beyond three years; the reported 88% eugonadism, 77% symptom improvement and 8% side effects refer to this subgroup. It is not seven years of follow-up in all 400, and cannot exclude rare or delayed harms.<sup>[[15]](#ref15)</sup>
- Testosterone responses do not ensure sexual-symptom or fertility improvement. The small randomized clomiphene/anastrozole comparison found biochemical differences but no significant improvement in semen or patient-reported outcomes over 12 weeks.<sup>[[16]](#ref16)</sup>

### Other clomiphene series

| Study | n | Design | Findings |
|---|---|---|---|
| Keihani 2020 | 332 | Two-center retrospective | Mean testosterone rise 329 ng/dL; 73% rose by at least 200 ng/dL; median 6 weeks to first follow-up. Baseline LH and FSH did not predict response, so most men with normal or mildly raised gonadotropins can be expected to respond.<sup>[[23]](#ref23)</sup> |
| Ramasamy 2014 | 93 | Age-matched retrospective, clomiphene vs testosterone injection vs gel (31 each) | Median testosterone 247 to 504 ng/dL on clomiphene, versus 224 to 1,104 (injection) and 230 to 412 (gel). Symptom satisfaction (qADAM) was similar in all arms; injection users reported higher libido than clomiphene users (4 vs 3).<sup>[[24]](#ref24)</sup> |
| Taylor 2010 | 104 | Retrospective; clomiphene 50 mg every other day vs testosterone gel | Mean post-treatment testosterone 573 vs 553 ng/dL; ADAM score 4.9 to 2.1 on clomiphene. Monthly drug cost was about $83 versus $265–270 for gel (2009 prices).<sup>[[25]](#ref25)</sup> |
| Anno 2026 | 54 | One-year retrospective; 50 mg every other day | Testosterone rose and stayed up over 12 months and the Aging Males' Symptoms score fell, but the sexual-function domain did not improve significantly. Baseline LH was the only independent predictor of testosterone rise.<sup>[[26]](#ref26)</sup> |

All are uncontrolled or retrospective. They show that clomiphene reliably raises testosterone, not that it matches testosterone therapy for sexual symptoms; counsel that libido and erectile function may not improve even when the laboratory value normalizes.

### Titration and monitoring

The AUA testosterone guideline gives practical rules for SERM treatment:<sup>[[2]](#ref2)</sup>

- A serum LH after starting the SERM guides decisions.
- If testosterone stays low and LH is low or normal, consider dose escalation.
- If testosterone is low and LH is high, the testis is likely the problem and escalation is unlikely to help.
- First follow-up blood work is best no sooner than four weeks after starting clomiphene, anastrozole or hCG, then every 6–12 months. The guideline notes anecdotal concern about clomiphene tachyphylaxis, which is one reason for regular testosterone checks.

### Visual warning and other precautions

**Stop clomiphene and obtain prompt ophthalmic assessment for new blurred vision, flashes, spots or other visual symptoms.** The label warns that symptoms can persist and can be irreversible, particularly with greater dose or duration; “usually reversible” is not sufficient counseling. Avoid driving when affected.<sup>[[5]](#ref5)</sup>

Check liver disease/history of liver dysfunction, uncontrolled thyroid/adrenal disease and organic intracranial lesions such as pituitary tumors against the label contraindications. Breast symptoms, increased estradiol and mood changes can occur. Clomiphene labeling mentions thromboembolism only in the setting of ovarian hyperstimulation in women, and the AUA table lists venous thromboembolism and stroke for tamoxifen; the male thrombotic risk of clomiphene is not quantified, so weigh the patient's own risk factors.<sup>[[2]](#ref2)[[5]](#ref5)[[15]](#ref15)</sup>

## Enclomiphene

Two phase III trials reported by Kim 2016 compared enclomiphene with testosterone gel/placebo in overweight men with secondary hypogonadism over 16 weeks. Enclomiphene raised testosterone/LH/FSH and maintained sperm concentrations, while gel suppressed gonadotropins and sperm output. These are short-term endocrine/semen outcomes; they do not establish long-term reproductive success or superiority in symptoms/safety.<sup>[[17]](#ref17)</sup>

The 2014 Wiehle phase II trial also found increased hormones with conserved sperm counts; these findings concern biochemical and semen outcomes, not established pregnancy benefit.<sup>[[18]](#ref18)</sup>

The 2013 Wiehle phase II pharmacodynamic study randomized 48 men with secondary hypogonadism (testosterone below 350 ng/dL, low-to-normal LH) to enclomiphene 6.25, 12.5 or 25 mg daily or 5 g of transdermal testosterone gel for six weeks. Day-42 morning testosterone was 604 ng/dL on 25 mg versus 500 ng/dL on gel (not significantly different). Enclomiphene raised LH and FSH, gel suppressed them, and the effects of enclomiphene persisted for at least a week after stopping. IGF-1 fell in both groups, more with enclomiphene.<sup>[[27]](#ref27)</sup>

The 2026 BSSM position statement regards enclomiphene as promising but recommends experienced specialist/research use, with counseling about its unlicensed status and limited long-term evidence. Its discussion of potentially fewer adverse effects rests mainly on small retrospective comparisons; it does not establish head-to-head randomized superiority over clomiphene. No FDA-approved male dosing schedule exists. Availability through compounding does not imply approval, proven product equivalence or proven long-term safety.<sup>[[6]](#ref6)[[19]](#ref19)</sup>

## Anastrozole and estradiol management

Aromatase inhibition can lower estradiol and raise testosterone in selected men with low testosterone and elevated estradiol. AUA/ASRM allows consideration in that phenotype, but obesity alone or an isolated estradiol result is not an automatic indication. Address reversible contributors and inappropriate testosterone dosing first. A T/E2 ratio is assay- and unit-dependent and should not be treated as a universal therapeutic target.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup>

The AUA table lists anastrozole 0.05–1 mg every one to three days (not FDA-approved in men); male studies have used several schedules, including 1 mg daily, and this is not a default prescription for every man receiving testosterone. The AUA guidance advises against extended aromatase-inhibitor courses because of bone loss, careful titration to keep estradiol in range, and stopping the drug if estradiol stays high.<sup>[[2]](#ref2)</sup> In the 26-man Helo trial, clomiphene 25 mg/day produced higher testosterone than anastrozole 1 mg/day over 12 weeks, while anastrozole increased the T/E2 ratio more. Neither arm significantly improved semen measures or patient-reported outcomes. A 23-man obesity trial likewise found better hormone values with anastrozole plus weight loss but no additional symptom or strength benefit over weight loss alone.<sup>[[16]](#ref16)[[20]](#ref20)</sup>

### Dose-response and other data

Leder 2004 randomized 37 men aged 62–74 years with testosterone below 350 ng/dL to anastrozole 1 mg daily, 1 mg twice weekly or placebo for 12 weeks. Both regimens raised testosterone and lowered estradiol modestly, staying within the normal male range. Hematocrit, quality-of-life, IIEF and AUA symptom scores did not change; PSA rose only in the twice-weekly group. The physiologic consequences were not established.<sup>[[28]](#ref28)</sup>

| Regimen (n) | Bioavailable T (ng/dL) | Total T (ng/dL) | Estradiol (pg/mL) |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 mg daily (12) | 99 to 207 | 343 to 572 | 26 to 17 |
| 1 mg twice weekly (11) | 115 to 178 | 397 to 520 | 27 to 17 |

Other applications reported for anastrozole:

- **Oligozoospermia with low testosterone and a low T/E2 ratio.** In an 86-man retrospective series on 1 mg daily, 95.3% had higher testosterone and lower estradiol. Only a subset of oligozoospermic men, about 25% of the cohort, improved sperm concentration and total motile count, in proportion to the rise in T/E2. Men with azoospermia, cryptozoospermia or normozoospermia showed no semen improvement.<sup>[[29]](#ref29)</sup>
- **Testosterone pellets.** In a retrospective review of 38 men (65 insertions), adding anastrozole 1 mg daily to pellets was associated with a longer time to reinsertion (198 vs 128 days), higher testosterone at more than 120 days and less gonadotropin suppression. This is an uncontrolled signal and does not justify routine use.<sup>[[30]](#ref30)</sup>

Bone health is a material concern. A one-year randomized trial of 69 men aged ≥60 found reduced lumbar-spine BMD with anastrozole 1 mg/day versus placebo despite higher testosterone. Avoid excessive estradiol suppression; assess skeletal risk and reconsider long-term use rather than automatically adding an osteoporosis drug to compensate. Female breast-cancer fracture/lipid rates cannot be presented as male-event estimates.<sup>[[21]](#ref21)[[7]](#ref7)</sup>

Shorter exposure looks different: in the 12-week Leder study, anastrozole did not change bone-resorption or formation markers or total-body BMD, but 12 weeks says little about long-term skeletal effects.<sup>[[31]](#ref31)</sup> For scale, in the ATAC bone substudy of postmenopausal women, 5 years of anastrozole reduced median lumbar-spine BMD by 6.08% and total-hip BMD by 7.24% versus small gains on tamoxifen; extrapolation to men is directional only.<sup>[[32]](#ref32)</sup> A review of estradiol in men concludes that estradiol supports the hypothalamic-pituitary-testicular axis, bone, body composition and glucose metabolism, and that testosterone rather than an aromatase inhibitor is the treatment of choice for male hypogonadism.<sup>[[33]](#ref33)</sup>

AUA guidance treats aromatase inhibitors as an option only in the fertility-preserving pathway, and its Table 6 lists anastrozole adverse effects of hot flashes, hypertension, nausea, back and bone pain, dyspnea and peripheral edema.<sup>[[2]](#ref2)</sup>

Do not routinely add anastrozole simply to extend pellet intervals, normalize a laboratory estradiol result or prevent hypothetical gynecomastia. Evidence for these strategies is limited and does not establish improved patient outcomes. If breast symptoms persist, evaluate their cause and reassess the primary testosterone regimen before choosing additional treatment.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[2]](#ref2)</sup>

## Tamoxifen, raloxifene and combinations

Tamoxifen and raloxifene are not routine testosterone substitutes; clomiphene dominates SERM use in urologic practice. The AUA table lists tamoxifen 20 mg orally daily (half-life 5–7 days; adverse effects include liver enzyme changes, ocular disturbances including cataracts, and thromboembolic events including DVT and stroke).<sup>[[2]](#ref2)</sup>

In a crossover study of 10 healthy men given each drug for 2 weeks, both SERMs raised LH, FSH and testosterone, but tamoxifen (20 mg) had the stronger gonadal effect (LH about +70% vs +30%; testosterone about +40% vs +25% with raloxifene 60 mg). Tamoxifen alone lowered IGF-1 by about 25% and raised SHBG by about 20%, which can blunt free-testosterone gains; raloxifene did not lower IGF-1.<sup>[[34]](#ref34)</sup> In 284 men with idiopathic oligozoospermia, tamoxifen 20 mg, toremifene 60 mg and raloxifene 60 mg each increased gonadotropins, more with tamoxifen and toremifene than with raloxifene.<sup>[[35]](#ref35)</sup> These are hormone data in small or uncontrolled studies.

Short endocrine studies showing LH/testosterone changes do not establish improved fertility, and endocrine doses should not be imported from oncology or osteoporosis indications without a defined endocrine rationale. Tamoxifen's separate male breast-cancer approval does not approve it for androgen deficiency. Consider its thromboembolic and other label risks before off-label use.<sup>[[8]](#ref8)</sup>

Combining clomiphene, hCG and/or anastrozole is a specialist decision tied to a defined endocrine problem. More agents and higher testosterone do not necessarily produce better symptoms or fertility. When fertility is the goal, follow semen outcomes and couple-level factors alongside hormonal response, and discuss assisted reproduction when appropriate.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[10]](#ref10)</sup>

## Combination strategies at a glance

| Combination | Rationale | What the evidence shows |
|---|---|---|
| Clomiphene + hCG | Central SERM plus peripheral LH-receptor agonist | Habous 2018 RCT: testosterone rose in all arms without significant difference; combination had greater questionnaire improvement.<sup>[[14]](#ref14)</sup> |
| Clomiphene + anastrozole | SERM raises testosterone but also estradiol; AI limits the estradiol rise | Limited published data. |
| Testosterone + low-dose hCG | Maintain intratesticular testosterone | Hsieh 2013, retrospective, 26 men; AUA/ASRM 2024 finds the evidence insufficient to guarantee fertility.<sup>[[11]](#ref11)[[1]](#ref1)</sup> |
| Testosterone pellets + anastrozole | Limit aromatization; lengthen interval | Mechlin 2014, retrospective, 38 men: 198 vs 128 days.<sup>[[30]](#ref30)</sup> |
| hCG + FSH | hCG drives Leydig cells, FSH drives Sertoli cells and spermatogenesis | Muir 2025 meta-analysis: better sperm outcomes than hCG alone in gonadotropin deficiency.<sup>[[10]](#ref10)</sup> |

## Clinical decision-making

- **Fertility desired now:** avoid exogenous testosterone. hCG (with FSH if hypogonadotropic) or a SERM, chosen by cause and semen response.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup>
- **Fertility desired later:** a stimulatory agent as primary therapy is preferable to testosterone; adding low-dose hCG to testosterone rests on limited evidence.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[11]](#ref11)</sup>
- **Fertility not a concern:** testosterone remains the standard; clomiphene or hCG are alternatives for men who prefer to avoid exogenous testosterone or its effects on erythrocytosis and testicular size, with the limits of the evidence above.<sup>[[24]](#ref24)[[25]](#ref25)</sup>
- **Raised estradiol with symptoms on testosterone:** first review the testosterone dose and formulation; if an aromatase inhibitor is used, titrate to estradiol (AUA table 0.05–1 mg every 1–3 days), stop it if estradiol stays high, and avoid prolonged courses.<sup>[[2]](#ref2)</sup>
- **Obesity-related low testosterone:** treat reversible contributors; anastrozole plus weight loss did not improve symptoms beyond weight loss alone.<sup>[[20]](#ref20)</sup>
- **After testosterone-induced azoospermia:** hCG with or without FSH or a SERM; recovery is variable and can take up to two years.<sup>[[2]](#ref2)</sup>
- **Primary testicular failure (high LH/FSH):** SERMs and aromatase inhibitors are unlikely to work.<sup>[[2]](#ref2)</sup>
