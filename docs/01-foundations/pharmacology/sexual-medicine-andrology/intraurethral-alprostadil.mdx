# Intraurethral Alprostadil

**MUSE** is an alprostadil urethral suppository approved in the United States for erectile dysfunction (ED). It offers a needle-free option, usually with lower efficacy than intracavernosal treatment. Treatment choice should reflect effectiveness, adverse effects, access and patient preference; prior failure of every oral option is not a universal prerequisite. The AUA recommends discussing intraurethral therapy and performing an in-office test, and the EAU permits it as an alternative initial option for appropriately informed patients.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[2]](#ref2)[[3]](#ref3)</sup> It suits men in whom PDE5 inhibitors are contraindicated or ineffective, and men or partners who prefer to avoid oral drugs or injections.<sup>[[2]](#ref2)</sup>

For related treatments, see [Intracavernosal injection agents](/docs/foundations/pharmacology/sexual-medicine-andrology/intracavernosal-injection-agents), [PDE5 inhibitors](/docs/foundations/pharmacology/sexual-medicine-andrology/pde5-inhibitors), and [Testosterone replacement](/docs/foundations/pharmacology/sexual-medicine-andrology/testosterone-replacement). MUSE pellets, topical alprostadil cream and injectable alprostadil are different formulations; their instructions and evidence are not interchangeable.

## Mechanism and formulation

Alprostadil is prostaglandin E1 (PGE1), chemically identical to the endogenous compound. It relaxes cavernosal smooth muscle and promotes arterial inflow and corporal veno-occlusion. Drug absorbed from the urethral mucosa reaches the erectile bodies through communicating vessels between the corpus spongiosum and the corpora cavernosa. In the label's Doppler studies, a 500-microgram intraurethral dose increased cavernosal artery diameter and produced a 5- to 10-fold rise in peak systolic velocity.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup>

Absorption is rapid (about 80% of a dose within 10 minutes), and peripheral venous alprostadil is low or undetectable, which is why systemic drug interactions are minimal and systemic effects are mainly those of local absorption and hypotension.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup> Urethral delivery is far less efficient than injection. In a crossover comparison, the most-used doses were 1000 micrograms for MUSE (mean 922.5 micrograms) and 40 micrograms for intracavernosal alprostadil (mean 26.1 micrograms).<sup>[[6]](#ref6)</sup>

### The delivery system

The MUSE pellet is a micro-suppository of alprostadil in polyethylene glycol 1450, 1.4 mm in diameter and 3 or 6 mm long, seated in the tip of a translucent hollow applicator. The components are medical-grade polypropylene and each system is sealed in its own foil pouch. The pellet dissolves in the urethra and does not require an injection.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup>

The current US label lists 250, 500 and 1000 microgram systems. A 125-microgram dose appears in the historical trials and some non-US guidance; it should not be presented as a current US marketed strength. A label establishes the approved presentation, not real-time pharmacy stock.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[3]](#ref3)</sup>

## US dosing and administration

| Decision | Current US label |
|---|---|
| Initial dose | **250 micrograms under medical supervision** |
| Titration | Adjust stepwise on separate occasions to the lowest dose sufficient for intercourse; monitor symptoms of hypotension and demonstrate correct technique |
| Maximum frequency | **Two single-dose systems in 24 hours**; the US label does not specify an additional seven-dose weekly ceiling |
| Expected response | Usually begins within 5–10 minutes and lasts approximately 30–60 minutes, with individual variation |
| Storage | Unopened pouches refrigerated at 2–8°C; room temperature 20–25°C for up to 14 days before use; do not expose above 30°C |

These are product-specific instructions.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup> EAU guidance describes a 500-microgram starting strategy, supported partly by an older open randomized trial; that is different from the US label's starting dose. In Ekman's trial, 166 men were randomized and 142 were included in efficacy analysis; a 500-microgram start improved early response but produced more penile pain during the first four weeks. It does not justify replacing supervised individual titration with an automatic higher dose.<sup>[[3]](#ref3)[[4]](#ref4)</sup>

Doses needed in practice are high. In the pivotal trials, about 10%, 20%, 30% and 40% of randomized men were titrated to 125, 250, 500 and 1000 micrograms, respectively.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup> In a retrospective clinic series (n = 270), the effective dose was 500 micrograms in 49.2% and 1000 micrograms in 42.2% of successful users.<sup>[[13]](#ref13)</sup> The AUA notes that higher doses bring more adverse events, so the lowest dose expected to work should be used, and it requires in-office instruction and dose titration before the medication is prescribed for home use.<sup>[[2]](#ref2)</sup>

### Administration checklist

The current US Instructions for Use provide the complete illustrated technique. The main steps are:<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup>

1. Urinate immediately beforehand; insert within 10 minutes of urination. Wash and dry hands.
2. Open the pouch, remove the protective cover without operating the button, and verify that the pellet is present. Avoid touching the stem and tip.
3. Gently stretch the penis to its full length and straighten the urethra. Insert to the applicator collar without force; withdraw slightly and reposition if there is discomfort or pulling.
4. Depress the button fully and hold for five seconds, then release and gently move the applicator side to side to detach the pellet.
5. Withdraw while keeping the penis upright and inspect the tip. If the pellet remains, follow the label's reinsertion steps.
6. With the penis upright and stretched, roll it between the hands for at least 10 seconds. Replace the cover and discard the single-use applicator.

Teaching pearls that patients most often miss:

- **Urinate first.** Residual urine in the urethra helps the pellet dissolve and disperse along the urethra.<sup>[[2]](#ref2)</sup>
- **Straighten and hold the penis upright** during insertion; the AUA describes placing the stem about 3 cm into the urethra, and the label instructs insertion to the applicator collar.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[2]](#ref2)</sup>
- **Roll, then move.** Rolling the penis between the hands disperses the medication. The AUA also advises walking or standing for about 10 minutes afterward to aid blood flow.<sup>[[2]](#ref2)</sup>
- **Expect a warm, sensitive penis** for a few hours; the label calls this normal.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup>
- **Use the dose chosen in the office** and no more than two systems in 24 hours; change the dose only as the prescriber instructs.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup>

Do not substitute older or another country's instructions for the supplied product leaflet. Check comfort, dexterity and vision during teaching, particularly after reconstruction or with altered sensation.

## What efficacy estimates actually mean

### Pivotal placebo-controlled trial

Padma-Nathan and colleagues enrolled 1,511 men. Of these, 996 (65.9%) achieved an intercourse-sufficient erection during clinic titration. Men responding in clinic entered the randomized home phase; among those reporting at least one home treatment, 299/461 (64.9%) receiving alprostadil versus 93/500 (18.6%) receiving placebo reported successful intercourse at least once over three months.<sup>[[5]](#ref5)</sup>

The 64.9% home result describes selected clinic responders with reported home use, not all men starting MUSE. Likewise, the report that about seven in ten administrations led to intercourse applies to men responsive to treatment; it is not an all-patient success probability.<sup>[[5]](#ref5)</sup>

The AUA review found that in-office positive-test rates ranged from 20% to 65.9% across studies, that intercourse success ranged from 29.5% to 78.1% under differing definitions, and that only 73% of doses in the largest study were successful. A positive office test does not guarantee success at home.<sup>[[2]](#ref2)</sup>

### Clinical-practice experience

Outside the pivotal trials, results are more modest and more variable:

| Study | n | Design | Result |
|---|---|---|---|
| Guay 2000 | 270 (229 followed) | Retrospective, after patient and partner education and office trial | 56% success (intercourse in more than 66% of attempts); of failures, 61.4% lacked efficacy and 38.6% had side effects (genital pain or urethral bleeding)<sup>[[13]](#ref13)</sup> |
| Kim 2000 (Korea) | 334 | Multicenter, open | 59.3% had a score 4–5 erection in clinic; 78.1% of the 228 men in home treatment had intercourse at least once over two months<sup>[[14]](#ref14)</sup> |
| Ekman 2000 | 142 analyzed | Randomized open-label | 68% reported intercourse at least once in 12 weeks<sup>[[4]](#ref4)</sup> |
| Khan 2002 (UK) | 100 | Clinical practice | 35% initially successful; only 43% of those still used it at six months<sup>[[15]](#ref15)</sup> |
| Fulgham 1998 | 115 | Urology practice, manufacturer algorithm | No more than 30% reached a score 4–5 erection in the office at any dose; 41.2% met strict criteria for orthostatic hypotension; 18.6% were still using MUSE at follow-up<sup>[[16]](#ref16)</sup> |

Set these against the trials: pain, insufficient erections and cost drive discontinuation, and follow-up and patient education substantially affect results.<sup>[[13]](#ref13)[[15]](#ref15)[[16]](#ref16)</sup>

### Comparison with injections and continuation

In a randomized crossover study of 111 men, intracavernosal alprostadil produced intercourse-sufficient erections after 82.5% of administrations, compared with 53.0% for MUSE with optional constriction support. Patients and partners more often preferred injections. A separate randomized study of 60 men also favored injections for efficacy, but favored MUSE for comfort and continuation.<sup>[[6]](#ref6)[[7]](#ref7)</sup>

| Outcome | Injection (alprostadil alfadex) | MUSE + optional constriction band |
|---|---|---|
| Shabsigh 2000 (n = 111, crossover): erections sufficient for intercourse, per administration | **82.5%** | 53.0% |
| At least one sufficient erection | **92.6%** | 61.8% |
| At least 75% of erections sufficient | **75.0%** | 36.8% |
| Shokeir 1999 (n = 60; 20 micrograms injection vs 1 mg MUSE): intercourse at home, at least once | **87%** | 53% |
| Home treatment rated "easy" | 40% | **90%** |
| Urogenital pain | 47% | **7%** |
| Withdrawal before 3 months | 67% | **17%** |

The Shabsigh authors advised offering injection as first choice when oral therapy fails or is contraindicated; Shokeir concluded that MUSE is less effective but easier and better accepted.<sup>[[6]](#ref6)[[7]](#ref7)</sup>

These differing experiences argue against promising that a needle-free route will always be more comfortable or acceptable. Response in a supervised office trial, home usability, partner experience and follow-up are more useful than an isolated efficacy percentage. Long-term discontinuation remains common.<sup>[[3]](#ref3)[[6]](#ref6)[[7]](#ref7)</sup> EAU summarizes intercourse-sufficient erections in 30–65.9% of men, local pain in 29–41%, dizziness with possible hypotension in 1.9–14%, and penile fibrosis or priapism in under 1%; these ranges span heterogeneous studies.<sup>[[3]](#ref3)</sup>

## Safety and contraindications

:::warning[Rare priapism is still an emergency]
The US label reports two cases of priapism among 1,511 patients (0.13%). Although the original publication abstract reported none, the current label must not be replaced with a claim of zero risk. A rigid erection lasting four hours or longer requires urgent medical assessment. See [Priapism management](/docs/foundations/pharmacology/sexual-medicine-andrology/priapism-management).<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[5]](#ref5)</sup>
:::

| Risk | Practical implication |
|---|---|
| Penile/urethral pain and spotting | Common local adverse effects. In the US label's home-treatment table, penile pain occurred in 32%, urethral burning in 12%, and minor bleeding/spotting in 5% of active-treatment patients |
| Hypotension and syncope | During clinic titration, symptomatic hypotension occurred in about 3% and syncope in 0.4%; supervise titration and prescribe the lowest effective dose |
| Antihypertensives | Additive hypotension is possible; review baseline pressure, orthostasis and other vasoactive drugs |
| Anticoagulation or bleeding disorder | Urethral abrasion may cause more bleeding. Assess risk individually; this is not an instruction to stop prescribed anticoagulants automatically |
| Partner burning/itching | Reported in 5.8% with active treatment versus 0.8% with placebo; the label states that medication versus resumption of intercourse as the cause is uncertain |

Rates above come from the product label and reflect its trial populations and observation periods.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup> During in-clinic titration the label also reports penile pain in 36%, urethral pain in 13% and testicular pain in 5%, mostly mild and transient; dizziness in 4%; minor urethral bleeding or spotting in about 3%; and withdrawal for adverse reactions in about 7%. Fewer than 2% discontinued for adverse reactions during home treatment.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup> Penile fibrosis was not reported in Ekman's randomized trial or in the post-prostatectomy MUSE subgroup, and priapism is rare; this is the main safety advantage over injection and a reason to consider MUSE where those complications would be especially harmful, though not a claim of zero risk.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[4]](#ref4)[[17]](#ref17)</sup> Avoid driving or hazardous activities when dizziness or fainting could cause injury; postmarketing syncope has occurred within an hour of use.

The US contraindications include:<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup>

- Alprostadil hypersensitivity.
- Urethral stricture, balanitis, severe hypospadias/curvature, or acute or chronic urethritis.
- Conditions predisposing to priapism or venous thrombosis/hyperviscosity, including sickle cell anemia or trait, thrombocythemia, polycythemia and multiple myeloma.
- A medical condition making sexual activity inadvisable.
- Intercourse with a pregnant partner unless a condom barrier is used.

Use in patients with penile implants has not been studied; this is different from a labeled absolute contraindication. Prior reconstruction and spinal cord injury also require individualized anatomical and safety assessment rather than being automatically classified as label contraindications.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup>

MUSE provides no contraception or protection against sexually transmitted infections. The label recommends adequate contraception when the partner can become pregnant and specifically requires a condom barrier with a pregnant partner. Do not promise that condoms eliminate every partner symptom.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup>

## Special populations and combinations

### Spinal cord injury and constriction support

The frequently cited Bodner report involved 15 men with spinal cord injury, all previously successful with injections. After hypotension in the first three men, the remaining participants used a constriction ring. Erections remained less rigid than with injections, and the three men trying home MUSE discontinued it. This small uncontrolled experience supports caution and supervised testing; it does not establish a universally safe or effective ring-plus-MUSE protocol for spinal cord injury.<sup>[[8]](#ref8)</sup>

Bodner concluded that MUSE in spinal cord injury should always be used after placing a constriction ring, because of hypotension.<sup>[[8]](#ref8)</sup> If a specialist selects constriction support, teach safe removal and skin monitoring, particularly when sensation is reduced. EAU guidance for constriction rings advises removal within 30 minutes to avoid ischemic injury. A ring is not a guarantee against systemic hypotension.<sup>[[3]](#ref3)</sup>

The ring is also studied more generally. In Ekman's trial, 8% of men reached a sufficient erection only at 1000 micrograms plus a pubic band, and the Shabsigh comparison allowed an optional constriction band with MUSE.<sup>[[4]](#ref4)[[6]](#ref6)</sup> The band is intended to keep blood in the corpora and improve rigidity.

### After radical prostatectomy

MUSE can provide an assisted erection after prostatectomy when anatomy and safety permit. That differs from proving recovery of spontaneous erectile function.

Costabile's retrospective analysis of the MUSE trials identified 384 men whose erectile dysfunction followed radical prostatectomy (at least 3 months earlier). In this subgroup, 70.3% had an erection sufficient for intercourse in clinic and 57.1% of those on active drug had intercourse at least once at home, an overall success of about 40%. Clinic hypotension was lower than in other organic causes (0.8% vs 4.2%) but urethral pain or burning was higher (18.3% vs 10.4%). No urinary tract infection, fibrosis or priapism occurred.<sup>[[17]](#ref17)</sup>

In McCullough's open randomized comparison, 139 men started nightly intraurethral alprostadil and 73 started nightly sildenafil; 97 and 59 completed treatment, respectively. No final endpoint differed significantly between groups. Attrition, lack of an untreated control and assessment involving sildenafil after washout limit claims of equivalent rehabilitation efficacy or prevention of irreversible fibrosis.<sup>[[9]](#ref9)</sup>

### Combination therapy and topical cream

Selected refractory patients may receive specialist-directed combinations, since alprostadil (cAMP pathway) and PDE5 inhibitors (cGMP pathway) act by different mechanisms. Do not automatically extrapolate between routes, though. A review of nine publications on PDE5 inhibitor plus alprostadil (intracavernosal, intraurethral or topical) found improved IIEF scores over either drug alone, including after prostatectomy, without combination side effects causing discontinuation. The ISSM standard of practice grades this as level 3 evidence that a PDE5 inhibitor plus transurethral or intracavernosal therapy outperforms either monotherapy.<sup>[[18]](#ref18)[[19]](#ref19)</sup>

The Garrido-Abad study often cited for adding alprostadil to a PDE5 inhibitor used topical cream, with 170 patients assigned by preference (72 alprostadil alone, 98 combination), not randomized MUSE pellets. IIEF-5 rose from 12.4 to 17.1 with the combination but did not change significantly with alprostadil alone (12.2 to 12.7). It cannot establish a standard MUSE-plus-PDE5 regimen.<sup>[[10]](#ref10)</sup>

Topical alprostadil cream (Vitaros/Virirec, 200 or 300 micrograms with a permeation enhancer) is applied to the urethral meatus and is available in some European countries. EAU lists it, like MUSE, as a weak-strength alternative first-line option; common effects are penile erythema, burning and pain that usually settle within about two hours. A 2025 meta-analysis of 11 randomized and 4 non-randomized studies found that both topical and intraurethral alprostadil beat placebo, with heterogeneous, low-quality evidence. Confirm local approval, supply and instructions; topical cream is not an interchangeable substitute for a US urethral suppository or intracavernosal preparation.<sup>[[3]](#ref3)[[11]](#ref11)[[12]](#ref12)</sup>

## Comparison across alprostadil formulations

| Formulation | Route | Dose range | Main advantages | Main limitations |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **MUSE** | Intraurethral pellet | 250, 500 or 1000 micrograms (US label) | Needle-free; no injection-site fibrosis reported in the trials cited here; simple technique | Lower efficacy than injection; penile and urethral pain; hypotension; partner symptoms; high discontinuation<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[6]](#ref6)</sup> |
| **Intracavernosal alprostadil** | Injection | Typically tens of micrograms | Higher efficacy and patient/partner preference in crossover data | Needle required; penile pain; priapism and fibrosis risk<sup>[[6]](#ref6)[[7]](#ref7)</sup> |
| **Topical alprostadil cream** (Vitaros/Virirec) | Meatal cream | 200 or 300 micrograms | No applicator; used alone or with a PDE5 inhibitor | Regional availability; heterogeneous, low-quality evidence<sup>[[3]](#ref3)[[10]](#ref10)[[12]](#ref12)</sup> |

## Practical follow-up

Expect substantial discontinuation. Trial completion was high in a selected, motivated population, but in practice series only a minority kept using MUSE at six months or later, with insufficient efficacy and pain the leading reasons.<sup>[[5]](#ref5)[[13]](#ref13)[[15]](#ref15)[[16]](#ref16)</sup> Schedule a check early to address pain, dose, technique and cost before patients quietly stop.

Reassess actual intercourse success, discomfort, dizziness, technique and partner experience. Adjust the dose with the prescriber, or discuss another treatment if the benefit does not justify the burden. Never increase frequency or combine vasoactive preparations simply because an office dose was inadequate. Route-specific dosing and the emergency plan should accompany every prescription.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[2]](#ref2)</sup>
