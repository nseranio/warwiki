# Priapism Management — Pharmacologic Agents

**Ischemic priapism** requires urgent treatment. This is the drug reference; use the [clinical priapism page](/docs/special-populations/05a-trauma-emergencies/priapism) for diagnosis and the [shunts and decompression page](/docs/surgical-techniques/04j-sexual-dysfunction/priapism-shunts-decompression) for operative decisions. AUA/SMSNA 2022 recommends intracavernosal phenylephrine with corporal aspiration, with or without irrigation, as first-line therapy before operative intervention (Moderate Recommendation, Grade C).<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup>

## Ischemic versus non-ischemic priapism

Priapism is an erection that persists for hours beyond, or is unrelated to, sexual stimulation. Ischemic (low-flow) priapism has little or no cavernous blood flow and hypoxic, hypercarbic, acidotic corporal blood gas. It is a medical emergency, and delay risks cavernosal fibrosis and erectile dysfunction. Non-ischemic (high-flow) priapism is usually arterial, often after perineal or penile trauma. AUA/SMSNA 2022 advises initial observation for confirmed non-ischemic priapism, which is not an emergency (Expert Opinion). It calls for corporal blood gas at initial presentation (Clinical Principle), with penile duplex Doppler available when the subtype remains indeterminate (Expert Opinion). Treat according to the subtype and ischemia, as recurrent ischemic episodes can last less than four hours.<sup>[[10]](#ref10)[[3]](#ref3)[[1]](#ref1)</sup>

Acute ischemic priapism does not follow a single pathway. Depending on patient goals, resources and clinician experience, treatment may be intracavernosal phenylephrine alone, aspiration with or without irrigation, distal shunting, or non-emergent prosthesis placement.<sup>[[10]](#ref10)[[1]](#ref1)</sup>

## Phenylephrine

Phenylephrine activates α₁ receptors, contracting cavernosal smooth muscle to promote detumescence. Its relative lack of β activity favors it over mixed sympathomimetics; cardiovascular and neurologic complications remain possible.<sup>[[2]](#ref2)[[1]](#ref1)</sup>

### Adult intracavernosal regimen

| Source | Regimen | Important distinction |
|---|---|---|
| AUA/SMSNA 2022, Appendix A (Expert Opinion) | 100–500 micrograms in 1 mL normal saline, at intervals at least 5 minutes apart, for up to 1 hour | Prefer pharmacy-prepared syringes. Stop injections for blood-pressure changes; persistent ischemia requires escalation, sometimes sooner with prolonged presentation.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup> |
| **EAU 2026** | **200 micrograms every 3–5 minutes; maximum 1 mg within 1 hour** | Concentration 100–500 micrograms/mL in normal saline. Lower doses in children or severe cardiovascular disease; this is a distinct regimen, not an interchangeable universal ceiling.<sup>[[2]](#ref2)</sup> |

Check the amount and final concentration before injection. The AUA preparation contains 100–500 micrograms/mL; concentrated stock vials are not the ready-to-inject preparation. Intracavernosal administration for priapism is off-label.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup> Pediatric dosing needs a specialist protocol rather than extrapolation from an adult syringe.

Appendix B's sample aspiration and irrigation cycle allows 3–5 minutes before repetition; Appendix A's phenylephrine dosing instruction specifies at least 5 minutes between doses.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup>

AUA/SMSNA 2022 calls for blood pressure and heart rate monitoring during phenylephrine treatment (Clinical Principle); its discussion also advises monitoring afterward.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup> EAU additionally specifies checks every 15 minutes for 1 hour afterward. Hypertension, reflex bradycardia, tachycardia, palpitations and sporadic subarachnoid hemorrhage have been reported. EAU (panel opinion) regards sympathomimetics as contraindicated in malignant or poorly controlled hypertension and with concurrent monoamine oxidase inhibitors, and its algorithm also lists cerebrovascular disease; AUA describes cautious, lower-dose titration with monitoring for MAOI users. Resolve this guideline difference with specialist assessment in the monitored emergency setting.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[2]](#ref2)</sup>

### Technique

Severe penile pain may warrant a dorsal, circumferential or shaft penile block before corporal aspiration and injection.<sup>[[2]](#ref2)</sup> Aspiration and injection can use the same access. A 19-gauge butterfly needle is a common teaching variant. AUA/SMSNA Appendix B gives a sample protocol using a 16–18-gauge butterfly needle connected to a 30–60-mL Luer-lock syringe; it does not establish a superior needle or aspiration technique.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup>

1. Insert the needle into the lateral corpus cavernosum near the penile base, at about 3 or 9 o'clock, avoiding the dorsal neurovascular bundle and the ventral urethra.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup>
2. Aspirate dark, deoxygenated blood. Typical ischemic corporal blood gas values are pO₂ below 30 mmHg, pCO₂ above 60 mmHg and pH below 7.25.<sup>[[23]](#ref23)[[1]](#ref1)</sup>
3. **Irrigate** with normal saline until bright-red blood returns, if it does not return with aspiration alone.
4. Inject phenylephrine as above, then compress the corpora for about a minute.
5. Repeat at the chosen interval with blood pressure and pulse monitoring during and after treatment.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup>

Muruve and Hosking treated 9 patients with 0.5 mg phenylephrine in 2 mL saline without aspiration or irrigation (8 detumesced after 1–6 injections), and concluded that routine aspiration was not necessary.<sup>[[14]](#ref14)</sup> The EAU systematic review found that injection combined with aspiration, with or without irrigation, was successful in 70–100% of cases, against 0–100% for sympathomimetic injection alone.<sup>[[15]](#ref15)</sup> AUA/SMSNA 2022 recommends intracavernosal phenylephrine with corporal aspiration, with or without irrigation, as first-line therapy (Moderate Recommendation, Grade C). EAU 2026 recommends aspiration and washout first for other ischemic episodes, adding a sympathomimetic if priapism persists (strong); after intracavernosal vasoactive injection, it recommends a sympathomimetic first (strong).<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[2]](#ref2)</sup>

### Efficacy and hemodynamic safety

The first three rows are retrospective phenylephrine cohorts reporting detumescence and hemodynamic observations; these data do not establish comparative efficacy or cardiovascular safety. The fourth row is a systematic review including retrospective studies, prospective single-arm studies and randomized trials.<sup>[[11]](#ref11)[[12]](#ref12)[[13]](#ref13)[[15]](#ref15)</sup>

| Study | Population | Result |
|---|---|---|
| **Sidhu 2018** | 74 encounters; median phenylephrine dose 1,000 micrograms | 53/58 (91%) of those receiving phenylephrine detumesced at the bedside; small statistically significant falls in heart rate (−4.2/min) and blood pressure (−1.8/−5.4 mmHg); no adverse events<sup>[[11]](#ref11)</sup> |
| **Scarberry 2022** | 123 patients; 97 received phenylephrine | 62/97 (63.9%) resolved without surgery. Resolution tracked duration (mean 8.8 h in those who resolved vs 57.3 h in those who did not). Resolution did not differ significantly between lower- and higher-dose groups (median 500 versus 2,000 micrograms per hour); this retrospective comparison does not establish dose equivalence. 21 patients (17.1%) were judged at hemodynamic risk, and phenylephrine was stopped for hemodynamic change in 4<sup>[[12]](#ref12)</sup> |
| **Ridyard 2016** | 58 patients, 136 encounters; high-concentration phenylephrine, doses reported up to 12 mg | Nonsurgical detumescence in 86% of patients (94% counting repeat visits); all patients presenting within 36 hours resolved nonsurgically; no reported phenylephrine-related complications or associated symptoms in this series<sup>[[13]](#ref13)</sup> |
| **EAU systematic review 2024** | 41 retrospective and 3 prospective single-arm studies, 3 RCTs | Longer time to resolution was associated with more erectile dysfunction at follow-up (30–70%), especially beyond 24 hours<sup>[[15]](#ref15)</sup> |

Hemodynamic effects may be under-reported, so document baseline and repeat blood pressure and pulse rather than assuming they are benign.<sup>[[12]](#ref12)</sup> AUA/SMSNA identifies intracavernosal phenylephrine as the preferred agent, citing its efficacy and alpha-1 selectivity, which may limit cardiovascular effects.<sup>[[24]](#ref24)[[1]](#ref1)</sup> Epinephrine and norepinephrine, which also act on β receptors, have been used historically and are largely replaced by phenylephrine for that reason.<sup>[[24]](#ref24)</sup> Reported sympathomimetic side effects include angina and headache; case reports describe severe complications including subarachnoid hemorrhage and myocardial infarction with cardiogenic shock, although AUA notes that causation is uncertain in some reports.<sup>[[23]](#ref23)[[24]](#ref24)[[1]](#ref1)</sup>

### Avoid delays in definitive care

- EAU 2026 asks that management start as early as possible (within four to six hours) and follow a stepwise sequence (strong): aspiration and washout until fresh red blood returns; a sympathomimetic injection if priapism persists; repeat both before any surgery (strong). Distal shunting is the first surgical step (weak), with peno-scrotal decompression as an alternative first-line option (weak). Sympathomimetic injection replaces aspiration as the first step when priapism follows an intracavernosal vasoactive injection (strong).<sup>[[2]](#ref2)</sup>
- Aspiration and phenylephrine are complementary. AUA Appendix A advises proceeding to definitive treatment when an erection persists despite repeated injections and aspiration or irrigation for approximately 1 hour; presentation beyond 36 hours may warrant earlier escalation.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup>
- AUA/SMSNA recommends a distal corporoglanular shunt, with or without tunneling, after phenylephrine and aspiration fail (Moderate Recommendation, Grade C). Consider tunneling if ischemia persists after a distal shunt (Moderate Recommendation, Grade C). Evidence is inadequate to quantify the benefit of proximal shunting after distal-shunt failure (Moderate Recommendation, Grade C). Persistent tumescence after shunting requires corporal blood gas or color duplex Doppler before repeat surgery (Moderate Recommendation, Grade C).<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup>
- AUA/SMSNA advises counseling that erectile-function recovery is unlikely after an ischemic episode lasting more than 36 hours (Moderate Recommendation, Grade B). It permits consideration of a prosthesis in untreated ischemia lasting more than 36 hours or disease refractory to shunting, with or without tunneling (Expert Opinion), with discussion of early versus delayed placement (Moderate Recommendation, Grade C). EAU 2026 uses presentation beyond 48 hours or refractory disease for its prosthesis discussion (weak), and advises delaying implantation after a shunt to lower infection and erosion risk (strong).<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[2]](#ref2)</sup>

## Prolonged erection after an ED injection

An erection lasting 4 hours or more requires immediate medical assessment. AUA/SMSNA 2022 instructs patients receiving ICI teaching or an in-office induced erection to return to the office or emergency department for an erection lasting more than 4 hours (Expert Opinion). For post-injection erections lasting 4 hours or less that require intervention, it names intracavernosal phenylephrine as initial treatment (Expert Opinion), and recommends phenylephrine when conservative management fails (Moderate Recommendation, Grade C). Rigidity, duration and the injected agent guide treatment of earlier erections; partial erections do not necessarily count toward the four-hour criterion. Oral pseudoephedrine or terbutaline must not delay effective treatment; evidence for oral rescue is inconsistent. Avoid ice or cold exposure in sickle cell disease.<sup>[[3]](#ref3)[[1]](#ref1)</sup>

The oral evidence is one placebo-controlled trial of 75 patients with prostaglandin E1-induced prolonged erections: detumescence occurred in 36% with terbutaline, 28% with pseudoephedrine and 12% with placebo, and only terbutaline was significantly better than placebo.<sup>[[16]](#ref16)</sup> The EAU review likewise rates oral β₂-agonists as mildly to moderately effective, and ice packs, exercise, cold enemas and ejaculation as less effective (1–55%).<sup>[[15]](#ref15)</sup> Terbutaline can cause tremor, tachycardia and palpitations. EAU 2026 reports success of 30–60% in three placebo-controlled trials of injection-induced priapism and lists 5 mg orally for an erection lasting over 2.5 hours after an intracavernosal injection; use it cautiously with coronary disease, fluid overload, edema or hypokalemia.<sup>[[2]](#ref2)</sup>

The prescribing injection service should provide a clear rescue or contact plan during [ICI training](/docs/foundations/pharmacology/sexual-medicine-andrology/intracavernosal-injection-agents). Home phenylephrine is reserved for selected patients with recurrent ischemic priapism who have specific training and an individualized plan. It aborts episodes; it does not prevent them.<sup>[[3]](#ref3)</sup>

## Recurrent ischemic (“stuttering”) priapism

AUA/SMSNA 2022 advises informing patients that optimal prevention strategies are unknown (Conditional Recommendation, Grade C), and that hormonal regulators may impair fertility and sexual function (Strong Recommendation, Grade B).<sup>[[3]](#ref3)</sup> Balance episode burden, underlying disease, sexual function, fertility and adverse effects with the patient. EAU advises avoiding hormonal suppression before sexual maturation. EAU 2026 recommends managing each acute episode as ischemic priapism (strong). Its weak recommendations cover hormonal therapy (GnRH agonists or antagonists) or antiandrogens for frequent relapses; digoxin, alpha-adrenergic agonists, baclofen, gabapentin or terbutaline only for frequent, uncontrolled relapses; and interim home sympathomimetic injection for acute episodes.<sup>[[2]](#ref2)</sup> It notes little efficacy from oral baclofen, and describes digoxin at 0.25–0.5 mg daily in idiopathic cases.<sup>[[2]](#ref2)[[3]](#ref3)</sup>

### PDE5 inhibitors and the major randomized evidence

Daily sildenafil or tadalafil has been used off-label for prevention; AUA/SMSNA describes conflicting efficacy, with observational reports suggesting fewer or shorter episodes.<sup>[[3]](#ref3)</sup> The rationale is that in sickle cell disease and idiopathic recurrent priapism, reduced nitric oxide bioavailability and PDE5 dysregulation impair the mechanism that terminates an erection, and continuous low-level PDE5 inhibition is proposed to recondition that regulation.<sup>[[17]](#ref17)[[25]](#ref25)</sup> The principle is regimented daily dosing, not on-demand use; Burnett's randomized trial gave sildenafil 50 mg daily, unrelated to sleep or sexual activity.<sup>[[4]](#ref4)</sup> Start during a flaccid interval, not as treatment for an acute erection.<sup>[[2]](#ref2)</sup>

| Evidence | What it establishes |
|---|---|
| **Burnett 2006: 7 patients** (4 sickle cell, 3 idiopathic), retrospective | Sildenafil or tadalafil off-label: recurrences alleviated or resolved in 6/7; erectile function unchanged in 6 and improved in 1; no limiting adverse effects. Feasibility data only.<sup>[[17]](#ref17)</sup> |
| **Burnett 2014: 13 patients with sickle cell disease** | Sildenafil 50 mg daily for 8 weeks did **not** improve the primary outcome versus placebo in the blinded phase. Improvement in 5/8 during open treatment cannot establish comparative efficacy.<sup>[[4]](#ref4)</sup> |
| **Hou and Burnett 2021: 24 evaluable patients**, retrospective (12 idiopathic, 11 sickle cell) | Median 3 months of regimented PDE5 inhibitor: emergency department visits fell 4.4-fold, 22/24 (92%) reported improvement and 9 reported resolution. Among 17 who relapsed after stopping, priapism duration and frequency increased. Uncontrolled, short follow-up.<sup>[[18]](#ref18)</sup> |
| **Cochrane 2020: 3 trials, 102 participants** | Evidence for preventing sickle-cell-associated episodes was low or very low certainty. Sildenafil may have little or no effect; etilefrine, ephedrine and stilboestrol remained uncertain. The review predates PIN.<sup>[[5]](#ref5)</sup> |
| **PIN, Idris 2025: 64 men with sickle cell anemia** | Hydroxyurea plus placebo versus hydroxyurea plus tadalafil: 2.5 versus 3.02 priapism events per participant-month (usual-care-to-tadalafil IRR **0.8, 95% CI 0.3–1.9**), with no significant difference. This phase 2 feasibility trial had a median 10-month follow-up and was not proof of equivalence.<sup>[[6]](#ref6)</sup> |

PIN's reductions from pretreatment episode rates in both groups are not a randomized estimate of hydroxyurea's effect: there was no group without hydroxyurea. The trial does not establish that adding tadalafil prevents recurrence. See [PDE5 inhibitors](/docs/foundations/pharmacology/sexual-medicine-andrology/pde5-inhibitors) for contraindications and interactions.<sup>[[6]](#ref6)</sup>

### Ketoconazole with glucocorticoid replacement

This is off-label specialist treatment, supported by small uncontrolled series. Hoeh and Levine reported on-treatment resolution in 16/17 patients; the absence of a comparison group prevents ranking it above other options. Their historical regimen is not a validated general prescribing protocol. AUA/SMSNA includes ketoconazole with prednisone among reported preventive approaches, with caution about liver toxicity and frequent liver-function testing.<sup>[[7]](#ref7)[[3]](#ref3)</sup>

The authors' current regimen, described as their own, is ketoconazole 200 mg three times daily with prednisone 5 mg daily for 2 weeks, then ketoconazole 200 mg nightly for 6 months. They stop at 6 months because most patients did not need to resume. On treatment, 16/17 had complete resolution and none needed an emergency visit for priapism; one patient stopped after 4 days because of nausea and vomiting. Among the 14 men who discontinued ketoconazole (mean post-treatment follow-up 36.7 months), 11/14 (78.6%) still had partial or complete resolution, and 4/14 (29%) reported no further episodes.<sup>[[7]](#ref7)</sup> An earlier series of 8 patients titrated ketoconazole and prednisone to a testosterone of about 200 ng/dL, with monthly review; mean testosterone fell from 468 to 275 ng/dL, and mean IIEF-5 was 24.8.<sup>[[19]](#ref19)</sup>

Oral ketoconazole has boxed warnings for serious or fatal liver injury and QT-related drug interactions. It is contraindicated in acute or chronic liver disease. Check baseline liver tests and medication interactions; the US label calls for weekly ALT throughout treatment, with interruption if ALT exceeds normal or rises more than 30% from baseline, or liver-injury symptoms develop. The series' 600 mg/day ketoconazole induction exceeds the US tablet label's 400 mg/day recommended maximum for approved fungal indications; the label notes reduced adrenal corticosteroid secretion at 400 mg/day or higher and warns of adrenal insufficiency. Prednisone co-treatment does not remove all risk.<sup>[[7]](#ref7)[[8]](#ref8)</sup> Suppressing testosterone also requires fertility and sexual-function counseling.<sup>[[8]](#ref8)</sup>

### Etilefrine

Oral etilefrine, an α-adrenergic agonist, has been used mainly outside the United States. EAU 2026 quotes oral doses of 5–10 mg daily with response up to 72%, which differs from the dose range in the Okpala report below; a randomized trial found no difference between etilefrine and ephedrine.<sup>[[2]](#ref2)</sup> Okpala reported a good clinical response in 13/18 adults (72%) taking 50–100 mg daily, with no hypertension or sexual dysfunction over 1–48 months.<sup>[[20]](#ref20)</sup> Virag combined preventive oral etilefrine with self-administered intracavernosal etilefrine for episodes lasting over 1 hour: in 6 patients, all were protected from acute episodes and 4 needed no injections.<sup>[[21]](#ref21)</sup> Both are small uncontrolled series, and the Cochrane review rates the randomized etilefrine and ephedrine evidence as very low certainty.<sup>[[5]](#ref5)</sup>

### Other preventive approaches

Pseudoephedrine, baclofen, dutasteride and androgen-suppressing agents have limited evidence. They should not appear as a fixed sequence with guaranteed efficacy or routine dose recipes. Ephedrine and etilefrine were compared with placebo in a Cochrane-included trial, with very low certainty of benefit. Drug selection and monitoring remain individualized; hormonal treatment may be unacceptable when fertility is a priority.<sup>[[3]](#ref3)[[5]](#ref5)</sup>

Reported case-series dosing for the named 5-alpha-reductase inhibitors and antiandrogens: finasteride tapered from 5 to 3 to 1 mg daily over 120 days in 35 patients with sickle cell disease, and 1 mg daily in four patients or 1 mg twice daily in one patient in a separate five-patient pediatric sickle-cell series;<sup>[[26]](#ref26)[[27]](#ref27)</sup> dutasteride 0.5 mg daily, tapered after response;<sup>[[28]](#ref28)</sup> and low-dose bicalutamide, described as controlling recurrent priapism in a three-patient series (two with sickle cell disease and one with spinal cord injury) without major side effects reported.<sup>[[29]](#ref29)</sup> A separate case report described tapering bicalutamide to weekly and then every-other-week dosing after daily sildenafil was added; earlier chronic bicalutamide had caused gynecomastia requiring mastectomy, reduced libido, and erectile dysfunction.<sup>[[30]](#ref30)</sup> The mechanism of 5-alpha-reductase inhibitors in recurrent priapism is unclear. Central androgen ablation with intramuscular leuprolide acetate should seldom be used and only after informed discussion of its short- and long-term hormonal side effects, given the fertility and sexual-function trade-offs above.<sup>[[29]](#ref29)</sup>

## Sickle cell disease

Coordinate with hematology. AUA/SMSNA advises against delaying standard acute ischemic priapism treatment for disease-specific systemic interventions and against exchange transfusion as primary treatment (both Expert Opinion). Its discussion suggests considering simple packed-red-cell transfusion to a hemoglobin of 9–10 g/dL before general anesthesia if operative shunting is required; this is not an exchange-transfusion target or an automatic order for every episode. Longer-term hydroxyurea or transfusion decisions belong with the patient and hematologist.<sup>[[3]](#ref3)</sup>

In PIN, both arms took hydroxyurea and priapism rates fell by 58.3% (usual care) and 66.3% (tadalafil) from prerandomization rates in a post hoc pre-post comparison; with no non-hydroxyurea arm, this trial cannot isolate hydroxyurea's effect on priapism. Disease-modifying therapy decisions belong with the patient's hematologist for overall sickle cell disease care.<sup>[[3]](#ref3)[[6]](#ref6)</sup> Among 40 trial participants with semen samples at baseline, trial exit and 3 months after stopping hydroxyurea, median sperm concentration, motility and normal morphology fell during treatment and returned to pretreatment levels after stopping; counsel men about fertility.<sup>[[6]](#ref6)</sup>

## Historical alternatives: methylene blue and mixed sympathomimetics

Methylene blue inhibits guanylate cyclase and so blocks the nitric oxide-cGMP relaxation pathway. In Martínez Portillo's series, corporal blood was aspirated before a reported 5 mL methylene-blue injection; the dye was left for 5 minutes, aspirated, and followed by 5 minutes of penile compression. The reported volume does not specify a drug dose.<sup>[[9]](#ref9)</sup> Transient burning and blue discoloration of the penis are the main side effects.<sup>[[9]](#ref9)</sup> A 2001 methylene-blue series included 12 patients: 10 had injection-induced erections and all resolved with methylene blue after aspiration; the two others (leukemia-associated and idiopathic high-flow) did not respond and needed phenylephrine and pudendal embolization respectively. In deHoll's 11-patient mixed-treatment series, 67% of methylene-blue recipients had immediate detumescence; all patients with injection- or trazodone-induced priapism responded.<sup>[[22]](#ref22)</sup> These small, selected series do not establish safety or superiority to phenylephrine, and they did not test a standard “after phenylephrine failure” pathway. EAU 2026 lists methylene blue (50–100 mg intracavernosally, left 5 minutes, then aspirated with 5 minutes of compression) as supported only by small retrospective series in short-term pharmacologically induced priapism; it should not delay established emergency treatment. For its separate hemolysis and serotonergic interaction risks, see [methylene blue](/docs/foundations/pharmacology/intraoperative-adjuncts/visualization-agents/methylene-blue).<sup>[[2]](#ref2)[[9]](#ref9)</sup>

## Treatment sequence at a glance

| Situation | Sequence |
|---|---|
| Acute ischemic priapism | Corporal aspiration with or without irrigation and blood gas; intracavernosal phenylephrine with blood pressure and pulse monitoring; reassess after an unsuccessful cycle and escalate to a distal shunt (with tunneling if needed); prosthesis in selected prolonged or refractory cases.<sup>[[10]](#ref10)[[1]](#ref1)</sup> |
| Prolonged erection after an ED injection | Assess duration, rigidity and the injected agent. AUA/SMSNA uses phenylephrine as initial treatment when intervention is needed at 4 hours or less; erections beyond 4 hours follow the acute ischemic pathway. EAU lists oral terbutaline for selected earlier injection-induced erections; oral treatment must not delay detumescence when urgent treatment is needed.<sup>[[3]](#ref3)[[16]](#ref16)[[2]](#ref2)</sup> |
| Recurrent ischemic priapism | Treat each acute episode as above; individualize prevention (regimented PDE5 inhibitor, ketoconazole with glucocorticoid, or other agents) with fertility counseling; consider selected home phenylephrine rescue; in sickle cell disease, coordinate hydroxyurea and other disease-modifying decisions with hematology.<sup>[[3]](#ref3)[[6]](#ref6)</sup> |
