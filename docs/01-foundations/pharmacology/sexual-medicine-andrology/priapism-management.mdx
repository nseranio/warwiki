# Priapism Management — Pharmacologic Agents

**Ischemic priapism requires urgent treatment.** This is the drug reference; use the [clinical priapism page](/docs/special-populations/05a-trauma-emergencies/priapism) for diagnosis and the [shunts and decompression page](/docs/surgical-techniques/04j-sexual-dysfunction/priapism-shunts-decompression) for operative decisions. The AUA/SMSNA recommends intracavernosal phenylephrine with aspiration, with or without irrigation, before surgical escalation.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup>

## Ischemic versus non-ischemic priapism

Priapism is an erection that persists for hours beyond, or is unrelated to, sexual stimulation. **Ischemic (low-flow) priapism** has little or no cavernous blood flow and hypoxic, hypercarbic, acidotic corporal blood gas. It is a medical emergency, and delay risks cavernosal fibrosis and erectile dysfunction. **Non-ischemic (high-flow) priapism** is usually arterial, often after perineal or penile trauma. It is not an emergency, and treatment follows the patient's goals and local expertise. Corporal blood gas or penile Doppler separates the two; treat according to the subtype, not the duration alone.<sup>[[10]](#ref10)[[3]](#ref3)</sup>

Acute ischemic priapism does not follow a single pathway. Depending on patient goals, resources and clinician experience, treatment may be intracavernosal phenylephrine alone, aspiration with or without irrigation, distal shunting, or non-emergent prosthesis placement.<sup>[[10]](#ref10)</sup>

## Phenylephrine

Phenylephrine activates α₁ receptors, contracting cavernosal smooth muscle to promote detumescence. Its relative lack of β activity favors it over mixed sympathomimetics; **cardiovascular and neurologic complications remain possible**.<sup>[[2]](#ref2)</sup>

### Adult intracavernosal regimen

| Source | Regimen | Important distinction |
|---|---|---|
| **AUA/SMSNA 2022, Appendix A** | **100–500 micrograms in 1 mL normal saline**, at intervals **at least 5 minutes apart**, for up to approximately 1 hour | Prefer pharmacy-prepared syringes. Stop injections for blood-pressure changes; persistent ischemia requires escalation, sometimes sooner with prolonged presentation.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup> |
| **EAU 2026** | **200 micrograms every 3–5 minutes; maximum 1 mg within 1 hour** | Lower doses in children or severe cardiovascular disease; this is a distinct regimen, not an interchangeable universal ceiling.<sup>[[2]](#ref2)</sup> |

**Check the amount and final concentration before injection.** The AUA preparation contains **100–500 micrograms/mL**; concentrated stock vials are not the ready-to-inject preparation. Intracavernosal administration for priapism is off-label. Pediatric dosing needs a specialist protocol rather than extrapolation from an adult syringe.

Monitor **blood pressure and pulse during treatment**. EAU additionally specifies checks every 15 minutes for 1 hour afterward. Hypertension, reflex bradycardia, tachycardia and serious cerebrovascular events have been reported. EAU advises against sympathomimetics in malignant/poorly controlled hypertension or concurrent monoamine oxidase inhibitors; AUA describes cautious, lower-dose titration with monitoring for MAOI users. Resolve this guideline difference with specialist assessment in the monitored emergency setting.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[2]](#ref2)</sup>

### Technique

Aspiration and injection are done together through the same access. This is standard teaching; a 19-gauge butterfly needle is a common choice.

1. Insert the needle into the lateral corpus cavernosum, at about 3 or 9 o'clock, avoiding the dorsal neurovascular bundle and the ventral urethra.
2. **Aspirate** dark, deoxygenated blood. A corporal blood gas confirms ischemia; pO₂ below about 30 mmHg, pCO₂ above about 60 mmHg and pH below about 7.25 are the values usually quoted for ischemic priapism.<sup>[[23]](#ref23)</sup>
3. **Irrigate** with normal saline until bright-red blood returns, if it does not return with aspiration alone.
4. Inject phenylephrine as above, then compress the corpora for about a minute.
5. Repeat at the chosen interval with continuous blood pressure and pulse monitoring.

Muruve and Hosking treated 9 patients with 0.5 mg phenylephrine in 2 mL saline without aspiration or irrigation (8 detumesced after 1–6 injections), and concluded that routine aspiration was not necessary.<sup>[[14]](#ref14)</sup> The EAU systematic review found that injection combined with aspiration, with or without irrigation, was successful in 70–100% of cases, against 0–100% for sympathomimetic injection alone.<sup>[[15]](#ref15)</sup> In practice the two are combined from the outset.

### Efficacy and hemodynamic safety

These are retrospective series. They show what is achievable and how often hemodynamic changes were noticed, not comparative efficacy.

| Study | Population | Result |
|---|---|---|
| **Sidhu 2018** | 74 encounters; median phenylephrine dose 1,000 micrograms | 53/58 (91%) of those receiving phenylephrine detumesced at the bedside; small statistically significant falls in heart rate (−4.2/min) and blood pressure (−1.8/−5.4 mmHg); no adverse events<sup>[[11]](#ref11)</sup> |
| **Scarberry 2022** | 123 patients; 97 received phenylephrine | 62/97 (63.9%) resolved without surgery. Resolution tracked duration (mean 8.8 h in those who resolved vs 57.3 h in those who did not). No dose dependence between about 500 and 2,000 micrograms per hour. 21 patients (17.1%) were judged at hemodynamic risk, and phenylephrine was stopped for hemodynamic change in 4<sup>[[12]](#ref12)</sup> |
| **Ridyard 2016** | 58 patients, 136 encounters; high-concentration phenylephrine | Nonsurgical detumescence in 86% of patients (94% counting repeat visits); all patients presenting within 36 hours resolved nonsurgically; no reported complications<sup>[[13]](#ref13)</sup> |
| **EAU systematic review 2024** | 41 retrospective and 3 prospective single-arm studies, 3 RCTs | Longer time to resolution was associated with more erectile dysfunction at follow-up (30–70%), especially beyond 24 hours<sup>[[15]](#ref15)</sup> |

Hemodynamic effects may be under-reported, so document baseline and repeat blood pressure and pulse rather than assuming they are benign.<sup>[[12]](#ref12)</sup> Intracavernosal phenylephrine is regarded as superior to other agents because of its efficacy and limited systemic effects.<sup>[[24]](#ref24)</sup> Epinephrine and norepinephrine, which also act on β receptors, have been used historically and are largely replaced by phenylephrine for that reason.<sup>[[24]](#ref24)</sup>

### Avoid delays in definitive care

- Aspiration and phenylephrine are complementary. A fully rigid erection after an unsuccessful treatment cycle needs reassessment and prompt urologic escalation; **24–48 hours is not a waiting period before shunting**.
- A distal shunt, with or without tunneling, is the usual operative next step. Proximal shunting is not a routine mandatory rung. Persistent tumescence after shunting should be assessed with corporal blood gas or Doppler before another intervention.
- Very prolonged ischemia carries a poor erectile-function prognosis. Discuss prosthesis placement in selected untreated prolonged or refractory cases; duration alone is not an automatic implant order.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup>

## Prolonged erection after an ED injection

An erection lasting **4 hours or more requires immediate medical assessment**. An earlier, persistent post-injection erection may also need treatment. AUA distinguishes these shorter iatrogenic erections from established ischemic priapism and recommends intracavernosal phenylephrine when intervention is needed. Oral pseudoephedrine or terbutaline should not delay effective treatment; evidence for oral rescue is inconsistent. Avoid ice/cold exposure in sickle cell disease.<sup>[[3]](#ref3)</sup>

The oral evidence is one placebo-controlled trial of 75 patients with prostaglandin E1-induced prolonged erections: detumescence occurred in 36% with terbutaline, 28% with pseudoephedrine and 12% with placebo, and only terbutaline was significantly better than placebo.<sup>[[16]](#ref16)</sup> The EAU review likewise rates oral β₂-agonists as mildly to moderately effective, and ice packs, exercise, cold enemas and ejaculation as less effective (1–55%).<sup>[[15]](#ref15)</sup> Terbutaline can cause tremor, tachycardia and palpitations.

The prescribing injection service should provide a clear rescue/contact plan during [ICI training](/docs/foundations/pharmacology/sexual-medicine-andrology/intracavernosal-injection-agents). Home phenylephrine is reserved for selected patients with recurrent ischemic priapism who have specific training and an individualized plan. It aborts episodes; it does not prevent them.<sup>[[3]](#ref3)</sup>

## Recurrent ischemic (“stuttering”) priapism

**There is no established best preventive medication or evidence-based first/second-line drug hierarchy.** Balance episode burden, underlying disease, sexual function, fertility and adverse effects with the patient. Hormonal suppression can impair fertility and sexual function; avoid it before sexual maturation.<sup>[[2]](#ref2)[[3]](#ref3)</sup>

### PDE5 inhibitors and the major randomized evidence

Daily sildenafil or tadalafil has been used off-label for prevention, paradoxically aiming to normalize dysregulated erection signaling. The rationale is that in sickle cell disease and idiopathic recurrent priapism, reduced nitric oxide bioavailability and PDE5 dysregulation impair the mechanism that terminates an erection, and continuous low-level PDE5 inhibition is proposed to recondition that regulation.<sup>[[17]](#ref17)[[25]](#ref25)</sup> The key principle is regimented daily dosing, not on-demand use; Burnett's randomized trial gave sildenafil 50 mg daily, unrelated to sleep or sexual activity.<sup>[[4]](#ref4)</sup> Start during a **flaccid interval**, not as treatment for an acute erection.<sup>[[2]](#ref2)</sup>

| Evidence | What it establishes |
|---|---|
| **Burnett 2006: 7 patients** (4 sickle cell, 3 idiopathic), retrospective | Sildenafil or tadalafil off-label: recurrences alleviated or resolved in 6/7; erectile function unchanged in 6 and improved in 1; no limiting adverse effects. Feasibility data only.<sup>[[17]](#ref17)</sup> |
| **Burnett 2014: 13 patients with sickle cell disease** | Sildenafil 50 mg daily for 8 weeks did **not** improve the primary outcome versus placebo in the blinded phase. Improvement in 5/8 during open treatment cannot establish comparative efficacy.<sup>[[4]](#ref4)</sup> |
| **Hou and Burnett 2021: 24 evaluable patients**, retrospective (12 idiopathic, 11 sickle cell) | Median 3 months of regimented PDE5 inhibitor: emergency department visits fell 4.4-fold, 22/24 (92%) reported improvement and 9 reported resolution. Among 17 who relapsed after stopping, priapism duration and frequency increased. Uncontrolled, short follow-up.<sup>[[18]](#ref18)</sup> |
| **Cochrane 2020: 3 trials, 102 participants** | Evidence for preventing sickle-cell-associated episodes was low or very low certainty. Sildenafil may have little or no effect; etilefrine, ephedrine and stilboestrol remained uncertain. The review predates PIN.<sup>[[5]](#ref5)</sup> |
| **PIN, Idris 2025: 64 men with sickle cell anemia** | Hydroxyurea plus tadalafil versus hydroxyurea plus placebo: no significant difference in episode rates (reported IRR **0.8, 95% CI 0.3–1.9**). This phase 2 feasibility trial had a median 10-month follow-up and was not proof of equivalence.<sup>[[6]](#ref6)</sup> |

PIN's reductions from pretreatment episode rates in **both** groups are not a randomized estimate of hydroxyurea's effect: there was no group without hydroxyurea. The trial does not establish that adding tadalafil prevents recurrence. See [PDE5 inhibitors](/docs/foundations/pharmacology/sexual-medicine-andrology/pde5-inhibitors) for contraindications and interactions.<sup>[[6]](#ref6)</sup>

### Ketoconazole with glucocorticoid replacement

This is **off-label specialist treatment**, supported by small uncontrolled series. Hoeh and Levine reported on-treatment resolution in 16/17 patients; the absence of a comparison group prevents ranking it above other options. Their historical regimen is not a validated general prescribing protocol.<sup>[[7]](#ref7)</sup>

The authors' current regimen, described as their own, is ketoconazole 200 mg three times daily with prednisone 5 mg daily for 2 weeks, then ketoconazole 200 mg nightly for 6 months. They stop at 6 months because most patients did not need to resume. On treatment, 16/17 had complete resolution and none needed an emergency visit for priapism; one patient stopped after 4 days because of nausea and vomiting. After discontinuation (mean follow-up 36.7 months), 78.6% still had partial or complete resolution, and 29% reported no further episodes.<sup>[[7]](#ref7)</sup> An earlier series of 8 patients titrated ketoconazole and prednisone to a testosterone of about 200 ng/dL, with monthly review; mean testosterone fell from 468 to 275 ng/dL, and mean IIEF-5 was 24.8.<sup>[[19]](#ref19)</sup>

Oral ketoconazole has boxed warnings for **serious or fatal liver injury** and QT-related drug interactions. It is contraindicated in acute or chronic liver disease. Check baseline liver tests and medication interactions; the US label calls for **weekly ALT throughout treatment**, with interruption if ALT exceeds normal or rises more than 30% from baseline, or liver-injury symptoms develop. Cortisol suppression can cause adrenal insufficiency; glucocorticoid co-treatment does not remove all risk. Suppressing testosterone also requires fertility and sexual-function counseling.<sup>[[8]](#ref8)</sup>

### Etilefrine

Oral etilefrine, an α-adrenergic agonist, has been used mainly outside the United States. Okpala reported a good clinical response in 13/18 adults (72%) taking 50–100 mg daily, with no hypertension or sexual dysfunction over 1–48 months.<sup>[[20]](#ref20)</sup> Virag combined preventive oral etilefrine with self-administered intracavernosal etilefrine for episodes lasting over 1 hour: in 6 patients, all were protected from acute episodes and 4 needed no injections.<sup>[[21]](#ref21)</sup> Both are small uncontrolled series, and the Cochrane review rates the randomized etilefrine and ephedrine evidence as very low certainty.<sup>[[5]](#ref5)</sup>

### Other preventive approaches

Pseudoephedrine, baclofen, dutasteride and androgen-suppressing agents have limited evidence. They should not appear as a fixed sequence with guaranteed efficacy or routine dose recipes. Ephedrine and etilefrine were compared with placebo in a Cochrane-included trial, with very low certainty of benefit. Drug selection and monitoring remain individualized; hormonal treatment may be unacceptable when fertility is a priority.<sup>[[3]](#ref3)[[5]](#ref5)</sup>

## Sickle cell disease

Coordinate with hematology, but **do not postpone corporal treatment for exchange transfusion**. Exchange is not the primary emergency treatment. Longer-term hydroxyurea or transfusion decisions should address the patient's overall sickle cell disease; transfusion targets and peri-anesthetic transfusion are individualized rather than automatic orders for every priapism episode.<sup>[[3]](#ref3)</sup>

In PIN, both arms took hydroxyurea and priapism rates fell by 58.3% (usual care) and 66.3% (tadalafil) from prerandomization rates in a post hoc pre-post comparison; with no non-hydroxyurea arm, this cannot isolate hydroxyurea's effect, but it supports including disease-modifying therapy in the plan. Sperm concentration, motility and morphology fell on hydroxyurea and recovered 3 months after stopping, so counsel men about fertility.<sup>[[6]](#ref6)</sup>

## Historical alternatives: methylene blue and mixed sympathomimetics

Methylene blue inhibits guanylate cyclase and so blocks the nitric oxide-cGMP relaxation pathway. In the reported technique, blood is aspirated, 5 mL of methylene blue is injected intracavernosally and left for 5 minutes, then aspirated, and the penis is compressed for a further 5 minutes. Transient burning and blue discoloration of the penis are the main side effects.<sup>[[9]](#ref9)</sup> A 2001 methylene-blue series included 12 patients: 10 had injection-induced erections and all resolved with methylene blue after aspiration; the two others (leukemia-associated and idiopathic high-flow) did not respond and needed phenylephrine and pudendal embolization respectively. A second small series (11 patients, mixed causes) found immediate detumescence in 67% overall and in all patients with injection- or trazodone-induced priapism.<sup>[[22]](#ref22)</sup> These small, selected series do not establish safety or superiority to phenylephrine, and they did not test a standard “after phenylephrine failure” pathway. EAU discusses it among historical alternatives; it should not delay established emergency treatment. For its separate hemolysis and serotonergic interaction risks, see [methylene blue](/docs/foundations/pharmacology/intraoperative-adjuncts/visualization-agents/methylene-blue).<sup>[[2]](#ref2)[[9]](#ref9)</sup>

## Treatment sequence at a glance

| Situation | Sequence |
|---|---|
| Acute ischemic priapism | Corporal aspiration with or without irrigation and blood gas; intracavernosal phenylephrine with blood pressure and pulse monitoring; reassess after an unsuccessful cycle and escalate to a distal shunt (with tunneling if needed); prosthesis in selected prolonged or refractory cases.<sup>[[10]](#ref10)</sup> |
| Prolonged erection after an ED injection | Establish how long it has lasted; oral terbutaline is an option but should not delay treatment; intracavernosal phenylephrine when intervention is needed or the erection reaches 4 hours.<sup>[[3]](#ref3)[[16]](#ref16)</sup> |
| Recurrent ischemic priapism | Treat each acute episode as above; individualize prevention (regimented PDE5 inhibitor, ketoconazole with glucocorticoid, or other agents) with fertility counseling; consider selected home phenylephrine rescue; in sickle cell disease, include hydroxyurea and hematology input.<sup>[[3]](#ref3)</sup> |
