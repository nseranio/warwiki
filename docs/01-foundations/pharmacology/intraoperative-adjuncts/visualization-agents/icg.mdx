# Indocyanine Green (ICG)

**Indocyanine green (ICG)** is a tricarbocyanine near-infrared (NIR) fluorescent dye that binds rapidly to plasma proteins after IV injection and is cleared almost exclusively by hepatic excretion into bile, with a plasma half-life of 3–4 minutes. When excited by 750–800 nm light, ICG emits at 830 nm, a wavelength that is invisible to the naked eye but visualized by dedicated NIR imaging systems (Firefly, SPY, Rubina, PINPOINT). This combination (rapid plasma binding, rapid clearance, biliary excretion, and NIR emission through several millimeters of tissue) makes ICG the dominant intraoperative fluorophore in GU reconstruction.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[2]](#ref2)</sup>

---

## Pharmacology

| Property | Value |
|---|---|
| **Class** | Tricarbocyanine NIR fluorescent dye |
| **Molecular weight** | 775 Da |
| **Plasma half-life** | 3–4 minutes |
| **Protein binding** | &gt;95% (plasma proteins, primarily albumin) |
| **Distribution** | Predominantly intravascular initially; fluorescence is not a direct quantitative measure of tissue viability |
| **Clearance** | Hepatic — excreted into bile; no renal or urinary excretion |
| **Peak excitation** | ~800 nm |
| **Peak emission** | ~830 nm |
| **FDA approval** | Current IC-GREEN label includes vessels, blood flow and tissue perfusion; retrograde urinary instillation and GU sentinel mapping remain off-label |

### Why the pharmacology matters

- **Rapid plasma half-life** means multiple doses can be given per case as anatomy evolves
- **No urinary excretion.** ICG given IV does not appear in urine; this is the distinction from indigo carmine or methylene blue and is why IV ICG is not useful for ureteral visualization from within the urinary tract. IV ICG can instead assess perfusion of an exposed ureteral segment before anastomosis, but it does not fill the urinary column or reliably trace an unexposed ureter.<sup>[[5]](#ref5)[[6]](#ref6)[[10]](#ref10)</sup>
- **Biliary excretion.** ICG can faintly highlight the biliary tree, which is rarely clinically relevant in GU work but worth knowing

## Dosing

### IV perfusion dosing — current US IC-GREEN label

For an adult image sequence, 1.25–5 mg IV, followed immediately by a 10 mL saline flush. For extremity perfusion through skin, the adult range is 3.75–10 mg. Additional sequences may be obtained during the procedure, without exceeding 2 mg/kg total. This is a ceiling, not a target or a fixed repeat-every-5-minutes protocol. Reconstitute the 25 mg vial with 10 mL Sterile Water for Injection (2.5 mg/mL); other vial presentations have their own instructions. Use within 6 hours and discard unused solution. Use an imaging device authorized for the intended ICG application.<sup>[[5]](#ref5)</sup>

### Direct urinary instillation and lymphatic mapping

Retrograde intraureteral or intravesical ICG is off-label. Concentration, volume and catheter handling should follow a defined specialist protocol; published case-series recipes are not interchangeable with the IV label. Penile or prostate lymphatic mapping also must not borrow a generic injection recipe from the separately labeled cervical or uterine cancer indication. Do not force injection against resistance.

### Hypersensitivity and iodide

The January 2026 US IC-GREEN label contraindicates use in patients with hypersensitivity to indocyanine green. It does not list shellfish allergy or a nonspecific “iodine allergy” as that contraindication. Clarify the actual prior drug and reaction, check the specific product label, and obtain allergy or anesthesia input when needed. Anaphylaxis, including fatal reactions, has occurred: monitor every patient and have resuscitation personnel and equipment available. Sodium iodide in IC-GREEN can interfere with thyroid radioiodine uptake for at least one week.<sup>[[5]](#ref5)</sup>

## GU Reconstruction Applications

### 1. Perfusion assessment

The original and highest-yield urologic application. IV ICG highlights the arterial perfusion of any tissue: ureteral stump viability, bowel mesentery before urinary diversion, bladder flap vascularity, phalloplasty flap perfusion, omental pedicle viability.

- **Ureter after reimplantation.** Helps assess perfusion of the distal segment after dissection
- **Ureter before ureteroenteric anastomosis.** IV ICG (or SPY in open surgery) permits trimming to well-perfused ureter. Single-center comparisons after cystectomy report lower ureteroenteric stricture rates with ICG (robotic RARC with extracorporeal diversion, 196 ICG versus 196 prior controls: estimated stricture risk at 6 months 2% versus 12% and at 24 months 3% versus 14%, adjusted HR 0.20 (95% CI 0.08–0.50); open diversion, 1/55 SPY versus 31/277 historic control patients (1.8% versus 11.1%), with median follow-up 17.5 versus 58.6 months). Both are non-randomized, with era and follow-up differences.<sup>[[6]](#ref6)[[7]](#ref7)</sup> Across four observational robotic-cystectomy comparisons (886 patients; 1,739 ureters), a 2026 meta-analysis estimated a per-ureter RR of 0.31 (95% CI 0.08–1.16; I² 76.4%); certainty was very low.<sup>[[12]](#ref12)</sup>
- **Ileal conduit before anastomosis.** Helps assess bowel-segment perfusion
- **Kidney perfusion during partial nephrectomy.** A retrospective series describes IV ICG after pedicle clamping to confirm ischemia, followed by unclamping to identify the tumor margin and reclamping before excision; fluorescence remains an imaging adjunct rather than an isolated indication for tissue excision.<sup>[[3]](#ref3)</sup>
- **Bladder flap for Boari.** Helps assess perfusion of the flap before tubularization
- **Omental flap.** May supplement visual assessment of pedicle perfusion after mobilization
- **Bowel anastomosis in urinary diversion.** Early identification of poorly perfused anastomotic segments. The randomized evidence is colorectal: a 2026 meta-analysis of nine RCTs (4,754 patients) found ICG angiography lowered anastomotic leak (RR 0.66), with benefit in left-sided and rectal resections but not right-sided ones.<sup>[[8]](#ref8)</sup> Whether this benefit extends to ileo-ileal or ileocolonic anastomoses in urinary diversion was not tested in these trials.<sup>[[8]](#ref8)</sup>

### 2. Ureteral identification

Two distinct techniques (IV versus intraureteral):

**Intraureteral ICG (retrograde):**

- Ureteral catheter placed cystoscopically; instillation proceeds retrograde according to the local protocol
- The urinary column within the ureter fluoresces under NIR imaging
- An adjunct for intraoperative ureter localization during robotic pelvic surgery. A 2026 meta-analysis (17 mostly non-randomized ICG studies, 569 patients, versus 20 stent studies) found injection succeeded in every ureter and reported ureteral injury of 0.18% versus 1.12% with stents. There are no head-to-head trials, so ICG is not shown to replace stents<sup>[[9]](#ref9)</sup>
- In robot-assisted ureteral reconstruction, ICG injected off-label into the diseased ureter above and below the stricture (through a ureteral catheter, a nephrostomy tube or both) was used to identify the ureter and localize the proximal and distal stricture margins under NIR imaging. In a retrospective series of 26 reconstructions in 25 patients (ureterolysis, pyeloplasty, ureteroureterostomy and ureteroneocystostomy), there were no perioperative complications attributed to ICG, and all procedures were clinically and radiologically successful at a mean follow-up of 12 months; the series was small and short-term<sup>[[10]](#ref10)</sup>
- Particularly valuable in fibrotic / reoperative / malignant fields where ureter is hard to identify by dissection alone

**IV ICG for ureter:**

- IV injection does not fill the ureter (no urinary excretion)
- IV ICG has been used to assess perfusion of an exposed distal ureter before anastomosis; this does not establish localization of an unexposed ureter<sup>[[6]](#ref6)[[7]](#ref7)</sup>
- Intraureteral instillation is the reported ICG method for tracing the ureteral course beneath overlying tissue<sup>[[9]](#ref9)[[10]](#ref10)</sup>
- See also [pudexacianinium (ASP-5354)](/docs/foundations/pharmacology/intraoperative-adjuncts/visualization-agents/pudexacianinium), an investigational IV NIR agent that is excreted into urine

### 3. Lymphatic mapping

- **Pelvic lymph node dissection** during radical prostatectomy, cystectomy (within functional-urology scope where it overlaps)
- **Sentinel node mapping** in penile cancer (outside primary WARWIKI scope but referenced for completeness)
- The US label covers lymphatic mapping only in cervical and uterine cancer (interstitial); urologic use is off-label
- Intradermal or peritumoral ICG injection, then lymphatic channels fluoresce, then sentinel node identification

### 4. Vesicourethral / bladder-neck identification (robotic)

- **Y-V plasty for bladder-neck or vesicourethral stenosis.** In Granieri’s seven-patient robotic series (six bladder-neck contractures and one vesicourethral anastomotic stenosis), a cystoscope was advanced to the narrowing and Firefly was used for localization. Kurobe and colleagues classify this as near-infrared cystoscopic transillumination without ICG injection. See [Y-V plasty](/docs/foundations/surgical-principles/flaps/y-v-plasty).<sup>[[4]](#ref4)[[11]](#ref11)</sup>
- **Vesicourethral anastomotic stenosis repair.** Similar rationale

### 5. Penile reconstruction

- **Perfusion assessment after Peyronie's degloving**
- **Phalloplasty flap assessment.** RFFF, ALT, SCIP perfusion
- **Penile shaft coverage flap viability**

### 6. Fistula localization (emerging)

Intravesical ICG appears among dye options in a specialist description of the diagnostic VVF tampon test; methylene blue is usually used.<sup>[[13]](#ref13)</sup> In one VVF repair within a 37-case urologic series, IV ICG was used to assess bladder and vaginal-wall perfusion during repair.<sup>[[14]](#ref14)</sup>

## NIR Imaging Hardware

ICG fluorescence requires dedicated imaging:

| System | Platform | Workflow |
|---|---|---|
| **Firefly** | Intuitive da Vinci (Si, Xi, X, SP, 5) | Toggle between white-light and NIR modes at the console |
| **SPY-PHI** | Stryker (open-field imaging) | Handheld NIR imager for blood flow and tissue perfusion<sup>[[15]](#ref15)</sup> |
| **PINPOINT** | Novadaq/Stryker | Laparoscopic NIR video system |
| **IMAGE1 S Rubina** | Karl Storz | Laparoscopic NIR with side-by-side display |
| **Visera Elite III** | Olympus | Laparoscopic NIR |

Firefly is integrated into da Vinci systems; the other listed NIR platforms support open and laparoscopic imaging.

## Technique Pearls

- **Give the bolus at the right moment.** The label expects vessel fluorescence within 5–15 seconds; tissue-level perfusion may take longer. Coordinate with anesthesia so the bolus is given just before the assessment rather than 5 minutes ahead
- **Don't dose too early in the case.** ICG clears in 3–4 minutes; if given before the tissue is fully mobilized, the fluorescence will have washed out
- **Track cumulative dose** and residual background fluorescence; fluorescence intensity depends on dose, timing, camera settings and tissue conditions
- **Intraureteral retrograde dosing** is generally used rather than IV when the goal is to see the ureteral course
- **Firefly auto-gain** can over-brighten adjacent tissues; toggle the NIR view intermittently to recalibrate
- **Clarify prior dye reactions**; alternative dyes have their own risks and pudexacianinium remains investigational

---

## Adverse Events

ICG has 65+ years of clinical use with the following safety profile:

- **Anaphylaxis.** Potentially fatal; postmarketing reports do not provide a reliable incidence or establish shellfish allergy as a predictor<sup>[[5]](#ref5)</sup>
- **Other reported reactions.** The US IC-GREEN label lists urticaria without an estimable frequency; the UK Verdye label also lists nausea and describes flushing among symptoms of anaphylactoid reactions.<sup>[[5]](#ref5)[[16]](#ref16)</sup>
- **Predominantly hepatic clearance.** Does not require urinary excretion for perfusion imaging; this does not mean risk-free in CKD
- ICG is not a gadolinium contrast agent; apply its own label and hypersensitivity precautions

---

## Interpretation and cost

ICG highlights fluorescence associated with delivery and tissue uptake; it does not by itself prove durable anastomotic viability or exclude ischemia. Standardized thresholds and improvement in clinical outcomes remain procedure-specific questions. Acquisition prices vary by product and contract; the imaging system and workflow contribute to total cost.
