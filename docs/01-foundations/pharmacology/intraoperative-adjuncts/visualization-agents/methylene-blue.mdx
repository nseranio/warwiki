# Methylene Blue

**Methylene blue** is a visible blue dye and an active drug. The US PROVAYBLUE indication is acquired methemoglobinemia in adults and children; fistula localization, urinary leak testing and other surgical dye uses are off-label. Its redox and monoamine-oxidase effects mean that dye use still requires medication and contraindication review.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup>

## Formulation and pharmacology

PROVAYBLUE contains 5 mg/mL; other products may have different concentrations. Verify the actual vial rather than assuming every injectable product is 1% (10 mg/mL). Methylene blue appears in urine, although urinary color and the timing of efflux can vary. Its visible-light role differs from fluorescence imaging with a compatible camera; an ICG-oriented NIR system is not automatically suitable.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[2]](#ref2)</sup>

## Surgical applications

### Fistula localization

Dilute intravesical methylene blue can help identify communication between the bladder and vagina, and an office dye test allows same-day diagnosis.<sup>[[3]](#ref3)</sup> The classic **three-swab (tampon) test** places three separate gauze swabs in the vagina (apex, mid, distal), fills the bladder retrograde through a catheter with lightly tinted saline, has the patient cough, Valsalva or walk, then inspects the swabs. Blue on the upper swab points to a vesicovaginal fistula; blue only on the distal swab suggests urethral leakage or a distal urethrovaginal fistula; a swab that is only wet with clear fluid raises the question of a ureteral source. In a double-dye examination, oral phenazopyridine colors upper-tract urine orange while the bladder contains blue solution. Blue vaginal staining supports a bladder communication; orange leakage without blue suggests a ureteral source. Mixed colors, urethral leakage, contamination, intermittent leakage and inadequate filling can complicate interpretation. Swab position alone does not define the entire anatomy, and a negative test does not exclude a fistula. Follow the [clinical fistula evaluation](/docs/clinical-conditions/03f-fistulas/in-females/vesicovaginal) pathway and obtain anatomical confirmation as indicated.

### Urinary anastomosis and bladder leak testing

Gentle filling with saline or a locally specified dilute dye can identify visible extravasation. Select volume and pressure for the repair; a universal 200–300 mL fill is inappropriate for every reconstructed bladder or urethra. Neither a negative leak test nor a visible ureteral jet excludes later ischemic failure.

### Ureteral visualization

IV methylene blue has been studied as an off-label aid, including near-infrared fluorescence imaging. In a 12-patient feasibility study, IV doses of 0.25–1 mg/kg showed both ureters within 10 minutes, with signal lasting at least 60 minutes and no clear dose effect.<sup>[[4]](#ref4)</sup> In laparoscopic colorectal surgery (n = 10, 0.125–1.0 mg/kg), the ureter was detected in only five patients, and the signal appeared only after the ureter was already visible in white light.<sup>[[5]](#ref5)</sup> The WSES guideline describes limited clinical evidence; use depends on the intended imaging method, patient safety and local protocol.<sup>[[2]](#ref2)</sup> Concentrated intravesical dye can obscure the field and is not a universal way to “darken the mucosa” to find orifices. Do not use an unverified intraventricular or intrathecal dye technique.

## Labeled methemoglobinemia treatment

For PROVAYBLUE, the usual label regimen is 1 mg/kg IV over 5–30 minutes. One additional 1 mg/kg dose may be given after one hour if methemoglobin remains above 30% or symptoms persist. Persistent disease after two doses requires an alternative approach and expert toxicology or hematology support. For eGFR 15–59 mL/min/1.73 m², the label recommends a single 1 mg/kg dose; do not automatically repeat. If dilution is needed, use the label's 5% dextrose instructions; sodium chloride can reduce solubility. These IV treatment instructions are separate from off-label bladder instillation protocols.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup>

## Contraindications and important precautions

- **G6PD deficiency is a contraindication**, as are severe hypersensitivity reactions to methylene blue or another thiazine dye. Do not portray an unvalidated low-dose dye regimen as an established exception.
- **Boxed warning: serotonin syndrome**, potentially fatal, with serotonergic drugs and opioids. Review SSRIs, SNRIs, MAO inhibitors and other interacting drugs with anesthesia/pharmacy. Avoid concomitant use; urgent unavoidable treatment requires the label's monitoring precautions. The label advises avoiding serotonergic drugs for **72 hours after the last dose**. Do not assume local instillation or a small dose is interaction-free: serotonin syndrome has been reported after a 7 mg subcutaneous injection for sentinel node mapping in a patient taking fluoxetine who also received fentanyl,<sup>[[6]](#ref6)</sup> and after methylene blue infusion for parathyroid localization in a patient on antidepressants.<sup>[[7]](#ref7)</sup> These reports involve parenteral dosing; the risk from dilute intravesical use is not quantified, but absorption through inflamed or freshly repaired urothelium is plausible.
- **Hemolysis** may occur, sometimes delayed; efficacy can also be limited in some causes of methemoglobinemia.
- The label notes that intra-amniotic injection of a methylene blue class product in the second trimester was associated with neonatal intestinal atresia and fetal death. Otherwise pregnancy requires an individualized benefit/risk assessment; the label includes pediatric treatment rather than a universal “never IV in neonates” rule. Breastfeeding interruption for up to 8 days is advised after treatment.
- Renal impairment increases exposure, and hepatic impairment may prolong toxicity and interaction risk.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup>

## Monitoring and practical points

Methylene blue may cause an artifactual decrease, not increase, in pulse-oximeter saturation. Inform anesthesia, assess for true hypoxemia and use an appropriate alternative measurement when needed. Do not dismiss a low reading solely because dye was given; the label advises an arterial blood sample for oxygen saturation if it is needed during or shortly after an infusion. The label also reports a fall in the Bispectral Index after methylene blue, so tell anesthesia when the dye is given IV. Urine color and some laboratory assays can also be affected. Record the product, dose, concentration and route; use a reviewed local dilution protocol for surgical applications.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup>

Practical points for dye use:

- Dilute before instilling. Undiluted 5–10 mg/mL solution stains the field and can hide the leak.
- Have the dye, catheter and swabs ready before the test; a dye test cannot be improvised mid-case.
- Blue staining of the field can degrade later white-light or fluorescence perfusion assessment, so sequence ICG or other imaging first when both are planned.
- Blue-green urine and transient staining of skin are expected and resolve; hypersensitivity and hemolysis are the events to watch for.
- Dye-test volumes and concentrations vary by unit. Teaching examples use roughly 25 mg in 250 mL saline for a bladder-filling tampon test; these are institutional conventions, not label-derived doses.
