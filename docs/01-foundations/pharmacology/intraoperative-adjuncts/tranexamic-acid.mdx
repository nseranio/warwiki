# Tranexamic Acid

**Tranexamic acid (TXA)** is a synthetic lysine-analogue antifibrinolytic that **reduces intraoperative blood loss and transfusion** across urologic surgery, with a generally favorable thromboembolic-safety profile. For the reconstructive and pelvic surgeon the relevant questions are narrow: *when does the blood-loss reduction justify giving it, and when is it dangerous?* — and the answer to the second is uniquely urologic.<sup>[[8]](#ref8)[[9]](#ref9)</sup>

---

## Mechanism — and Why the Urinary Tract Is Special

TXA competitively blocks the lysine-binding site on plasminogen, preventing its conversion to plasmin and thereby **stabilizing fibrin clots**.<sup>[[3]](#ref3)</sup> The urinary tract is **uniquely rich in plasminogen activators** — urokinase and high urinary plasminogen drive local fibrinolysis and clot breakdown.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[3]](#ref3)</sup> TXA's antifibrinolytic action directly counteracts this local fibrinolytic milieu, which is both why it is pharmacologically well-suited to urologic bleeding **and** why it carries a urology-specific hazard (clot retention in the upper tract — see Safety). TXA is **renally excreted**, so it concentrates exactly where the fibrinolysis is.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[14]](#ref14)</sup>

---

## Evidence Across Urologic Surgery

The best general evidence is the **POISE-3 urologic subanalysis** (1,124 patients across laparoscopic/robotic, open, transurethral, and percutaneous procedures): TXA (1 g IV at incision and 1 g at closure) **significantly reduced major bleeding (6.1% vs 9.5%; HR 0.63, 95% CI 0.41–0.97)** with **no significant thrombosis signal** (HR 1.12, 0.79–1.58) and no interaction by surgical approach, cancer status, or antithrombotic use.<sup>[[8]](#ref8)</sup> A meta-analysis of 13 RCTs (1,814 patients) across urologic surgery concurred: lower blood loss (SMD −0.705), reduced transfusion (OR 0.43), and no increased thromboembolic risk (OR 0.66).<sup>[[9]](#ref9)</sup>

The procedure-specific trials that built this base — drawn largely from prostate, cystectomy, and stone surgery — are summarized here as the evidence the general recommendation rests on:

| Setting | Key finding |
|---|---|
| **Prostate surgery** | Meta-analysis (9 RCTs): reduced EBL (SMD −1.93) and transfusion (RR 0.61) without ↑ DVT/PE.<sup>[[4]](#ref4)</sup> Landmark RRP RCT (n=200): transfusion **55% → 34%** (NNT 5), no thromboembolism at 6 mo.<sup>[[5]](#ref5)</sup> TURP benefit attributed to urokinase release from prostatic tissue.<sup>[[2]](#ref2)</sup> |
| **Radical cystectomy** | **Conflicting.** The TACT RCT (n=344, weight-based protocol) found **no transfusion reduction** (37.0% vs 37.4%), hypothesized to reflect concurrent intraoperative heparin in 84%.<sup>[[6]](#ref6)</sup> A propensity-matched series using a simplified **1 g bolus** showed transfusion **47% → 19%**.<sup>[[7]](#ref7)</sup> The TXA–anticoagulation interaction is unresolved. |
| **PCNL** | Cochrane review (10 RCTs): systemic and topical TXA may reduce transfusion and improve stone-free rates, greatest benefit in higher-risk anatomy; probably no thromboembolic effect.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup> |

## Hematuria Management (Non-Surgical)

Beyond the OR, TXA is used to control gross hematuria. A systematic review (7 studies, 970 participants) found it effective for both postoperative and non-postoperative hematuria, with route mattering:<sup>[[10]](#ref10)</sup>

- **IV** TXA performed best for **postoperative** hematuria;
- **oral and intravesical irrigation** were more advantageous in **non-surgical** settings;
- a pilot RCT of **intravesical** TXA for painless gross hematuria reduced irrigation-volume requirements (without a difference in hemoglobin decline).<sup>[[11]](#ref11)</sup>

The upper-tract contraindication below applies with equal force to medical hematuria management.

---

## Dosing

Doses vary widely; confirm against institutional protocol.

| Setting | Protocol |
|---|---|
| General urologic surgery (POISE-3) | 1 g IV at surgery start + 1 g at end<sup>[[8]](#ref8)</sup> |
| Radical prostatectomy | 500 mg IV load + 250 mg/h infusion intraop<sup>[[5]](#ref5)</sup> |
| Radical cystectomy (TACT) | 10 mg/kg IV load + 5 mg/kg/h infusion intraop<sup>[[6]](#ref6)</sup> |
| Radical cystectomy (simplified) | 1 g IV bolus preoperatively<sup>[[7]](#ref7)</sup> |
| Post-prostatectomy hematuria | 10–15 mg/kg IV q8h → 20 mg/kg PO q8h until hematuria resolves<sup>[[4]](#ref4)</sup> |
| Intravesical (hematuria) | Local instillation via Foley during bladder irrigation<sup>[[11]](#ref11)</sup> |

**Renal impairment requires dose reduction** (renal excretion → accumulation).<sup>[[14]](#ref14)</sup>

---

## Safety and Contraindications

**Thromboembolism.** Multiple meta-analyses and the POISE-3 subanalysis show **no significant increase in VTE** with TXA in urologic surgery.<sup>[[8]](#ref8)[[9]](#ref9)[[4]](#ref4)[[12]](#ref12)</sup> The HALT-IT trial (GI bleeding) did detect a small but real VTE signal (0.8% vs 0.4%), so the risk is best described as **real but small**.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup>

**Upper urinary tract bleeding — the critical urologic contraindication.** TXA is traditionally **contraindicated when the bleeding source is renal or ureteral**: stabilized clots can be retained in the ureter and cause **obstructive uropathy and potentially permanent renal injury**.<sup>[[14]](#ref14)</sup> A systematic review put the actual rate of obstructing urinary-tract clot at **&lt;1%**, but it remains a standard contraindication and the single most important urology-specific caution.<sup>[[15]](#ref15)</sup>

**Other.** Dose-dependent **seizure** risk above ~2 g/day;<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup> in cancer surgery, a meta-analysis of 16 RCTs (1,830 patients) found reduced blood loss/transfusion with **no increase** in complications, reoperation, mortality, or stay.<sup>[[16]](#ref16)</sup>

---

## Guideline Status and Reconstructive Relevance

Major urologic guidelines currently make **no formal recommendation** on TXA; adoption is variable, and the evidence best supports perioperative use **when bleeding risk is high** — and urologic surgery is itself classified high-bleeding-risk by NCCN in the anticoagulation-management context.<sup>[[8]](#ref8)[[13]](#ref13)</sup> For the reconstructive and urogynecologic surgeon, TXA is a generalizable **perioperative hemostatic adjunct** for higher-blood-loss pelvic and vaginal work, complementary to local measures such as [hydrodissection with a vasoconstrictor](/docs/foundations/pharmacology/intraoperative-adjuncts/hydrodissection-agents) and [energy vessel sealing](/docs/surgical-techniques/04g-prolapse-repair/vaginal-hysterectomy#vessel-sealing-vs-suture-ligation) — with the firm exception of suspected upper-tract bleeding.
