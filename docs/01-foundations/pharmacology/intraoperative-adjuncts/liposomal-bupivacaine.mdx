# Liposomal Bupivacaine (Exparel)

Liposomal bupivacaine is a multivesicular-liposomal formulation of bupivacaine designed to release drug over up to 72 hours from a single injection, theoretically an ideal ERAS adjunct for wound infiltration and peripheral or fascial-plane blocks. The evidence has not supported the theory. Across 76+ RCTs reviewed in a comprehensive *Anesthesiology* 2021 analysis, only 11% (4/36) of surgical-infiltration trials comparing liposomal bupivacaine (LB) with unencapsulated bupivacaine or ropivacaine showed a clinically relevant and statistically significant primary-outcome benefit, and manufacturer-related financial conflicts were associated with higher odds of reporting positive pain outcomes (OR 14.31).<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[2]](#ref2)</sup> The CLEVELAND trial (online December 2025; print March 2026) found no significant opioid or pain-score differences between its pre-incision four-quadrant TAP regimens. That result applies to the studied mixed abdominal-surgery population, not every block or operation.<sup>[[9]](#ref9)</sup>

This article is the drug-class pharmacology hub: formulation, preparation rules (they matter), FDA-approved doses and indications, comparison evidence versus standard bupivacaine in infiltration, TAP, and urologic contexts, and cost. For the broader local-anesthetic class (lidocaine, ropivacaine, standard bupivacaine across 12 urologic use cases) see [Local anesthetics](/docs/foundations/pharmacology/neuropathic-pelvic-pain/local-anesthetics). For ERAS workflow context see [ERAS](/docs/foundations/perioperative-care/perioperative-protocols/eras).

## Pharmacology

### Formulation

Multivesicular liposomes: microscopic honeycomb-like lipid-based particles with multiple aqueous chambers separated by lipid bilayers. Upon injection, liposomes gradually erode and release bupivacaine over an extended period. Like all amide-type local anesthetics, released bupivacaine blocks sodium channels and inhibits nerve impulse generation and conduction.<sup>[[3]](#ref3)[[4]](#ref4)</sup>

### Pharmacokinetics

| Parameter | Detail |
|---|---|
| **Absorption** | Depends on total dose, route, vascularity. Systemic plasma levels observed for **96 h** after local infiltration, **120 h** after interscalene block, **168 h** after sciatic / adductor-canal block<sup>[[4]](#ref4)</sup> |
| **Protein binding** | **95%** (plasma) |
| **Distribution** | Highly perfused organs; crosses placenta (F/M ratio 0.2–0.4) |
| **Metabolism** | Hepatic metabolism of bupivacaine — caution in hepatic disease |
| **Systemic levels** | **Do not correlate with local efficacy** |

## FDA-Approved Indications &amp; Dosing

| Indication | Dose | Volume | Age |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Local infiltration (adults)** | Up to **266 mg** (20 mL of 1.3%) | Dilute with NS or LR up to **1:14** (final concentration ≥ 0.89 mg/mL) | ≥ 18 yr <sup>[[4]](#ref4)</sup> |
| **Local infiltration (pediatric)** | **4 mg/kg** (max 266 mg) | Same dilution rules | Label dosing subsection: 6 to &lt;17 yr |
| **Interscalene brachial plexus block** | **133 mg** (10 mL) | 10 mL | Adults |
| **Sciatic nerve block (popliteal fossa)** | **133 mg** (10 mL) | 10 mL | Adults |
| **Adductor canal block** | **133 mg** + 50 mg **bupivacaine HCl 0.5%** (10 mL each = 20 mL total) | 20 mL | Adults <sup>[[4]](#ref4)</sup> |

Source: current US EXPAREL label. Infiltration is indicated from age 6; the pediatric dosing subsection specifies ages 6 to less than 17, so confirm adolescent dosing rather than extending a table by assumption. TAP blocks are not among the three labeled perineural indications. EXPAREL is not dose-interchangeable with standard bupivacaine.<sup>[[4]](#ref4)</sup>

## Preparation and Drug-Interaction Rules — Critical and Unique

**Preparation rules specific to liposomal bupivacaine.** These rules are different from standard bupivacaine and are the most common source of drug-waste and lost sustained-release effect.

- **Avoid admixture of EXPAREL with drugs other than label-permitted bupivacaine HCl.** Bupivacaine HCl may be given in the same syringe or immediately before EXPAREL when the bupivacaine HCl:EXPAREL milligram ratio does not exceed 1:2; this is not restricted to adductor-canal block. Toxicity is additive, and site-specific dose limits still apply. Lidocaine and other non-bupivacaine local anesthetics may cause immediate release of encapsulated bupivacaine if given together locally, potentially affecting safety and efficacy.<sup>[[4]](#ref4)</sup>
- Lidocaine may be administered ≥ 20 min before liposomal bupivacaine (label wording: EXPAREL may follow lidocaine after a delay of 20 minutes or more). Other non-bupivacaine locals have no data supporting pre-administration.
- Avoid additional local anesthetics within 96 h after EXPAREL; this warning is not limited to the same site. Reconcile the entire anesthetic plan, including label-permitted immediate-release adjuncts for the indicated nerve blocks, with anesthesia and pharmacy.
- **Do not dilute with water or hypotonic agents.** Disrupts liposomal particles. Use 0.9% preservative-free NS or LR only.<sup>[[4]](#ref4)</sup>
- Use a 25-gauge or larger-bore needle to preserve liposomal particle integrity; inject slowly with frequent aspiration for blood to reduce inadvertent intravascular injection.<sup>[[4]](#ref4)</sup>
- **Invert vial multiple times** to re-suspend particles immediately before withdrawal. Let topical povidone-iodine dry before injecting; EXPAREL should not contact antiseptic solution.<sup>[[4]](#ref4)</sup>
- **Storage and prepared product:** refrigerate vials at 2–8 °C. Sealed, intact vials may be held at 20–25 °C for up to 30 days; do not re-refrigerate. Do not use a vial suspected to have been frozen or exposed above 40 °C for an extended period. Use diluted EXPAREL within 4 h.<sup>[[4]](#ref4)</sup>
- **Methemoglobinemia risk** with concurrent nitrates or nitrites, other local anesthetics, dapsone, sulfonamides, antimalarials, or acetaminophen.<sup>[[4]](#ref4)</sup>

## Evidence — Infiltration vs Standard Bupivacaine

The most-studied comparison and the most consistently negative signal in local-anesthesia literature.

| Source | Finding |
|---|---|
| **Ilfeld 2021 *Anesthesiology*** comprehensive review of **76 RCTs** | Only **11% (4/36)** of surgical-infiltration RCTs showed clinically relevant and statistically significant primary-outcome benefit versus unencapsulated bupivacaine or ropivacaine. **92% (11/12)** of trials suggested better analgesia with a peripheral nerve block using unencapsulated bupivacaine than with surgical infiltration of LB; this comparison changes both technique and formulation. Among **low-risk-of-bias trials, only 14% (4/28)** reported significant differences — vs **84% (16/19)** of high-risk-of-bias trials<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup> |
| **Ji 2021 *J Surg Res*** SR of 63 RCTs, n = 6,770 | LB did not demonstrate significant pain relief in **74.6%** of RCTs; no opioid reduction in **85.7%** overall (**83.3%** vs standard bupivacaine). **Financial COI association: OR 14.31 for pain (p = 0.0001), OR 12.35 for opioid reduction (p = 0.024)**. Among 265 unpublished ClinicalTrials.gov RCT records, 47.5% were withdrawn, terminated, suspended or completed without available results — substantial reporting gaps<sup>[[2]](#ref2)</sup> |
| **Dinges 2021 RAPM** meta + trial sequential analysis, 23 RCTs, n = 1,867 | Pain ↓ **0.37 points on 10-point scale** at 24 h (p &lt; 0.05) — **statistically significant, below the paper's &gt;1.5-point clinical-relevance threshold**. 15% morphine-equivalent ↓ at 24 h. **No significant pain difference at 72 h**<sup>[[5]](#ref5)</sup> |
| **Hamilton 2017 Cochrane** | Lower cumulative 0–72 h pain vs placebo (very-low-certainty); **no difference vs standard bupivacaine** (very-low-certainty)<sup>[[6]](#ref6)</sup> |
| **Sandhu 2021 *JAMA Network Open*** RCT, n = 280, sternotomy / thoracotomy / laparotomy (cardiovascular department) | No improvement in 3-day pain scores, and **more** adjunctive opioid with LB (median 41.5 vs 33.0 MEU; p = 0.03)<sup>[[7]](#ref7)</sup> |
| **Saad Sayed 2025 meta** (8 RCTs, n = 810, abdominal surgery) | No significant pain score difference POD 0, 1, 2, 3. No morphine reduction any day. **LB not superior to standard bupivacaine** in the surgical plane for abdominal procedures<sup>[[8]](#ref8)</sup> |

**Across administration routes:** compared with bupivacaine HCl, a meta-analysis found a 0.37-point lower pain score at 24 h, below its clinical-relevance threshold, with no significant pain-score difference at 72 h. In a separate review, manufacturer-related financial conflicts were associated with higher odds of reported pain relief (OR 14.31), not a proven causal or 14-fold probability effect.<sup>[[5]](#ref5)[[2]](#ref2)</sup>

## Evidence — TAP Blocks

Particularly relevant for the reconstructive urologist; TAP blocks are a standard ERAS adjunct for major abdominopelvic surgery.

### CLEVELAND — 2025 online / 2026 print<sup>[[9]](#ref9)</sup>

Triple-blinded 3-arm RCT (261 in the modified intention-to-treat analysis) comparing LB plus plain bupivacaine TAP versus plain bupivacaine TAP versus saline placebo TAP for major abdominal surgery:<sup>[[9]](#ref9)</sup>

- Opioid consumption 0–24 h: 26 MME (LB) versus 33 MME (plain bupivacaine) versus 31 MME (saline); no significant differences
- 24–48 h opioid consumption: comparable
- Pain scores at 24, 48, 72 h: similar across all arms
- Authors' conclusion: single-shot four-quadrant TAP with LB, plain bupivacaine, or normal saline produced similar postoperative opioid consumption and pain scores

The trial questions routine pre-incision four-quadrant TAP in this mixed surgical population. A nonsignificant difference does not prove equivalence or establish that all TAP approaches lack benefit.

### EXPLANE 2022<sup>[[10]](#ref10)</sup>

Multicenter RCT (n = 498 analyzed), LB TAP versus continuous epidural for major abdominal surgery; stopped at a prespecified futility boundary:<sup>[[10]](#ref10)</sup>

- Pain at rest: TAP non-inferior to epidural (difference 0.09 points; p &lt; 0.05 for non-inferiority)
- **Less hypotension with TAP** (31% versus 48%)<sup>[[10]](#ref10)</sup>
- Pain met noninferiority; the coprimary opioid outcome showed higher use with TAP (+21 mg over 3 days).<sup>[[10]](#ref10)</sup>
- Important for cystectomy / major-reconstruction patients who are often volume-depleted post-op

### Hussain 2024 *Anesthesiology* meta<sup>[[11]](#ref11)</sup>

Abdominal fascial-plane blocks, LB versus plain local anesthetics: primary outcome (AUC pain 24–72 h) not different (SMD −0.21; p = 0.058). Unchanged in the COI- and mixing-with-plain-bupivacaine subgroup. The authors found no evidence-based preference for LB.

## Evidence — Urologic Surgery Specifically

### Radical cystectomy with diversion

| Study | Design | Finding |
|---|---|---|
| **Chu 2021** (retrospective cohort) | LB infiltration vs **epidural** at fascial closure | LB: **shorter LOS** (4.9 vs 5.9 d); less opioid; lower cost. Confounded by epidural-related hypotension and immobility<sup>[[12]](#ref12)</sup> |
| **Schmidt 2021** (prospective standard-bupivacaine cohort, n = 28, vs historical LB cohort, n = 34) | LB infiltration vs **high-volume standard bupivacaine** infiltration | **No statistically significant differences** in PACU opioid (LB 6.5 vs standard bupivacaine 9.0 mean MED; p = 0.29), later hospital opioid (LB 42.1 vs standard bupivacaine 36.8 mean MED; p = 0.81), POD 1/2/3 pain scores, or LOS (LB 4.9 vs standard bupivacaine 5.0 mean days). Authors: "no significant difference"<sup>[[13]](#ref13)</sup> |

**Zeng 2026** (RCT, n = 160, open cystectomy) randomized thoracic epidural, rectus sheath block, and surgeon infiltration with LB or standard bupivacaine. Pain scores were similar at 24, 48 and 96 h; at 72 h, epidural and standard-bupivacaine infiltration had lower resting VAS than LB infiltration. Opioid use at individual time points, treatment-related side effects and LOS were similar across groups, but patients assigned to thoracic epidural used less cumulative opioid during the first 72 h than patients assigned to standard-bupivacaine infiltration. Thoracic epidural was not associated with more hypotension, acute kidney injury or delayed ambulation.<sup>[[19]](#ref19)</sup> Unlike the historical cystectomy cohort, this trial randomized patients to both bupivacaine formulations within one study.<sup>[[19]](#ref19)</sup>

The retrospective epidural comparison is vulnerable to changes in care and patient selection. Hypotension or mobility differences are possible explanations, not proven mediators. The direct formulation comparison did not detect benefit over standard bupivacaine; it does not prove exact equivalence.

### Laparoscopic / robotic urologic surgery

**Knight 2015 RCT** (n = 206): LB versus 0.25% bupivacaine, with no difference in total opioid (15 versus 17.3 mg MME; p = 0.39), pain scores, LOS, or time to first opioid. "No significant difference between liposomal bupivacaine and 0.25% bupivacaine."<sup>[[14]](#ref14)</sup>

**Gong 2025 RCT** (106 analyzed; 53 per arm) compared lateral supra-arcuate quadratus lumborum block with 133 mg LB versus 75 mg bupivacaine HCl for laparoscopic partial or radical nephrectomy.<sup>[[28]](#ref28)</sup> The trial reported lower 48-hour morphine-equivalent use with LB (20.7 versus 31.8 mg) and a higher 24-hour QoR-15 score (118.6 versus 108.4).<sup>[[28]](#ref28)</sup> The single-center, unequal-dose comparison used a block outside EXPAREL's three labeled nerve-block indications; superiority across urologic surgery was not assessed.<sup>[[28]](#ref28)[[4]](#ref4)</sup>

### Pediatric urologic surgery

**Baby ORIOLES** (RCT, 104 randomized and 102 with complete 48-h follow-up, ages 6–18, penile block or inguinal / scrotal infiltration) stopped at its futility boundary: adding LB to bupivacaine HCl showed no statistically significant difference in the 48-h opioid-free rate (60% versus 62%), pain scores or adverse events.<sup>[[20]](#ref20)</sup>

### Perineal reconstruction (urethroplasty, AUS, sling)

In a single-surgeon prospective series of 67 men, a nonopioid pathway that included intraoperative LB mixed with 0.25% bupivacaine was associated with opioid prescriptions after surgery in 6/67 (9%); 15/67 (22%) contacted the clinic about pain within 30 days, most often about bladder spasm.<sup>[[21]](#ref21)</sup> There was no comparison group, so this shows a workable pathway, not an LB effect.<sup>[[21]](#ref21)</sup> In a separate 43-patient BMG urethroplasty RCT, LB at the oral harvest site versus no added anesthetic beyond harvest hydrodissection lowered POD1 intravenous morphine equivalents by a mean 8.58 mg (95% CI 1.59–15.56 mg), without a detected POD2 opioid, oral-pain, or oral-morbidity benefit; the trial did not compare LB with plain bupivacaine.<sup>[[27]](#ref27)</sup> Randomized urogynecology evidence is mixed and comparator-dependent. For retropubic slings, LB versus saline lowered 4-hour post-discharge pain (3.5 versus 13.0 mm; absolute scores were low in both arms) and the number using opioids on day 2 in 109 analyzed women; LB versus bupivacaine HCl with lidocaine in 57 women did not yield a clinically important day-1 pain benefit and had lower day-1 QoR-15 scores (124 versus 144).<sup>[[22]](#ref22)[[23]](#ref23)</sup> For prolapse repair, LB versus saline did not improve pain or opioid use after robotic sacrocolpopexy with posterior repair (64 analyzed).<sup>[[24]](#ref24)</sup> In a separate randomized trial across posterior vaginal-wall operations (100 participants), LB infiltration versus saline did not significantly reduce cumulative vaginal pain on postoperative days 1–7 (P = .14) or morphine-equivalent use (P = .81).<sup>[[30]](#ref30)</sup> LB plus plain bupivacaine pudendal block reduced POD1–3 vaginal pain after posterior colporrhaphy (120 enrolled) versus plain bupivacaine alone, without reducing opioid use.<sup>[[25]](#ref25)</sup> In a sacrospinous-suspension RCT (83 randomized), LB pudendal block versus no block did not improve overall pain to a clinically important degree, opioid use, or quality of recovery.<sup>[[26]](#ref26)</sup>

### Surgeon-administered TAP in cystectomy

**Roebuck 2022**, a retrospective cohort (n = 178) comparing surgeon-administered TAP with no TAP, associated TAP with lower POD 0–3 opioid use (106.4 versus 192.2 MME) and shorter LOS (5.6 versus 7.7 d); formulations were not compared.<sup>[[15]](#ref15)</sup>

## Quality of Recovery — The Most Favorable Signal

**Zhang 2025 meta** included 11 RCTs (n = 1,357) across operations and non-LB comparators, rather than bupivacaine HCl alone. The 72-hour QoR pool included 8 RCTs (n = 1,124) and was heterogeneous (I² = 82.95%). In scale subgroups, the QoR-15 mean difference of 3.32 was below the cited 6-point MCID; the QoR-40 point estimate of 8.66 exceeded its 6.3-point MCID, but that two-trial subgroup was not statistically significant.<sup>[[18]](#ref18)</sup>

- Quality of Recovery (QoR) at 72 h: SMD 0.52 (95% CI 0.20–0.85)<sup>[[18]](#ref18)</sup>
- ↓ pain scores at 24, 48, 72 h
- The five-study pooled analysis found no significant difference in cumulative 3-day morphine use (MD −3.84 mg; 95% CI −11.74 to 4.06; p = 0.34); a four-study sensitivity analysis excluding one trial favored LB.<sup>[[18]](#ref18)</sup>
- ↓ PONV
- ↑ patient satisfaction

**Interpretation:** the analgesic advantage on traditional pain scales is small-to-none, but the overall recovery experience may be improved. Weigh against cost and the industry-bias concerns in the broader literature.

## Cost

EXPAREL acquisition cost is generally higher than generic bupivacaine; use local pharmacy prices rather than a fixed national per-vial estimate.

- **Hamilton 2022 *JAMA Surgery*.** In 533 knee-replacement patients, adding LB to bupivacaine HCl did not improve 72-hour quality of recovery or pain versus bupivacaine HCl alone. The LB arm was economically dominated on point estimates, although 1-year QALY and total-cost differences were not statistically significant; its probability of cost-effectiveness was 37% at £20,000/QALY.<sup>[[17]](#ref17)</sup>
- **Babazade 2019** cost-effectiveness analysis. IVPCA was most cost-effective; TAP with LB narrowly dominated; epidural clearly dominated.<sup>[[16]](#ref16)</sup>

Cost-effectiveness is procedure- and comparator-specific. Limited evidence of routine clinical superiority should be considered alongside local cost.

## Safety Profile

EXPAREL retains serious bupivacaine toxicity risks and has formulation-specific compatibility restrictions; trials cannot establish that serious harms are absent.<sup>[[4]](#ref4)[[6]](#ref6)</sup>

- **Common adverse reactions (≥ 10%) vary by route and age:** adult infiltration: nausea, constipation, vomiting; adult nerve block: nausea, pyrexia, headache, constipation; pediatric infiltration (ages 6 to &lt;17 years): nausea, vomiting, constipation, hypotension, anemia, muscle twitching, blurred vision, pruritus and tachycardia.<sup>[[4]](#ref4)</sup>
- **Cardiovascular toxicity:** toxic bupivacaine concentrations can cause AV block, ventricular arrhythmias, cardiac arrest, decreased cardiac output and hypotension. Monitor cardiovascular and neurologic status during and after injection.<sup>[[4]](#ref4)</sup>
- **CNS toxicity:** restlessness, anxiety, tinnitus, dizziness, blurred vision, tremors, convulsions, unconsciousness, respiratory depression
- **Methemoglobinemia:** all patients are at risk with local anesthetics; G6PD deficiency, pre-existing methemoglobinemia, cardiopulmonary compromise and concurrent oxidizing agents increase susceptibility. Cyanosis can begin immediately or hours later and requires prompt evaluation and treatment.<sup>[[4]](#ref4)</sup>
- **Hepatic or renal impairment.** Bupivacaine is hepatically metabolized: consider increased LAST monitoring in moderate to severe hepatic disease. Renal impairment may increase the risk of EXPAREL-associated adverse reactions; increase monitoring.<sup>[[4]](#ref4)</sup>
- **Chondrolysis risk.** Not approved for intra-articular use
- **Obstetrical paracervical block is contraindicated.** There are no studies of EXPAREL in pregnant women; the label advises counseling about potential fetal risks based on animal data.<sup>[[4]](#ref4)</sup>
- **Sensory or motor block:** temporary sensory or motor loss may last up to 5 days, depending on injection site and dose; inform patients in advance.<sup>[[4]](#ref4)</sup>
- **Not recommended routes:** epidural, intrathecal, intravascular or intra-articular administration; other perineural blocks are not established labeled indications

For the LAST (Local Anesthetic Systemic Toxicity) framework and 20%-lipid-emulsion rescue protocol, see [Local anesthetics](/docs/foundations/pharmacology/neuropathic-pelvic-pain/local-anesthetics).<sup>[[29]](#ref29)</sup>

## Practical Pearls

- **The preponderance of evidence does not support routine use of liposomal bupivacaine over standard bupivacaine** in wound infiltration or TAP for reconstructive / urologic surgery. Among the 36 surgical-infiltration comparisons in the 76-RCT review, 4 (11%) demonstrated a clinically relevant benefit; that percentage is not the denominator for every studied indication.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[5]](#ref5)</sup>
- **Financial COI is a major interpretive concern.** Industry-affiliated studies are associated with higher odds of positive pain outcomes (OR 14.31).<sup>[[2]](#ref2)</sup>
- **CLEVELAND** found similar outcomes among the three tested regimens; avoid extending this null result to all surgery-specific TAP protocols.<sup>[[9]](#ref9)</sup>
- In radical cystectomy with diversion, a retrospective cohort associated LB infiltration with shorter stay and less opioid use than epidural, but cannot establish a formulation effect or its mediator. In the 160-patient RCT, LB infiltration had higher resting pain at 72 h than standard-bupivacaine infiltration and epidural; overall side effects and stay were similar, and epidural was not associated with more hypotension or delayed ambulation.<sup>[[12]](#ref12)[[13]](#ref13)[[19]](#ref19)</sup>
- **LB TAP may be considered when epidural is contraindicated, with anesthesia input.** EXPLANE compared LB TAP with epidural in major abdominal surgery: resting pain met noninferiority and hypotension was less frequent with LB TAP (MAP &lt;65 mmHg: 31% versus 48%), while the opioid coprimary outcome failed noninferiority (+21 mg morphine equivalents over 3 days).<sup>[[10]](#ref10)</sup> In a cystectomy-specific RCT, thoracic epidural was not associated with more hypotension than the other studied techniques.<sup>[[19]](#ref19)</sup>
- **Preparation rules matter.** Lidocaine admixture (or lidocaine given less than 20 min before LB) can release the encapsulated drug and destroy the sustained-release mechanism. Hypotonic dilution disrupts liposomal particles. Use a 25-gauge or larger-bore needle and NS or LR only.<sup>[[4]](#ref4)</sup>
- **96-hour warning** applies to additional local-anesthetic exposure, not just the injection site.<sup>[[4]](#ref4)</sup>
- **Check local cost and procedure-specific benefit.** Historical knee and abdominal surgery cost-effectiveness estimates depend on the local price and procedure.<sup>[[16]](#ref16)[[17]](#ref17)</sup>
- Quality-of-recovery data (2025) are the most favorable signal for LB; worth knowing about, but should be weighed against the broader evidence base and industry-bias concerns.<sup>[[18]](#ref18)</sup>
