# Perioperative Antibiotic Prophylaxis

> **Category:** Pharmacology > Infection &amp; Prophylaxis
> **Last updated:** September 2026

---

## Overview

**Perioperative antibiotic prophylaxis** aims to reduce surgical site infection (SSI) by achieving adequate tissue and urinary antibiotic concentrations at the time of incision. The foundational guidance for urologic prophylaxis is the AUA Best Practice Statement (BPS) 2020 on urologic procedures and antimicrobial prophylaxis<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup> and the ASHP/IDSA 2013 antimicrobial prophylaxis in surgery guideline.<sup>[[2]](#ref2)</sup>

A second framework is the device-specific evidence for IPP, AUS, mesh and sacrocolpopexy, particularly the 2023 retrospective PUMP analysis: vancomycin plus gentamicin alone (one AUA BPS option) had a higher adjusted hazard of device explantation or salvage for infection after primary IPP placement than other regimens (HR 2.7; 95% CI 1.4–5.4), despite similar crude rates of that endpoint (1.0% versus 1.2%).<sup>[[4]](#ref4)</sup> See [Penile implants — infection](/docs/surgical-techniques/04j-sexual-dysfunction/penile-implants/infection) and [Prosthetic infection &amp; biofilm protocols](/docs/foundations/pharmacology/infection-prophylaxis/prosthetic-infection-biofilm) for the full device-specific detail.

---

## Mechanism of Action

Perioperative prophylaxis provides time-limited antimicrobial coverage during the window of maximum contamination risk, from skin incision to closure. The mechanistic requirements are:

1. **Adequate tissue concentration at time of incision**, which requires correct timing
2. **Broad enough coverage for expected contaminants**, with agent selection by procedure type
3. **Sufficient duration to cover the operative window**, with redosing according to intravascular half-life
4. **Short enough total exposure to preserve microbiome and avoid resistance**, meaning single-dose or &lt;24-hour courses

## Agents Used

| Agent | Primary role | Typical dose (normal renal function) | Redose interval |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Cefazolin** | **Workhorse** for most urologic procedures; skin + common gram-negatives | 2 g IV (3 g if ≥120 kg) | q4h |
| **Vancomycin** | Selected MRSA coverage or β-lactam allergy; the AUA BPS advises against routine use because it is less active than cefazolin against methicillin-sensitive *S. aureus* | 15 mg/kg IV | Usually no intraoperative redose |
| **Gentamicin** | Gram-negative coverage; paired with cefazolin or another first-/second-generation cephalosporin for implanted prostheses (AUA BPS Table V; aztreonam substitutes in renal insufficiency) | 5 mg/kg IV on dosing weight (single dose) | Single dose typically |
| **Piperacillin-tazobactam** | Broad gram-negative + anaerobic coverage; urinary diversion / bowel-involving cases | 3.375 g IV | q2h |
| **Ceftriaxone** | Colorectal-entry regimens, combined with metronidazole; long half-life | 2 g IV | Long t½ — single dose usually sufficient |
| **Metronidazole** | Anaerobic coverage — combine with cephalosporin for bowel cases | 500 mg IV | Usually no intraoperative redose |
| **Clindamycin** | β-lactam allergy; skin coverage | 900 mg IV | q6h |
| **Ciprofloxacin / levofloxacin** | Oral or IV option for endoscopic GU procedures when local susceptibility supports it; see fluoroquinolone cautions below | Ciprofloxacin 400 mg IV or levofloxacin 500 mg IV | No intraoperative redose |
| **Aztreonam** | Gram-negative alternative; check ceftazidime allergy because of shared side chain | 2 g IV | q4h |


Adult reference doses and redosing intervals above follow ASHP guidance for normal renal function; intervals run from the start of the preceding dose. Gentamicin is dosed on actual body weight; when actual weight exceeds ideal body weight (IBW) by more than 20%, use the dosing weight IBW + 0.4 × (actual − IBW).<sup>[[2]](#ref2)</sup> Cefazolin rises to 3 g at 120 kg or more (ASHP notes 2 g is reasonable above 80 kg). Antifungal selection is discussed in the device and [antifungal](/docs/foundations/pharmacology/infection-prophylaxis/antifungals) hubs; it is not routine prophylaxis for every implant.<sup>[[2]](#ref2)[[21]](#ref21)</sup>

## Indications in Urology — AUA BPS 2020 Framework

The AUA BPS categorizes urologic procedures by infection risk. Single-dose perioperative prophylaxis is the standard for most clean-contaminated procedures.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup>

### Choose by procedure, culture and local susceptibility

| Procedure | Prophylaxis framework |
|---|---|
| **TURP / HoLEP** | Single dose for all cases (EAU strong recommendation for TURP). AUA Table V: cefazolin or TMP-SMX; alternatives amoxicillin-clavulanate, an aminoglycoside or aztreonam (each ± ampicillin). Adjust to culture and local guidance |
| **TURBT** | AUA supports single-dose prophylaxis; EAU makes a weak recommendation for prophylaxis in patients at high risk of postoperative sepsis (systematic-review evidence of benefit is limited) |
| **Ureteroscopy / PCNL** | Identify and treat preoperative infection / bacteriuria. Ureteroscopy: AUA Table V lists all cases (benefit undetermined for uncomplicated diagnostic ureteroscopy), TMP-SMX or a first-/second-generation cephalosporin, single dose; EAU weak. PCNL: cephalosporin-based regimen for under 24 hours (AUA); EAU strong for a single dose |
| **Urethroplasty** | EAU 2026 strongly recommends intraoperative antibiotic prophylaxis for urethral surgery (supporting evidence level 4) and recommends treating preoperative infection or colonization. AUA Table V lists cefazolin, with cefoxitin, cefotetan or ampicillin-sulbactam as alternatives, single dose. Buccal harvest or a perineal incision alone does not justify prolonged postoperative antibiotics, including through catheter removal.<sup>[[28]](#ref28)</sup> |
| **Sling / mesh / sacrocolpopexy** | Vaginal surgery for incontinence: single dose; AUA Table V lists a second-generation cephalosporin (cefoxitin, cefotetan) but states cefazolin gives equivalent vaginal-anaerobe coverage in sling procedures. Follow the relevant vaginal / abdominal protocol; merely entering the peritoneum does not itself require added anaerobic coverage. The one placebo-controlled midurethral-sling trial (n = 59, stopped early) found no statistically significant difference in wound infection (1/29 versus 0/30), but was underpowered to establish equivalence<sup>[[31]](#ref31)</sup> |
| **IPP / AUS** | Standard perioperative prophylaxis remains indicated; the BPS advises covering coagulase-negative staphylococci and gram-negative bacilli including *Pseudomonas*. AUA Table V (implanted prostheses including AUS, IPP and sacral neuromodulators): an aminoglycoside plus a first-/second-generation cephalosporin, aztreonam plus the cephalosporin, or vancomycin paired with an aminoglycoside or aztreonam (a footnote states that routine vancomycin is not recommended); the AUA alternative column includes an aminopenicillin with a β-lactamase inhibitor.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup> ASHP separately lists clindamycin with or without an aminoglycoside or aztreonam for patients with β-lactam allergy.<sup>[[2]](#ref2)</sup> Table V gives a duration of under 24 hours, while the BPS text advises no doses after closure. ASHP: cefazolin, with a single aminoglycoside dose considered for penile prosthesis. See device-specific discussion |
| **Cystectomy / bowel-containing diversion** | Match coverage to bowel segment, obstruction/contamination and local protocol; a nonobstructed small-bowel segment and colorectal entry are not identical exposures. Small-bowel segments (AUA Table V): cefazolin, single dose. When colon or rectum is entered, use cefazolin, cefoxitin, cefotetan or ceftriaxone, each paired with metronidazole, or use ertapenem, with oral neomycin plus erythromycin or metronidazole and mechanical bowel preparation, and add anaerobic coverage (ASHP reports postoperative SSI rates ranging from 12% to 39% across colorectal studies using a first-generation cephalosporin alone)<sup>[[2]](#ref2)</sup> |

A separate randomized trial examined a postoperative UTI endpoint rather than wound infection: 159 women were enrolled; after eight protocol exclusions, 151 were randomized and 149 were analyzed (nitrofurantoin 100 mg twice daily for three postoperative days, n = 74; placebo, n = 75). UTI within 6 weeks occurred in 17.6% of the nitrofurantoin group versus 32% with placebo (P = .04); 37 of 149 participants (24.8%) had a UTI overall. This older trial tested a postoperative course, not a perioperative single dose; it does not support routine extension beyond the current single-dose framework.<sup>[[25]](#ref25)</sup>

The AUA and EAU recommendations are not identical for every procedure. Avoid turning this overview into a universal cefazolin-or-gentamicin order set.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[2]](#ref2)[[20]](#ref20)</sup>

### Procedures usually not requiring prophylaxis

Healthy, low-risk adults generally do not require prophylaxis for simple cystoscopy or routine urodynamics (EAU 2026: strong recommendation against). Intradetrusor onabotulinumtoxinA injection is a separate procedure: the US adult-bladder BOTOX label requires no active UTI and specifies non-aminoglycoside antibiotic prophylaxis 1–3 days before injection, on the injection day and 1–3 days afterward; see the [intradetrusor injection page](/docs/surgical-techniques/04f-incontinence-procedures/procedures/intradetrusor-botox).<sup>[[29]](#ref29)</sup> The BPS makes an exception for urodynamics: give a single dose (trimethoprim-sulfamethoxazole is the named agent; first- or second-generation cephalosporin, amoxicillin-clavulanate or an aminoglycoside are alternatives) in neurogenic lower urinary tract dysfunction, immunosuppression, known or suspected urinary tract abnormality with recent instrumentation, or recent antimicrobial use. Routine catheter exchange does not become an antibiotic indication merely because asymptomatic bacteriuria is present, although the BPS allows a dose at catheter, stent or nephrostomy-tube removal or a trial of void when other risk factors are present. SWL without bacteriuria or suspected infection generally does not require prophylaxis. Check patient-specific infection risks and the actual procedure.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[3]](#ref3)[[7]](#ref7)[[20]](#ref20)</sup>

## Preoperative urine culture and asymptomatic bacteriuria

The AUA BPS 2020 advises that urinary symptoms suggestive of infection be evaluated before any urologic procedure with dipstick, microscopy and/or formal culture, scaled to procedural risk. It states that urine culture should not be performed without accompanying urine microscopy because of common sample contamination and colonization. Defer elective procedures when active infection requires treatment. For urgent infection or obstruction, obtain cultures when feasible and start appropriate treatment and source control; do not delay necessary drainage while waiting for susceptibility results.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup>

The IDSA asymptomatic-bacteriuria guideline (2019)<sup>[[7]](#ref7)</sup> provides the cleanest framework:

| Clinical scenario | IDSA 2019 recommendation | Strength / evidence |
|---|---|---|
| Endoscopic urologic procedure with mucosal trauma (TURP, TURBT, URS) | **Screen for and treat ASB** | Strong / Moderate |
| Prior to AUS or penile prosthesis implantation | **Do not routinely screen or treat ASB; still give standard perioperative prophylaxis** | Weak / Very low |
| When treating ASB before a GU procedure | Use **culture-targeted** therapy, not empiric | Weak / Very low |
| Duration when treating ASB for GU procedure | **1–2 doses** starting 30–60 min preop; not a prolonged course | Weak / Low |

The prosthetic-device exception exists because implant infections are typically caused by biofilm-producing skin flora rather than urinary pathogens; in a retrospective series of 721 AUS and IPP procedures, the 454 with preoperative urine cultures had similar device-infection rates with and without bacteriuria (3% vs 4.3%) at a median 15 months of follow-up, and only 1 of 15 device infections matched the preop urine organism.<sup>[[7]](#ref7)</sup>

In the retrospective multicenter TOCUS cohort (2,389 patients), positive preoperative urine cultures and prior UTI were associated with febrile UTI or SSI within 30 days. The TOCUS cohort was observational and did not test whether a longer prophylaxis course eliminates risk; continue culture-directed planning and postoperative clinical surveillance.<sup>[[15]](#ref15)</sup>

## Device-Specific Protocols

**IPP:** retrospective multicenter PUMP analyses found associations between regimen choice and infection. In the 5,261-patient study, antifungal use had adjusted OR 0.22 (78% lower odds, not a 92% risk reduction).<sup>[[5]](#ref5)</sup> These data do not establish a universal three-drug regimen or a causal antifungal benefit. Prolonged IV prophylaxis and postoperative oral antibiotics have not shown a clear preventive benefit in these observational studies.<sup>[[4]](#ref4)[[5]](#ref5)[[6]](#ref6)</sup>

**AUS and mesh:** do not extrapolate the IPP antifungal association into a routine recommendation. National IPP/AUS cohort analyses found mixed associations between guideline adherence and downstream outcomes; confounding limits causal interpretation, and they do not prove a particular regimen is microbiologically inadequate.<sup>[[13]](#ref13)[[14]](#ref14)</sup>

Use the [prosthetic infection &amp; biofilm hub](/docs/foundations/pharmacology/infection-prophylaxis/prosthetic-infection-biofilm) for the device-specific evidence and manufacturer handling restrictions. InhibiZone and hydrophilic devices have different preparation requirements; do not transfer a dip or irrigation recipe between products. Operative handling belongs on the [implant infection page](/docs/surgical-techniques/04j-sexual-dysfunction/penile-implants/infection).

## Prostate biopsy — current EAU 2026 guidance

Transperineal biopsy is preferred for its lower infectious risk and antibiotic stewardship. EAU 2026 weakly recommends omitting perioperative antibiotics in transperineal biopsy patients without infection risk factors. This does not apply automatically to active infection or high-risk patients.<sup>[[20]](#ref20)</sup>

The NORAPP trial randomized 555 patients; 553 were analyzed (277 prophylaxis, 276 none). Neither group had sepsis or UTI requiring hospitalization. Outpatient UTI occurred in 1/277 versus 3/276. The prespecified noninferiority margin was 4%; patients with high infection risk or ongoing infection were excluded.<sup>[[9]](#ref9)</sup>

**Transrectal biopsy:** use rectal povidone-iodine preparation plus locally appropriate prophylaxis. EAU prioritizes rectal-swab/stool-culture targeting, followed by augmented prophylaxis when appropriate, and recommends against fluoroquinolones for prostate-biopsy prophylaxis in the EU. Fosfomycin requires local regulatory and safety review; its prostate-biopsy indication was withdrawn in Germany. These recommendations supersede treating older fluoroquinolone-duration or fosfomycin analyses as a universal regimen.<sup>[[20]](#ref20)</sup>

The Pilatz 2020 review (59 RCTs, 14,153 participants) supported antibiotic prophylaxis and targeted over empirical regimens, but rated certainty low or very low. Increasing fluoroquinolone resistance remains a reason to use current local susceptibility data.<sup>[[8]](#ref8)[[18]](#ref18)</sup>

The 2025 Xiao Cochrane publication is a review protocol, not a completed systematic review demonstrating efficacy or safety.<sup>[[10]](#ref10)</sup>

## Radical cystectomy — duration of prophylaxis

Cystectomy with urinary diversion is frequently associated with prolonged (&gt;48 h) prophylaxis in practice. Duration evidence differs by endpoint and approach: the 2024 open-cystectomy RCT supports 24-hour prophylaxis for 90-day SSI, while a small 2025 robot-assisted-cystectomy RCT found fewer symptomatic 90-day UTIs after 30 days of postdischarge nitrofurantoin or TMP-SMX (0/37 versus 10/40 with usual care); the result does not establish a universal extended regimen.<sup>[[11]](#ref11)[[26]](#ref26)</sup>

Thurnheer 2024 (*JAMA Network Open*, single-center RCT, 193 patients) found 24-hour PAP noninferior to an extended course (median 8 days, until catheter/stent removal). Ninety-day SSI occurred in 8.4% versus 12.2%; risk difference −3.8% (90% CI −11.1% to 3.4%), within the prespecified 10% noninferiority margin.<sup>[[11]](#ref11)</sup>

**Mohamed 2026** (systematic review and meta-analysis, 4 studies, n = 680):<sup>[[12]](#ref12)</sup>

- **No statistically significant difference detected** between short-term (≤24 h) and extended PAP on:
  - SSI: RR 0.71 (95% CI 0.43–1.17; p = 0.18)<sup>[[12]](#ref12)</sup>
  - Febrile UTI: RR 1.19 (95% CI 0.91–1.56; p = 0.20)<sup>[[12]](#ref12)</sup>
  - Length of stay: MD 0.76 days (p = 0.67)
- **Interpretation:** supports short prophylaxis rather than routine continuation until device removal; nonsignificant pooled differences alone do not prove equivalence in every subgroup

The 2024 SSI trial does not support routine perioperative continuation until catheter or stent removal after open cystectomy; the 2025 postdischarge RCT addresses symptomatic UTI after robot-assisted surgery and requires separate interpretation. Treatment of a documented infection is a separate indication with its own agent and duration.<sup>[[2]](#ref2)[[11]](#ref11)[[26]](#ref26)</sup>

## Patient- and procedure-level risk factors for SSI

**Grabe 2012 EAU tentative classification**<sup>[[16]](#ref16)</sup> describes useful domains of urologic SSI risk:

| Domain | Risk factors |
|---|---|
| **Patient — general** | Higher ASA, advanced age, malnutrition / hypoalbuminemia, obesity, uncontrolled hyperglycemia, smoking, immune dysfunction |
| **Patient — urologic** | Bacteriuria, indwelling catheter, stone disease, obstruction, prior urogenital infection |
| **Procedure-related** | Surgical-field contamination class, operative duration, tissue handling / invasiveness, length of postoperative catheterization |

The Seidelman 2023 JAMA SSI review<sup>[[17]](#ref17)</sup> summarizes the cross-specialty evidence:

- Administer within 60 minutes of incision
- Cefazolin is the most commonly used agent; redose every 4 hours for lengthy procedures
- A systematic review of 28 RCTs (n = 9,478) found no clear difference in surgical-site infection between single preoperative and multiple-dose prophylaxis (fixed-effect OR 1.06; 95% CI 0.89–1.25)<sup>[[30]](#ref30)</sup>
- Prolonged AP is increasingly associated with acute kidney injury and other harms
- **Stop antibiotics when the wound is closed**

The Japanese 2015-edition guideline is a historical comparator.<sup>[[19]](#ref19)</sup> The Japanese Urological Association 2023 edition, published in 2025, recommends cephamycin prophylaxis within 72 hours for cystectomy with gastrointestinal diversion (evidence level IVa; recommendation grade C1). This differs in duration from the 2024 open-cystectomy trial, which found 24 hours noninferior to a median eight days for 90-day SSI.<sup>[[11]](#ref11)[[27]](#ref27)</sup>

## Administration and safety

Use the adult table above with the local protocol. Start most agents within 60 minutes before incision; allow up to 120 minutes for vancomycin or fluoroquinolone administration. Vancomycin infusion duration depends on dose and infusion-rate limits. Redose short-half-life agents for long cases or substantial blood loss (about 1,500 mL); renal impairment may lengthen redosing intervals even when the initial prophylactic dose is unchanged.<sup>[[2]](#ref2)</sup>

For most operations, stop prophylaxis at closure; the AUA BPS advises no postoperative doses even when a drain is in place. A 24-hour cystectomy pathway has randomized support; a drain, catheter, or stent alone does not justify continuing prophylaxis. Active infection requires a separate treatment plan.<sup>[[11]](#ref11)[[17]](#ref17)[[22]](#ref22)</sup>

### Allergy history changes the choice

The 2022 drug-allergy parameter permits cefazolin in most patients with penicillin allergy, including prior penicillin anaphylaxis, because its side chain is dissimilar. This does not establish safety after a cefazolin reaction, severe cutaneous adverse reaction (SJS/TEN, DRESS), or severe drug-related organ injury. Assess the culprit, reaction and timing; use allergy expertise for severe histories. Aztreonam shares a side chain with ceftazidime. Vancomycin infusion reactions and DRESS are different syndromes and require different management.<sup>[[21]](#ref21)</sup>

### Fluoroquinolone cautions

Fluoroquinolones carry a boxed warning for disabling and potentially irreversible tendon, muscle, joint, peripheral-nerve and central nervous system reactions; the FDA advises reserving them for patients with no alternative in uncomplicated UTI, sinusitis and bronchitis.<sup>[[24]](#ref24)</sup> Later FDA communications added aortic aneurysm or dissection and dysglycemia warnings. A single perioperative dose is a different exposure from a treatment course, but prefer an effective non-fluoroquinolone agent where susceptibility allows, and avoid them in patients with tendon disorders, aortic aneurysm or dissection risk, diabetes on hypoglycemics, or prior fluoroquinolone reaction. In the EU, fluoroquinolone prophylaxis for prostate biopsy and other perioperative use is suspended.<sup>[[20]](#ref20)[[24]](#ref24)</sup>

### Renal function, pregnancy and monitoring

Avoid unnecessary nephrotoxic combinations and distinguish a prophylactic dose from a therapeutic course. Serious MRSA treatment uses AUC-guided vancomycin monitoring (target AUC/MIC 400–600, assuming MIC 1 mg/L); an old trough-only target of 15–20 mg/L is not a prophylaxis protocol. Pregnancy requires agent- and gestation-specific selection with obstetric input.<sup>[[23]](#ref23)</sup>

### MRSA prevention

Follow institutional screening or decolonization policy for selected procedures involving prosthetic material. A common carrier regimen uses intranasal mupirocin plus chlorhexidine body cleansing; the strongest evidence is from cardiac/orthopedic surgery, with extrapolation to other prosthetic procedures. Reserve perioperative vancomycin for a specific indication such as known MRSA colonization rather than automatically adding it to every clean operation.<sup>[[22]](#ref22)</sup>

## Evidence interpretation

Guidelines contain recommendations of differing certainty; their publication does not make every statement “Level 1.” PUMP and national device cohorts are observational. NORAPP and the cystectomy-duration trial are randomized studies with defined populations and noninferiority margins. The Xiao Cochrane protocol has no completed pooled outcome to apply. Use these distinctions when adapting a local pathway.
