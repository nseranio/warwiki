# Antifungals

Antifungals play a focused role in urology across six domains: (1) symptomatic Candida cystitis and pyelonephritis (the most common fungal urologic infection); (2) fungus balls / mycetoma in the bladder or renal pelvis; (3) perioperative antifungal prophylaxis for urologic procedures in patients with funguria; (4) penile prosthesis infection prevention, an evolving area supported mainly by observational studies; (5) renal aspergillosis; and (6) rare fungal infections of the prostate, epididymis, and testes.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[2]](#ref2)</sup>

The cornerstone principle that shapes every one of these decisions is pharmacokinetic: fluconazole is the drug of choice for most urinary Candida infections because it is essentially the only azole excreted in active form into the urine at concentrations exceeding the MIC for most Candida isolates. Lipid amphotericin B formulations and echinocandins do not achieve therapeutic urinary concentrations and are unsuitable for routine ascending Candida UTI. Hematogenous renal candidiasis is different: tissue-active therapy, including an echinocandin, may be appropriate as part of candidemia treatment.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[2]](#ref2)</sup>

For related classes, see [UTI treatment antibiotics](/docs/foundations/pharmacology/infection-prophylaxis/uti-treatment-antibiotics), [Perioperative antibiotic prophylaxis](/docs/foundations/pharmacology/infection-prophylaxis/perioperative-antibiotic-prophylaxis), and [Prosthetic infection &amp; biofilm protocols](/docs/foundations/pharmacology/infection-prophylaxis/prosthetic-infection-biofilm).

## Agents — which antifungals reach the urine?

| Agent | Urinary excretion | Urinary role |
|---|---|---|
| **Fluconazole** | About 80% of a dose is recovered unchanged in urine (US label) | **Drug of choice** for most Candida UTI<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[2]](#ref2)</sup> |
| **Flucytosine** | Excreted as active drug; broad Candida activity (except *C. krusei*) | Alternative; limited by toxicity (marrow suppression, hepatitis) and rapid resistance when used alone<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup> |
| **AmB deoxycholate** | Achieves adequate urinary concentrations even at low doses | Fluconazole-resistant species, fungus-ball local irrigation; systemic treatment is IV and nephrotoxic; local irrigation is a separate specialist use<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup> |
| **Lipid AmB formulations** (L-AmB, ABLC) | **Do NOT** achieve adequate urinary concentrations | Avoid for isolated ascending Candida UTI<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup> |
| **Echinocandins** (caspofungin, micafungin, anidulafungin) | Minimal urinary excretion | Poor choice for ascending urinary infection; a role remains in hematogenous renal candidiasis<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup> |
| **Voriconazole, posaconazole, itraconazole, isavuconazole** | Poor urinary concentrations | Poor choice for ascending Candida UTI; tissue-targeted use (e.g., renal parenchymal aspergillosis)<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[11]](#ref11)</sup> |

## Asymptomatic candiduria — when NOT to treat

The most important clinical decision regarding antifungals in urology is recognizing that most candiduria represents colonization rather than infection and does not require antifungal therapy.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup>

**IDSA 2016 Candidiasis guideline:**

- Antifungal treatment is not recommended for asymptomatic candiduria unless the patient is at high risk for dissemination
- **High-risk groups** requiring treatment: neutropenic patients, very low-birth-weight infants (&lt;1,500 g), and patients undergoing urologic manipulation
- High-risk neutropenic patients and infants are treated as for candidemia; patients facing urologic manipulation receive a short peri-procedural course (see below)
- First-line interventions: remove or replace an unnecessary catheter, eliminate broad-spectrum antibacterials where feasible, address diabetes and obstruction<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup>
- **EAU Urological Infections 2026** agrees: asymptomatic funguria generally needs no antifungal, but treatment should be considered in neutropenic patients, infants under 1,500 g, and before urologic intervention that breaches the mucosa. Non-antimicrobial measures come first; catheter replacement alone can clear funguria, and an obstructed system needs prompt stent or nephrostomy drainage<sup>[[17]](#ref17)</sup>
- The 2026 urologist's manual (Bausch, written to supplement the EAU Urological Infections guideline) takes the same position: only 2–11% of funguria isolates are symptomatic, catheter replacement belongs in every treatment plan, fluconazole is first-line, and asymptomatic funguria is treated only in exceptional cases<sup>[[16]](#ref16)</sup>

**Overtreatment is endemic.** Jacobs 2018 multi-institutional study found 43% of asymptomatic candiduria cases were overtreated, driven primarily by unnecessary fluconazole use.<sup>[[3]](#ref3)</sup> Antifungal stewardship in candiduria is low-hanging fruit in most institutions.

## Symptomatic Candida cystitis

Per IDSA 2016:<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup>

| Species | Regimen |
|---|---|
| **Fluconazole-susceptible** | **Fluconazole 200 mg (3 mg/kg) PO daily × 2 weeks** (strong recommendation) |
| **Fluconazole-resistant *C. glabrata*** | AmB deoxycholate 0.3–0.6 mg/kg IV daily × 1–7 days **OR** oral flucytosine 25 mg/kg QID × 7–10 days |
| ***C. krusei*** | AmB deoxycholate 0.3–0.6 mg/kg IV daily × 1–7 days |

**EAU 2026** lists fluconazole, AmB deoxycholate and oral flucytosine as the agents, with species susceptibility deciding the choice. Empirical therapy is usually a two-week fluconazole course with a double first-day loading dose. *C. krusei* and *C. glabrata* are more than 90% fluconazole-resistant as a group, so treat *C. glabrata* with fluconazole only when the isolate is confirmed susceptible (many urinary isolates are susceptible-dose-dependent and need higher dosing). Echinocandins reach the renal tissue but not the urine, so they suit pyelonephritis rather than cystitis; micafungin is an exception to consider for cystitis from fluconazole-resistant *C. glabrata*, *C. krusei* or *C. auris*.<sup>[[17]](#ref17)</sup>

**AmB deoxycholate bladder irrigation** (50 mg/L sterile water daily × 5 days) may be useful for cystitis due to fluconazole-resistant species, but evidence is low quality and relapse is common; it treats the bladder only, not pyelonephritis or candidemia.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[4]](#ref4)</sup> EAU 2026 describes continuous irrigation with 50 mg/L for five days as an option in localized fluconazole-resistant cases after upper-tract and systemic spread are excluded. In an RCT it cleared funguria earlier than fluconazole (96% versus 73%) but one-month mortality was higher (41% versus 22%), so select patients carefully.<sup>[[17]](#ref17)</sup>

**Remove or replace the indwelling catheter** whenever feasible before initiating antifungal therapy.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup>

## Candida pyelonephritis

Ascending Candida pyelonephritis requires systemic therapy; hematogenous renal candidiasis from candidemia is treated as candidemia, not as a primary UTI.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup>

| Species | Regimen |
|---|---|
| **Fluconazole-susceptible** | **Fluconazole 200–400 mg (3–6 mg/kg) PO daily × 2 weeks** (strong recommendation; low-quality evidence) |
| **Fluconazole-resistant *C. glabrata*** | AmB deoxycholate 0.3–0.6 mg/kg IV daily × 1–7 days with or without flucytosine 25 mg/kg QID; flucytosine alone × 2 weeks is a weaker alternative |
| ***C. krusei*** | AmB deoxycholate 0.3–0.6 mg/kg IV daily × 1–7 days |

## Fungus balls (mycetoma)

Fungus balls in the bladder or renal pelvis cause obstruction and preclude cure with antifungals alone. Management requires a combined medical and procedural approach.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup>

- **Fluconazole-susceptible:** fluconazole 200–400 mg PO daily × 2 weeks
- **Fluconazole-resistant *C. glabrata*:** AmB deoxycholate 0.3–0.6 mg/kg daily × 1–7 days ± flucytosine 25 mg/kg QID; flucytosine monotherapy × 2 weeks may also be considered
- ***C. krusei*:** AmB deoxycholate 0.3–0.6 mg/kg daily × 1–7 days
- **Eliminate obstruction** with percutaneous nephrostomy, ureteral stent, or endoscopic removal as anatomy allows
- Consider removing or replacing nephrostomy tubes and stents, when feasible
- **Local irrigation via nephrostomy with AmB deoxycholate** (IDSA: 25–50 mg in 200–500 mL sterile water) achieves high local concentrations and is recommended by IDSA when a nephrostomy tube is in place (strong recommendation, low-quality evidence)<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup>
- Send fungal culture with susceptibilities. The AUA statement calls for antifungal therapy at the time of the procedure and afterward, with 5–7 days the majority opinion<sup>[[5]](#ref5)</sup>

## Perioperative antifungal prophylaxis for urologic procedures

Per the AUA Best Practice Statement on Urologic Procedures and Antimicrobial Prophylaxis (2020):<sup>[[5]](#ref5)</sup>

- **Asymptomatic funguria alone** does not require antifungal prophylaxis for simple nephrostomy exchange or ureteral stenting (in the absence of neutropenia or other high-risk features)
- **Antifungal prophylaxis IS recommended** for patients with asymptomatic funguria undergoing specific intermediate- and high-risk procedures, including:
  - Resective / enucleative / ablative outlet procedures (TURP, HoLEP)
  - TURBT
  - Ureteroscopy
  - PCNL
  - All endoscopic procedures
  - Procedures using high-pressure irrigation
  - Procedures with planned surgical entry into the urinary tract
- **Drug and duration come from IDSA:** the AUA statement names the procedures but gives no antifungal agent or prophylaxis duration for asymptomatic funguria. IDSA recommends fluconazole 400 mg (6 mg/kg) daily or AmB deoxycholate 0.3–0.6 mg/kg daily for several days before and after urologic manipulation (strong recommendation, low-quality evidence). Write the chosen local protocol explicitly rather than attributing the IDSA schedule to AUA.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[5]](#ref5)</sup>
- **Neutropenic patients with funguria and urinary-tract obstruction** are named separately in the AUA list as needing antifungal prophylaxis, and IDSA treats neutropenic candiduria as candidemia. Involve infectious disease early; the AUA statement sets no separate duration for this group<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[5]](#ref5)</sup>
- **Agent choice:** IDSA offers oral fluconazole or AmB deoxycholate. Fluconazole is usually chosen because it reaches the urine in active form; reserve AmB deoxycholate for fluconazole-resistant species or when fluconazole cannot be used (allergy, QT prolongation or interacting drugs, acute liver injury; see the label)<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[15]](#ref15)</sup>
- **Symptomatic fungal UTI at the time of tube / stent exchange** requires full treatment (not single-dose prophylaxis), with fungicidal levels established before the procedure<sup>[[5]](#ref5)</sup>

See [Perioperative antibiotic prophylaxis](/docs/foundations/pharmacology/infection-prophylaxis/perioperative-antibiotic-prophylaxis) for the full BPS framework.

## Penile prosthesis — observational prevention evidence

Fungal infection is a relevant part of the IPP infection spectrum, often in patients with diabetes or excess weight. In the Gross 2019 retrospective series, 26 of 217 infected implants involved fungi (12%); this is a proportion of infected devices, not the risk among all implants.<sup>[[14]](#ref14)</sup> The broader literature is reviewed by Natsos 2024.<sup>[[6]](#ref6)</sup>

**Abou Chawareb 2025** studied 5,261 primary or revision IPP procedures at 16 centers. IV antifungal use was associated with lower adjusted infection odds (OR 0.22, p &lt; 0.001; 78% lower odds, not 92% lower risk). Prolonged IV prophylaxis and perioperative oral antibiotics did not show a protective association. This retrospective study cannot establish a causal benefit or an optimal drug or dose for every patient.<sup>[[7]](#ref7)</sup>

Antifungal prophylaxis may be considered within a local implant protocol informed by patient risk, cultures, susceptibility, interactions and antimicrobial stewardship. Routine fluconazole for every implant, or amphotericin substitution for all non-albicans species, is not established by these data. Systemic prophylaxis and experimental device dipping are separate interventions.

Antifungal adoption varies; a 2025 multicenter SMSNA/SUPS/GURS survey found antifungal use in 25.9% of primary, 72.3% of diabetic, 65.2% of salvage, and 48.2% of revision cases.<sup>[[8]](#ref8)</sup> Bench testing found preserved antibacterial activity with selected antifungal-containing dips; this does not establish clinical infection prevention or compatibility of every mixture.<sup>[[9]](#ref9)</sup>

For the full prosthetic-infection framework (dipping solutions, Irrisept device-specific effects, salvage protocols) see [Prosthetic infection &amp; biofilm protocols](/docs/foundations/pharmacology/infection-prophylaxis/prosthetic-infection-biofilm) and [Penile implants — infection](/docs/surgical-techniques/04j-sexual-dysfunction/penile-implants/infection).

## Radical cystectomy

**Pariser 2016** (single-institution, n = 386 radical cystectomies with urinary diversion): switching from cefoxitin to a culture-directed regimen that added fluconazole 400 mg (alongside ampicillin-sulbactam and gentamicin) dropped the overall infection rate from 41% to 30% (p = 0.043), with significant reductions in wound infection (14% to 6%) and fungal infection (10% to 3%).<sup>[[10]](#ref10)</sup> This before/after study changed several agents simultaneously and cannot isolate a fluconazole effect. It supports reviewing local microbiology, not routine antifungal prophylaxis after every cystectomy; prior azole exposure can increase resistance concerns.

## Renal aspergillosis

Rare and typically from hematogenous dissemination in immunocompromised patients. Per IDSA aspergillosis guidelines:<sup>[[11]](#ref11)</sup>

- Combined medical and urologic management is required
- **Parenchymal disease:** voriconazole is first-line for invasive aspergillosis; tissue is the target, not urine, so voriconazole's poor urinary excretion is not limiting
- **Renal pelvis disease / aspergillus fungus balls:** local instillation of AmB deoxycholate via nephrostomy is a specialist means of achieving high local concentrations; no role in parenchymal disease
- **Ureteral obstruction:** decompress with nephrostomy or stent
- **Larger abscesses** may require surgical drainage; nephrectomy is a last resort for refractory parenchymal disease

## Rare genitourinary fungal infections

- **Candida prostatitis, epididymitis, orchitis.** Rare; occur in diabetic, immunosuppressed, or recently instrumented patients. Candida epididymo-orchitis may require drainage or orchiectomy for definitive diagnosis, though oral fluconazole alone has been effective in reported cases.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[12]](#ref12)</sup>
- **Endemic mycoses** (blastomycosis, histoplasmosis, coccidioidomycosis). Rare GU involvement of prostate, epididymis, testis. Communicate with microbiology early so specimens are processed correctly (extended incubation, dedicated fungal media).<sup>[[13]](#ref13)</sup> Treatment follows endemic-mycosis guidelines: typically itraconazole for mild / moderate disease or amphotericin B for severe / disseminated disease.

## Evidence Summary

| Question | Evidence and limitation |
|---|---|
| Candida UTI and fungus balls | IDSA guidance; most urinary recommendations rely on low-quality evidence, with moderate-quality evidence for susceptible Candida cystitis.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup> |
| Funguria before urinary surgery | AUA BPS defines the procedures that warrant prophylaxis (consensus statement); IDSA supplies drug and duration (low-quality evidence).<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[5]](#ref5)</sup> |
| IPP antifungal prophylaxis | Large retrospective PUMP cohort; association rather than randomized treatment effect.<sup>[[7]](#ref7)</sup> |
| Radical cystectomy | Single-center before/after multicomponent prophylaxis change.<sup>[[10]](#ref10)</sup> |
| Renal aspergillosis | IDSA weak recommendation based on low-quality evidence.<sup>[[11]](#ref11)</sup> |

## Clinical Positioning

- **Confirm infection and site before treating candiduria.** Catheter management and relief of obstruction often matter more than an antifungal prescription.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[3]](#ref3)</sup>
- Use the separate cystitis and pyelonephritis tables above; flucytosine is not an option for C. krusei. Resistant organisms and fungus balls merit infectious-disease input.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup>
- Choose tissue-active therapy for candidemia with renal involvement or renal aspergillosis rather than relying on urine penetration alone.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[11]](#ref11)</sup>
- Review renal dosing, QT/CYP interactions and liver toxicity before fluconazole. For repeated dosing, the US label gives a loading dose of 50–400 mg, then reduces the daily dose to 50% at CrCl ≤50 mL/min without dialysis; patients on hemodialysis receive 100% of the dose after each session (a 3-hour session lowers plasma levels by about 50%).<sup>[[15]](#ref15)</sup> The label contraindicates co-administration with QT-prolonging CYP3A4 substrates (erythromycin, pimozide, quinidine) and advises caution with hypokalemia or heart failure. Fluconazole also raises phenytoin, sulfonylurea and warfarin effect, so check the medication list first.<sup>[[15]](#ref15)</sup> The label reports 50–200 mg daily for Candida UTI in small noncomparative studies; the 200 mg (3 mg/kg) IDSA dose sits at the top of that range.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[15]](#ref15)</sup> Flucytosine requires renal adjustment and close blood-count/liver monitoring; amphotericin requires renal/electrolyte monitoring.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup>
- Keep implant prophylaxis distinct from treatment of an established fungal device infection. Observational prophylaxis findings do not replace source control or culture-directed treatment.
