# UTI Suppressive and Prophylactic Therapy

**Recurrent urinary tract infection (rUTI)**, defined as ≥2 symptomatic, culture-confirmed UTIs in 6 months or ≥3 in 12 months, is one of the most common chronic problems in functional urology and urogynecology. The overarching goal of prophylaxis is to improve quality of life while minimizing antimicrobial exposure.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[2]](#ref2)</sup> The AUA/CUA/SUFU 2025 guideline supports shared selection of antibiotic and non-antibiotic prevention; methenamine is now a conditional option for eligible women with rUTI. It does not require every patient to complete a fixed sequence before antibiotic prophylaxis. These AUA recommendations concern recurrent localized cystitis in women without complicating factors; pregnancy, catheterization, neurogenic dysfunction and reconstructed urinary tracts require population-specific guidance.<sup>[[21]](#ref21)</sup>

Treat an active symptomatic infection before beginning methenamine prophylaxis. In an otherwise uncomplicated, nonpregnant woman whose symptoms resolve, do not require a routine negative test-of-cure culture. Reassess and culture persistent or recurrent symptoms.<sup>[[21]](#ref21)[[22]](#ref22)</sup>

For adjacent topics, see [UTI treatment antibiotics](/docs/foundations/pharmacology/infection-prophylaxis/uti-treatment-antibiotics), [Non-antibiotic UTI prevention](/docs/foundations/pharmacology/infection-prophylaxis/non-antibiotic-uti-prevention), and [Recurrent UTI (clinical)](/docs/special-populations/05e-womens-health/recurrent-uti).

## Prevention framework

Use this as a menu for individualized prevention, not a mandatory treatment ladder. Consider non-antibiotic options and stewardship while matching therapy to recurrence pattern, eligibility, tolerability, and preferences.<sup>[[4]](#ref4)[[5]](#ref5)[[6]](#ref6)[[21]](#ref21)</sup>

| Strategy | Interventions |
|---|---|
| **Behavioral / non-pharmacologic** | Consider increased water for low-volume drinkers; review spermicide use and relevant voiding/bowel dysfunction |
| **Non-antibiotic prophylaxis** | Vaginal estrogen (AUA 2025: recommend for peri- and postmenopausal women with rUTI and no contraindication; Moderate Recommendation, Grade B)<sup>[[21]](#ref21)</sup>, methenamine hippurate, cranberry proanthocyanidins |
| **Self-start / symptomatic therapy** | Pre-prescribed treatment course for patients who can reliably self-diagnose |
| **Antibiotic prophylaxis** | Post-coital single dose or continuous low dose, selected by recurrence pattern |
| **Immunoprophylaxis (where available)** | OM-89, MV140, Urovac — not FDA-approved in the US |

## Non-antibiotic prophylaxis

### Vaginal estrogen — first-line in postmenopausal women

Vaginal estrogen restores Lactobacillus colonization, lowers vaginal pH, and reduces uropathogen colonization, which is the mechanistic basis of GSM-driven rUTI. Available as vaginal cream, tablets or inserts (e.g., estradiol 10 µg tablets), and the estradiol ring. An older meta-analysis found an imprecise effect (RR 0.42; 95% CI 0.16–1.10).<sup>[[7]](#ref7)</sup> The AUA/CUA/SUFU 2025 guideline recommends vaginal estrogen for eligible peri- and postmenopausal women with rUTI (Moderate Recommendation; Evidence Level: Grade B).<sup>[[21]](#ref21)</sup> Use the [estrogen hub](/docs/foundations/pharmacology/hormonal-therapies/vaginal-topical-estrogen) for contemporary evidence and product-specific safety.

**Systemic estrogen should not be used for UTI prevention.**<sup>[[4]](#ref4)</sup> See [GSM](/docs/special-populations/05e-womens-health/gsm) for the full treatment framework.

### Methenamine hippurate — AUA-endorsed alternative to daily antibiotics

Methenamine is an option for women seeking to reduce daily antibiotic exposure (AUA 2025 Conditional Recommendation, Grade C). The US label limits use to prophylaxis after the infection has been eradicated with other antimicrobials, dosed at 1 g twice daily in adults, and contraindicates it in renal insufficiency, severe hepatic insufficiency, severe dehydration and concurrent sulfonamide therapy.<sup>[[29]](#ref29)</sup> Do not routinely pair it with vitamin C. Use the [methenamine prescribing section](/docs/foundations/pharmacology/infection-prophylaxis/non-antibiotic-uti-prevention#methenamine-hippurate) for the regimen, urine-pH and label distinction, and contraindication check.<sup>[[21]](#ref21)</sup>

The ALTAR trial (Harding 2022, *BMJ*, multicenter open-label non-inferiority RCT) randomized 240 women with rUTI; the primary modified intention-to-treat analysis included 205 (103 methenamine, 102 antibiotic) with at least 6 months of data.<sup>[[8]](#ref8)[[9]](#ref9)</sup>

- Methenamine hippurate 1 g BID versus daily low-dose antibiotics (nitrofurantoin 50–100 mg, trimethoprim 100 mg, or cefalexin 250 mg) over 12 months
- During treatment, symptomatic, antibiotic-treated UTIs occurred at 1.38 versus 0.89 episodes/person-year (absolute difference 0.49; 90% CI 0.15–0.84), within the predefined non-inferiority margin of 1 UTI/person-year<sup>[[8]](#ref8)</sup>
- During treatment, resistance to at least one antibiotic in perineal-swab *E. coli* was found in 46/64 (72%) tested antibiotic-arm participants versus 39/70 (56%) tested methenamine-arm participants (p = 0.05).<sup>[[9]](#ref9)</sup>
- The one-UTI/person-year non-inferiority margin does not imply identical recurrence rates. After treatment, multidrug-resistant *E. coli* in perineal swabs was found in 9/45 (20%) tested methenamine-arm participants versus 2/39 (5%) tested antibiotic-arm participants (p = 0.06).<sup>[[9]](#ref9)</sup>

**ImpresU trial** (Heltveit-Olsen 2025, *CMI*, triple-blind placebo-controlled phase IV RCT, 289 recruited / 281 analyzed women ≥70 y):<sup>[[10]](#ref10)</sup>

- During the 6-month course, methenamine reduced antibiotic-treated UTIs versus placebo by approximately 25% (IRR 0.75; 95% CI 0.57–1.0; p = 0.049).<sup>[[10]](#ref10)</sup>
- **After discontinuation:** During the 6-month follow-up, antibiotic-treated UTI incidence was higher in the former methenamine group than in the placebo group (IRR 1.7; 95% CI 1.3–2.3), suggesting that the treatment-period benefit did not persist.<sup>[[10]](#ref10)</sup>

**Emerging caveat:** the *frmRAB* operon can confer formaldehyde resistance in some *E. coli* strains; the clinical impact is under investigation.<sup>[[11]](#ref11)</sup>

### Cranberry products

The AUA 2025 guideline recommends offering cranberry as an option (Moderate, Grade B). Declared PAC content helps product selection, but a ≥36 mg threshold does not guarantee efficacy. WikiGuidelines also discusses selected populations.<sup>[[6]](#ref6)</sup> Meta-analysis: RR 0.53 (95% CI 0.33–0.83) for recurrence.<sup>[[7]](#ref7)</sup> Evidence is insufficient in older adults, patients with bladder-emptying problems, and pregnancy.<sup>[[6]](#ref6)</sup>

### D-mannose — negative in the largest rigorous RCT

D-mannose binds type-1 fimbriae and theoretically blocks *E. coli* adherence to the urothelium. Small trials suggested benefit, but the MERIT RCT (Hayward 2024, *JAMA Intern Med*) randomized 598 women in primary care with rUTI and was negative: among 583 with primary-outcome data, 150/294 (51.0%) assigned D-mannose versus 161/289 (55.7%) assigned placebo contacted ambulatory care for a clinically suspected UTI over 6 months.<sup>[[12]](#ref12)</sup> The authors explicitly concluded D-mannose should not be recommended to prevent future UTIs in this population.

### Increased water intake

One RCT showed benefit from an additional 1.5 L of water daily in healthy premenopausal women who initially drank less than 1.5 L/day.<sup>[[6]](#ref6)</sup> Offer increased intake to low-volume drinkers when appropriate; do not prescribe the same extra volume to every patient or override fluid restrictions.

### Probiotics

Evidence for vaginal or oral *Lactobacillus* products is heterogeneous across strains, routes, and populations, with uncertain preventive benefit; a generic probiotic is not an established adjunct to vaginal estrogen.<sup>[[5]](#ref5)[[6]](#ref6)</sup>

## Antibiotic prophylaxis

For women meeting rUTI criteria, antibiotic prophylaxis may be chosen after discussing benefits, harms, and alternatives; prior failure of every non-antibiotic option is not an AUA requirement.<sup>[[21]](#ref21)</sup> A systematic review and meta-analysis of 11 placebo-controlled trials (Jent 2022) showed antibiotic prophylaxis reduces UTI risk by 85% (RR 0.15; 95% CI 0.08–0.29), with no significant efficacy difference among nitrofurantoin, trimethoprim (± sulfamethoxazole), and norfloxacin in head-to-head comparisons.<sup>[[13]](#ref13)</sup> Study quality was low, and most placebo-controlled trials predate 1995, so present-day resistance may make prophylaxis less effective.<sup>[[13]](#ref13)[[21]](#ref21)</sup> Benefit is confined to the treatment period; UTI frequency returns to baseline after stopping. EAU 2026 reserves continuous or post-coital prophylaxis for women in whom non-antimicrobial measures have failed (Strong), a narrower sequence than AUA 2025. After counseling, EAU says to offer either continuous low-dose or post-coital prophylaxis once behavioral and non-antimicrobial measures have failed, choosing by whether episodes follow intercourse and by patient preference. It found no difference in efficacy between the two approaches, no evidence on the best duration (trials ran 3 to 12 months), and recurrence after stopping, especially with three or more infections a year. EAU regimens are nitrofurantoin 50 or 100 mg daily, fosfomycin trometamol 3 g weekly and trimethoprim 100 mg daily, with cephalexin 125 or 250 mg or cefaclor 250 mg daily in pregnancy; agent choice follows local resistance. EAU also strongly supports self-administered short-course therapy for compliant patients.<sup>[[21]](#ref21)[[24]](#ref24)</sup>

NICE NG112 (UK; last updated December 2024) uses a stepwise order for nonpregnant women: behavioral and hygiene advice first, then vaginal estrogen, then single-dose antibiotic prophylaxis for an identifiable trigger, then methenamine hippurate as an alternative to daily antibiotics, and a trial of daily antibiotics only if these fail. It advises reviewing daily prophylaxis at least every 6 months and choosing the agent from recent culture results and local resistance data, rotating agents by local policy and using a different antibiotic from the one used to treat the acute infection.<sup>[[28]](#ref28)</sup> NICE lists trimethoprim and nitrofurantoin (when eGFR is 45 mL/min or higher) as first-choice oral agents and amoxicillin or cefalexin as second choice.<sup>[[28]](#ref28)</sup>

### Continuous prophylaxis

An initial time-limited course with planned reassessment is appropriate. Most trial evidence covers 6–12 months; continuing prophylaxis for years is not established by that evidence.<sup>[[21]](#ref21)</sup>

| Agent | Typical continuous dose | Practical notes |
|---|---|---|
| **Nitrofurantoin** | 50–100 mg nightly | Use only when organism and patient are suitable; avoid in chronic lung disease and monitor pulmonary, hepatic and neuropathic toxicity. The historically reported 0.001% pulmonary and 0.0003% hepatic estimates do not establish risk during prolonged prophylaxis. In an audit of 3,400 older users, 5 (0.15%) had highly suspicious serious lung or liver reactions; long-term exposure was more common among these cases. Monitor throughout suppression because lung injury can occur early or after many months.<sup>[[21]](#ref21)</sup> Modern guidance often uses CrCl 30, while US labeling remains more restrictive; see treatment hub<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[2]](#ref2)</sup> |
| **TMP-SMX** | 40/200 mg nightly or 3×/week | Use prior culture susceptibility; check hyperkalemia, renal function and interactions. The 20% threshold concerns empirical acute treatment, not a rule overriding an individual susceptible isolate<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[2]](#ref2)</sup> |
| **Trimethoprim** (monotherapy) | 100 mg nightly | May avoid the sulfonamide component, but trimethoprim-related hyperkalemia / renal effects remain. NICE also warns of first-trimester teratogenic risk (folate antagonist)<sup>[[3]](#ref3)[[28]](#ref28)</sup> |
| **Cephalexin** | 125–250 mg nightly | ALTAR comparator (250 mg); NICE lists 125 mg at night as a second-choice option<sup>[[8]](#ref8)[[28]](#ref28)</sup> |
| **Fosfomycin** | 3 g PO every 10 days (AUA 2025)<sup>[[21]](#ref21)</sup>; EAU 2026 lists 3 g once weekly<sup>[[24]](#ref24)</sup> | Limited prophylaxis trial data; guideline intervals differ |

### Post-coital prophylaxis

For women whose UTIs are temporally related to intercourse, a single dose taken before or after intercourse is an effective option that may reduce total exposure; comparative studies have not shown a significant difference from continuous dosing.<sup>[[2]](#ref2)[[3]](#ref3)[[13]](#ref13)</sup>

| Agent | Post-coital dose |
|---|---|
| **TMP-SMX** | 40/200 mg or 80/400 mg single dose<sup>[[21]](#ref21)</sup> |
| **Nitrofurantoin** | 50–100 mg single dose |
| **Cephalexin** | 250 mg single dose |

NICE gives single trigger-exposure doses of trimethoprim 200 mg, nitrofurantoin 100 mg, amoxicillin 500 mg and cefalexin 500 mg, and advises returning for review within 6 months.<sup>[[28]](#ref28)</sup>

### Self-start (patient-initiated) therapy

AUA 2025 allows patient-initiated treatment for select patients with rUTI (Conditional, Grade C). Selected reliable patients may use a pre-agreed short treatment course while awaiting urine-culture results; obtain a specimen before treatment when feasible and adjust to results. Eligibility is not defined by a ≤2-UTI/year cutoff. Include instructions for fever, flank pain, pregnancy, persistent symptoms and other reasons for reassessment. Self-start dosing is not a substitute for prophylaxis in every patient: in one small crossover trial (n = 38), intermittent self-administered TMP-SMX was followed by more UTIs than daily TMP-SMX.<sup>[[2]](#ref2)[[3]](#ref3)[[21]](#ref21)</sup>

## Immunoprophylaxis

These products are not FDA-approved in the US. Current EAU guidance limits immunomodulatory prophylaxis to a well-regulated clinical trial (Weak); older regional recommendations should be read in that context.<sup>[[24]](#ref24)</sup>

- **OM-89 (Uro-Vaxom).** Oral immunostimulant containing lyophilized *E. coli* lysate. Meta-analysis of 4 trials (n = 891) showed RR 0.61 (95% CI 0.48–0.78) for experiencing at least one UTI during prophylaxis; the review reported a good safety profile.<sup>[[7]](#ref7)</sup> Grade A recommendation in the Latin American rUTI consensus.<sup>[[7]](#ref7)[[14]](#ref14)</sup>
- **MV140 (Uromune).** Sublingual polybacterial preparation. A frail-elderly cohort is observational. A completed 240-woman placebo-controlled RCT reported a secondary UTI-free outcome in 215 women in its protocol-defined primary analysis population: during the nine-month efficacy period after the first three months of intervention, 39/70 (56%) in the three-month MV140 group and 40/69 (58%) in the six-month MV140 group were UTI-free versus 19/76 (25%) with placebo; it was industry-funded and does not establish efficacy in every complex population.<sup>[[23]](#ref23)</sup><sup>[[15]](#ref15)</sup>
- **Urovac.** Vaginal mucosal vaccine; low-certainty pooled evidence found lower recurrence at 20 weeks (263 participants, RR 0.75; 95% CI 0.63–0.89), with a larger apparent effect after booster dosing.<sup>[[16]](#ref16)</sup>
- **Systematic review** (Aziminia 2019, 10 RCTs, 1,537 participants overall; 1,495 contributed to the pooled recurrence outcome): vaccines reduced rUTI versus placebo (RR 0.74; 95% CI 0.67–0.81), with low-quality evidence and substantial heterogeneity.<sup>[[16]](#ref16)</sup>

## Special populations

### Pregnancy

After treatment of a recurrent UTI during pregnancy (at least two UTIs diagnosed in that pregnancy), ACOG 2023 says clinicians may consider suppression for the remainder of pregnancy with a once-daily susceptible agent; evidence is limited. Common regimens are nitrofurantoin 100 mg orally daily or cephalexin 250–500 mg orally daily, selected by culture and pregnancy-specific safety.<sup>[[17]](#ref17)</sup>

### Kidney transplant recipients

TMP-SMX used for *Pneumocystis* prophylaxis after transplantation can also reduce UTI; coordinate any change with the transplant team.<sup>[[19]](#ref19)</sup> Do not layer methenamine onto TMP-SMX; the US methenamine label excludes concurrent sulfonamides and renal insufficiency. See the [methenamine safety section](/docs/foundations/pharmacology/infection-prophylaxis/non-antibiotic-uti-prevention#methenamine-hippurate).<sup>[[22]](#ref22)</sup> IDSA 2019 recommends against routine screening or treatment of asymptomatic bacteriuria more than one month after renal transplantation (strong recommendation; high-quality evidence) and finds evidence insufficient in the first month.<sup>[[18]](#ref18)</sup> EAU 2026 gives a Strong recommendation against screening or treating asymptomatic bacteriuria in renal transplant recipients without an early-month carveout; reconcile early postoperative decisions with the transplant team.<sup>[[24]](#ref24)</sup>

### Spinal cord injury / neurogenic bladder

Reassess emptying, bladder pressures, stones and catheter practice. AUA/SUFU advises against daily prophylaxis with an indwelling catheter (Strong) or with CIC without recurrent UTI (Moderate). For CIC users with recurrent UTI, oral prophylaxis may be offered after discussing antimicrobial resistance (Conditional, Grade C); bladder instillation is an Expert Opinion option. EAU 2026 is more cautious: avoid continuous long-term antibiotics for rUTI (Strong), and offer weekly-cycled prophylaxis only to selected patients with SCI who perform intermittent catheterization and have recurrent UTI.<sup>[[20]](#ref20)[[25]](#ref25)[[27]](#ref27)</sup>

The AnTIC RCT randomized 404 adults using intermittent self-catheterization (361 in the primary analysis): prophylaxis reduced antibiotic-treated symptomatic UTIs from 2.6 to 1.3/person-year (IRR 0.52), but resistant urinary isolates increased. This mixed catheterizing population supports individualized benefit–harm decisions, not prophylaxis for all SCI or catheterized patients.<sup>[[26]](#ref26)</sup>

### Post-reconstruction / diversion patients

- **Continent cutaneous diversions, neobladder, augmentation cystoplasty.** Colonization is expected; do not screen for or treat asymptomatic bacteriuria during routine follow-up. IDSA 2019 recommends screening and treating bacteriuria before endoscopic urologic procedures expected to cause mucosal trauma (strong recommendation; moderate-quality evidence); it suggests culture-directed treatment (weak recommendation; very-low-quality evidence) with one or two perioperative doses beginning 30–60 minutes before the procedure (weak recommendation; low-quality evidence).<sup>[[18]](#ref18)[[24]](#ref24)</sup>
- **Recurrent symptomatic UTI** in diversion patients should trigger evaluation for stones, retained mucus, incomplete emptying, or obstructed efferent limb before escalating antibiotic strategy
- **IPP and AUS patients** need syndrome- and culture-directed UTI treatment; device presence alone does not mandate broader antibiotics or suppression. Assess suspected device infection separately

## Monitoring and duration

- Agree a time-limited course and review date; studies of continuous antimicrobial prophylaxis for recurrent cystitis have lasted 3–12 months, with no established optimal duration.<sup>[[2]](#ref2)[[3]](#ref3)[[24]](#ref24)</sup>
- Do not present multi-year prophylaxis as proven safe or resistance-free; continued use requires individualized reassessment.<sup>[[21]](#ref21)</sup>
- Benefit may diminish after stopping; ImpresU found more antibiotic-treated episodes after discontinuation, which does not by itself justify indefinite therapy.<sup>[[6]](#ref6)[[10]](#ref10)</sup>
- Periodic reassessment of strategy, including de-escalation to non-antibiotic prophylaxis or symptomatic self-start therapy, is appropriate.<sup>[[2]](#ref2)</sup>
- Screen for emerging nitrofurantoin pulmonary or hepatic toxicity in patients on long-term suppression; audit TMP-SMX recipients for hyperkalemia and renal function. In a meta-analysis cited by AUA 2025, antibiotics prescribed for UTI were associated with resistance to the prescribed antibiotic in patients' urinary bacteria (five studies, 14,348 participants; pooled OR 2.5 within two months and 1.33 within 12 months).<sup>[[21]](#ref21)</sup>

## Clinical Positioning

- **Discuss eligible non-antibiotic options early** and select a prevention strategy together; use the prescribing hub for the methenamine regimen and safety checks.<sup>[[6]](#ref6)[[21]](#ref21)</sup>
- Methenamine met ALTAR's prespecified non-inferiority margin in women with rUTI. Check population applicability and contraindications; this is not a blanket recommendation for catheterized, neurogenic, or renal-insufficient patients.<sup>[[8]](#ref8)[[9]](#ref9)[[22]](#ref22)</sup>
- D-mannose evidence no longer supports routine use. The 2024 *JAMA Intern Med* MERIT trial is the largest and most rigorous data point and is negative.<sup>[[12]](#ref12)</sup>
- **For coitus-related rUTI, post-coital prophylaxis** may reduce antibiotic exposure while preventing recurrence.<sup>[[13]](#ref13)</sup>
- **Self-start therapy** is an option for selected reliable patients with a culture and follow-up plan.<sup>[[2]](#ref2)</sup>
- **Set a review date.** Reassess prevention benefit, toxicity, and recurrent episodes; benefit may diminish after treatment ends and evidence does not establish multi-year safety.<sup>[[10]](#ref10)[[21]](#ref21)</sup>
- Nitrofurantoin and TMP-SMX are common prophylactic options. Avoid fluoroquinolones for routine rUTI prophylaxis because of potential disabling tendon, peripheral-nerve and CNS effects, psychiatric symptoms (anxiety, confusion, hallucinations), QT prolongation, seizures and *C. difficile*. The US boxed warning covers the tendon, nerve and CNS harms plus exacerbation of myasthenia gravis; aortic aneurysm/dissection risk appears separately under Warnings and Precautions. In patients at increased aortic risk, reserve fluoroquinolones for situations with no alternative antibacterial treatment.<sup>[[21]](#ref21)[[30]](#ref30)[[31]](#ref31)[[32]](#ref32)</sup>
- Do not treat asymptomatic bacteriuria in transplant recipients more than one month after transplantation, in SCI patients or in catheter patients outside the IDSA 2019 exceptions. IDSA does not address diversions; the same principle is applied to them by extension. Treatment is not shown to prevent symptomatic UTI and promotes antimicrobial resistance.<sup>[[18]](#ref18)[[20]](#ref20)</sup>
- In neurogenic patients, investigate correctable causes and match prophylaxis to the catheter and recurrence setting. CIC with recurrent UTI differs from asymptomatic bacteriuria or an indwelling catheter.<sup>[[20]](#ref20)</sup>
- Immunoprophylaxis remains investigational in the US. Review current jurisdictional availability and trial evidence rather than applying older regional grades universally.<sup>[[14]](#ref14)[[15]](#ref15)[[16]](#ref16)</sup>
