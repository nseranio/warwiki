# UTI Suppressive and Prophylactic Therapy

**Recurrent urinary tract infection (rUTI)**, defined as ≥2 symptomatic, culture-confirmed UTIs in 6 months or ≥3 in 12 months, is one of the most common chronic problems in functional urology and urogynecology. The overarching goal of prophylaxis is to improve quality of life while minimizing antimicrobial exposure.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[2]](#ref2)</sup> The AUA/CUA/SUFU 2025 guideline supports shared selection of antibiotic and non-antibiotic prevention; methenamine is now a conditional option for eligible women with rUTI. It does not require every patient to complete a fixed sequence before antibiotic prophylaxis.<sup>[[21]](#ref21)</sup>

Treat an active symptomatic infection before beginning methenamine prophylaxis. In an otherwise uncomplicated, nonpregnant woman whose symptoms resolve, do not require a routine negative test-of-cure culture. Reassess and culture persistent or recurrent symptoms.<sup>[[21]](#ref21)[[22]](#ref22)</sup>

For adjacent topics, see [UTI treatment antibiotics](/docs/foundations/pharmacology/infection-prophylaxis/uti-treatment-antibiotics), [Non-antibiotic UTI prevention](/docs/foundations/pharmacology/infection-prophylaxis/non-antibiotic-uti-prevention), and [Recurrent UTI (clinical)](/docs/special-populations/05e-womens-health/recurrent-uti).

## Prevention framework

Use this as a menu for individualized prevention, not a mandatory treatment ladder. Consider non-antibiotic options and stewardship while matching therapy to recurrence pattern, eligibility, tolerability, and preferences.<sup>[[4]](#ref4)[[5]](#ref5)[[6]](#ref6)[[21]](#ref21)</sup>

| Strategy | Interventions |
|---|---|
| **Behavioral / non-pharmacologic** | Consider increased water for low-volume drinkers; review spermicide use and relevant voiding/bowel dysfunction |
| **Non-antibiotic prophylaxis** | Vaginal estrogen (first-line in postmenopausal women), methenamine hippurate, cranberry proanthocyanidins |
| **Self-start / symptomatic therapy** | Pre-prescribed treatment course for patients who can reliably self-diagnose |
| **Antibiotic prophylaxis** | Post-coital single dose or continuous low dose, selected by recurrence pattern |
| **Immunoprophylaxis (where available)** | OM-89, MV140, Urovac — not FDA-approved in the US |

## Non-antibiotic prophylaxis

### Vaginal estrogen — first-line in postmenopausal women

Vaginal estrogen restores Lactobacillus colonization, lowers vaginal pH, and reduces uropathogen colonization, which is the mechanistic basis of GSM-driven rUTI. Available as vaginal cream, tablets or inserts (e.g., estradiol 10 µg tablets), and the estradiol ring. An older meta-analysis found an imprecise effect (RR 0.42; 95% CI 0.16–1.10); current AUA guidance recommends vaginal estrogen in eligible peri- and postmenopausal women. Use the [estrogen hub](/docs/foundations/pharmacology/hormonal-therapies/vaginal-topical-estrogen) for contemporary evidence and product-specific safety.<sup>[[7]](#ref7)</sup>

**Systemic estrogen should not be used for UTI prevention.**<sup>[[4]](#ref4)</sup> See [GSM](/docs/special-populations/05e-womens-health/gsm) for the full treatment framework.

### Methenamine hippurate — AUA-endorsed alternative to daily antibiotics

Methenamine is an option for women seeking to reduce daily antibiotic exposure (AUA 2025 Conditional Recommendation, Grade C). Do not routinely pair it with vitamin C. Use the [methenamine prescribing section](/docs/foundations/pharmacology/infection-prophylaxis/non-antibiotic-uti-prevention#methenamine-hippurate) for the regimen, urine-pH and label distinction, and contraindication check.<sup>[[21]](#ref21)</sup>

The ALTAR trial (Harding 2022, *BMJ*, multicenter open-label non-inferiority RCT, n = 240 women with rUTI):<sup>[[8]](#ref8)[[9]](#ref9)</sup>

- Methenamine hippurate 1 g BID versus daily low-dose antibiotics (nitrofurantoin 50–100 mg, trimethoprim 100 mg, or cefalexin 250 mg) over 12 months
- Incidence 1.38 versus 0.89 episodes/person-year (absolute difference 0.49), within the predefined non-inferiority margin of 1 UTI/person-year
- Antibiotic resistance in *E. coli* from perineal swabs was significantly higher in the antibiotic arm (72% versus 56%; p = 0.05) during treatment
- Non-inferiority used a one-UTI/person-year margin; it does not establish identical recurrence rates. Resistance findings depended on timing: post-treatment multidrug resistance was numerically higher with methenamine (20% versus 5%; p = 0.06).

**ImpresU trial** (Heltveit-Olsen 2025, *CMI*, triple-blind placebo-controlled phase IV RCT, 289 recruited / 281 analyzed women ≥70 y):<sup>[[10]](#ref10)</sup>

- 6-month course: methenamine reduced antibiotic-treated UTIs by approximately 25% (IRR 0.75; 95% CI 0.57–1.0; p = 0.049)
- **Rebound effect after discontinuation:** UTI incidence rose in the methenamine group (IRR 1.7; 95% CI 1.3–2.3), suggesting prophylactic benefit is confined to the treatment period

**Emerging caveat:** the *frmRAB* operon can confer formaldehyde resistance in some *E. coli* strains; the clinical impact is under investigation.<sup>[[11]](#ref11)</sup>

### Cranberry products

The AUA 2025 guideline recommends offering cranberry as an option (Moderate, Grade B). Declared PAC content helps product selection, but a ≥36 mg threshold does not guarantee efficacy. WikiGuidelines also discusses selected populations.<sup>[[6]](#ref6)</sup> Meta-analysis: RR 0.53 (95% CI 0.33–0.83) for recurrence.<sup>[[7]](#ref7)</sup> Evidence is insufficient in older adults, patients with bladder-emptying problems, and pregnancy.<sup>[[6]](#ref6)</sup>

### D-mannose — negative in the largest rigorous RCT

D-mannose binds type-1 fimbriae and theoretically blocks *E. coli* adherence to the urothelium. Small trials suggested benefit, but the MERIT RCT (Hayward 2024, *JAMA Intern Med*, n = 598 women in primary care with rUTI) was negative: 51.0% of D-mannose patients versus 55.7% of placebo patients experienced a medically attended UTI over 6 months.<sup>[[12]](#ref12)</sup> The authors explicitly concluded D-mannose should not be recommended to prevent future UTIs in this population.

### Increased water intake

One RCT showed benefit from an additional 1.5 L of water daily in healthy premenopausal women who initially drank less than 1.5 L/day.<sup>[[6]](#ref6)</sup> Offer increased intake to low-volume drinkers when appropriate; do not prescribe the same extra volume to every patient or override fluid restrictions.

### Probiotics

Evidence for vaginal or oral *Lactobacillus* products is heterogeneous across strains, routes, and populations, with uncertain preventive benefit; a generic probiotic is not an established adjunct to vaginal estrogen.<sup>[[5]](#ref5)[[6]](#ref6)</sup>

## Antibiotic prophylaxis

For women meeting rUTI criteria, antibiotic prophylaxis may be chosen after discussing benefits, harms, and alternatives; prior failure of every non-antibiotic option is not an AUA requirement.<sup>[[21]](#ref21)</sup> A systematic review and meta-analysis of 11 placebo-controlled trials (Jent 2022) showed antibiotic prophylaxis reduces UTI risk by 85% (RR 0.15; 95% CI 0.08–0.29), with no significant efficacy difference among nitrofurantoin, trimethoprim (± sulfamethoxazole), and norfloxacin in head-to-head comparisons. Study quality was low, and most placebo-controlled trials predate 1995, so present-day resistance may make prophylaxis less effective.<sup>[[13]](#ref13)[[21]](#ref21)</sup> Benefit is confined to the treatment period; UTI frequency returns to baseline after stopping. EAU 2026 reserves continuous or post-coital prophylaxis for women in whom non-antimicrobial measures have failed (Strong), a narrower sequence than AUA 2025. After counseling, EAU says to offer either continuous low-dose or post-coital prophylaxis once behavioral and non-antimicrobial measures have failed, choosing by whether episodes follow intercourse and by patient preference. It found no difference in efficacy between the two approaches, no evidence on the best duration (trials ran 3 to 12 months), and recurrence after stopping, especially with three or more infections a year. EAU regimens are nitrofurantoin 50 or 100 mg daily, fosfomycin trometamol 3 g weekly and trimethoprim 100 mg daily, with cephalexin 125 or 250 mg or cefaclor 250 mg daily in pregnancy; agent choice follows local resistance. EAU also strongly supports self-administered short-course therapy for compliant patients.<sup>[[21]](#ref21)[[24]](#ref24)</sup>

### Continuous prophylaxis

An initial time-limited course with planned reassessment is appropriate. Most trial evidence covers 6–12 months; continuing prophylaxis for years is not established by that evidence.<sup>[[21]](#ref21)</sup>

| Agent | Typical continuous dose | Practical notes |
|---|---|---|
| **Nitrofurantoin** | 50–100 mg nightly | Use only when organism and patient are suitable; avoid in chronic lung disease and monitor pulmonary, hepatic and neuropathic toxicity. Serious pulmonary and hepatic reactions are rare (reported around 0.001% and 0.0003%) but cluster in long-term users, and chronic pulmonary reactions typically appear one to six months into therapy. Modern guidance often uses CrCl 30, while US labeling remains more restrictive; see treatment hub<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[2]](#ref2)</sup> |
| **TMP-SMX** | 40/200 mg nightly or 3×/week | Use prior culture susceptibility; check hyperkalemia, renal function and interactions. The 20% threshold concerns empirical acute treatment, not a rule overriding an individual susceptible isolate<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[2]](#ref2)</sup> |
| **Trimethoprim** (monotherapy) | 100 mg nightly | May avoid the sulfonamide component, but trimethoprim-related hyperkalemia / renal effects remain<sup>[[3]](#ref3)</sup> |
| **Cephalexin** | 125–250 mg nightly | ALTAR comparator; reasonable β-lactam option<sup>[[8]](#ref8)</sup> |
| **Fosfomycin** | 3 g PO every 10 days (EAU 2026 lists 3 g once weekly) | Emerging; limited prophylaxis trial data, and no agreed interval<sup>[[24]](#ref24)</sup> |

### Post-coital prophylaxis

For women whose UTIs are temporally related to intercourse, a single dose taken before or after intercourse is an effective option that may reduce total exposure; comparative studies have not shown a significant difference from continuous dosing.<sup>[[2]](#ref2)[[3]](#ref3)[[13]](#ref13)</sup>

| Agent | Post-coital dose |
|---|---|
| **TMP-SMX** | 40/200 mg or 80/400 mg single dose |
| **Nitrofurantoin** | 50–100 mg single dose |
| **Cephalexin** | 250 mg single dose |

### Self-start (patient-initiated) therapy

AUA 2025 allows patient-initiated treatment for select patients with rUTI (Conditional, Grade C). Selected reliable patients may use a pre-agreed short treatment course while awaiting urine-culture results; obtain a specimen before treatment when feasible and adjust to results. Eligibility is not defined by a ≤2-UTI/year cutoff. Include instructions for fever, flank pain, pregnancy, persistent symptoms and other reasons for reassessment. Self-start dosing is not a substitute for prophylaxis in every patient: in one small crossover trial (n = 38), intermittent self-administered TMP-SMX was followed by more UTIs than daily TMP-SMX.<sup>[[2]](#ref2)[[3]](#ref3)[[21]](#ref21)</sup>

## Immunoprophylaxis

These products are not FDA-approved in the US. Current EAU guidance limits immunomodulatory prophylaxis to a well-regulated clinical trial (Weak); older regional recommendations should be read in that context.<sup>[[24]](#ref24)</sup>

- **OM-89 (Uro-Vaxom).** Oral immunostimulant containing lyophilized *E. coli* lysate. Meta-analysis of 4 trials (n = 891) showed RR 0.61 (95% CI 0.48–0.78) for recurrence; good safety profile. Grade A recommendation in the Latin American rUTI consensus.<sup>[[7]](#ref7)[[14]](#ref14)</sup>
- **MV140 (Uromune).** Sublingual polybacterial preparation. A frail-elderly cohort is observational. A completed 240-woman placebo-controlled RCT found 56–58% UTI-free with MV140 versus 25% with placebo during the nine-month efficacy period; it was industry-funded and does not establish efficacy in every complex population.<sup>[[23]](#ref23)</sup><sup>[[15]](#ref15)</sup>
- **Urovac.** Vaginal mucosal vaccine; RR 0.75 (95% CI 0.63–0.89) with booster-dependent efficacy.<sup>[[16]](#ref16)</sup>
- **Systematic review** (Aziminia 2019, 10 RCTs, n = 1,537): vaccines reduced rUTI versus placebo (RR 0.74; 95% CI 0.67–0.81), low-quality evidence with substantial heterogeneity.<sup>[[16]](#ref16)</sup>

## Special populations

### Pregnancy

After treating a UTI in pregnancy in a woman with prior rUTI, ACOG 2023 states clinicians may consider initiating antimicrobial suppression for the remainder of pregnancy using a once-daily low dose of a susceptible agent; preferably as a lower single daily dose. Evidence is limited.<sup>[[17]](#ref17)</sup>

### Kidney transplant recipients

TMP-SMX used for *Pneumocystis* prophylaxis after transplantation can also reduce UTI; coordinate any change with the transplant team.<sup>[[19]](#ref19)</sup> Do not layer methenamine onto TMP-SMX; the US methenamine label excludes concurrent sulfonamides and renal insufficiency. See the [methenamine safety section](/docs/foundations/pharmacology/infection-prophylaxis/non-antibiotic-uti-prevention#methenamine-hippurate).<sup>[[22]](#ref22)</sup> IDSA recommends against routine screening or treatment of asymptomatic bacteriuria more than one month after renal transplantation; evidence is insufficient within the first month.<sup>[[18]](#ref18)</sup>

### Spinal cord injury / neurogenic bladder

Reassess emptying, bladder pressures, stones and catheter practice. AUA/SUFU advises against daily prophylaxis with an indwelling catheter (Strong) or with CIC without recurrent UTI (Moderate). For CIC users with recurrent UTI, oral prophylaxis may be offered after discussing antimicrobial resistance (Conditional, Grade C); bladder instillation is an Expert Opinion option. EAU 2026 is more cautious: avoid continuous long-term antibiotics for rUTI (Strong), and offer weekly-cycled prophylaxis only to selected patients with SCI who perform intermittent catheterization and have recurrent UTI.<sup>[[20]](#ref20)[[25]](#ref25)[[27]](#ref27)</sup>

The AnTIC RCT randomized 404 adults using intermittent self-catheterization (361 in the primary analysis): prophylaxis reduced antibiotic-treated symptomatic UTIs from 2.6 to 1.3/person-year (IRR 0.52), but resistant urinary isolates increased. This mixed catheterizing population supports individualized benefit–harm decisions, not prophylaxis for all SCI or catheterized patients.<sup>[[26]](#ref26)</sup>

### Post-reconstruction / diversion patients

- **Continent cutaneous diversions, neobladder, augmentation cystoplasty.** Colonization is expected; treat only symptomatic infection
- **Recurrent symptomatic UTI** in diversion patients should trigger evaluation for stones, retained mucus, incomplete emptying, or obstructed efferent limb before escalating antibiotic strategy
- **IPP and AUS patients** need syndrome- and culture-directed UTI treatment; device presence alone does not mandate broader antibiotics or suppression. Assess suspected device infection separately

## Monitoring and duration

- Agree a time-limited course and review date; studies commonly cover 6–12 months.<sup>[[2]](#ref2)[[3]](#ref3)</sup>
- Do not present multi-year prophylaxis as proven safe or resistance-free; continued use requires individualized reassessment.<sup>[[21]](#ref21)</sup>
- Benefit may diminish after stopping; ImpresU found more antibiotic-treated episodes after discontinuation, which does not by itself justify indefinite therapy.<sup>[[6]](#ref6)[[10]](#ref10)</sup>
- Periodic reassessment of strategy, including de-escalation to non-antibiotic prophylaxis or symptomatic self-start therapy, is appropriate.<sup>[[2]](#ref2)</sup>
- Screen for emerging nitrofurantoin pulmonary or hepatic toxicity in patients on long-term suppression; audit TMP-SMX recipients for hyperkalemia and renal function. Any antibiotic course raises the odds of resistance in that patient's urinary bacteria (pooled OR about 2.5 within two months, 1.3 at 12 months).<sup>[[21]](#ref21)</sup>

## Clinical Positioning

- **Discuss eligible non-antibiotic options early** and select a prevention strategy together; use the prescribing hub for the methenamine regimen and safety checks.<sup>[[6]](#ref6)[[21]](#ref21)</sup>
- Methenamine met ALTAR's prespecified non-inferiority margin in women with rUTI. Check population applicability and contraindications; this is not a blanket recommendation for catheterized, neurogenic, or renal-insufficient patients.<sup>[[8]](#ref8)[[9]](#ref9)[[22]](#ref22)</sup>
- D-mannose evidence no longer supports routine use. The 2024 *JAMA Intern Med* MERIT trial is the largest and most rigorous data point and is negative.<sup>[[12]](#ref12)</sup>
- **For coitus-related rUTI, post-coital prophylaxis** may reduce antibiotic exposure while preventing recurrence.<sup>[[13]](#ref13)</sup>
- **Self-start therapy** is an option for selected reliable patients with a culture and follow-up plan.<sup>[[2]](#ref2)</sup>
- **Set a review date.** Reassess prevention benefit, toxicity, and recurrent episodes; benefit may diminish after treatment ends and evidence does not establish multi-year safety.<sup>[[10]](#ref10)[[21]](#ref21)</sup>
- **Nitrofurantoin and TMP-SMX are the workhorses;** fluoroquinolones are not recommended for routine rUTI prophylaxis: their harms (boxed warning for tendinitis and tendon rupture, QT prolongation, seizures, *C. difficile*) generally outweigh the benefit.<sup>[[21]](#ref21)</sup>
- Do not treat asymptomatic bacteriuria in transplant, SCI, diversion, or catheter patients outside the IDSA 2019 exceptions; it does not prevent symptomatic UTI and accelerates resistance.<sup>[[18]](#ref18)[[20]](#ref20)</sup>
- In neurogenic patients, investigate correctable causes and match prophylaxis to the catheter and recurrence setting. CIC with recurrent UTI differs from asymptomatic bacteriuria or an indwelling catheter.<sup>[[20]](#ref20)</sup>
- Immunoprophylaxis remains investigational in the US. Review current jurisdictional availability and trial evidence rather than applying older regional grades universally.<sup>[[14]](#ref14)[[15]](#ref15)[[16]](#ref16)</sup>
