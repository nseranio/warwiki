# Vitamin B12 Supplementation in Urinary Diversion

Vitamin B12 deficiency is the best-characterized long-term nutritional complication of urinary reconstruction with ileal segments, a silent, slow-onset, and partly irreversible problem that the reconstructive urologist should plan to monitor for the rest of the patient's life. Intrinsic-factor-dependent B12 absorption occurs in the terminal ileum; a small proportion of a high oral dose can also be absorbed passively elsewhere.<sup>[[4]](#ref4)[[5]](#ref5)</sup> Hepatic B12 stores can delay clinical manifestations for up to 10 years after deficiency begins;<sup>[[15]](#ref15)</sup> in an ileocolic neobladder series, one patient with malabsorptive deficiency had neurological symptoms 53 months after surgery.<sup>[[6]](#ref6)</sup> By then, neurological damage (subacute combined degeneration, peripheral neuropathy, cognitive change) may not fully reverse with repletion; monitor B12 over time and treat confirmed or clinically suspected deficiency promptly.<sup>[[5]](#ref5)[[6]](#ref6)[[14]](#ref14)</sup>

In 303 men with infertility, serum B12 was associated with total testosterone; semen-quality evidence is heterogeneous, and cross-sectional erectile-dysfunction studies report B12 associations in opposite directions.<sup>[[2]](#ref2)[[3]](#ref3)[[17]](#ref17)[[18]](#ref18)</sup> This article covers both: the diversion-surveillance story first, then the andrology adjunct.

For adjacent long-term diversion topics see [Mucus management](/docs/foundations/pharmacology/urinary-diversion-specific/mucus-management) and [Urinary acidifiers &amp; alkalinizers](/docs/foundations/pharmacology/urinary-diversion-specific/urinary-acidifiers).

## Why Ileal Diversion Causes B12 Deficiency

| Anatomic factor | Effect on B12 absorption |
|---|---|
| **Extensive terminal ileal resection or prior ileal surgery** | Increased risk of deficiency; gastroenterology guidance advises B12 monitoring in this group<sup>[[4]](#ref4)</sup> |
| **Loss of ~ 50 cm of terminal ileum** | Historical cohort: reduced absorption in **20/25 Kock pouch** patients versus **0/29 ileal-neobladder** patients; both groups included patients with low serum B12.<sup>[[7]](#ref7)</sup> In a later series, no patient with 50–60 cm of ileum had a low B12 or abnormal Schilling test, whereas patients with an ileocecal segment did<sup>[[8]](#ref8)</sup> |
| **Use of ileocecal segment** (Indiana, Mainz) | Potential loss of distal-ileal absorptive function; assess the actual bowel segment and remaining anatomy<sup>[[8]](#ref8)</sup> |
| **Colonic neobladder** (ascending, sigmoid) | Avoids direct loss of ileal absorption if ileum is preserved; other causes of B12 deficiency remain possible<sup>[[9]](#ref9)</sup> |

In Ganesan's 70-patient urinary-reconstruction series, ileocecal segment use was associated with B12 malabsorption, while no patient in the 50–60 cm ileal group had a low B12 level or abnormal Schilling test; plan surveillance around the bowel segment used.<sup>[[8]](#ref8)[[9]](#ref9)</sup>

## Historical Series by Diversion Type

These small cohorts used different tests and follow-up durations. Schilling results measure absorption, not the same outcome as a low serum level.

| Diversion | Ileal length used | Low serum B12 | Schilling-confirmed malabsorption | Typical latency |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Ileal conduit** | 15–20 cm | Uncommon in early series (0/25 below 200 pg/mL);<sup>[[11]](#ref11)</sup> five low B12 values in another series came from a combined group of ileal conduits and enterocystoplasties<sup>[[8]](#ref8)</sup> | Variable | Often years; tissue deficiency more common than serum levels suggest<sup>[[10]](#ref10)</sup> |
| **Ileal neobladder (Studer)** | 40–60 cm | 13.6%<sup>[[9]](#ref9)</sup> | Not routinely tested | 9 mo – 3 yr |
| **Kock pouch** | 70 cm distal ileum in Olofsson<sup>[[31]](#ref31)</sup> | 4/25 (16%) had low serum B12 at 8–25 mo<sup>[[7]](#ref7)</sup>; about 10% developed true deficiency in a separate 97-patient series sampled up to 18 yr<sup>[[31]](#ref31)</sup> | 20/25 (80%) had reduced absorption at 8–25 mo<sup>[[7]](#ref7)</sup>; 3/9 (33%) in another series<sup>[[11]](#ref11)</sup> | Pannek mean follow-up 13 mo; long-term measurements extend to 18 yr in Olofsson<sup>[[7]](#ref7)[[31]](#ref31)</sup> |
| **Ileocolic neobladder (including Mainz)** | Ileocecal segment | 3/18 (16.6%) in the Mainz cohort<sup>[[9]](#ref9)</sup>; 6/24 (25%) in another ileocolic cohort<sup>[[6]](#ref6)</sup> | 3/24 (13%) in the latter cohort<sup>[[6]](#ref6)</sup> | Mainz: low values at 5–6 yr<sup>[[9]](#ref9)</sup>; other cohort: follow-up 6–53 mo<sup>[[6]](#ref6)</sup> |
| **Indiana pouch** | Ileocecal segment | None below 200 pg/mL in one series<sup>[[11]](#ref11)</sup> | **67% (4/6)**<sup>[[11]](#ref11)</sup> | Variable |
| **Colonic neobladder** | 0 cm ileum | 0/39 (9 ascending, 30 sigmoid) had low serum B12<sup>[[9]](#ref9)</sup> | Not reported | No low values; study-wide follow-up 3 mo–11 yr (mean 3.4 yr)<sup>[[9]](#ref9)</sup> |
| **Pediatric ileal enterocystoplasty** | 15–25 cm | At ≥7 yr, 6/29 (21%) low and 12/29 (41%) low-normal<sup>[[12]](#ref12)</sup>; in a separate series, 16/128 (13%) low and 36/128 (28%) low or low-normal at mean follow-up 83 mo<sup>[[13]](#ref13)</sup> | Not routine | Risk rises after 7 yr |

**Serum B12 alone underestimates the true burden.** In Sagalowsky &amp; Frenkel 2002, complete cobalamin profiles (serum B12 plus MMA plus homocysteine) in 41 diversion patients showed tissue deficiency prevalence significantly higher than serum B12 alone would suggest.<sup>[[10]](#ref10)</sup>

## Clinical Manifestations — What Not to Miss

B12 deficiency produces two categories of damage with very different reversibility.<sup>[[5]](#ref5)</sup>

**Hematologic (typically reversible)**

- Megaloblastic anemia, macrocytosis, hypersegmented neutrophils, pancytopenia
- No patient in the major urologic series developed frank megaloblastic anemia; this does not prove surveillance was the cause or that anemia is required for diagnosis<sup>[[6]](#ref6)[[9]](#ref9)[[11]](#ref11)</sup>

**Neurologic (potentially irreversible)**

- **Subacute combined degeneration** of the spinal cord (dorsal and lateral column demyelination)
- Peripheral neuropathy: paresthesias, numbness, gait ataxia
- Cognitive dysfunction, dementia, psychiatric change
- One patient in Steiner et al.'s ileocolic neobladder series presented with neurologic symptoms at 53 months postoperatively, the only symptomatic case in that cohort and a cautionary tale about long latency<sup>[[6]](#ref6)</sup>
- Neurologic B12 deficiency can occur without anemia or macrocytosis; do not use a normal blood count to exclude it<sup>[[5]](#ref5)</sup>

**Other**: glossitis, infertility, venous thromboembolism (including cerebral venous sinus thrombosis).<sup>[[5]](#ref5)</sup>

**Neurologic damage may not fully reverse with repletion.** This, not anemia, is the reason annual surveillance is worth the effort.

## Monitoring and Screening

The EAU bladder cancer guideline states that B12 should be measured annually after cystectomy with bowel diversion, citing low levels in 17% of patients with bowel diversion.<sup>[[14]](#ref14)</sup> This is guideline text rather than a graded recommendation, and the panel's follow-up table records no consensus on B12 timing.<sup>[[14]](#ref14)</sup> NCCN post-cystectomy follow-up specifies annual B12 testing after the first year, based on clinical judgment (category 2A).<sup>[[26]](#ref26)</sup> The AUA/ASCO/SUO guideline discussion (Statement 31, Expert Opinion) advises assessing B12 during follow-up, because the risk of deficiency and neurologic damage is increased after resection of more than 60 cm of ileum and when the terminal ileum is used.<sup>[[25]](#ref25)</sup> Continue surveillance rather than assuming preserved serum levels early after surgery eliminate later risk. In neurogenic bowel-segment reconstruction, the AUA/SUFU guideline text (Statement 58 discussion) advises monitoring B12 over time, with supplementation if appropriate, when terminal ileum is used.<sup>[[21]](#ref21)</sup>

- Record the actual resected or bypassed ileal segment, baseline nutritional status and other causes such as dietary deficiency, gastric disease or medication use.
- Use the laboratory's total or active-B12 thresholds; consider MMA for an indeterminate result with compatible symptoms. Interpret MMA with renal function because renal impairment can raise it independently. Homocysteine is less specific.<sup>[[10]](#ref10)[[15]](#ref15)[[22]](#ref22)</sup>
- **Schilling testing is historical, not a routine contemporary diagnostic step.** Do not wait for it before treating clinically significant deficiency.
- Pediatric cohorts show rising risk with longer follow-up; plan monitoring with the pediatric reconstruction team and test earlier for symptoms, nutritional concerns or extensive ileal loss rather than treating year 5 as a prohibition on earlier testing.<sup>[[12]](#ref12)[[13]](#ref13)</sup>
- When neurologic or significant hematologic manifestations suggest deficiency, arrange prompt treatment and clinical follow-up; a low or borderline B12 value is not the only relevant outcome.<sup>[[5]](#ref5)[[22]](#ref22)</sup>

## Replacement: Choose the Route for the Cause and Severity

Parenteral replacement is appropriate for clinically important malabsorption, neurologic disease or unreliable oral response or adherence. Typical schedules use 1 mg IM three times weekly for 2 weeks without neurologic deficits; with neurologic deficits, the cited schedule is every other day for up to 3 weeks or until no further improvement is noted.<sup>[[15]](#ref15)</sup> NICE NG239 does not set an injection schedule; it defers to the product's summary of product characteristics and recommends lifelong IM replacement after complete terminal ileal resection.<sup>[[22]](#ref22)</sup> The loading and maintenance regimen depends on the selected cyanocobalamin or hydroxocobalamin product and local protocol; a monthly maintenance dose is not an adequate loading instruction for a symptomatic patient.<sup>[[4]](#ref4)[[16]](#ref16)[[22]](#ref22)</sup>

High-dose oral therapy can work through passive absorption. The 2018 Cochrane review (3 trials, 153 participants) found low-certainty evidence for comparable short-term serum-level correction with oral and IM therapy, but the review did not provide usable evidence on clinical neurologic outcomes.<sup>[[23]](#ref23)</sup>

The OB12 trial (2020; 283 adults aged ≥65 years) found oral B12 1 mg daily for 8 weeks noninferior to IM therapy for serum normalization at 8 weeks. Oral treatment then fell to 1 mg weekly; noninferiority was not established at 52 weeks. This was not a urinary-diversion trial or a test of continuing oral 1 mg daily throughout maintenance.<sup>[[24]](#ref24)</sup>

NICE's adult guideline recommends lifelong IM replacement after complete terminal ileal resection or total gastrectomy, or when autoimmune gastritis is the cause or suspected cause of vitamin B12 deficiency, and consideration of IM therapy for other malabsorption. Symptoms may begin to improve within 2 weeks but can take up to 3 months. Offer an initial follow-up at 3 months or earlier according to symptom severity, or at 1 month during pregnancy or breastfeeding; assess symptoms at follow-up and do not repeat the initial diagnostic test during IM replacement.<sup>[[22]](#ref22)</sup> If oral therapy is chosen for malabsorption, use at least 1 mg/day and assess response. A limited ileal diversion is not automatically equivalent to complete terminal ileal resection.<sup>[[22]](#ref22)</sup>

Vanderbrink's pediatric ileocystoplasty cohort reported short-term serum improvement with 250 µg/day oral therapy; it provides no adult-dose or pediatric neurologic-efficacy estimate.<sup>[[13]](#ref13)</sup> In a later cohort from the same center, 4/23 (17%) had normal serum B12 at final measurement a mean 49 months after the first abnormal result; replacement was documented in 16/23 (14 oral, 2 IM), so this is not an oral-treatment failure rate.<sup>[[27]](#ref27)</sup> Monitoring or empirical supplementation has been proposed after diversion; choose an individualized plan and continue follow-up rather than assuming a supplement eliminates deficiency risk.<sup>[[10]](#ref10)</sup>

## B12 and Male Reproductive / Sexual Health

### Erectile dysfunction

- **Xu 2021 (n = 184).** ED patients had lower B12 levels (256 versus 337.5 pg/mL; p &lt; 0.05)<sup>[[3]](#ref3)</sup>
- **Chen 2019 (n = 1,381, China).** Paradoxically found higher B12 in men with ED (718.5 versus 688.7 pg/mL; p = 0.015); higher B12 associated with mild ED (OR 1.62)<sup>[[17]](#ref17)</sup>; the cross-sectional design cannot establish direction or mechanism
- **Homocysteine is a proposed link.** B12 deficiency can raise homocysteine, and elevated homocysteine may impair endothelial nitric-oxide signaling.<sup>[[3]](#ref3)[[17]](#ref17)</sup>

### Male infertility and androgen profile

- **Rastegar Panah 2024 (n = 303 infertile men).** Serum B12 positively associated with total testosterone (ρ = 0.19; p = 0.001); highest B12 tertile had 56% lower odds of testosterone deficiency (adjusted OR 0.44; 95% CI 0.22–0.87)<sup>[[2]](#ref2)</sup>
- **Banihani 2017 review.** Describes heterogeneous clinical and preclinical evidence concerning sperm count, motility and DNA damage, with proposed pathways including reduced homocysteine toxicity, reduced oxidative stress, reduced NO-mediated damage, and anti-inflammatory effects<sup>[[18]](#ref18)</sup>
- **Mathew 2026 review.** B12 status may influence reproductive outcomes through one-carbon metabolism and oxidative-stress pathways, but causal effects on human gamete quality remain unconfirmed.<sup>[[19]](#ref19)</sup>
- B12 deficiency is listed as an uncommon cause of infertility in the NEJM clinical-practice review<sup>[[5]](#ref5)</sup>

**Practical takeaway.** Investigate B12 when symptoms or nutritional or anatomic risk justify it, particularly after ileal resection or diversion.

## Other Urologic Considerations

### Metabolic acidosis after ileal diversion

Ileal urinary diversion can lead to B12 deficiency and, through ammonium-chloride reabsorption and bicarbonate loss by bowel mucosa, hyperchloremic metabolic acidosis; chronic acidosis can contribute to bone demineralization.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup> See [Urinary acidifiers &amp; alkalinizers](/docs/foundations/pharmacology/urinary-diversion-specific/urinary-acidifiers) for oral bicarbonate dosing.

### Bone health

In a meta-analysis of four prospective studies of adults and older adults (7,475 participants), each 50 pmol/L higher B12 concentration was associated with a borderline 4% lower fracture risk (RR 0.96, 95% CI 0.92–1.00), while chronic acidosis after bowel diversion can contribute to bone demineralization; monitor B12 and acid–base status as separate follow-up needs.<sup>[[30]](#ref30)[[1]](#ref1)[[14]](#ref14)</sup>

### Pediatric considerations

- Adequate B12 is important for childhood neurodevelopment; requirements are age-specific<sup>[[12]](#ref12)</sup>
- **28% of pediatric ileocystoplasty patients** had low or low-normal B12 at mean follow-up of 83 mo<sup>[[13]](#ref13)</sup>
- **Patients after childhood bowel reconstruction.** Folate was low in 4/27 (14.8%) tested patients; assess nutritional status and counsel women of childbearing age.<sup>[[20]](#ref20)</sup> When B12 and folate deficiency coexist, replace B12 first to prevent neurologic injury.<sup>[[20]](#ref20)[[15]](#ref15)</sup>

## Practical Clinical Summary

| Scenario | Review and action |
|---|---|
| Cystectomy with bowel diversion | EAU advises annual B12 measurement in its guideline text.<sup>[[14]](#ref14)</sup> NCCN specifies annual testing after the first year based on clinical judgment (category 2A).<sup>[[26]](#ref26)</sup> Investigate and replace deficiency according to cause and severity. |
| Complete terminal ileal resection | Adult NICE guidance recommends lifelong IM replacement; distinguish this from partial ileal use.<sup>[[22]](#ref22)</sup> |
| Pediatric augmentation | For children with neurogenic lower urinary tract dysfunction after augmentation cystoplasty, the 2023 Canadian guideline recommends yearly serum chemistries including B12 and lifelong follow-up (weak recommendation, level III evidence).<sup>[[29]](#ref29)</sup> Use an age- and presentation-appropriate replacement regimen.<sup>[[12]](#ref12)[[13]](#ref13)</sup> |
| Colonic reconstruction with preserved ileum | Direct ileal loss is avoided, but this is not a blanket exemption from post-cystectomy surveillance or evaluation of other B12 risks.<sup>[[9]](#ref9)[[14]](#ref14)</sup> |
| Sexual/fertility concerns | Treat proven deficiency; current associations do not justify B12 as a stand-alone treatment in replete patients.<sup>[[2]](#ref2)[[3]](#ref3)[[17]](#ref17)[[18]](#ref18)</sup> |
