# Dantrolene

> **Category:** Pharmacology > Voiding & Outlet

---

## Overview

Dantrolene is a direct-acting skeletal-muscle relaxant. It weakens striated muscle contraction without acting on the nerves that drive it. That property made it attractive in the 1970s and 1980s for **external urethral sphincter (EUS) hypertonicity** and detrusor-sphincter dyssynergia (DSD), especially in suprasacral spinal cord injury (SCI), because the sphincter is striated and the detrusor is smooth muscle.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[2]](#ref2)</sup>

The urologic role today is historical and off-label. The evidence is a handful of small uncontrolled series from 1976 to 1980. No randomized trial has tested it for DSD. A 2025 scoping review found that all muscle-relaxant studies for DSD were published in 1980 or earlier, and noted that guidelines no longer encourage muscle relaxants for this use.<sup>[[3]](#ref3)</sup> The EAU Neuro-Urology guideline (limited update March 2026) does not mention dantrolene. It recommends alpha-blockers to lower outlet resistance and describes intrasphincteric botulinum toxin A for DSD.<sup>[[4]](#ref4)</sup>

Practical points:

- Dantrolene is rarely the right drug for outlet obstruction. Alpha-blockers, intrasphincteric botulinum toxin, sphincterotomy or a urethral stent, and catheterization are the working options (see below).
- Its main limits are generalized muscle weakness and a boxed warning for fatal and non-fatal hepatotoxicity.
- Reconstructive surgeons meet it mostly as a drug the patient is already taking for spasticity, or as the anesthesia drug for malignant hyperthermia (MH).

---

## Mechanism of Action

Dantrolene uncouples excitation from contraction in skeletal muscle. It suppresses calcium release from the sarcoplasmic reticulum through ryanodine receptors. RyR1 is the skeletal-muscle isoform.<sup>[[5]](#ref5)</sup> The label describes relaxation at a site beyond the myoneural junction, "directly on the muscle itself", with a slightly greater effect on fast fibers than slow ones.<sup>[[6]](#ref6)</sup>

Two consequences matter for the urinary tract:

- **Skeletal-muscle selectivity.** In an in vitro canine detrusor preparation, dantrolene had no statistically significant inhibitory effect on acetylcholine-induced detrusor contractions.<sup>[[7]](#ref7)</sup> It should therefore not weaken detrusor contraction directly, though this was tested only in a bath assay.
- **Weakness is not selective.** Dantrolene relaxes all skeletal muscle, including limb, respiratory and pelvic-floor muscle. The label lists drowsiness, dizziness, weakness, malaise, fatigue and diarrhea as the commonest effects, and notes central effects of uncertain extent.<sup>[[6]](#ref6)</sup>

The animal data on the sphincter are mixed. In normal and T10 paraplegic dogs with bladder-stimulation-induced DSD, dantrolene had little effect on electrically evoked urethral sphincter contraction, while baclofen reduced urethral resistance.<sup>[[8]](#ref8)</sup> In men, IV dantrolene 0.5 mg/kg reduced external sphincter resistance measured by sphincterometry.<sup>[[9]](#ref9)</sup>

## Indications in Reconstructive Urology

**FDA-labeled uses (not urologic).** Oral dantrolene is labeled for spasticity from upper motor neuron disorders such as SCI, stroke, cerebral palsy and MS, where relief should help restore residual function. It is contraindicated when spasticity is needed to sustain posture, balance or locomotion. It is also labeled for oral preoperative prophylaxis and post-crisis follow-up in MH-susceptible patients.<sup>[[6]](#ref6)</sup> Intravenous dantrolene is the specific treatment for an MH crisis.

**Urologic use (off-label, historical).** Two urologic problems drew interest:

- **EUS hypertonicity or spasm with functional outlet obstruction.** Murdock and colleagues reported benefit in selected patients with neurologic and non-neurologic external sphincter spasm identified by sphincter EMG, cystometry and voiding cystourethrography.<sup>[[2]](#ref2)</sup>
- **DSD with high residual urine in SCI.** Hackler's series is the most cited report (see Evidence Summary).<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup>

A 2015 case report describes a woman with cerebral palsy and refractory neurogenic detrusor overactivity whose symptoms improved when dantrolene was added to an antimuscarinic and mirabegron. She had started dantrolene for spasticity after spinal surgery.<sup>[[10]](#ref10)</sup> A single case does not support use for detrusor overactivity, and dantrolene has no direct detrusor action.

**Current options for DSD and sphincter hypertonicity.** Use these first:

| Option | Position | Where covered |
|---|---|---|
| Alpha-blockers | EAU 2026: prescribe to decrease bladder outlet resistance (strong). Effects on PVR and dysreflexia reported.<sup>[[4]](#ref4)</sup> Evidence in DSD is low quality but more favorable than for muscle relaxants.<sup>[[3]](#ref3)</sup> | [Alpha-Blockers](/docs/foundations/pharmacology/voiding-outlet/alpha-blockers) |
| Intrasphincteric botulinum toxin A | Can treat DSD, but the effect wears off within months and needs repeat injection. Dose and technique are not settled and use is off-label.<sup>[[4]](#ref4)</sup> | [Botulinum Toxin](/docs/foundations/pharmacology/neuromodulation-adjuncts/botulinum-toxin) |
| External sphincterotomy or urethral stent | Options for refractory DSD in men who can use a condom catheter. Sphincterotomy is irreversible and often repeated. | [Neurogenic Bladder](/docs/clinical-conditions/03d-nlutd/neurogenic-bladder) |
| Intermittent catheterization | Standard emptying strategy when the outlet cannot be relaxed safely. | [Neurogenic Bladder](/docs/clinical-conditions/03d-nlutd/neurogenic-bladder) |
| Baclofen and other systemic relaxants | Limited urologic evidence; agent-specific effects. | [Skeletal Muscle Relaxants](/docs/foundations/pharmacology/voiding-outlet/skeletal-muscle-relaxants) |

## Dosing &amp; Administration

The label gives an oral schedule for spasticity only. No label or guideline gives a urologic regimen.

| Setting | Regimen |
|---|---|
| Labeled spasticity titration (adults) | 25 mg daily for 7 days, then 25 mg three times daily for 7 days, then 50 mg three times daily for 7 days, then 100 mg three times daily. Each level is held for 7 days. If no further benefit appears at the next dose, return to the previous dose. |
| Labeled ceiling | 100 mg four times daily (400 mg/day). Higher doses are not to be used. |
| Stop rule | Discontinue if there is no observable benefit after a total of 45 days. Prescribe the lowest effective dose. |
| Historical urologic series | Hackler: five of the eight responders needed up to 600 mg/day, which is above the current label ceiling.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup> |

Do not use the 1980 doses as a prescribing target. The boxed warning describes a markedly higher hepatic risk at 800 mg/day or more, and fatal cases in the manufacturer's reports had a higher mean dose than nonfatal ones.<sup>[[6]](#ref6)[[13]](#ref13)</sup>

For MH, oral prophylaxis and IV crisis dosing follow the product label and anesthesia protocols. See [Anesthesia](/docs/foundations/perioperative-care/intraoperative-care/anesthesia).

## Contraindications &amp; Precautions

**Boxed warning (Dantrium capsules).** The label states that dantrolene "has a potential for hepatotoxicity, and should not be used in conditions other than those recommended." Symptomatic hepatitis, fatal and non-fatal, has occurred at various doses. The incidence in patients taking up to 400 mg/day is much lower than at 800 mg/day or more, and even sporadic short courses at higher doses markedly increased risk. Overt hepatitis has most often appeared between the third and twelfth month. Risk appears greater in women, in patients over 35 and in patients taking other hepatotoxic drugs.<sup>[[6]](#ref6)</sup>

The evidence behind the warning:

| Source | Finding |
|---|---|
| Utili 1977 | Hepatic injury in 19 of 1,044 patients (1.8%) monitored for at least 60 days on long-term therapy; 6 with icteric hepatitis (0.6%); 3 deaths (0.3%). Analysis of 50 cases (those 19 plus 31 reported after marketing) found a 28% case fatality; 85% of the fatalities were at 300 mg/day or more.<sup>[[11]](#ref11)</sup> |
| Chan 1990 | Of 122 analyzable manufacturer-reported cases through 1987: 47 asymptomatic transaminase rises, 36 with jaundice, 27 deaths. Mean dose was 582 mg/day in fatal cases and 263 mg/day in nonfatal cases.<sup>[[13]](#ref13)</sup> |
| Pinder 1977 review | Liver chemistry abnormalities in 0.7 to 1% of patients on long-term treatment, symptomatic hepatitis in 0.35 to 0.5% and fatal hepatitis in 0.1 to 0.2%.<sup>[[12]](#ref12)</sup> |

These reports are passive or early surveillance data with different denominators and definitions. The figures are not interchangeable.

**Monitoring.** Obtain baseline liver function tests (AST, ALT, alkaline phosphatase, total bilirubin) and repeat at appropriate intervals. Stop the drug if tests become abnormal, or if hepatitis symptoms or jaundice appear. Reinitiation is considered only when the benefit is of major importance.<sup>[[6]](#ref6)</sup>

**Contraindications and cautions from the label:**

- Active hepatic disease such as hepatitis or cirrhosis is a contraindication.
- Spasticity that supports posture, balance or locomotion is a contraindication. In an ambulatory patient with incomplete SCI, sphincter relaxation may cost limb function.
- Use caution with impaired pulmonary function (especially obstructive disease), severely impaired cardiac function and prior liver disease.
- Additive drowsiness with sedatives and tranquilizers. Caution with estrogen therapy, because hepatotoxicity was more frequent in women over 35 receiving estrogen. Dantrolene may potentiate vecuronium-induced neuromuscular block.
- Patients should not drive or do hazardous work while taking it. Photosensitivity is possible.<sup>[[6]](#ref6)</sup>

**Urogenital adverse effects listed on the label:** increased urinary frequency, urinary incontinence and/or nocturia, difficult urination and/or urinary retention, crystalluria, hematuria and difficult erection.<sup>[[6]](#ref6)</sup> These matter in a patient already treated for voiding dysfunction. New frequency, incontinence or retention after starting dantrolene for spasticity may be drug-related. A weak sphincter may also cause reflex incontinence in a man who relied on a high-tone sphincter with a condom catheter.

## Perioperative Considerations

- Ask about dantrolene use in patients with SCI, MS or cerebral palsy scheduled for reconstruction. A long-term user needs a current LFT panel, and the label advises stopping if abnormal.
- Dantrolene may potentiate vecuronium-induced neuromuscular block; tell the anesthesia team.<sup>[[6]](#ref6)</sup>
- Weakness from oral dantrolene can affect transfers, catheterization technique and respiratory reserve. Plan for this in a patient with high-level SCI.
- Oral dantrolene is not a substitute for a sphincter procedure. If outlet obstruction threatens the upper tracts, use an established option from the table above.
- Dantrolene is the specific treatment for MH. Reconstructive operating rooms should follow the institution's MH protocol.

## Evidence Summary

| Indication | Evidence level | Key study | Notes |
|---|---|---|---|
| EUS hypertonicity with high PVR in SCI | Uncontrolled case series (15 patients) | Hackler 1980: 8 of 15 benefited (PVR under 100 mL, maintained on condom drainage). Urethral pressure fell by a mean of 77 cm H₂O (49%) in responders and 21 cm H₂O (16%) in failures. Five of the eight needed up to 600 mg/day.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup> | All had closure pressures above 100 cm H₂O and PVR above 150 mL. Detrusor hyporeflexia may have contributed to failures. The authors concluded it would not replace external sphincterotomy in complete male SCI, and that a low effective dose would be needed to avoid weakness. |
| External sphincter spasm, neurologic and non-neurologic | Small uncontrolled series | Murdock 1976: reported benefit in a selected group.<sup>[[2]](#ref2)</sup> | The abstract gives no numbers. |
| Sphincter resistance, pharmacodynamic | Physiologic study | Pedersen 1978: IV 0.5 mg/kg reduced external sphincter resistance on sphincterometry.<sup>[[9]](#ref9)</sup> | Measures a pressure effect, not clinical voiding outcomes. |
| Detrusor effect | In vitro canine bath | Harris 1980: no significant inhibition of acetylcholine-induced detrusor contraction.<sup>[[7]](#ref7)</sup> | Supports selectivity; not a human study. |
| DSD model | Canine | Teague 1978: little effect on evoked sphincter contraction; baclofen was effective.<sup>[[8]](#ref8)</sup> | Conflicts with the human sphincterometry data. |
| Detrusor overactivity | Single case report | Bulchandani 2015 (cerebral palsy, on antimuscarinic and mirabegron).<sup>[[10]](#ref10)</sup> | Not a treatment recommendation. |
| Overview of oral DSD therapy | Scoping review, 15 studies, 257 patients | Tousignant 2025: alpha-blockers "promising", evidence lacking overall; all muscle-relaxant studies were 1980 or earlier.<sup>[[3]](#ref3)</sup> | The review's summary table lists Hackler as 4 patients with a 50% success rate; the primary paper reports 8 of 15. Use the primary paper. |

## Practical Pearls

- Dantrolene has no proven role in modern DSD care. Start with an alpha-blocker, botulinum toxin, catheterization or an outlet procedure as the situation demands.
- If a patient is already on dantrolene for spasticity, check LFTs, and review new urinary symptoms against the label's urogenital adverse effects.
- Treat the boxed warning as dose- and time-related: risk rises at higher doses, in women, after age 35, and after months of use. Stop at 45 days without benefit.
- A patient with incomplete SCI who walks on spastic legs may lose function on dantrolene. That is a labeled contraindication.
- The 1980 dose range (up to 600 mg/day) is above the label ceiling and should not be copied.
