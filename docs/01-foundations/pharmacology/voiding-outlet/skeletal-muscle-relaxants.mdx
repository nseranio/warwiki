# Skeletal Muscle Relaxants

**Skeletal muscle relaxants (SMRs)** are used in urology to relax three different anatomic targets — the **external urethral sphincter** (in detrusor sphincter dyssynergia), the **pelvic floor musculature** (in high-tone pelvic floor dysfunction and myofascial pelvic pain), and indirectly the **detrusor** (via spinal-cord-acting agents). Botulinum toxin A is covered separately: evidence and licensing differ between intradetrusor treatment and off-label sphincter or pelvic-floor injection. This page addresses systemic SMRs (baclofen, tizanidine, dantrolene, diazepam, cyclobenzaprine) and compounded vaginal diazepam, whose urologic evidence is generally limited.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[2]](#ref2)</sup>

For agents covered separately:

- **[Botulinum toxin](/docs/foundations/pharmacology/neuromodulation-adjuncts/botulinum-toxin)** — intradetrusor for OAB/NDO; intrasphincteric for DSD; pelvic-floor injection for myofascial pain
- **[Alpha-blockers](/docs/foundations/pharmacology/voiding-outlet/alpha-blockers)** — smooth-muscle relaxants of prostate / bladder neck / ureter (BPH and MET)
- **[Antispasmodics](/docs/foundations/pharmacology/neuropathic-pelvic-pain/antispasmodics)** — agents used primarily for neuropathic pelvic-pain syndromes
- For the clinical conditions these drugs target, see [Underactive Bladder](/docs/clinical-conditions/03b-voiding-outlet/underactive-bladder), [Neurogenic LUTD](/docs/clinical-conditions/03d-nlutd/neurogenic-bladder), and [Chronic Pelvic Pain](/docs/clinical-conditions/03h-pelvic-pain/chronic-pelvic-pain)

## Anatomic targets

| Target | Muscle type | Agent classes |
|---|---|---|
| **External urethral sphincter** | Striated | **Baclofen** (oral, intrathecal); **tizanidine**; **dantrolene**; intrasphincteric BoNT-A |
| **Pelvic floor** (levator ani, obturator internus) | Striated | **Diazepam** (oral, vaginal); **cyclobenzaprine** (off-label); BoNT-A injection |
| **Detrusor** (indirectly) | Smooth | **Intrathecal baclofen** (spinal-cord effect on EUS motoneurons and detrusor reflexes); intradetrusor BoNT-A |
| **Bladder neck / prostate / ureter** | Smooth | **Alpha-blockers** (covered separately) |

---

## Baclofen — for DSD and neurogenic spasticity

A GABA-B receptor agonist used principally for limb spasticity in spinal-cord pathology, with possible secondary effects on detrusor sphincter dyssynergia (DSD) and bladder reflexes. A limb-spasticity indication does not establish reliable bladder benefit.<sup>[[3]](#ref3)</sup>

### Mechanism in urology

- **Oral:** central GABA-B agonism depresses pudendal-to-pudendal nerve reflex via spinal cord presynaptic hyperpolarization → reduced external urethral sphincter resistance<sup>[[4]](#ref4)</sup>
- **Intrathecal (ITB):** direct lumbosacral GABA-B activation in the **nucleus of Onuf** inhibits EUS motoneurons → urethral relaxation during detrusor contraction. Experimental work in spinal-cord-injured rats suggests different dose responses for urethral and detrusor effects; it does not establish a human bladder-specific therapeutic window<sup>[[5]](#ref5)[[6]](#ref6)</sup>

### Oral baclofen evidence

In an older uncontrolled series of 25 SCI patients with DSD, oral baclofen reduced post-void residual significantly in **73% of cases** within ~5 weeks. This is the proportion reported to improve, not a 73% reduction in each patient or proof of equivalence to sphincterotomy.<sup>[[4]](#ref4)</sup> The principal limitation is poor blood–brain barrier penetration: tolerable oral doses often produce sedation and weakness without reaching effective spinal cord concentrations for DSD.<sup>[[3]](#ref3)</sup>

### Intrathecal baclofen for genitourinary effects

In a prospective study of 10 patients with severe spasticity from spinal-cord pathology, acute bolus testing was blinded against placebo; chronic infusion was compared with pretreatment values:<sup>[[5]](#ref5)</sup>

- **Eliminated uninhibited bladder contractions** in all patients with irritative voiding and urge incontinence
- **Abolished DSD in 40%**
- Achieved **72% increase in bladder capacity** and 16% improvement in compliance (in non-cervical SCI patients)
- 1 of 3 patients with an indwelling catheter converted to clean intermittent self-catheterization

**Interpretation:** mean capacity, compliance, sensation and voiding pressures were not significantly different across the whole small cohort. The capacity/compliance percentages above apply to a subgroup. Reassess bladder emptying and storage after ITB changes; benefit is not assured and pumps should not be implanted solely on these historical bladder findings.<sup>[[3]](#ref3)[[5]](#ref5)</sup>

### Oral dosing<sup>[[7]](#ref7)</sup>

The label titration starts at 5 mg three times daily, increasing by 5 mg per dose every 3 days as tolerated, to a maximum of 80 mg daily (20 mg four times daily). In spinal cord injury, use the lowest effective dose and taper slowly. Baclofen should be used with caution in patients with a history of autonomic dysreflexia, since nociceptive stimuli or abrupt withdrawal can precipitate an episode.

### Adverse effects relevant to LUT<sup>[[7]](#ref7)</sup>

Sedation, weakness, dizziness, headache; LUT-specific: urinary frequency (2–6%) and rarely enuresis, urinary retention, dysuria, impotence, ejaculatory failure, nocturia. Baclofen is predominantly renally eliminated: impaired kidney function can produce accumulation, profound sedation and respiratory depression, requiring dose reduction or an alternative. Abrupt withdrawal of oral or especially intrathecal treatment can cause seizures, hyperthermia, severe spasticity and multiorgan failure. Avoid abrupt discontinuation; pump interruption requires urgent specialist management.

## Tizanidine — central alpha-2 agonist for spasticity

A centrally acting alpha-2 adrenergic agonist used for spasticity from multiple sclerosis and spinal cord injury. It is a common alternative when baclofen is not tolerated, but there are no controlled data for detrusor-sphincter dyssynergia or voiding dysfunction, so any urologic effect is incidental to the spasticity indication.<sup>[[26]](#ref26)</sup>

- **Dosing (label):** 2 mg every 6–8 hours as needed, up to three doses in 24 hours; titrate by 2–4 mg per dose every 1–4 days to a maximum of 36 mg daily. Use lower individual doses if creatinine clearance is below 25 mL/min or in hepatic impairment.
- **Liver injury:** hepatocellular injury occurs; the label advises aminotransferases at baseline and 1 month after reaching the maximum dose, or if injury is suspected.
- **CYP1A2 interactions:** strong CYP1A2 inhibitors (fluvoxamine, ciprofloxacin) are contraindicated because of marked hypotension, sedation and psychomotor impairment. Avoid moderate or weak inhibitors where possible. Ciprofloxacin is relevant in urologic practice, for example when treating a UTI in a spinal cord injury patient already taking tizanidine.
- **Other:** hypotension, sedation and dry mouth are common; taper rather than stop abruptly after prolonged high-dose use. Alpha-2 agonism can compound hypotension in patients also taking alpha-blockers or antihypertensives.

## Dantrolene — for external sphincter hypertonicity (largely historical)

The only **peripherally acting** oral SMR — interferes with calcium release from the sarcoplasmic reticulum to reduce excitation–contraction coupling in skeletal muscle.<sup>[[8]](#ref8)[[9]](#ref9)</sup>

### Selectivity matters

Dantrolene acts on **striated muscle only**: it has **no significant effect on detrusor smooth-muscle contractility**, which makes it conceptually attractive for isolated EUS hypertonicity that spares the detrusor.<sup>[[10]](#ref10)</sup>

### Evidence

- In 15 SCI patients with PVR &gt; 150 mL secondary to EUS hypertonicity (urethral closure pressures &gt; 100 cm H₂O), **8 of 15 (53%) benefited** from dantrolene — successful patients had a mean urethral pressure decrease of 77 cm H₂O (49%). However, **5 of the 8 responders received doses up to 600 mg/day**. These historical doses exceed the current oral label ceiling and are **not a prescribing recommendation**<sup>[[8]](#ref8)</sup>
- Early reports also showed efficacy for non-neurogenic external sphincter spasm causing functional outlet obstruction<sup>[[9]](#ref9)</sup>
- Reported as a salvage option for refractory neurogenic detrusor overactivity in cerebral palsy when combined with antimuscarinics and β3-agonists<sup>[[11]](#ref11)</sup>

### Limitations

- **Fatal and nonfatal hepatitis** is a boxed-warning risk; the current oral label ceiling is **100 mg four times daily**, with baseline/ongoing liver assessment, lowest effective dosing and discontinuation if no benefit within 45 days<sup>[[12]](#ref12)</sup>
- Generalized muscle weakness limits functional utility, particularly in incomplete SCI where ambulation is preserved
- Intrasphincteric BoNT-A is an off-label alternative in selected DSD, with transient effects and limited evidence; systemic toxin effects remain possible<sup>[[23]](#ref23)</sup>
- Detrusor hyporeflexia frequently coexists and contributes to treatment failure

### Current role

Urologic sphincter use is historical and off-label. A single cerebral-palsy case report does not establish dantrolene as an NDO salvage treatment. Active hepatic disease, or spasticity needed for posture/function, contraindicates oral use.<sup>[[11]](#ref11)[[12]](#ref12)</sup>

## Diazepam — for high-tone pelvic floor dysfunction

The most-studied SMR for myofascial pelvic pain and high-tone pelvic floor disorders, available as oral tablets and as **compounded vaginal suppositories** (10 mg in several small studies; no FDA-approved vaginal formulation).<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[13]](#ref13)</sup>

### Pharmacokinetics — vaginal vs oral

Compounded **10 mg vaginal diazepam** has a distinctive PK profile: peak concentration 31.0 ng/mL at ~3.1 h, bioavailability 70.5%, and a **terminal half-life of 82 hours** in **eight healthy volunteers**. Three reported fatigue. Lower peaks do not mean absent systemic exposure; repeated daily dosing accumulates. This PK study does not demonstrate clinical efficacy or validate a routine schedule.<sup>[[14]](#ref14)</sup>

### Evidence — mixed at best

| Study | Design | Result |
|---|---|---|
| Stone systematic review<sup>[[13]](#ref13)</sup> | 5 studies (2 observational, 3 RCTs) | Observational studies report 71–96% subjective improvement; **two RCTs found no significant difference** in FSFI or VAS pain scores, and the third (diazepam + TENS) improved dyspareunia but not VAS pain |
| Crisp triple-blinded RCT (n = 21)<sup>[[15]](#ref15)</sup> | 10 mg vaginal diazepam nightly × 4 wk vs placebo | **No improvement** in resting pelvic-floor EMG or subjective outcomes |
| Murina RCT — vestibulodynia<sup>[[16]](#ref16)</sup> | Vaginal diazepam **+ TENS** vs placebo + TENS | Improved dyspareunia and relaxation measures, but **no significant between-group VAS pain improvement** |
| Rogalski retrospective (n = 26)<sup>[[17]](#ref17)</sup> | Vaginal diazepam + PT + trigger-point injections | Improved pelvic-floor muscle tone at rest, squeeze, and relaxation phases |

**Practical interpretation:** small studies do not establish a routine benefit, including as an adjunct. NICE NG210 explicitly recommends **not offering vaginal diazepam for pelvic-floor dysfunction, even with high tone**. Discuss the uncertainty if considering specialist off-label use; pelvic-floor physical therapy remains central.<sup>[[24]](#ref24)</sup><sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[13]](#ref13)[[15]](#ref15)</sup>

### Oral diazepam

Pelvic-floor use is off-label. Oral and systemically absorbed vaginal diazepam carry sedation, cognitive, dependence and withdrawal risks. Opioids, alcohol and other CNS depressants increase respiratory-depression/overdose risk; an established course should be tapered rather than abruptly stopped.<sup>[[25]](#ref25)</sup> In vitro work shows diazepam has **no direct effect** on human bladder smooth-muscle contractility — its urologic effects are centrally mediated.<sup>[[18]](#ref18)</sup>

## Cyclobenzaprine and Other Centrally Acting Antispasmodics

A tricyclic-related agent prescribed off-label for myofascial pelvic pain and CP/CPPS, with very limited supporting evidence.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[19]](#ref19)[[20]](#ref20)</sup>

- **No high-quality studies** demonstrate improvement in pelvic-pain scales with cyclobenzaprine specifically
- **Anticholinergic adverse effects** (dry mouth, urinary retention, sedation) can worsen voiding dysfunction. The label concerns short-term acute musculoskeletal spasm (up to 2–3 weeks), not chronic pelvic pain; avoid MAOIs within 14 days, relevant cardiac conduction/heart-failure conditions and hyperthyroidism. Serotonergic combinations can cause serotonin syndrome<sup>[[19]](#ref19)</sup>
- Other centrally acting agents (methocarbamol, metaxalone, carisoprodol) have **no established urologic role**
- The ACOG Chronic Pelvic Pain Practice Bulletin notes that SMRs have **limited high-quality evidence** for chronic myofascial pelvic pain and should be used as part of multimodal management rather than as primary therapy<sup>[[2]](#ref2)</sup>

For the broader pelvic-pain pharmacotherapy framework, see [Antispasmodics](/docs/foundations/pharmacology/neuropathic-pelvic-pain/antispasmodics).

## SMRs for Postoperative Urinary Retention

Methocarbamol, meprobamate, chlormezanone, diazepam, and carisoprodol have all been studied for postoperative urinary retention (POUR) on the rationale that they reduce reflex pelvic-floor and EUS contracture (or anxiety-related voiding inhibition). The Cochrane review found **insufficient evidence** to support routine use of any drug — including SMRs — for the treatment of POUR. That 2010 review included only seven studies / 494 participants and did not establish efficacy for alpha-blocker monotherapy in **treating established POUR**. Prevention trials are a different question; drugs must not delay decompression of a significantly distended bladder.<sup>[[21]](#ref21)</sup>

## Evidence Summary

| Agent | Setting | Evidence | Bottom line |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Oral baclofen** | DSD in SCI | PVR improved in 73% of cases in a 25-patient series<sup>[[4]](#ref4)</sup> | Uncontrolled historical evidence; renal/CNS toxicity limits use |
| **Intrathecal baclofen** | DSD + detrusor hyperreflexia in SCI | 10-patient study: DSD abolished in 40%; capacity improvement confined to a subgroup<sup>[[5]](#ref5)</sup> | Reassess bladder effects; not consistent across the whole cohort |
| **Tizanidine** | Spasticity in MS/SCI | No controlled DSD or voiding data; label-based dosing and interaction warnings<sup>[[26]](#ref26)</sup> | Alternative to baclofen for spasticity; check CYP1A2 inhibitors and LFTs |
| **Dantrolene** | EUS hypertonicity in SCI | 8/15 benefited in an old series; some received doses above today’s label ceiling<sup>[[8]](#ref8)</sup> | Historical/off-label; hepatotoxicity and weakness are limiting |
| **Vaginal diazepam** | High-tone pelvic floor dysfunction | Stone meta: no benefit in RCTs; helpful adjunct in some series<sup>[[13]](#ref13)[[15]](#ref15)[[16]](#ref16)[[17]](#ref17)</sup> | No established routine benefit; NICE recommends against use |
| **Cyclobenzaprine** | Myofascial CPP | No high-quality urologic data<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup> | Off-label; consider only as multimodal adjunct |
| **POUR (any SMR)** | Postoperative urinary retention | Cochrane: insufficient evidence<sup>[[21]](#ref21)</sup> | Not recommended as primary therapy |
| **Functional BOO in NLUTD (any approach)** | Surgical / medical management | Cochrane: limited evidence for any single approach<sup>[[22]](#ref22)</sup> | Multimodal, individualized strategy |

## Clinical Positioning

- **Intradetrusor BoNT-A** has established OAB/NDO indications; **intrasphincteric DSD treatment is off-label with limited evidence**. These are separate clinical decisions — see [Botulinum toxin](/docs/foundations/pharmacology/neuromodulation-adjuncts/botulinum-toxin).<sup>[[23]](#ref23)</sup>
- **Baclofen** may affect bladder function when treating neurologic spasticity; monitor emptying and storage rather than promising secondary urologic improvement.
- **Dantrolene** is historical/off-label for urinary sphincter dysfunction; do not extrapolate an old high-dose series or a case report into a modern rescue protocol.
- **Vaginal diazepam** has substantial systemic absorption and no established routine efficacy; NICE recommends against its use for pelvic-floor dysfunction.<sup>[[24]](#ref24)</sup>
- **Cyclobenzaprine and other antispasmodics** lack high-quality urologic evidence — and their anticholinergic burden can worsen voiding dysfunction
- **No SMR is routinely indicated** for postoperative urinary retention<sup>[[21]](#ref21)</sup>
- The **urologic adverse effects** of systemically administered SMRs (urinary retention with baclofen and dantrolene; anticholinergic effects with cyclobenzaprine) must be considered when these agents are prescribed for non-urologic indications in patients with pre-existing LUTS<sup>[[7]](#ref7)[[12]](#ref12)[[19]](#ref19)</sup>
