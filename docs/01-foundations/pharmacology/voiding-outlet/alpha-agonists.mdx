# Alpha Agonists

> **Category:** Pharmacology > Voiding & Outlet

---

## Overview

Alpha-adrenergic agonists raise bladder-neck and urethral smooth-muscle tone. In urology they are used for three purposes: retrograde ejaculation or anejaculation (the best-supported use), selected stress urinary incontinence (weak evidence, rarely used now), and neurogenic orthostatic hypotension in spinal cord injury (midodrine). They are just as important as a cause of functional outlet obstruction and urinary retention, most often from over-the-counter decongestants.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[2]](#ref2)[[3]](#ref3)</sup>

The ejaculatory and adult stress-incontinence regimens described here are off-label. Midodrine is labeled for symptomatic orthostatic hypotension, pseudoephedrine for nasal congestion, and imipramine for depression and childhood enuresis.<sup>[[11]](#ref11)[[12]](#ref12)[[17]](#ref17)</sup>

---

## Mechanism of Action

The bladder neck, proximal urethra and (in men) prostate, seminal vesicles, vas and ejaculatory ducts carry α1-adrenoceptors. Stimulation contracts this smooth muscle.

- **Direct agonists** (midodrine, via its active metabolite desglymidodrine; phenylephrine) bind α1 receptors.
- **Mixed or indirect agents** (pseudoephedrine, ephedrine, phenylpropanolamine) release norepinephrine and also act directly.
- **Imipramine** is not an agonist. It blocks noradrenaline reuptake, which raises adrenergic tone at the bladder neck and seminal tract. It is grouped with the sympathomimetics for ejaculatory disorders.<sup>[[3]](#ref3)[[5]](#ref5)</sup>

The same action explains the main harms: closing the bladder neck causes retention in men with BPH or in women with outlet obstruction, and systemic α1 stimulation raises blood pressure.

## Indications in Reconstructive and Functional Urology

### Retrograde ejaculation and anejaculation

Retrograde ejaculation means semen enters the bladder during climax, usually because the bladder neck fails to close during emission. Bladder-neck surgery and neurologic lesions can cause it. Reduced or absent antegrade ejaculation with alpha-blockers does not by itself establish retrograde flow.<sup>[[18]](#ref18)</sup> EAU Neuro-Urology 2026 states that sympathomimetics that contract the bladder neck (imipramine, ephedrine, pseudoephedrine, phenylpropanolamine) may reverse it. If antegrade ejaculate is not achieved, sperm can be recovered from post-ejaculatory urine.<sup>[[3]](#ref3)</sup>

The evidence is uncontrolled and heterogeneous. A systematic review of 88 retrograde-ejaculation studies called for controlled trials.<sup>[[5]](#ref5)</sup>

| Study | Design and regimen | Result |
|---|---|---|
| Arafa 2008 (diabetic men, n = 33)<sup>[[6]](#ref6)</sup> | Sequential courses: imipramine 25 mg twice daily, pseudoephedrine 120 mg twice daily, then both | Among 23 men with complete retrograde ejaculation, 11 regained antegrade ejaculate with pseudoephedrine and 16 with the combination |
| Shoshany 2017 (n = 20)<sup>[[7]](#ref7)</sup> | Pseudoephedrine 60 mg every 6 h the day before semen analysis, plus two doses on the day | Antegrade sperm recovered in 7 of 12 complete cases; ≥50% count increase in 5 of 8 partial cases |
| Conduit 2024, after RPLND (single-arm phase II)<sup>[[8]](#ref8)</sup> | Pseudoephedrine 60 mg every 6 h for six doses | Primary endpoint (&gt;39 million sperm) met in 4 of 22 (18%); efficacy lower than expected |

**Practical use.** A short course is timed to semen collection; post-ejaculatory urine is interpreted alongside the antegrade specimen, and assisted reproduction is planned in parallel. Urinary sperm alone does not establish partial retrograde ejaculation.<sup>[[7]](#ref7)</sup> In Shoshany’s mixed-etiology series, antegrade sperm appeared in 7/12 men with complete retrograde ejaculation, while 5/8 with partial retrograde ejaculation had a sperm-count increase of at least 50%<sup>[[7]](#ref7)</sup>; after RPLND, 4/22 met Conduit’s prespecified threshold of &gt;39 million sperm per ejaculate<sup>[[8]](#ref8)</sup>. These populations and endpoints differ.<sup>[[7]](#ref7)[[8]](#ref8)</sup>

For delayed ejaculation, AUA/SMSNA finds insufficient evidence to assess the risk-benefit ratio of oral pharmacotherapy (Statement 23; Expert Opinion). Its Table 5 reports pseudoephedrine 60–120 mg 120–150 minutes before sexual activity, ephedrine 15–60 mg one hour before sexual activity and imipramine 25–75 mg daily as regimens with possible efficacy. These are reported DE regimens, with uncertain benefit, and do not replace the retrograde-ejaculation or SCI study schedules.<sup>[[18]](#ref18)</sup>

### Spinal cord injury: anejaculation and sperm retrieval

Soler’s uncontrolled series reported antegrade or retrograde ejaculation with adjunctive midodrine during penile vibratory stimulation; a later pilot randomized 23 men, of whom 20 completed treatment, and reported antegrade ejaculation in 1/10 midodrine recipients versus 2/10 placebo recipients.<sup>[[4]](#ref4)[[19]](#ref19)</sup> Among 158 men who failed vibration alone, midodrine was given 30–120 minutes before weekly stimulation, escalating by 7.5 mg to a 30 mg maximum. Antegrade or retrograde ejaculation was reported in 102/158 (64.6%); response was more frequent with complete lesions and upper-motor-neuron lesions above T10.<sup>[[4]](#ref4)[[20]](#ref20)</sup> The mean midodrine dose required for ejaculation in Soler’s responders was 18.7 mg.<sup>[[4]](#ref4)</sup> Mean arterial pressure rose by up to about 10 mm Hg, and systolic pressure exceeded 200 mm Hg in some men with tetraplegia.<sup>[[4]](#ref4)</sup>

EAU Neuro-Urology 2026 describes oral midodrine as a possible adjunct to vibrostimulation. It strongly recommends counseling men with SCI at or above T6 and fertility clinics about potentially life-threatening autonomic dysreflexia.<sup>[[3]](#ref3)</sup> Monitor blood pressure throughout each attempt.

### Neurogenic orthostatic hypotension

In spinal cord injury and other autonomic failure, midodrine treats symptomatic orthostatic hypotension. Consensus guidance pairs treatment with monitoring and management of supine hypertension.<sup>[[15]](#ref15)</sup> In a small SCI crossover trial of chronic dosing, midodrine reduced hypotensive readings but increased blood-pressure variability and autonomic-dysreflexia symptom intensity without improving orthostatic symptoms.<sup>[[21]](#ref21)</sup>

### Stress urinary incontinence

Adrenergic agonists are not a routine option. The Cochrane review (22 randomized trials, 1,099 women, 673 receiving an adrenergic drug; PPA in 11 trials, midodrine in 2) found limited evidence that they beat placebo for pad changes, incontinence episodes and subjective symptoms, and too little evidence to compare them with pelvic floor training or estrogen. Adverse effects were reported by more than a quarter of women, with similar numbers in adrenergic, placebo and alternative-drug groups; recognized adrenergic effects led to treatment cessation in 4%.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup> Arrhythmias and severe hypertension have also been reported.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup> No trial included men.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup> In a placebo-controlled midodrine phase II trial, urethral closure pressure did not change at 4 weeks, although global assessments favored treatment.<sup>[[9]](#ref9)</sup>

EAU 2026 female LUTS guidance describes only "weak evidence" for adrenergic agonists and recommends offering duloxetine (where licensed), not an adrenergic agonist, to selected women with SUI unresponsive to conservative care who wish to avoid invasive treatment (weak recommendation).<sup>[[2]](#ref2)</sup> The AUA/SUFU surgical-treatment guideline (2017, amended 2023) gives no recommendation on adrenergic drug therapy.<sup>[[16]](#ref16)</sup> NICE NG123 advises against offering imipramine (along with flavoxate and propantheline) for urinary incontinence or overactive bladder in women.<sup>[[14]](#ref14)</sup>

## Dosing and Administration

Doses are for reference. Confirm against the current label and local protocol.

| Agent | Labeled dose | Urologic (off-label) regimens in the studies above |
|---|---|---|
| Midodrine | Orthostatic hypotension: 10 mg three times daily, in the daytime only; **not after the evening meal or within 4 h of bedtime**. Start at 2.5 mg in renal impairment.<sup>[[11]](#ref11)</sup> | In the Soler cohort of men with SCI and anejaculation after failed vibrostimulation, midodrine started at 7.5 mg for tetraplegia or 15 mg for paraplegia, then was escalated weekly up to 30 mg and given 30–120 min before repeat vibrostimulation<sup>[[4]](#ref4)[[20]](#ref20)</sup> |
| Pseudoephedrine | OTC 60 mg every 4–6 h, maximum 4 tablets (240 mg) in 24 h, age 12 and older<sup>[[12]](#ref12)</sup> | 60 mg every 6 h for a short course around semen collection<sup>[[7]](#ref7)[[8]](#ref8)</sup>; 120 mg twice daily in diabetic retrograde ejaculation<sup>[[6]](#ref6)</sup> |
| Imipramine | Depression: outpatients start at 75 mg/day and may increase to 150 mg/day; doses over 200 mg/day are not recommended. Childhood enuresis (age 6 and older): 25 mg one hour before bedtime. The label gives no retrograde-ejaculation dose, has a boxed warning for suicidality in children, adolescents and young adults, and contraindicates MAO inhibitors. It calls for extreme caution with cardiovascular disease (cardiac surveillance at all doses), prior urinary retention, narrow-angle glaucoma or seizure disorder.<sup>[[17]](#ref17)</sup> | 25 mg twice daily in the diabetic series<sup>[[6]](#ref6)</sup> |
| Ephedrine, PPA | No labeled urologic dose | Ephedrine has been reported for DE, with uncertain risk-benefit (AUA/SMSNA Statement 23 and Table 5). PPA is off the US market.<sup>[[18]](#ref18)[[13]](#ref13)</sup> |

## Contraindications and Precautions

- **Midodrine boxed warning:** it can cause marked elevation of supine blood pressure, so it should be used only in patients whose lives are considerably impaired despite standard care. Contraindicated with severe organic heart disease, acute renal disease, **urinary retention**, pheochromocytoma and thyrotoxicosis; do not use in patients with persistent and excessive supine hypertension. Use cautiously in any retention problem, because desglymidodrine acts on bladder-neck alpha receptors. Avoid MAO inhibitors and linezolid. Avoid concurrent pressor drugs such as phenylephrine, pseudoephedrine or ephedrine; if coadministration cannot be avoided, monitor blood pressure closely. Use caution with cardiac glycosides (bradycardia or AV block) and beta-blockers. Animal studies showed embryo resorption and reduced fetal weight (former Pregnancy Category C). Pediatric safety is not established.<sup>[[11]](#ref11)</sup>
- **Pseudoephedrine (OTC label):** do not use with a prescription MAOI or within 2 weeks of stopping one. Ask a clinician first with heart disease, hypertension, thyroid disease, diabetes or **difficulty urinating from an enlarged prostate**. Pregnant or breastfeeding patients should ask a health professional first.<sup>[[12]](#ref12)</sup> The imipramine label advises avoiding sympathomimetic decongestants because tricyclic antidepressants can potentiate catecholamine effects.<sup>[[17]](#ref17)</sup>
- **Functional outlet obstruction:** EAU lists decongestant alpha-agonists such as pseudoephedrine as a cause of functional female bladder outlet obstruction and retention.<sup>[[2]](#ref2)</sup> The AUA 2026 BPH guideline likewise lists chronic decongestant use among medications that increase outlet resistance and should be captured in the history.
- **PPA:** the FDA asked manufacturers to stop marketing it in November 2000 after the Hemorrhagic Stroke Project. In a case-control study of adults aged 18–49, PPA-containing appetite-suppressant use within the preceding three days was associated with hemorrhagic stroke in women (adjusted odds ratio 16.6, 95% CI 1.5–182).<sup>[[10]](#ref10)[[13]](#ref13)</sup>
- **Spinal cord injury:** rapid blood-pressure rises and autonomic dysreflexia; monitor during dosing and stimulation.<sup>[[3]](#ref3)[[4]](#ref4)</sup>

## Perioperative Considerations

- Ask about OTC decongestants before and after outlet or prolapse surgery in patients with new retention or a weak stream. Stopping the drug may resolve the obstruction.
- Do not give midodrine or pseudoephedrine to patients on MAOIs or linezolid.
- Sympathomimetics can compound perioperative hypertension and tachyarrhythmia; review them at pre-anesthesia assessment.

## Practical Pearls

- Ask every man with retention or a weak stream, and every woman with unexplained retention, about decongestants.
- For men seeking fertility after RPLND who have aspermia or a low-volume ejaculate, obtain post-orgasmic urinalysis to distinguish retrograde flow from failure of emission (AUA/ASRM Clinical Principle).<sup>[[23]](#ref23)</sup> For confirmed retrograde ejaculation after RPLND or with diabetes, a short pseudoephedrine course timed to collection may be discussed (AUA/ASRM Expert Opinion); for lymphadenectomy-related failure of emission, EAU 2026 places penile vibratory stimulation first.<sup>[[23]](#ref23)[[24]](#ref24)</sup> In Conduit's single-arm post-RPLND trial, 4/22 met the &gt;39-million-sperm endpoint; 7/16 without measurable ejaculate at screening produced an antegrade ejaculate after pseudoephedrine treatment, without a significant sperm-count change (P = 0.14).<sup>[[8]](#ref8)</sup> Plan sperm retrieval or assisted reproduction alongside any drug trial.
- Monitor blood pressure with midodrine in patients with spinal cord injury, and have a plan for autonomic dysreflexia.
