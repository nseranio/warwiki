# Alpha Agonists

> **Category:** Pharmacology > Voiding & Outlet

---

## Overview

Alpha-adrenergic agonists raise bladder-neck and urethral smooth-muscle tone. In urology they are used for three purposes: retrograde ejaculation or anejaculation (the best-supported use), selected stress urinary incontinence (weak evidence, rarely used now), and neurogenic orthostatic hypotension in spinal cord injury (midodrine). They are just as important as a cause of functional outlet obstruction and urinary retention, most often from over-the-counter decongestants.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[2]](#ref2)[[3]](#ref3)</sup>

Every use in this class is off-label for the urologic indication. Only midodrine (orthostatic hypotension) and OTC pseudoephedrine (nasal congestion) carry US labeling.

---

## Mechanism of Action

The bladder neck, proximal urethra and (in men) prostate, seminal vesicles, vas and ejaculatory ducts carry α1-adrenoceptors. Stimulation contracts this smooth muscle.

- **Direct agonists** (midodrine, via its active metabolite desglymidodrine; phenylephrine) bind α1 receptors.
- **Mixed or indirect agents** (pseudoephedrine, ephedrine, phenylpropanolamine) release norepinephrine and also act directly.
- **Imipramine** is not an agonist. It blocks noradrenaline reuptake, which raises adrenergic tone at the bladder neck and seminal tract. It is grouped with the sympathomimetics for ejaculatory disorders.<sup>[[3]](#ref3)[[5]](#ref5)</sup>

The same action explains the main harms: closing the bladder neck causes retention in men with BPH or in women with outlet obstruction, and systemic α1 stimulation raises blood pressure.

## Indications in Reconstructive and Functional Urology

### Retrograde ejaculation and anejaculation

Retrograde ejaculation follows bladder-neck surgery, retroperitoneal lymph node dissection, diabetic autonomic neuropathy, spinal cord injury, and alpha-blocker or BPH-procedure effects. EAU Neuro-Urology 2026 states that sympathomimetics that contract the bladder neck (imipramine, ephedrine, pseudoephedrine, phenylpropanolamine) may reverse it. If antegrade ejaculate is not achieved, sperm can be recovered from post-ejaculatory urine.<sup>[[3]](#ref3)</sup>

The evidence is uncontrolled and heterogeneous. A systematic review of 88 retrograde-ejaculation studies called for controlled trials.<sup>[[5]](#ref5)</sup>

| Study | Design and regimen | Result |
|---|---|---|
| Arafa 2008 (diabetic men, n = 33)<sup>[[6]](#ref6)</sup> | Sequential courses: imipramine 25 mg twice daily, pseudoephedrine 120 mg twice daily, then both | In complete retrograde ejaculation, antegrade ejaculate in about 48% on pseudoephedrine and about 62% on the combination |
| Shoshany 2017 (n = 20)<sup>[[7]](#ref7)</sup> | Pseudoephedrine 60 mg every 6 h the day before semen analysis, plus two doses on the day | Antegrade sperm recovered in 7 of 12 complete cases; ≥50% count increase in 5 of 8 partial cases |
| Conduit 2024, after RPLND (single-arm phase II)<sup>[[8]](#ref8)</sup> | Pseudoephedrine 60 mg every 6 h for six doses | Primary endpoint (&gt;39 million sperm) met in 4 of 22 (18%); efficacy lower than expected |

**Practical use:** give the drug for a short course timed to semen collection, check a post-ejaculatory urine sample, and plan assisted reproduction in parallel. Response rates in the series above were about 50–60% in diabetic and idiopathic cases and lower (18%) after RPLND.

### Spinal cord injury: anejaculation and sperm retrieval

Oral midodrine improved ejaculation during penile vibratory stimulation. In 185 men who had failed vibration alone, midodrine was given 30–120 minutes before stimulation, weekly, increasing by 7.5 mg to a maximum of 30 mg. About 65% ejaculated (antegrade or retrograde), most often with complete lesions above T10. The mean effective dose was 18.7 mg. Mean arterial pressure rose by up to about 10 mm Hg, and systolic pressure exceeded 200 mm Hg in some men with tetraplegia.<sup>[[4]](#ref4)</sup>

EAU Neuro-Urology 2026 supports midodrine for improving sperm retrieval at vibrostimulation. It also warns that men with lesions at or above T6 can develop autonomic dysreflexia during sexual activity and ejaculation, so fertility clinics and patients need to know the risk.<sup>[[3]](#ref3)</sup> Monitor blood pressure throughout each attempt.

### Neurogenic orthostatic hypotension

In spinal cord injury and other autonomic failure, midodrine treats symptomatic orthostatic hypotension. Consensus guidance pairs treatment with monitoring and management of supine hypertension.<sup>[[15]](#ref15)</sup>

### Stress urinary incontinence

Adrenergic agonists are not a routine option. The Cochrane review (22 randomized trials, 1,099 women, 673 receiving an adrenergic drug; PPA in 11 trials, midodrine in 2) found limited evidence that they beat placebo for pad changes, incontinence episodes and subjective symptoms, and too little evidence to compare them with pelvic floor training or estrogen. Just over a quarter of women reported adverse effects, and rare serious events (arrhythmia, hypertension) were reported. No trial included men.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup> In a placebo-controlled midodrine phase II trial, urethral closure pressure did not change at 4 weeks, although global assessments favored treatment.<sup>[[9]](#ref9)</sup>

EAU 2026 female LUTS guidance describes only "weak evidence" for adrenergic agonists and recommends duloxetine, not an adrenergic agonist, as the drug option for selected women with SUI unresponsive to conservative care who wish to avoid invasive treatment (weak recommendation).<sup>[[2]](#ref2)</sup> The AUA/SUFU 2023 stress incontinence guideline does not recommend adrenergic agonists.<sup>[[16]](#ref16)</sup> NICE NG123 advises against offering imipramine (along with flavoxate and propantheline) for urinary incontinence or overactive bladder in women.<sup>[[14]](#ref14)</sup>

## Dosing and Administration

Doses are for reference. Confirm against the current label and local protocol.

| Agent | Labeled dose | Urologic (off-label) regimens in the studies above |
|---|---|---|
| Midodrine | Orthostatic hypotension: 10 mg three times daily, in the daytime only; **not after the evening meal or within 4 h of bedtime**. Start at 2.5 mg in renal impairment.<sup>[[11]](#ref11)</sup> | Anejaculation: 7.5 mg escalating weekly to a 30 mg maximum, given 30–120 min before vibrostimulation<sup>[[4]](#ref4)</sup> |
| Pseudoephedrine | OTC 60 mg every 4–6 h, maximum 4 tablets (240 mg) in 24 h, age 12 and older<sup>[[12]](#ref12)</sup> | 60 mg every 6 h for a short course around semen collection<sup>[[7]](#ref7)[[8]](#ref8)</sup>; 120 mg twice daily in diabetic retrograde ejaculation<sup>[[6]](#ref6)</sup> |
| Imipramine | Antidepressant labeling | 25 mg twice daily in the diabetic series<sup>[[6]](#ref6)</sup> |
| Ephedrine, PPA | No current urologic dosing to cite | Not recommended: PPA is off the US market; ephedrine lacks urologic trial data |

## Contraindications and Precautions

- **Midodrine boxed warning:** it can cause marked elevation of supine blood pressure, so it should be used only in patients whose lives are considerably impaired despite standard care. Contraindicated with severe organic heart disease, acute renal disease, **urinary retention**, pheochromocytoma and thyrotoxicosis. Use cautiously in any retention problem, because desglymidodrine acts on bladder-neck alpha receptors. Avoid MAO inhibitors and linezolid. Use caution with other vasoconstrictors (phenylephrine, pseudoephedrine, ephedrine), cardiac glycosides (bradycardia, AV block) and beta-blockers. Animal studies showed embryo resorption and reduced fetal weight (former Pregnancy Category C). Pediatric safety is not established.<sup>[[11]](#ref11)</sup>
- **Pseudoephedrine (OTC label):** do not use with a prescription MAOI or within 2 weeks of stopping one. Ask a clinician first with heart disease, hypertension, thyroid disease, diabetes or **difficulty urinating from an enlarged prostate**. Pregnant or breastfeeding patients should ask a health professional first.<sup>[[12]](#ref12)</sup>
- **Functional outlet obstruction:** EAU lists decongestant alpha-agonists such as pseudoephedrine as a cause of functional female bladder outlet obstruction and retention.<sup>[[2]](#ref2)</sup> The AUA 2026 BPH guideline likewise lists chronic decongestant use among medications that increase outlet resistance and should be captured in the history.
- **PPA:** the FDA asked manufacturers to stop marketing it in November 2000 after the Hemorrhagic Stroke Project. The case-control study found an adjusted odds ratio of 16.6 (95% CI 1.5–182) for hemorrhagic stroke with appetite-suppressant use in women.<sup>[[10]](#ref10)[[13]](#ref13)</sup>
- **Spinal cord injury:** rapid blood-pressure rises and autonomic dysreflexia; monitor during dosing and stimulation.<sup>[[3]](#ref3)[[4]](#ref4)</sup>

## Perioperative Considerations

- Ask about OTC decongestants before and after outlet or prolapse surgery in patients with new retention or a weak stream. Stopping the drug may resolve the obstruction.
- Do not give midodrine or pseudoephedrine to patients on MAOIs or linezolid.
- Sympathomimetics can compound perioperative hypertension and tachyarrhythmia; review them at pre-anesthesia assessment.

| Sperm retrieval in spinal cord injury | Large uncontrolled series | Soler 2007 | Midodrine adjunct to vibrostimulation; EAU 2026 supports |
| Female SUI | Weak (small, older, largely PPA trials) | Cochrane 2005 | No adrenergic recommendation in AUA/SUFU 2023; EAU offers duloxetine instead |
| Neurogenic orthostatic hypotension | Consensus guidance | Gibbons 2017 | Label indication for midodrine |

---

## Practical Pearls

- Ask every man with retention or a weak stream, and every woman with unexplained retention, about decongestants.
- For fertility after RPLND or in diabetes, a short pseudoephedrine course timed to collection is reasonable, but expect a minority of patients to respond after RPLND. Do not delay sperm retrieval or assisted reproduction while trialing it.
- Monitor blood pressure with midodrine in patients with spinal cord injury, and have a plan for autonomic dysreflexia.
