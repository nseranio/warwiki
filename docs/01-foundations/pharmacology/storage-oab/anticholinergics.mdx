# Anticholinergic / Antimuscarinic Agents

**Anticholinergic medications** (antimuscarinics) are a cornerstone of urologic pharmacotherapy, primarily for **overactive bladder (OAB)** and **neurogenic lower urinary tract dysfunction (NLUTD)**.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[2]](#ref2)</sup> They block muscarinic receptors (M2 and M3) in the detrusor muscle, inhibiting involuntary contractions, increasing bladder capacity, and reducing urgency and frequency.

The class has six FDA-approved agents<sup>[[41]](#ref41)</sup> with broadly similar efficacy and different tolerability and CNS penetration; comparative long-term dementia risk across agents remains uncertain.<sup>[[2]](#ref2)[[14]](#ref14)</sup> The 2024 AUA/SUFU guideline recommends offering either an antimuscarinic or a β3-agonist for idiopathic OAB (Strong Recommendation, Grade A).<sup>[[14]](#ref14)</sup> Its discussion and the 2022 SUFU white paper typically favor a β3-agonist trial first when considering long-term cognitive risk.<sup>[[11]](#ref11)[[14]](#ref14)</sup>

---

## Mechanism of Action

- **M3 receptors** produce direct detrusor smooth muscle contraction via extracellular calcium entry (L-type channels) and Rho kinase activation
- **M2 receptors** (more numerous) facilitate M3-mediated contractions and can reverse β-adrenoceptor-mediated relaxation<sup>[[3]](#ref3)[[4]](#ref4)</sup>
- **Antagonism of M3** leads to inhibition of detrusor contraction, which increases bladder capacity and reduces urgency
- **Off-target effects** at other muscarinic receptor populations produce the class side-effect profile

### Receptor selectivity

- **M3-selective:** darifenacin, solifenacin
- **Non-selective (M1–M5):** oxybutynin, tolterodine, trospium, fesoterodine
- Non-selective agents cause broader side effects (salivary glands, CNS, heart, gut)<sup>[[3]](#ref3)[[5]](#ref5)</sup>

### CNS penetration — the mechanism behind dementia risk

- **High CNS penetration:** oxybutynin (lipophilic, non-selective)
- **Low CNS penetration in a single-dose rat study:** darifenacin and 5-HMT (fesoterodine's active metabolite); solifenacin and tolterodine showed significant CNS penetration.<sup>[[50]](#ref50)</sup>
- **Minimal CNS penetration:** trospium (quaternary amine; poorly crosses BBB)<sup>[[3]](#ref3)[[6]](#ref6)</sup>

Blood-brain-barrier properties inform drug selection.<sup>[[14]](#ref14)</sup>

## FDA-Approved Agents

Six anticholinergic medications are FDA-approved for OAB in the United States.<sup>[[2]](#ref2)</sup>

| Agent | Receptor selectivity | CNS penetration | Typical dose | Distinguishing feature |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Oxybutynin (IR, ER, TD patch, topical gel)** | Non-selective | **High** | IR: 5 mg TID; ER: 5–30 mg daily; patch delivers **3.9 mg/day**, changed twice weekly<sup>[[24]](#ref24)</sup> | Oldest; most extensive evidence; highest dementia concern; **transdermal** avoids first-pass metabolism and reduces systemic side effects |
| **Tolterodine (IR, ER)** | Non-selective | Moderate | IR: 2 mg BID (1 mg BID with significant renal/hepatic impairment or a potent CYP3A4 inhibitor); ER: 4 mg daily (2 mg with CrCl 10–30 mL/min, Child-Pugh A/B, or a potent CYP3A4 inhibitor; not recommended with CrCl &lt;10 mL/min or Child-Pugh C)<sup>[[37]](#ref37)[[44]](#ref44)</sup> | Less dry mouth than IR oxybutynin<sup>[[7]](#ref7)</sup> |
| **Fesoterodine** | Non-selective (prodrug → active 5-HMT) | Low in preclinical studies (5-HMT)<sup>[[11]](#ref11)</sup> | Start 4 mg daily; increase to 8 mg if tolerated; maximum 4 mg with CrCl &lt;30 mL/min or a strong CYP3A4 inhibitor<sup>[[42]](#ref42)</sup> | Prodrug converted to 5-HMT, tolterodine's active metabolite; flexible dosing; not recommended with clinically significant bladder outlet obstruction<sup>[[7]](#ref7)[[42]](#ref42)</sup> |
| **Solifenacin** | **M3-selective** | Moderate | Start 5 mg daily; increase to 10 mg if tolerated, but do not exceed 5 mg with CrCl &lt;30 mL/min, Child-Pugh B, or a potent CYP3A4 inhibitor<sup>[[43]](#ref43)</sup> | Better QoL + efficacy vs. tolterodine in head-to-head; widely used<sup>[[7]](#ref7)</sup> |
| **Darifenacin** | **M3-selective** | Lower | Start 7.5 mg daily; increase to 15 mg if needed, but do not exceed 7.5 mg with Child-Pugh B or a potent CYP3A4 inhibitor<sup>[[38]](#ref38)</sup> | M3 selectivity in theory reduces CNS side effects |
| **Trospium (IR, ER)** | Non-selective | **Minimal (quaternary amine)** | IR: 20 mg twice daily; ER: 60 mg once each morning; take both on an empty stomach at least 1 hour before a meal<sup>[[13]](#ref13)[[25]](#ref25)</sup> | Lower BBB penetration; **CNS effects remain possible** and renal dosing matters<sup>[[13]](#ref13)</sup> |

International agents not available in the US include imidafenacin, propiverine, and propantheline.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[3]](#ref3)</sup>

## Clinical Efficacy

### Oral anticholinergics versus placebo (Cochrane 2023; 104 trials, 47,106 participants reported in 92 trials)<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup>

| Outcome | Effect vs. placebo |
|---|---|
| Micturition frequency | **–0.85 episodes / 24 hr** (95% CI 0.73–0.98 lower) |
| Urgency episodes | **–0.85 episodes / 24 hr** (95% CI 0.67–1.03 lower) |
| Patient-reported cure/improvement | **RR 1.38** (95% CI 1.15–1.66) |
| Quality of life | MD −4.41 (on −100 to 0 scale) |
| Withdrawal due to AEs | **RR 1.37** vs. placebo |

### Class efficacy

- Benefit over placebo is real but modest: about 0.85 fewer voids and 0.85 fewer urgency episodes per 24 hours in the 2023 Cochrane review<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup>
- **FDA regulatory trials** summarized for anticholinergics show 53–80% reductions in incontinence episodes and 16–32% reductions in urinary frequency from baseline; these are mostly short-term trial results, not placebo-adjusted effects<sup>[[2]](#ref2)</sup>
- In placebo arms of FDA regulatory trials summarized for women with urgency incontinence, incontinence episodes fell 30–47% from baseline; the review notes a likely effect of behavioral modification.<sup>[[2]](#ref2)</sup>

### Head-to-head comparisons<sup>[[7]](#ref7)[[8]](#ref8)</sup>

**Solifenacin versus IR tolterodine** (solifenacin superior):

- Quality of life (SMD −0.12)
- Patient-reported cure or improvement (RR 1.25)<sup>[[7]](#ref7)</sup>
- Leakage (WMD −0.30)
- Urgency episodes (WMD −0.43)
- **Less dry mouth** than IR tolterodine in the four-trial sensitivity analysis that excluded an ER-tolterodine trial (RR 0.69, 95% CI 0.51–0.94)<sup>[[7]](#ref7)</sup>

**Fesoterodine 8 mg versus ER tolterodine:** fesoterodine superior for all efficacy endpoints, but higher withdrawal due to adverse events (RR 1.45) and higher dry mouth (RR 1.80) at 12 weeks.<sup>[[7]](#ref7)</sup>

**Tolterodine versus oxybutynin:** similar efficacy; tolterodine has less dry mouth (RR 0.65) and fewer withdrawals for adverse events (RR 0.52).<sup>[[7]](#ref7)</sup>

**Extended-release &gt; immediate-release:** similar efficacy, significantly less dry mouth in ER formulations

### Dose comparisons

- **Tolterodine 1/2/4 mg twice daily:** similar leakage and micturition efficacy over 2–12 weeks; 2 and 4 mg twice daily caused more dry mouth than 1 mg twice daily.<sup>[[7]](#ref7)</sup>
- **Solifenacin 5 versus 10 mg once daily:** 10 mg reduced frequency and urgency more over 4–12 weeks, but caused more dry mouth.<sup>[[7]](#ref7)</sup>

## Adverse Effects

### Common anticholinergic burden<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[2]](#ref2)</sup>

| AE | Rate / relative risk |
|---|---|
| **Dry mouth** | Most common; about 25% (trospium, solifenacin, tolterodine) to 44% (oxybutynin); RR 3.50 vs. placebo<sup>[[14]](#ref14)</sup> |
| **Constipation** | Common; about 8-15%; RR 2.03 vs. placebo<sup>[[14]](#ref14)</sup> |
| **Dry eyes** | Frequent |
| **Blurred vision** | Up to about 11%; RR 1.58 vs. placebo<sup>[[14]](#ref14)</sup> |
| **Urinary retention** | RR 3.52 vs. placebo<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup> |
| **Withdrawal due to AEs** | RR 1.37 vs. placebo<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup> |

### Cognitive impairment and dementia risk

The most consequential modern controversy in OAB pharmacotherapy:<sup>[[9]](#ref9)[[10]](#ref10)[[11]](#ref11)</sup>

**French case-control study (4,810 cases, 24,050 controls):** OAB anticholinergic use was associated with OR 1.23 for dementia, with a clear dose-response relationship:<sup>[[9]](#ref9)</sup>

| Cumulative exposure | OR for dementia |
|---|---|
| 1–90 DDDs | 1.07 |
| 91–365 DDDs | 1.29 |
| **&gt;365 DDDs** | **1.48** |

**Agent-specific signal:** in the French case-control study above, oxybutynin and solifenacin carried a particularly marked increased risk of dementia, with no increased risk for trospium.<sup>[[9]](#ref9)</sup> A UK nested case-control study (58,769 dementia cases; 225,574 controls aged 55 years or older) found an association for bladder antimuscarinics at &gt;1095 total standardized daily doses within the 1–11 years before diagnosis (adjusted OR 1.65, 95% CI 1.56–1.75, versus nonuse).<sup>[[32]](#ref32)</sup>

- **Trospium:** limited brain penetration supports consideration when an antimuscarinic is needed.<sup>[[13]](#ref13)[[14]](#ref14)</sup>

These epidemiologic dementia associations describe long-term exposure and diagnosis, a different outcome from short-term prospective cognitive testing. In a 26-person crossover trial of adults aged at least 75 years with mild cognitive impairment, neither drug changed five composite cognitive scores from baseline over 21 days. In a secondary analysis, oxybutynin reduced power and continuity of attention versus placebo at 1–2 hours postdose.<sup>[[33]](#ref33)</sup> A systematic review of 18 studies similarly found no cognitive decline with trospium, darifenacin or fesoterodine, while oxybutynin and tolterodine were each linked to decline in about half of the studies examining them; solifenacin was not associated with cognitive decline in the two studies that assessed it directly, though one study linked it to increased dementia risk in patients with diabetes.<sup>[[34]](#ref34)</sup>

**Pooled data:** A 2025 meta-analysis of eight studies associated OAB anticholinergic use with higher dementia risk versus no drug therapy (RR 1.20, 95% CI 1.09–1.32) and versus mirabegron (RR 1.28, 95% CI 1.03–1.58); heterogeneity was very high, and confounding by indication remains possible.<sup>[[26]](#ref26)</sup> In a Danish nationwide nested case-control analysis of 58,242 new bladder-drug users aged 60–75 years at entry, anticholinergic use was associated with lower dementia incidence than mirabegron use (IRR 0.82, 95% CI 0.74–0.92); a separate general-population analysis found higher incidence versus nonuse (IRR 1.44, 95% CI 1.40–1.48).<sup>[[45]](#ref45)</sup>

### 2022 SUFU White Paper conclusions<sup>[[11]](#ref11)</sup>

- **Chronic use (&gt;3 months)** likely associated with increased dementia risk
- **Short-term use (&lt;4 weeks)** of most agents was considered likely safe for most patients, but SUFU advises avoiding immediate-release oxybutynin because short-term trials found cognitive effects; individual delirium and retention risks remain.
- **Dose–response associations** strengthen concern but do not eliminate confounding
- Lower CNS penetration with trospium, darifenacin and fesoterodine is a theoretical advantage<sup>[[14]](#ref14)</sup>
- **Patients should be counseled** about the dementia association before long-term initiation
- **Alternatives (β3-agonists, botulinum toxin, neuromodulation)** should be considered especially in elderly and cognitively-vulnerable patients

### Mechanism

Anticholinergic blockade of acetylcholine in the brain disrupts cholinergic neurotransmission required for learning and memory. Greater CNS penetration may raise concern for cognitive effects; antimuscarinics have no validated long-term dementia-risk ranking.<sup>[[3]](#ref3)[[12]](#ref12)[[11]](#ref11)[[14]](#ref14)</sup>

However, one propensity-matched cohort study (782 pairs, adults with normal cognition, MCI or dementia) found no significant association with clinically relevant cognitive decline, though dose and duration were unknown and oxybutynin or tolterodine users trended higher (CDR OR 1.65, 95% CI 0.98–2.77). The signal is not bulletproof, and residual confounding remains a concern.<sup>[[27]](#ref27)</sup>

## Contraindications and Precautions

### Contraindications<sup>[[2]](#ref2)[[13]](#ref13)</sup>

- **Uncontrolled narrow-angle glaucoma** (the 2024 AUA/SUFU guideline advises extreme caution in any narrow-angle glaucoma and coordination with the treating ophthalmologist)<sup>[[14]](#ref14)</sup>
- **Gastric retention / severe GI obstructive disorders**
- **Current urinary retention** is a labeled contraindication; a history of retention warrants extreme caution.<sup>[[13]](#ref13)[[14]](#ref14)</sup>

### Use with caution

| Situation | Consideration |
|---|---|
| **Elevated PVR** | Increased risk of urinary retention; consider an alternative or a cautious trial with repeat PVR. There is no accepted cutoff; most pivotal trials excluded PVR 150-200 mL<sup>[[14]](#ref14)</sup> |
| **Significant BPH** | May worsen obstructive symptoms; use with α-blocker if chosen |
| **Cardiac arrhythmia** | Can exacerbate tachyarrhythmias |
| **GI motility disorders** | Ulcerative colitis, intestinal atony, myasthenia gravis |
| **Solid oral potassium chloride** | Contraindicated when anticholinergic effects delay gastrointestinal tablet passage; solid tablets can cause ulcerative or stenotic gastrointestinal lesions.<sup>[[51]](#ref51)</sup> |
| **Renal impairment** | Trospium IR: **20 mg nightly if CrCl &lt;30 mL/min**; ER is **not recommended** at that clearance. Oxybutynin patch (Oxytrol) and gel (Gelnique) labels give no renal or hepatic dose guidance; the gel label states there is no experience in renal or hepatic impairment. Check each other agent’s label separately<sup>[[13]](#ref13)[[25]](#ref25)[[28]](#ref28)[[29]](#ref29)</sup> |
| **Elderly** | Higher risk of cognitive effects, falls, constipation. Trospium IR label: at 75 years or older, the dose may be titrated down to 20 mg once daily according to tolerability<sup>[[13]](#ref13)</sup> |
| **Parkinson's / MS / scleroderma / hypothyroidism** | Conditions affecting motility and cognition |

### AUA/SUFU guidelines<sup>[[14]](#ref14)</sup>

- **Discuss potential dementia and cognitive-impairment risk** with patients taking or about to be prescribed antimuscarinics (Clinical Principle).
- Use with extreme caution in narrow-angle glaucoma, impaired gastric emptying, or history of urinary retention (Clinical Principle).
- **Consider a PVR** to exclude incomplete emptying, especially with voiding or emptying symptoms; obtain one if there is a history of retention or risk of it, and weigh risk and benefit with the patient
- **Assess at 4–8 weeks** for efficacy and side effects (Expert Opinion); consider a PVR in men, older patients and those starting an antimuscarinic
- **Reassess efficacy and side effects** after initiation

## Clinical Positioning

### Contemporary paradigm

**2024 AUA/SUFU OAB guideline** positions pharmacotherapy as:<sup>[[14]](#ref14)</sup>

- Offer behavioral measures and discuss β3-agonists or antimuscarinics according to symptoms, adverse effects and preferences.
- A different-class combination can be considered after an inadequate single-drug response.
- **Botulinum toxin, sacral neuromodulation and PTNS** need not await completion of a fixed medication ladder; the guideline permits their selection without prior drug trials through shared decision-making.

**EAU 2026 (non-neurogenic female LUTS)** offers anticholinergics to women with OAB who fail conservative management (Strong) and advises shared decision-making, extended-release formulations whenever possible, early review of efficacy and adverse effects, and, if a drug fails, dose escalation, a different formulation or a β3 agonist alone or combined (all Strong). Immediate-release products allow flexible or on-demand dosing but cause more adverse events. EAU finds no anticholinergic clearly superior for cure or improvement and reports that most patients stop within three months. For older women it advises caution with long-term use, especially with existing or risk of cognitive dysfunction, and assessment of anticholinergic burden (Strong). Cognitive harm is cumulative with exposure (level 2); oxybutynin may worsen cognition, while darifenacin, fesoterodine, solifenacin and trospium have not been shown to in short-term studies (level 1b).<sup>[[31]](#ref31)</sup>

### When to choose antimuscarinic over β3-agonist

- **Patient has tried β3-agonist** without adequate response
- **Combination therapy:** synergistic effect in patients with suboptimal monotherapy
- **Patient preference / cost** (antimuscarinics are generic and cheaper)
- **Specific subpopulations** where antimuscarinic evidence is stronger (NLUTD, pediatric)

### When to avoid anticholinergics

- **Elderly patient with cognitive concerns:** use β3-agonist or trospium
- **Active cognitive impairment or dementia:** avoid where an alternative exists
- **BPH with elevated PVR:** assess emptying and discuss the potential for worsening retention before an antimuscarinic trial.<sup>[[14]](#ref14)</sup>
- **Narrow-angle glaucoma:** antimuscarinics are contraindicated when uncontrolled; use extreme caution when treated.<sup>[[13]](#ref13)[[14]](#ref14)</sup>

### Adherence

Long-term adherence is suboptimal; across three observational prescription-claims studies reporting both drug classes, 1-year persistence ranged 12–25% for antimuscarinics versus 32–38% for mirabegron.<sup>[[14]](#ref14)[[35]](#ref35)</sup> Drivers of discontinuation: dry mouth, constipation, cognitive side effects, inadequate efficacy.

## Combination Therapy

**Antimuscarinic plus β3-agonist** (for example, solifenacin 5 mg with mirabegron 25–50 mg) can improve symptoms after an inadequate single-drug response.<sup>[[36]](#ref36)[[14]](#ref14)</sup> In SYNERGY, solifenacin 5 mg plus mirabegron 50 mg improved micturition frequency (nominal P values), but the co-primary incontinence comparison versus mirabegron 50 mg did not reach statistical significance; a prespecified age subgroup analysis of 12-month SYNERGY II reported generally greater improvements in incontinence episodes and micturitions with that combination than with either monotherapy.<sup>[[39]](#ref39)[[40]](#ref40)</sup> In BESIDE, among patients still incontinent after a 4-week solifenacin 5 mg run-in, combination treatment was superior to solifenacin 5 mg for mean daily incontinence episodes, noninferior to solifenacin 10 mg for incontinence episodes in a 3-day diary (a key secondary endpoint), and superior to 10 mg for daily micturitions after 12 weeks.<sup>[[15]](#ref15)[[16]](#ref16)[[36]](#ref36)</sup> The AUA/SUFU 2024 guideline offers different-class combination after inadequate single-drug response (Conditional Recommendation, Grade B); in SYNERGY II, adverse events were 49% with combination versus 41-44% with monotherapy.<sup>[[14]](#ref14)</sup>

- **Additional frequency reduction** over monotherapy
- **Additional urgency or incontinence reduction**
- **Increased anticholinergic side effects** versus β3-agonist alone; the choice is a trade-off

## Special Populations

### Neurogenic Lower Urinary Tract Dysfunction (NLUTD)

Antimuscarinics are a mainstay of pharmacotherapy for bladder storage dysfunction in NLUTD. The 2021 AUA/SUFU NLUTD guideline (Statement 33; Conditional Recommendation, Grade C) permits antimuscarinics, β3 agonists, or both to improve bladder storage parameters<sup>[[17]](#ref17)[[18]](#ref18)[[19]](#ref19)</sup>; the 2026 EAU Neuro-Urology guideline recommends against using β3 agonists to reduce urodynamically proven neurogenic detrusor overactivity (Strong).<sup>[[46]](#ref46)</sup> Antimuscarinic storage effects include:

- Reliably increase maximum cystometric capacity and voided / catheterized volumes
- Decrease detrusor pressure
- May improve urgency and incontinence across NLUTD etiologies, including SCI, MS and spina bifida
- **No evidence** of superiority of any agent (guideline)
- May be combined with β3-agonists (after shared decision-making) or α-blockers (Statement 34 covers α-blockers alone for voiding parameters in spontaneous voiders)
- **Intravesical oxybutynin:** has been described for selected CIC-dependent NLUTD patients who cannot tolerate oral treatment.<sup>[[17]](#ref17)</sup>

### Pediatric neurogenic bladder

**Oxybutynin** is the most-used agent, effective at increasing capacity, lowering storage pressures, abolishing overactive contractions. Mirabegron and (rarely) gabapentin are alternatives in intolerant patients.<sup>[[20]](#ref20)</sup>

### Men with BPH

Antimuscarinics benefit men with predominantly storage symptoms and coexisting BPH, alone or combined with an α-blocker; the AUA/SUFU 2024 guideline supports antimuscarinic or β3 monotherapy, or α-blocker plus either class (Conditional Recommendation, Grade B). Antimuscarinics may raise PVR slightly but have not been associated with significantly more retention in men with BPH-related obstruction, although effects with a PVR above 200 mL are not well studied.<sup>[[14]](#ref14)</sup> Combination with an α-blocker improves OAB symptoms more than α-blocker alone. Check PVR before initiation and at follow-up. The AUA BPH guideline (2026) likewise allows an antimuscarinic alone or with an α-blocker for moderate-to-severe predominant storage LUTS (Conditional Recommendation, Grade C), and adds behavioral measures when storage symptoms persist on medication.<sup>[[14]](#ref14)[[21]](#ref21)[[22]](#ref22)[[30]](#ref30)</sup>

## Formulations

| Formulation | Advantages | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| **Extended-release oral** | Less dry mouth than IR; QD dosing | Default for most agents |
| **Immediate-release oral** | Faster onset; cheapest | Higher dry mouth burden |
| **Transdermal patch** (oxybutynin) | Bypasses first-pass metabolism; less dry mouth | Skin reactions in some patients |
| **Topical gel** (oxybutynin) | Application flexibility | Transfer risk to partner / children |
| **Intravesical instillation** (oxybutynin) | Fewer systemic adverse effects than oral dosing were reported in small NLUTD studies<sup>[[17]](#ref17)</sup> | Selected NLUTD / CIC patients with systemic intolerance |

## Progression to Advanced Therapies

With inadequate response, intolerance or preference for a procedure, discuss minimally invasive therapies. Under the 2024 AUA/SUFU guideline, medication failure is not required in every case before minimally invasive therapy (Expert Opinion).<sup>[[14]](#ref14)[[23]](#ref23)</sup> After an appropriate response to a minimally invasive procedure, discontinue oral OAB medication and restart pharmacotherapy if efficacy wanes (Expert Opinion).<sup>[[14]](#ref14)</sup>

- **Intradetrusor onabotulinumtoxinA:** FDA-labeled doses after inadequate response to or intolerance of an anticholinergic are 100 U for adult OAB and 200 U for adult urinary incontinence due to neurologic detrusor overactivity.<sup>[[47]](#ref47)[[17]](#ref17)</sup> For idiopathic OAB, measure PVR before injection and counsel about incomplete emptying and possible clean intermittent catheterization.<sup>[[14]](#ref14)</sup>
- **Sacral neuromodulation** (InterStim, Axonics)
- **Tibial nerve stimulation:** percutaneous PTNS or implantable eCoin and Revi systems.<sup>[[14]](#ref14)[[48]](#ref48)[[49]](#ref49)</sup> Revi treats urgency incontinence and is contraindicated during pregnancy and in men with BPH or other lower urinary tract obstruction.<sup>[[49]](#ref49)</sup>
