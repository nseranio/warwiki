# Phenazopyridine

Phenazopyridine hydrochloride is a **urinary analgesic**, an azo dye excreted in the urine that acts topically on the urothelium to blunt the pain, burning, urgency, and frequency caused by infection, trauma, endoscopic instrumentation, catheter passage, or the passage of sounds and bougies.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup> For the reconstructive urologist and urogynecologist it is a short-term bridge analgesic, useful in the 24–48 hours before empiric antibiotics take hold for a symptomatic UTI, after office cystoscopy, following dilation or DVIU, after intravesical chemodenervation with onabotulinumtoxinA, and around urethral-sound or transurethral-catheter procedures, and never a substitute for definitive therapy. The class sits in "legacy" not because it has fallen out of favor, but because its evidence base is thin by modern standards; it remains widely used for short-term symptoms. The cited US prescription product is listed as unapproved drug other; DailyMed listing does not itself establish FDA approval.

For the broader analgesic landscape (NSAIDs, opioids, adjuncts), see [NSAIDs & analgesics](/docs/foundations/pharmacology/neuropathic-pelvic-pain/nsaids-analgesics). For the central-neuromodulation comparators, see [Gabapentinoids](/docs/foundations/pharmacology/neuropathic-pelvic-pain/gabapentinoids), [SNRIs](/docs/foundations/pharmacology/neuropathic-pelvic-pain/snris), and [TCAs](/docs/foundations/pharmacology/neuropathic-pelvic-pain/tricyclic-antidepressants).

## Mechanism of Action

Phenazopyridine is an azo dye; its pharmacokinetic properties have not been formally characterized, but the drug and its metabolites are rapidly cleared through the kidneys, where it exerts a topical analgesic effect on the urothelial mucosa.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup> The precise molecular target was historically unknown.

A 2023 electrophysiology study (Luyts et al, *Eur J Pharmacol*) found TRPM8 inhibition in cells and mouse sensory neurons, a plausible but unvalidated mechanism for urinary analgesia.<sup>[[2]](#ref2)</sup>

- **TRPM8**: a cold- and menthol-sensitive cation channel expressed on bladder afferent C- and Aδ-fibers, upregulated in painful bladder disorders (bladder pain syndrome / interstitial cystitis, OAB).
- Phenazopyridine rapidly and reversibly inhibits TRPM8 in vitro (IC₅₀ 2–10 μM). Reported urine concentrations after therapeutic dosing (100–300 μM) exceed that range, but receptor exposure and target engagement in patients remain unknown.<sup>[[2]](#ref2)[[14]](#ref14)</sup>
- At 10 μM it does not significantly block other pain-relevant TRP channels (TRPA1, TRPV1, TRPM3), suggesting relative selectivity.
- This is consistent with the clinical observation that phenazopyridine works locally on the urothelium and does not produce systemic analgesia.

The TRPM8-inhibition model is mechanistically satisfying but has not yet been translated into validated biomarkers or predictive response data.

## Indications in Reconstructive Urology

Phenazopyridine is purely symptomatic; it neither treats infection nor modifies disease.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup> Its role in reconstructive and functional practice is narrow and tactical:

| Scenario | Rationale |
|---|---|
| **Symptomatic UTI, first 24–48 h of empiric antibiotics** | Provides symptom relief while antibacterial therapy takes effect<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[3]](#ref3)</sup> |
| **Post-office cystoscopy / flexible ureteroscopy** | Blunts post-procedure dysuria; endoscopic procedures are a labeled indication<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup> |
| **Post-urethral dilation or DVIU** | Short-term analgesia during the high-irritation window |
| **After passage of sounds, bougies, or transurethral catheter placement** | Described use in the product labeling<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup> |
| **Recurrent-UTI patients between cultures** | Symptomatic control while awaiting susceptibilities — does **not** substitute for culture-directed therapy. The 2025 AUA/CUA/SUFU rUTI guideline allows urinary analgesics while awaiting culture in select patients when safety is not compromised<sup>[[4]](#ref4)</sup> |
| **Office intradetrusor onabotulinumtoxinA** | Preprocedure 200 mg was noninferior to intravesical lidocaine for pain in one RCT of women with idiopathic OAB<sup>[[8]](#ref8)</sup>; also used as a bridge for transient post-injection dysuria (off-label) |
| **Ureteral-efflux visualization at cystoscopy** | Urine dye for white-light cystoscopy — see [below](#ureteral-efflux-visualization-at-cystoscopy) |
| **IC/BPS flares** | Occasional symptomatic adjunct; the AUA IC/BPS guideline names phenazopyridine only among over-the-counter self-care products (Statement 10) and "urinary analgesics" within general pain management (Statement 13), both Clinical Principles without an evidence-strength grade; limited by the 2-day rule when combined with antibacterials |

Out of scope: phenazopyridine is not an alternative to antibiotics for UTI, not a chronic-use analgesic, and not a treatment for inflammatory or neoplastic urothelial disease.

## Dosing &amp; Administration

| Formulation | Dose | Frequency | Key rule |
|---|---|---|---|
| 200 mg tablet | 1 tablet | TID **after meals** | Discontinue when symptoms controlled<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup> |
| 100 mg tablet | 2 tablets | TID after meals | Equivalent to 200 mg TID<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup> |
| OTC (95–99.5 mg) | Per product labeling; one maximum-strength brand (99.5 mg) directs 2 tablets TID with a full glass of water, adults and children ≥12 y<sup>[[6]](#ref6)</sup> | TID | Not more than 2 days (12 tablets) without a clinician<sup>[[6]](#ref6)</sup> |

**The 2-day rule:** when given concomitantly with an antibacterial for UTI, duration should not exceed 2 days; there is no evidence of added benefit beyond that and prolonged exposure increases the risk of accumulation-related adverse effects.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup>

Phenazopyridine can relieve symptoms while an antibacterial takes effect; in selected women with recurrent uncomplicated UTI, urinary analgesics may be used while awaiting culture when patient safety is not compromised. Do not let symptom relief delay diagnosis or indicated treatment, and discontinue phenazopyridine when symptoms are controlled.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[4]](#ref4)</sup> Empiric chronic use masks infection, delays culture-directed treatment, and exposes the patient to accumulation risk.

## Contraindications &amp; Precautions

**Absolute contraindications**<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup>

- **Renal insufficiency.** Renal elimination means that impaired clearance leads to drug accumulation and disproportionate toxicity
- **Hypersensitivity** to phenazopyridine or product ingredients
- The cited product also lists severe liver disease, severe hepatitis and pyelonephritis of pregnancy; consult the actual product label
- OTC labeling is more conservative: ask a doctor first with kidney or liver disease, and do not use with G6PD deficiency unless a physician approves<sup>[[6]](#ref6)</sup>

**Use with caution**<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup>

- **Advanced age.** Age-related decline in eGFR effectively reduces clearance
- **G6PD deficiency.** The label warns of oxidative hemolysis and hemolytic anemia. In a large Israeli database study of G6PD-deficient patients, hemolysis requiring admission was uncommon and phenazopyridine was prescribed uneventfully to hundreds or thousands, with a single suggested drug-associated case; this does not remove the label precaution<sup>[[9]](#ref9)</sup>
- **GI disturbances.** Label advises cautious use
- **Pregnancy.** Formerly Category B; no adequate human studies, use only if clearly indicated
- **Nursing.** No data on human-milk excretion

**Clinical warning signs that mandate discontinuation**<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup>

- Yellowish tinge of skin or sclera (suggests accumulation due to impaired renal excretion)
- Any systemic dermatologic or hematologic reaction

## Adverse Reactions &amp; Overdose

| Category | Details |
|---|---|
| **Reported; frequency not established** | Headache, rash, pruritus, nausea, vomiting, diarrhea<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup> |
| **Hematologic (overdose / renal impairment)** | **Methemoglobinemia** (urgent emergency/toxicology assessment; treatment depends on symptoms, level, renal function and G6PD status); **hemolytic anemia** (Heinz bodies and bite cells on smear are described in case reports) and sulfhemoglobinemia<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup> |
| **Renal / hepatic** | Renal and hepatic toxicity, usually with overdose; hypersensitivity hepatitis and jaundice also reported<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup> |
| **Dermatologic / other** | Yellow skin/scleral tinge (accumulation); rare anaphylactoid reactions; visual disturbance, renal calculi and aseptic meningitis are listed; tooth discoloration if tablets are crushed or held in the mouth<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup> |
| **Neoplasia (long-term exposure)** | The cited label describes phenazopyridine as reasonably anticipated to be a human carcinogen based on animal data; long-term dosing produced rat colorectal and female-mouse liver tumors. Human evidence is inadequate; one limited cohort of 2,214 exposed patients followed at least 3 years found no significant excess, so human risk remains unresolved.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup> |

**Methylene-blue paradox:** the treatment for phenazopyridine-induced methemoglobinemia is itself a dye the reconstructive urologist may recognize from intraoperative use; see [Methylene blue](/docs/foundations/pharmacology/intraoperative-adjuncts/visualization-agents/methylene-blue). PROVAYBLUE is contraindicated in G6PD deficiency.<sup>[[5]](#ref5)</sup> Methylene-blue dosing must follow its specific label and account for serotonergic medicines and renal function. Oral vitamin C is not an interchangeable rapid rescue for severe symptomatic methemoglobinemia; involve toxicology or hematology for alternative treatment.

## Perioperative Considerations

- **Review perioperative use with the surgical team.** Urinary discoloration can affect interpretation; its presence alone does not establish a universal preoperative stopping interval.
- **Urinalysis interference.** Phenazopyridine's orange-red color can interfere with colorimetric, photometric or fluorometric assays. The label names urine ketones, bilirubin, glucose (glucose-oxidase), albumin (reagent strips), urobilinogen, porphyrins and urinary steroids, and color-based urinalysis generally; stool color tests are also affected.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup> Inform the laboratory; use appropriate microscopy or culture and clinical assessment. Do not delay evaluation of suspected infection or bleeding for an arbitrary washout.
- **Clothing and contact-lens staining.** Counsel patients specifically about contact-lens staining; staining of contact lenses has been reported, and the OTC label warns of soft-lens staining from handling tablets.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[6]](#ref6)</sup>
- **Post-endoscopic or post-dilation use.** The label describes symptom relief after endoscopic procedures. In one RCT of women receiving office 100-U onabotulinumtoxinA for idiopathic OAB, preprocedure oral phenazopyridine was noninferior to intravesical lidocaine for procedural pain. That trial does not establish replacement of NSAIDs or anesthetic gel for dilation or DVIU.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[8]](#ref8)</sup>
- **Pregnancy.** The OTC label recommends a pregnancy test and clinician consultation before use in women who may be pregnant.<sup>[[6]](#ref6)</sup>
- **Catheter aftercare.** The label describes symptomatic relief after catheter passage. In a blinded RCT after gynecologic surgery, routine phenazopyridine did not improve bladder or overall pain or analgesic use (112 treated versus 107 placebo). A 24–48-hour course after difficult passage through an atrophic or strictured urethra remains a case-by-case option that this trial did not test.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[15]](#ref15)</sup>

## Ureteral-Efflux Visualization at Cystoscopy

Oral phenazopyridine turns urine orange, which makes ureteral jets easier to see with a white-light cystoscope. Use is off-label. Preoperative studies used 200 mg by mouth, but excretion timing varies; in a seven-patient cohort, catheter urine first showed dye a median 70 minutes after dosing (range 59–127 minutes). Give the dose with sufficient time before planned cystoscopy, keep a rescue dye available, and check renal, hepatic and G6PD precautions.<sup>[[7]](#ref7)[[11]](#ref11)</sup>

| Study | Design | Finding |
|---|---|---|
| Cohen 2017<sup>[[7]](#ref7)</sup> | Prospective cohort, pelvic reconstruction, n = 12 (7 phenazopyridine, 5 IV fluorescein) | Median time from dosing to visible color in catheter urine: 70 min (range 59–127) after 200 mg PO given 1 h before surgery, versus 5 min (3–9) after intraoperative IV fluorescein |
| Propst 2016<sup>[[10]](#ref10)</sup> | RCT, n = 104, phenazopyridine vs nothing | Time to visualize efflux did not differ (2 min 53 s vs 2 min 40 s; p = 0.77); surgeons reported less frustration. Fewer treated patients failed the postoperative voiding trial (9/47 vs 19/49), an exploratory secondary finding that does not establish retention prevention |
| Strom 2019<sup>[[11]](#ref11)</sup> | Retrospective, n = 207 | Phenazopyridine alone enabled visualization of ureteral flow in 190/207 (91.8%); a 200 mg dose was associated with greater effectiveness than 100 mg; the 17 failures needed rescue fluorescein |
| Stanley 2022<sup>[[12]](#ref12)</sup> | Three-arm RCT, n = 84 (placebo, riboflavin, phenazopyridine) | Urine staining was more intense with phenazopyridine than with riboflavin or placebo |

Plan the dose timing, because onset can be slow and variable, and keep a rapid agent such as [sodium fluorescein](/docs/foundations/pharmacology/intraoperative-adjuncts/visualization-agents/sodium-fluorescein) available when efflux is not seen. See the [visualization agents](/docs/foundations/pharmacology/intraoperative-adjuncts/visualization-agents) hub for agent comparison.

| **Acute symptomatic UTI bridge analgesia** | Two RCTs: blinded single-dose trial (n = 60) and open-label four-arm trial (n = 440) | Petrov 2020<sup>[[16]](#ref16)</sup>; Zaitsev and Davidov 2025<sup>[[17]](#ref17)</sup>; product labeling<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup>; Zelenitsky-Zhanel review<sup>[[3]](#ref3)</sup> | The 2020 trial found six-hour symptom benefit before antibiotics; the 2025 study reported faster pain and dysuria relief with a two-day phenazopyridine course added to fosfomycin, with follow-up through day six. ## Practical Pearls

- For eligible adults, 200 mg TID after meals for no more than 2 days with UTI treatment is a common prescription-label regimen; check contraindications and the actual product first.
- **Counsel about urine color up front.** Patients who see orange-red urine without warning present to the ED thinking they are bleeding; patients who are warned are unconcerned.
- **Warn about contact lenses.** Soft lenses can stain; recommend glasses for the duration of therapy and hand-washing after touching tablets.
- **Check renal function before prescribing in the elderly.** The most common preventable harm is accumulation in unrecognized CKD.
- **Review known G6PD deficiency and prior hemolysis in every patient**; a short course does not eliminate the hemolysis risk.
- **Tell the laboratory about use.** Some strip-based or color-based results can be unreliable, while clinical assessment, microscopy and culture may still be needed.
- **The 2-day rule exists for a reason.** The label states there is no evidence of additional benefit beyond 48 hours with an antibacterial. Toxicity (methemoglobinemia, hemolysis, renal and hepatic injury) is linked to overdose and impaired clearance, so older patients and CKD carry the most risk.
- **It is not a disease-modifying treatment for IC/BPS.** Chronic use has no guideline-grade support; patients who have been self-medicating with OTC AZO for years should be redirected to the [AUA IC/PBS framework](/docs/clinical-conditions/03h-pelvic-pain/ic-pbs).
