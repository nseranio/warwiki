# Parasympathomimetics — Legacy and Specialty Uses

Parasympathomimetics in urology (the direct muscarinic agonist bethanechol and the cholinesterase inhibitors neostigmine, pyridostigmine, and distigmine) are the classic pharmacologic approach to detrusor underactivity (DUA), nonobstructive urinary retention, and neurogenic bladder. The evidence has never been strong: the most recent systematic reviews call the data "inadequate to draw clear conclusions," and a 2007 review went further, stating the available literature does not support the use of parasympathomimetics for underactive bladder.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[2]](#ref2)[[3]](#ref3)</sup> They remain in the legacy category because no better pharmacologic alternative exists; modern urology still has no approved therapy that reliably augments detrusor voiding contraction strength.<sup>[[3]](#ref3)[[23]](#ref23)</sup> EAU Neuro-Urology 2026 makes a strong recommendation not to prescribe parasympathomimetics for underactive detrusor, and EAU Non-neurogenic Female LUTS 2026 (strong) advises against routine use in women with underactive bladder; the practical management of incomplete emptying is intermittent catheterization.<sup>[[24]](#ref24)[[25]](#ref25)</sup>

For the standard framework (muscarinic-receptor physiology, bethanechol dosing per FDA label, contraindications, and the role of CIC), see the [Cholinergic agonists](/docs/foundations/pharmacology/voiding-outlet/cholinergic-agonists) hub. This article focuses on the legacy and specialty-use material that does not belong in the main hub: the Bethanechol Supersensitivity Test, electromotive intravesical bethanechol, the Japanese distigmine experience, and small 2025 pyridostigmine trials that require replication before wider adoption.

## Classification — Direct vs Indirect

| Class | Agents | Mechanism |
|---|---|---|
| **Direct muscarinic agonists** | **Bethanechol** (FDA-approved for urology) | Quaternary amine; directly stimulates M₃ detrusor receptors; resistant to cholinesterase hydrolysis; does not cross the BBB<sup>[[4]](#ref4)</sup> |
| **Indirect — reversible AChE inhibitors** | **Neostigmine**, **pyridostigmine** | Inhibit ACh hydrolysis → ↑ synaptic ACh at parasympathetic terminals; require **intact parasympathetic innervation** to work<sup>[[5]](#ref5)[[15]](#ref15)[[17]](#ref17)</sup> |
| **Indirect — long-acting AChE inhibitor** | **Distigmine** (Japan) | Bis-carbamate with strong bladder-AChE binding; sustained effects in **animal experiments**, not a validated human IV dose or half-life recommendation<sup>[[6]](#ref6)[[7]](#ref7)[[8]](#ref8)[[22]](#ref22)</sup> |

The duration difference is the clinical story for distigmine: a single dose produces sustained bladder-contraction augmentation far outlasting its plasma concentration, and this does not extrapolate from neostigmine or pyridostigmine (approximately 3 h potentiation after washout).<sup>[[8]](#ref8)</sup>

## Bethanechol — Historical Diagnostic Roles

Bethanechol's role as a diagnostic probe of the bladder predates its role as a therapeutic, and much of the legacy literature is diagnostic rather than therapeutic.

### The Bethanechol Supersensitivity Test (BST)

The BST was introduced to distinguish neuropathic from non-neuropathic detrusor areflexia using Cannon's law of denervation supersensitivity: a denervated muscle responds disproportionately to its agonist. The test protocol:

- Subcutaneous bethanechol, typically 2.5–5 mg (5 mg in the Wheeler series), during cystometry<sup>[[13]](#ref13)</sup>
- A rise in intravesical pressure above a threshold (classically &gt; 15 cm H₂O above baseline; EAU 2026 quotes &gt; 25 cm H₂O) suggests denervation supersensitivity, indicating a neurogenic etiology<sup>[[24]](#ref24)</sup>

**The historical evidence:**

| Study | n | Findings |
|---|---|---|
| **Pavlakis 1983** | 63 | **98% sensitivity, 100% specificity** for neuropathic areflexia<sup>[[11]](#ref11)</sup> |
| **Sidi 1988** | 57 | **90% sensitivity, 95.6% specificity**; combined with rhabdosphincter EMG and bulbocavernosus reflex latency → accuracy ~ 100%<sup>[[10]](#ref10)</sup> |
| **Blaivas 1980** | 33 | **24% false-negative rate, 50% false-positive rate** — "insufficient for diagnosis alone"<sup>[[12]](#ref12)</sup> |
| **Wheeler 1988** | 7 | Documented **borderline or positive BST (19–55 cm H₂O after 5 mg SC) in neurologically normal women** — further eroded its specificity<sup>[[13]](#ref13)</sup> |

EAU Neuro-Urology 2026 states there is currently no indication for the test, noting equivocal results in practice.<sup>[[24]](#ref24)</sup> It is now largely of historical interest. Modern MRI, urodynamics, and neurophysiologic assessment have displaced it, but the concept of pharmacologic provocation of detrusor contractility informed the more modern electromotive test below.<sup>[[13]](#ref13)</sup>

### Electromotive intravesical bethanechol

A modernization: intravesical bethanechol 20 mg in 150 mL saline delivered via pulsed electromotive current through an electrode catheter. In the Riedl 2000 series of 45 patients with detrusor areflexia:<sup>[[14]](#ref14)</sup>

- 24 / 26 patients with neurologic disease showed mean pressure increase of 34 cm H₂O
- Only 3 / 11 patients with chronic bladder distension without neurologic disease responded
- **Oral bethanechol (25 mg once daily, 6 weeks) restored spontaneous voiding in 9 / 11 electromotive-positive patients treated, and in 0 / 4 without a pressure rise**

This effectively inverts the old BST: instead of asking "is this neurogenic?", the electromotive test asks "will this patient respond to oral bethanechol?", and it does so with a predictive rather than descriptive value. This small historical study does not establish a validated contemporary response-selection test; EAU 2026 notes no follow-up was published.<sup>[[14]](#ref14)[[24]](#ref24)</sup>

## Bethanechol — Therapeutic Evidence Today

**The aggregate picture is weak.**<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[2]](#ref2)</sup>

| Source | Findings |
|---|---|
| **Moro 2022 SR/meta** (12 trials, n = 3,024) | Statistically significant reduction in patients with urinary retention (**RR 0.55**, 95% CI 0.3–0.98; low-quality evidence); PVR reduction at ≤ 1 week (MD **−77.5 mL**) but **no difference at 1 month** or in overall postvoid volume; no difference in adverse events (moderate-quality evidence); authors concluded overall evidence **insufficient for firm evidence-based conclusions**<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup> |
| **Barendrecht 2007 SR** (10 RCTs) | 3 positive / 6 negative / 1 harmful; "evidence does not support use"<sup>[[2]](#ref2)</sup> |
| **Buckley-Lapitan Cochrane (POUR; 7 studies, n = 494)** | Cholinergic agents alone showed no significant association with successful treatment; cholinergic + sedative combination was associated with spontaneous voiding (RR 1.39; 95% CI 1.07–1.82) but with significant heterogeneity between the two studies<sup>[[5]](#ref5)</sup> |
| **EAU Neuro-Urology 2026; EAU Female LUTS 2026** | "Do not prescribe parasympathomimetics for underactive detrusor" (strong); "Do not routinely recommend parasympathomimetics for treatment of women with UAB" (strong); oral parasympathomimetics do not improve clinical or urodynamic parameters (LE 1b)<sup>[[24]](#ref24)[[25]](#ref25)</sup> |
| **Gaitonde NAMCS 2003–2013** | Among women's visits for LUTS, neurogenic bladder or retention, bethanechol was prescribed at 0.8% — **92% of prescriptions by urologists**, 79% continued from prior prescriber; mean age 62.3, for detrusor atony (35%), retention (20%), neurogenic bladder (18%)<sup>[[9]](#ref9)</sup> |

Bethanechol continues to be prescribed despite a weak evidence base; prescribing records do not establish prescribers' reasoning, but persistent use reflects the unmet need.<sup>[[3]](#ref3)[[9]](#ref9)</sup>

**Critical safety concern:** if the external sphincter fails to relax while bethanechol contracts the bladder, urine can be forced retrograde up the ureter, causing vesicoureteral reflux and, in the setting of bacteriuria, ascending pyelonephritis.<sup>[[4]](#ref4)</sup> Exclude bladder outlet obstruction and confirm reasonable sphincter coordination before initiating. For the full framework, see the [Cholinergic agonists hub](/docs/foundations/pharmacology/voiding-outlet/cholinergic-agonists).

### Bethanechol label essentials (US)

| Item | Label content<sup>[[4]](#ref4)</sup> |
|---|---|
| **Indication** | Acute postoperative and postpartum nonobstructive (functional) urinary retention; neurogenic atony of the bladder with retention |
| **Dose** | Usual adult oral dose 10–50 mg three or four times daily, individualized. Titration: 5–10 mg, repeated at hourly intervals until response or a cumulative 50 mg. Take on an empty stomach (1 h before or 2 h after meals) to limit nausea and vomiting |
| **Time course** | Onset sometimes within 30 min, usually maximal at 60–90 min; duration about 1 h at usual doses |
| **Contraindications** | Hyperthyroidism, peptic ulcer, latent or active bronchial asthma, pronounced bradycardia or hypotension, vasomotor instability, coronary artery disease, epilepsy, parkinsonism; mechanical obstruction and bladder neck obstruction; questionable GI or bladder wall strength or integrity; recent bladder surgery or GI resection and anastomosis; peritonitis, acute GI inflammation, marked vagotonia; hypersensitivity |
| **Adverse effects** | Rare orally, more frequent with dose increases: abdominal cramps, nausea, belching, diarrhea, salivation, urinary urgency, headache, malaise, flushing and sweating, hypotension with reflex tachycardia, bronchoconstriction, lacrimation, miosis |
| **Interaction** | Ganglion-blocking drugs: a critical fall in blood pressure may occur (abdominal symptoms usually precede it) |
| **Overdose** | Atropine is the specific antidote (label adult dose 0.6 mg, repeated every 2 h as needed) |

## Neostigmine — Specialty Perioperative Use

Neostigmine's urologic footprint is limited to one clinical niche and one preclinical signal.

### Postoperative urinary retention after spinal anesthesia

**Senapathi 2018 RCT** (n = 36 post-spinal-anesthesia patients, IM neostigmine 0.5 mg versus placebo):<sup>[[15]](#ref15)</sup>

- Median time to first voiding 40 min (range 20–70) on neostigmine versus 75 min (55–135) control (p ≤ 0.05)
- Time to first voiding after the spinal block 280.8 versus 364.2 min (p ≤ 0.05)

The effect size is real, but the clinical relevance is limited; most POUR resolves with time and single-catheterization, and neostigmine's cholinergic burden (bradycardia, bronchospasm, GI cramping) is a real perioperative cost.

### Diabetic bladder dysfunction — preclinical

In a rat model of diabetic cystopathy, neostigmine potentiated electrical-field-stimulation–induced bladder contractions more in diabetic strips than in controls, suggesting that elevated bladder cholinesterase activity may contribute to DUA in late-stage diabetic bladder, and that AChE inhibition could become a therapeutic target in this specific population.<sup>[[16]](#ref16)</sup> This has not translated into human trials.

## Pyridostigmine — The Emerging Agent

Two small 2025 randomized trials reported favorable outcomes. EAU 2026 recommends against prescribing parasympathomimetics for underactive detrusor; these studies do not establish a routine add-on regimen.<sup>[[24]](#ref24)[[25]](#ref25)</sup>

### Post-TURP underactive bladder

**Zeinelabden 2025 double-blind RCT** (n = 66 BPH patients with preoperative UAB undergoing TURP, randomized postoperatively to pyridostigmine 120 mg/day × 3 months versus placebo):<sup>[[18]](#ref18)</sup>

- Significant improvement in IPSS (p = 0.001)
- Significant QoL improvement (p &lt; 0.05)
- Uroflowmetry and PVR improvements versus placebo

The single-center study followed patients for only three months, excluded diabetes or neurological disease and selected surgical complications, and reported mild adverse effects in 8/34 treated patients (23.5%). Durability and broader applicability remain uncertain.

### Acute urinary retention due to BPH

**Mohamed 2025 RCT** (n = 140, first-episode AUR due to BPH, pyridostigmine 60 mg/day plus silodosin 8 mg versus silodosin alone):<sup>[[19]](#ref19)</sup>

- Successful trial without catheter (TWOC): 82.9% versus 67.1% (combination superior)
- Significant IPSS and uroflowmetry improvement at 2 wk and 3 mo (p = 0.001, p = 0.003)

For the reconstructive / functional urologist this is the relevant bottom line: this is a study-specific signal and not permission to add pyridostigmine routinely in obstructed BPH. Mechanical urinary obstruction is a labeled contraindication.

### Autonomic failure with combined orthostasis and bladder dysfunction

- **Yamamoto 2006 case report** (66-year-old man with autonomic failure): pyridostigmine 180 mg/day improved both orthostatic hypotension and underactive detrusor bladder dysfunction<sup>[[20]](#ref20)</sup>
- The cholinergic side-effect profile (urinary urgency, increased GI motility) is often therapeutically advantageous in the autonomic-failure population with concurrent constipation<sup>[[21]](#ref21)</sup>

Pyridostigmine is contraindicated in mechanical intestinal or urinary obstruction, and the label advises particular caution in bronchial asthma; its US labeling is for myasthenia gravis, so these urologic uses are off-label. Exclude obstruction before adding it to an α-blocker in any BPH scenario.<sup>[[17]](#ref17)</sup>

## Distigmine — The Japanese Option

Distigmine (used in Japan for detrusor underactivity and myasthenia gravis) is pharmacologically distinct from other AChE inhibitors:

- In guinea pigs, 0.1 mg/kg IV increased maximum intravesical pressure for ≥12 h despite falling plasma levels. This is not a human dosing instruction<sup>[[22]](#ref22)</sup>
- Compared head-to-head with neostigmine, pyridostigmine, and ambenonium in guinea-pig detrusor, distigmine's potentiating effect was significantly more sustained (&gt; 12 h versus approximately 3 h)<sup>[[8]](#ref8)</sup>
- Mechanistically, distigmine selectively potentiates the cholinergic component of parasympathetic nerve–induced bladder contraction without affecting the purinergic (ATP-mediated) component<sup>[[6]](#ref6)[[7]](#ref7)</sup>

Distigmine is not a US urologic therapy; availability and labeling differ internationally. Laboratory duration findings do not establish human efficacy or safety, and cholinergic crisis is a concern.<sup>[[6]](#ref6)[[7]](#ref7)[[8]](#ref8)[[22]](#ref22)</sup>

## Practical Pearls

- **Exclude outlet obstruction before prescribing any of these.** The uniform, worst-case failure mode is detrusor-against-outlet pressure rise, with risk of reflux, upper-tract damage, and, in SCI patients, autonomic dysreflexia.<sup>[[4]](#ref4)</sup>
- **CIC remains the gold standard** for chronic atonic bladder. No parasympathomimetic outperforms well-supported intermittent catheterization for bladder emptying.<sup>[[3]](#ref3)</sup>
- **Cholinergic crisis requires urgent resuscitation and toxicology input.** Atropine antagonizes muscarinic effects; a bethanechol-label schedule is not a universal protocol for every cholinesterase-inhibitor poisoning.<sup>[[4]](#ref4)</sup>
- **The Bethanechol Supersensitivity Test is of historical interest only.** Quote Pavlakis and Sidi if asked; quote Blaivas and Wheeler to explain why it is no longer used.<sup>[[10]](#ref10)[[11]](#ref11)[[12]](#ref12)[[13]](#ref13)</sup>
- **Electromotive bethanechol testing remains investigational or historical**, without broad validation for routine selection.<sup>[[14]](#ref14)</sup>
- **Pyridostigmine plus α-blocker is worth knowing about for first-episode BPH AUR.** Mohamed 2025 showed 82.9% versus 67.1% TWOC success.<sup>[[19]](#ref19)</sup> Needs independent replication and must not override mechanical-obstruction contraindications.
- **Pyridostigmine in autonomic failure** is the rare case where the side-effect profile (↑ GI motility, urinary urgency) is therapeutically desired.<sup>[[20]](#ref20)[[21]](#ref21)</sup>
- **Distigmine does not exist in the US pharmacopeia.** Prolonged animal pharmacodynamic effects are not a 12-hour human elimination half-life or proof of clinical benefit.<sup>[[8]](#ref8)[[22]](#ref22)</sup>
- **The DUA pharmacologic pipeline is active.** Acotiamide, ASP8302, neurokinin-2 agonists, TRPV-4 modulators are all under investigation.<sup>[[23]](#ref23)</sup> None of these investigational mechanisms should displace established emptying and upper-tract protection strategies.
