# Imipramine

Imipramine is a tertiary-amine tricyclic antidepressant (TCA) with a broader urologic footprint than any other TCA: it is the only TCA with an FDA-approved urologic indication (temporary adjunctive therapy for childhood nocturnal enuresis in patients ≥ 6 years) and the TCA most often cited off-label for overactive bladder, stress urinary incontinence, mixed urinary incontinence, and, less often, as part of multimodal therapy for interstitial cystitis / bladder pain syndrome.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[2]](#ref2)[[3]](#ref3)</sup> It lives in "legacy" not because it is obsolete (it may be considered by an enuresis specialist after other treatments fail<sup>[[8]](#ref8)</sup>) but because its narrow therapeutic index, cardiotoxicity, and lethal overdose profile have pushed it behind modern antimuscarinics and β₃-agonists for most adult voiding indications.

For class-level pharmacology, comparative TCA selection, and imipramine's role alongside amitriptyline, nortriptyline, and doxepin, see the [TCAs hub](/docs/foundations/pharmacology/neuropathic-pelvic-pain/tricyclic-antidepressants). For the serotonergic-adrenergic alternative with a cleaner cardiac profile, see [SNRIs](/docs/foundations/pharmacology/neuropathic-pelvic-pain/snris) and [Desmopressin](/docs/foundations/pharmacology/storage-oab/desmopressin) (first-line for nocturnal enuresis and nocturnal polyuria).

## Mechanism of Action — Why Imipramine Works in the Urinary Tract

Several mechanisms have been proposed for its urinary effects. The HCl label states that its enuresis mechanism is not established; preclinical and physiologic findings do not prove the relative contribution of each pathway:<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup>

| Mechanism | Clinical effect |
|---|---|
| **Muscarinic antagonism** | ↓ detrusor contractility, ↑ bladder capacity, ↓ uninhibited contractions<sup>[[2]](#ref2)[[4]](#ref4)</sup> |
| **NE + 5-HT reuptake inhibition** | ↑ sympathetic tone at bladder neck / proximal urethra → ↑ outlet resistance; central modulation of the micturition reflex at spinal and supraspinal levels<sup>[[2]](#ref2)[[3]](#ref3)[[4]](#ref4)</sup> |
| **Direct detrusor smooth-muscle inhibition** | Inhibits bethanechol- and BaCl₂-induced contractions independent of muscarinic blockade<sup>[[2]](#ref2)</sup> |
| **Central arousal / sleep-architecture modulation** | Proposed to improve arousability from deep sleep; not established as the anti-enuretic mechanism<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup> |
| **Vasopressin-independent antidiuretic effect** | Proposed ↑ α-adrenergic stimulation of proximal renal tubule → ↑ sodium / water / urea reabsorption → ↓ nocturnal urine output; seen in enuretics with nocturnal polyuria after a single 1 mg/kg dose (15 patients aged 15–37 years)<sup>[[5]](#ref5)</sup> |

Antimuscarinic action alone is unlikely to explain the anti-enuretic effect: in one retrospective series, most children who had failed desmopressin, an alarm and an anticholinergic still responded to imipramine.<sup>[[8]](#ref8)</sup>

## Formulations and Dosing

| Formulation | Strengths | Typical urologic dosing |
|---|---|---|
| Imipramine HCl tablets | 10, 25, 50 mg | Enuresis: 25–75 mg QHS (see below); historical adult studies used varying doses, not a routine OAB regimen<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[11]](#ref11)</sup> |
| Imipramine pamoate capsules | 75, 100, 125, 150 mg | Depression formulation; do not substitute its high-strength capsules into pediatric enuresis dosing |

### Enuresis (only FDA-approved urologic indication)<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup>

- The label requires organic causes to be excluded first, with VCUG and cystoscopy "as necessary" when daytime frequency or urgency is present; uncomplicated monosymptomatic bedwetting does not need them. Refer atypical or refractory cases.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[22]](#ref22)</sup>
- **Children ≥6 years:** start 25 mg 1 hour before bedtime; the HCl label permits 50 mg nightly in children under 12 if inadequate after 1 week.
- **Children over 12:** the label permits up to 75 mg nightly; follow the exact product or local pediatric protocol at age boundaries.
- **Maximum:** 75 mg/day in children; do not exceed 2.5 mg/kg/day.
- A dose above 75 mg does not improve efficacy and increases side effects. For early-night wetters the label suggests 25 mg in mid-afternoon, repeated at bedtime.
- Effectiveness may decrease with continued use; relapse on discontinuation is common (NICE: more than two in three children relapse after a 3-month course). Taper rather than stop abruptly, and consider a drug-free period after a good response. NICE advises a 3-month initial course and medical review every 3 months during repeated courses.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[22]](#ref22)</sup>

### Off-label adult voiding indications

- **OAB / detrusor overactivity:** titrated up to 150 mg QHS (or until continent or side effects) in the Castleden 1981 case series of 10 elderly patients<sup>[[11]](#ref11)</sup>. There is no guideline-endorsed OAB dose. The label's antidepressant geriatric dosing starts at 30–40 mg/day and generally does not exceed 100 mg/day.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup>
- **SUI:** 75 mg/day (dose used in the Gilja 1984 open-label series)<sup>[[14]](#ref14)</sup>; but see the Kornholt 2019 negative RCT below before prescribing.<sup>[[15]](#ref15)</sup>

Imipramine has a narrow therapeutic index and is among the most lethal medications in overdose. The label states that children are more sensitive than adults and that an acute overdose of any amount in an infant or young child must be considered serious and potentially fatal; toxicity (dysrhythmia, hypotension, convulsions) develops rapidly and needs hospital monitoring.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup> Counsel parents about secure storage and the danger of exceeding the prescribed amount;<sup>[[22]](#ref22)</sup> dispense limited quantities; assess cardiac history and obtain ECG monitoring according to age or risk and the specialist protocol.

## Indications in Reconstructive and Functional Urology

### 1. Childhood nocturnal enuresis — FDA-approved

**Specialist refractory option**, not a routine treatment step, after behavioral therapy, [desmopressin](/docs/foundations/pharmacology/storage-oab/desmopressin), enuresis alarms, and antimuscarinics have failed.<sup>[[7]](#ref7)[[8]](#ref8)</sup>

**Efficacy data:**

- **Cochrane 2016 update:** imipramine yields approximately 1 fewer wet night per week versus placebo (MD −0.95; 95% CI −1.40 to −0.50; 4 trials, 347 children); 78% of children fail to achieve 14 consecutive dry nights on imipramine versus 95% on placebo (RR 0.74; 95% CI 0.61–0.90; 12 trials, 831 children). The effect is short-term: 96% were wetting again at follow-up versus 97% on placebo, and alarms were more effective than tricyclics. Trial quality was often poor.<sup>[[4]](#ref4)</sup> Other drug classes (for example indomethacin, diazepam, atomoxetine) have been tested in small, mostly poor-quality trials.<sup>[[6]](#ref6)</sup>
- **Imipramine plus oxybutynin** outperforms placebo more convincingly (33% versus 78% failure to achieve 14 dry nights; RR 0.43) in one trial of 47 children. This limited signal is not a prescribing endorsement: NICE advises against combining imipramine with an anticholinergic for bedwetting.<sup>[[22]](#ref22)</sup><sup>[[4]](#ref4)</sup>
- **Gepertz & Nevéus 2004** (retrospective, n = 49 therapy-resistant enuretic children who had failed desmopressin, alarm, and antimuscarinic): 64.6% responded (at least 50% fewer wet nights), and 22 of the 31 responders became completely dry.<sup>[[8]](#ref8)</sup>
- **Favorable predictors** in the Gepertz cohort: older age, low spontaneous bladder capacity.<sup>[[8]](#ref8)</sup>
- **Poor predictors:** constipation, daytime incontinence; treat these first.<sup>[[8]](#ref8)</sup>

**ECG:** the label calls for an ECG before larger-than-usual doses and at intervals thereafter, and cardiac surveillance at every dose in patients with cardiovascular disease. Use specialist pediatric protocol for the baseline ECG decision in children without known cardiac disease.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup>

### 2. Overactive bladder / detrusor overactivity — off-label

The dual action, decreased detrusor contractility plus increased outlet resistance, is theoretically attractive, but data are thin and old.

- **Castleden 1981** (n = 10 elderly patients, unstable detrusor contractions, imipramine titrated to 150 mg QHS): 6/10 became continent. In the 3 of those 6 who had repeat cystometry, mean capacity rose 105 mL, bladder pressure at capacity fell 18 cm H₂O and urethral pressure rose 30 cm H₂O. Uncontrolled and very small.<sup>[[11]](#ref11)</sup>
- The AHCPR guideline cautioned that TCAs be reserved for carefully evaluated patients given the side-effect profile.<sup>[[12]](#ref12)</sup> A Cochrane review of anticholinergics versus other OAB drugs found only a single trial against a tricyclic, so no comparative conclusion is possible.<sup>[[13]](#ref13)</sup> Reviews describe imipramine as possibly useful, alone or with an antimuscarinic, but never adequately assessed, and its mechanism in OAB is unresolved.<sup>[[9]](#ref9)[[10]](#ref10)</sup>
- **Guideline position:** the AUA/SUFU 2024 idiopathic OAB guideline does not list tricyclics among its pharmacologic options,<sup>[[23]](#ref23)</sup> and NICE NG123 advises not offering imipramine to women for urinary incontinence or OAB.<sup>[[24]](#ref24)</sup> Antimuscarinics, β₃-agonists and onabotulinumtoxinA have displaced it from routine use.<sup>[[23]](#ref23)</sup>

### 3. Stress urinary incontinence — off-label, low-quality evidence against

- **Gilja 1984** (open-label, n = 30 women, 75 mg/day × 4 weeks): 71% became continent with extension of functional urethral length.<sup>[[14]](#ref14)</sup>
- **Kornholt 2019** (double-blind, placebo-controlled, crossover RCT in 16 healthy women, single 50 mg dose): urethral opening pressure rose by only 6.5 cm H₂O at rest and 7.9 cm H₂O during squeeze; neither statistically significant nor clinically relevant.<sup>[[15]](#ref15)</sup> Authors recommend against off-label use for SUI.
- The cited literature does not establish efficacy in a therapeutic trial of women with SUI; the Kornholt experiment measured an acute surrogate in healthy volunteers.<sup>[[3]](#ref3)</sup>
- For urethral-closure-pressure augmentation, [duloxetine](/docs/foundations/pharmacology/neuropathic-pelvic-pain/snris) has a better evidence base (EU-approved though not US-approved for SUI).

### 4. Mixed urinary incontinence — off-label, no data

- Recommended on mechanistic grounds (antimuscarinic for urge component, NE-reuptake for stress component), but no data exist on effectiveness in mixed UI.<sup>[[3]](#ref3)</sup>
- In practice, treat the dominant symptom with first-line therapy for that subtype rather than pursuing imipramine as a two-in-one.

### 5. Interstitial cystitis / bladder pain syndrome — other TCAs preferred

- For IC/BPS, amitriptyline is the TCA of choice and is the one specifically recommended in AUA and international guidelines.<sup>[[16]](#ref16)[[17]](#ref17)[[18]](#ref18)</sup>
- Network meta-analysis (Di 2021): amitriptyline reduced ICSI scores by MD −4.9 (95% CI −9.0 to −0.76) versus placebo.<sup>[[19]](#ref19)</sup>
- Imipramine is occasionally used when amitriptyline fails or is poorly tolerated, but has no dedicated IC/BPS trial data.<sup>[[20]](#ref20)[[21]](#ref21)</sup>
- See the [TCAs hub](/docs/foundations/pharmacology/neuropathic-pelvic-pain/tricyclic-antidepressants) for class-level IC/BPS detail and the limitations of dose-tolerant subgroup findings.

## Contraindications &amp; Precautions

**Boxed warning and contraindications**<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup>

- **Suicidality in children, adolescents, and young adults** (antidepressant class boxed warning; the label weighs it against clinical need, and the enuresis indication is the only pediatric use it recognizes)
- **Concomitant MAOIs** are contraindicated (hyperpyretic crisis, severe convulsions; potentiation can be fatal). Allow at least 14 days when switching from an MAOI
- **Acute recovery period after MI**
- **Known hypersensitivity**, including cross-sensitivity to other dibenzazepines (desipramine)

**Cardiac**<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup>

- QT prolongation, arrhythmias, heart block, orthostatic hypotension, tachycardia
- The label calls for ECG assessment before larger-than-usual doses and at intervals thereafter; cardiac disease requires surveillance at any dose. Pediatric or refractory prescribing needs specialist cardiac-risk assessment
- Use with caution in structural or conduction disease

**Anticholinergic (urologic relevance)**<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup>

- **Urinary retention.** The same mechanism that helps OAB can precipitate retention in men with BOO, patients with underactive detrusor, or those on concurrent antimuscarinics
- Anatomically narrow angles: mydriasis can precipitate angle-closure glaucoma (the label lists a history of narrow-angle glaucoma or urinary retention under "extreme caution"; open-angle glaucoma is not a risk factor)
- Xerostomia, constipation, blurred vision
- **Cognition and anticholinergic burden:** confusional states with hallucinations and disorientation occur, especially in the elderly, and the label warns of hyponatremia (SIADH) with higher risk in older or volume-depleted patients. Add imipramine to the anticholinergic tally when weighing dementia risk: AUA/SUFU advises discussing dementia and cognitive risk with patients on antimuscarinics and using extreme caution in those already at risk<sup>[[23]](#ref23)</sup>

**Neurologic**<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup>

- Lowers seizure threshold; caution in epilepsy
- Sedation, tremor, dizziness

**Endocrine / sexual**<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup>

- SIADH, gynecomastia, decreased libido, ejaculatory dysfunction

**Drug interactions**<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup>

- **CYP2D6 inhibitors** (fluoxetine, paroxetine, bupropion) substantially raise imipramine levels
- **MAOIs.** Contraindicated
- **Other anticholinergics.** Cumulative burden; risk of paralytic ileus; the label calls for close supervision and dose adjustment
- **Sympathomimetics and CNS depressants.** Potentiated
- **Alcohol.** Increased sedation and lowered seizure threshold

**Pregnancy and lactation**<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup>

- Use only if potential benefit justifies potential fetal risk; excreted in breast milk.

## Perioperative Considerations

- **Coordinate elective-surgery management with anesthesia and the prescriber.** The HCl label advises withholding as long as the clinical situation allows, while withdrawal and psychiatric indications also matter; avoid an automatic continue or abrupt-stop rule.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup>
- **ECG review** before any procedure requiring general anesthesia or agents that prolong QT (ondansetron, macrolides, fluoroquinolones, methadone).
- **Flag urinary retention risk** after any pelvic, prolapse, or sling procedure; imipramine adds anticholinergic load at the same time the bladder is recovering from surgical edema and opioid analgesia.
- **Avoid concomitant anticholinergics** where possible in the perioperative window; if unavoidable, monitor for post-op ileus.
- **Be wary of serotonin syndrome.** Combinations with linezolid, methylene blue (at intraoperative doses), tramadol, and other serotonergic agents can precipitate it. See [methylene blue](/docs/foundations/pharmacology/intraoperative-adjuncts/visualization-agents/methylene-blue) for the specific intraoperative caveat.

## Practical Pearls

- **Imipramine is the TCA with FDA-approved enuresis indication.** Amitriptyline is the TCA of choice for IC/BPS. Do not substitute one for the other without a mechanistic rationale.
- **Assess cardiac risk and ECG requirements** before treatment and escalation; use the label and specialist pediatric protocol.
- **Dispense small quantities.** TCA overdose is among the most lethal in medicine, particularly in households with young children.
- **Anticipate decreased efficacy over time** for enuresis; plan for drug holidays and eventual taper rather than indefinite use.
- **Taper; do not stop abruptly.** Cholinergic rebound (nausea, headache, malaise) is common.
- **Assess daytime incontinence and constipation first** in children with enuresis; these predict poor imipramine response and have their own first-line therapies.
- **Do not prescribe for SUI** on imipramine alone based on the Gilja series; the Kornholt 2019 RCT is methodologically stronger and negative.
- **In mixed UI, treat the dominant symptom** with first-line therapy for that subtype rather than relying on imipramine's dual mechanism.
- **Screen carefully for BOO** in men and for underactive detrusor in any adult before prescribing; imipramine can precipitate retention.
