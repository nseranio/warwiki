# VTE Prophylaxis — Drug-Class Hub

VTE prevention after urologic surgery depends on the operation, surgical approach, extent of lymphadenectomy, individual thrombosis risk and bleeding risk. Extended prophylaxis is well established after high-risk abdominopelvic cancer surgery, particularly radical cystectomy. A four-week anticoagulant course is not a default for every reconstructive operation.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[2]](#ref2)[[12]](#ref12)</sup>

This is the drug-class hub for agent selection, dosing and safety. Procedure-specific decisions, assessment of bleeding risk, antiplatelet management and interruption of chronic anticoagulation belong in [Antithrombotic therapy](/docs/foundations/perioperative-care/perioperative-protocols/antithrombotic-therapy). Treatment doses for an established DVT or PE are different from the prophylactic doses below.

## VTE Risk in Urologic Surgery

Risk differs sharply by operation. Radical cystectomy has the highest symptomatic VTE rate among urologic operations, and most events occur after the patient goes home.

| Procedure | Reported symptomatic VTE | Note |
|---|---|---|
| **Radical cystectomy (open or robotic)** | **2.6–11.6%** across series; 3.16% in the 2011–2020 NSQIP cohort | Highest-risk urologic operation.<sup>[[21]](#ref21)[[20]](#ref20)</sup> |
| **Open radical prostatectomy** | 0.8–15.7% | Wide range by series; comparable to cystectomy at the upper end.<sup>[[21]](#ref21)</sup> |
| **Robotic radical prostatectomy** | 0.2–0.9% | Low baseline risk.<sup>[[21]](#ref21)</sup> |
| **Open partial or radical nephrectomy** | 1.0–4.4% | <sup>[[21]](#ref21)</sup> |
| **Robotic partial or radical nephrectomy** | 0.7–3.9% | <sup>[[21]](#ref21)</sup> |

After radical cystectomy, 55–58% of VTE events were diagnosed after discharge: mean postoperative day 15.2 for all VTE diagnoses in a 1,307-patient NSQIP cohort, and median postoperative day 20 (range 2–91) for postdischarge VTE cases in a 2,316-patient single-center open-cystectomy series.<sup>[[19]](#ref19)[[22]](#ref22)[[18]](#ref18)</sup> That timing is the rationale for extended prophylaxis.

In the NSQIP analysis of 207,861 urologic cancer operations, the strongest independent predictors of VTE were cystectomy (OR 2.96), blood transfusion (OR 2.29), age 80 or older (OR 1.98), BMI 40 or more (OR 1.82), heart failure (OR 1.75), steroid use (OR 1.55), thrombocytosis (OR 1.43) and leukocytosis (OR 1.41).<sup>[[20]](#ref20)</sup>

## Who Benefits, When to Start and How Long

| Source / setting | Practical implication |
|---|---|
| **ESAIC urology 2024** | Recommend pharmacologic prophylaxis for open radical cystectomy and open radical prostatectomy with extended lymphadenectomy (Grade 1A or 1B by risk stratum); suggest mechanical prophylaxis as an adjunct (Grade 2C). Most other urologic procedures require procedure and patient risk assessment. For ambulatory day surgery, recommend against both pharmacologic and mechanical prophylaxis (Grade 1B each).<sup>[[12]](#ref12)</sup> |
| **Timing in that urology guideline** | Start on postoperative day 1, with hemostasis and neuraxial requirements considered; continue for **2–4 weeks when pharmacologic prophylaxis is indicated**. This differs from some drug labels and cancer guidelines that start before surgery.<sup>[[12]](#ref12)</sup> |
| **AUA/ASCO/SUO 2024 muscle-invasive bladder cancer** | Statement 16: give perioperative pharmacologic prophylaxis to patients undergoing radical cystectomy (strong recommendation, Grade B), combined with mechanical prophylaxis. The discussion supports considering a first dose just before induction if bleeding risk allows, and notes that up to four weeks of postoperative coverage may be beneficial; optimal timing and duration are not defined.<sup>[[29]](#ref29)</sup> |
| **ITAC 2022 cancer guidance** | Four weeks of LMWH after major abdominal or pelvic cancer surgery in patients without high bleeding risk. This recommendation does not cover every benign reconstruction.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup> |
| **ASCO 2023 update** | Extended LMWH remains an option for high-risk cancer surgery; **apixaban or rivaroxaban may be offered after initial LMWH/UFH**, with a **weak recommendation** because postoperative trial evidence is limited.<sup>[[2]](#ref2)</sup> |
| **TURP and radical prostatectomy** | ASH 2019 conditionally suggests against routine pharmacologic prophylaxis (very-low-certainty evidence); additional risks such as prior VTE, thrombophilia or malignancy may alter the decision. ESAIC differs for open prostatectomy with extended lymphadenectomy (above).<sup>[[3]](#ref3)[[12]](#ref12)</sup> |

A discharge prescription should name the total intended course and stop date, including inpatient doses, rather than automatically adding another 28 days at discharge. Reduced mobility or discharge alone does not determine the duration.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[12]](#ref12)</sup>

### Extended-Prophylaxis Evidence

- A 2021 meta-analysis of abdominopelvic cancer surgery (68 studies, 1.63 million patients) found a 30-day VTE rate of 1.7%. Extended LMWH was associated with less clinical VTE (1.0% versus 2.1%; RR 0.48, 95% CI 0.31–0.74) without a significant increase in clinically relevant bleeding (4.0% versus 4.9%).<sup>[[23]](#ref23)</sup>
- An earlier meta-analysis of seven randomized and prospective studies (4,807 patients) reported less VTE overall (2.6% versus 5.6%; RR 0.44) and less proximal DVT (1.4% versus 2.8%). Symptomatic PE, major bleeding and mortality did not differ significantly, and the benefit was not significant when only the randomized trials were pooled.<sup>[[24]](#ref24)</sup>
- ACCP 2012 recommends four weeks of postoperative LMWH over limited-duration prophylaxis for high-risk patients having abdominal or pelvic cancer surgery (Grade 1B).<sup>[[25]](#ref25)</sup>
- Procedure-level data are thin. A review of major urologic cancer operations found extended prophylaxis of net benefit after open prostatectomy and open or robotic cystectomy; the balance is closer for minimally invasive prostatectomy and nephrectomy, and no procedure-specific trial shows benefit for those.<sup>[[21]](#ref21)</sup>

### Caprini: Useful Risk Assessment, Not a Universal Treatment Cutoff

Pannucci's 2017 meta-analysis included 13 studies; 11 studies with 14,776 patients contributed VTE data. Chemoprophylaxis was associated with significant VTE reduction at Caprini scores 7–8 (OR 0.60, 95% CI 0.37–0.97) and above 8 (OR 0.41, 0.26–0.65). The absence of a statistically significant reduction at scores ≤6 does not prove zero benefit in every lower-scoring patient or override a procedure-specific recommendation. Most participants also received mechanical prophylaxis. Do not assign a cystectomy patient a score by default without checking the actual risk factors.<sup>[[4]](#ref4)</sup>

ACCP 2012 ties the choice of prophylaxis to baseline risk. Scores are usually mapped as 0 (very low), 1–2 (low), 3–4 (moderate) and 5 or more (high).<sup>[[25]](#ref25)</sup> Across specialties, a Caprini score of 5 or more is associated with a significant rise in VTE, and thresholds of 7 to 11 with a steep rise.<sup>[[26]](#ref26)</sup>

| Baseline VTE risk (ACCP 2012) | Approach |
|---|---|
| Very low (&lt;0.5%) | Early ambulation only |
| Low (~1.5%) | Mechanical prophylaxis, preferably IPC (Grade 2C) |
| Moderate (~3%), not at high bleeding risk | LMWH or low-dose UFH (2B) or IPC (2C) |
| High (~6%), not at high bleeding risk | LMWH or low-dose UFH (1B); suggest adding stockings or IPC (2C); four weeks of LMWH after abdominal or pelvic cancer surgery (1B) |
| Moderate to high VTE risk with high bleeding risk | IPC until bleeding risk falls, then add pharmacologic prophylaxis (2C) |

### Pelvic Reconstructive and Urogynecologic Surgery

VTE risk after prolapse and incontinence surgery varies by procedure and patient factors (age, obesity, prior VTE, malignancy, prolonged operation or immobility). A 2024 systematic review modeled four-week symptomatic VTE risk without pharmacologic prophylaxis at 1.5% after minimally invasive sacrocolpopexy with hysterectomy (3 studies, n = 1,234; 1.2–4.6% across patient-risk strata), 0.2% after vaginal prolapse repair without mesh (13 studies, n = 73,626), and &lt;0.1% after vaginal sling surgery (7 studies, n = 55,472).<sup>[[30]](#ref30)</sup> Select prophylaxis by operative and patient risk rather than a fixed regimen. ACOG Practice Bulletin 232 (2021) is the current gynecologic-surgery guidance and replaces Practice Bulletin 84; it uses a risk-based approach that pairs mechanical prophylaxis with pharmacologic prophylaxis as risk rises. ASH 2019 suggests pharmacologic prophylaxis over none for major gynecologic surgery.<sup>[[16]](#ref16)[[3]](#ref3)</sup> In a retrospective Premier database cohort of 214,427 prolapse operations, a single perioperative pharmacologic dose (13.7% of patients) was associated with more composite bleeding complications within 3 months (1.1% versus 0.7%) and slightly more VTE (0.23% versus 0.16%).<sup>[[17]](#ref17)</sup> Confounding by indication limits causal interpretation of this retrospective VTE comparison; routine chemoprophylaxis for every prolapse repair is unsupported, and VTE was uncommon.<sup>[[17]](#ref17)</sup>

## Enoxaparin — Low-Molecular-Weight Heparin

Enoxaparin acts through antithrombin, with greater anti-Xa than anti-IIa activity. The label's ratio of approximately 14:1 describes activity–time AUC after 1.5 mg/kg, not a universal in-vitro potency ratio or a prophylactic dosing target.<sup>[[5]](#ref5)</sup>

| Adult setting | Dose / label distinction |
|---|---|
| Abdominal-surgery prophylaxis | **40 mg SC daily**; the US label starts two hours preoperatively for a usual 7–10 days. Urologic guideline timing and extended duration may differ. |
| CrCl &lt;30 mL/min | **30 mg SC daily** for abdominal-surgery prophylaxis. |
| 30 mg twice daily | In the US label, 30 mg twice daily is the hip/knee replacement regimen, not the regimen for abdominal or pelvic surgery. A trauma guideline uses this dose in selected patients, but trauma is not a labeled indication.<sup>[[5]](#ref5)[[31]](#ref31)</sup> |
| Low weight / obesity | Increased exposure or uncertain prophylactic efficacy warrants individualized assessment. The label does **not** prescribe universal 20-mg underweight or 40-mg-twice-daily obesity regimens. |

**Pharmacokinetics (label):** in healthy subjects, mean absolute bioavailability based on anti-Xa activity is approximately 100% after a 1.5 mg/kg subcutaneous dose; anti-Xa activity peaks 3–5 hours after the dose; half-life is 4.5 hours after a single dose and about 7 hours with repeated dosing. Clearance is renal: after repeated 40 mg subcutaneous once-daily doses, steady-state anti-Xa AUC is on average 65% higher when CrCl is below 30 mL/min. After a single 40 mg dose, anti-Xa exposure is 52% higher in women under 45 kg and 27% higher in men under 57 kg.<sup>[[5]](#ref5)</sup>

**Boxed warning:** epidural or spinal hematoma in anticoagulated patients having neuraxial anesthesia or spinal puncture may cause long-term or permanent paralysis; the boxed text states that the optimal timing between enoxaparin and neuraxial procedures is not known. Section 5.1 of the label advises delaying catheter placement or removal at least 12 hours after lower doses (30 mg once or twice daily, 40 mg once daily) and at least 24 hours after higher doses (0.75 or 1 mg/kg twice daily, 1.5 mg/kg daily), and notes that anti-Xa activity is still detectable at those times. It suggests waiting at least 4 hours after catheter removal for the next dose. With CrCl below 30 mL/min it suggests doubling the removal interval (at least 24 hours after 30 mg daily, 48 hours after 1 mg/kg daily).<sup>[[5]](#ref5)</sup>

**Strengths and limits:** once-daily dosing and predictable pharmacokinetics; subcutaneous injection is a compliance barrier, and renal accumulation requires dose review.

Monitor bleeding, CBC/platelets and renal function. Anti-Xa testing is selective, not routine; PT/aPTT do not reliably measure enoxaparin effect. Contraindications include active major bleeding, relevant hypersensitivity, and HIT within 100 days or circulating antibodies. Any HIT history warrants specialist review. Protamine reverses anti-Xa activity incompletely, at most approximately 60%.<sup>[[5]](#ref5)[[6]](#ref6)</sup>

## Unfractionated Heparin

UFH enhances antithrombin inhibition of thrombin and factor Xa. The label describes the most widely used low-dose regimen as 5,000 units SC two hours before surgery and every 8–12 hours thereafter for 7 days or until the patient is fully ambulatory, whichever is longer, by deep subcutaneous injection. Choose the actual surgical start time with the operative/anesthesia team. Severe renal impairment can favor UFH because of its shorter, more readily reversible effect; it still requires bleeding and platelet surveillance. Weight-based escalations or reductions need an institutional protocol, not an automatic BMI rule.<sup>[[13]](#ref13)[[12]](#ref12)</sup>

In severe renal impairment (CrCl below 30 mL/min), UFH is the usual alternative to LMWH because it does not accumulate renally and is fully reversible with protamine. The trade-offs are dosing two or three times daily, less predictable response, and a higher HIT risk than LMWH, so platelet counts need surveillance.<sup>[[13]](#ref13)[[25]](#ref25)</sup> UFH can cause hyperkalemia in chronic renal failure; check potassium before treatment in at-risk patients and periodically if treatment exceeds five days.<sup>[[13]](#ref13)</sup>

UFH can cause HIT and is not interchangeable with LMWH dose for dose. Neuraxial compatibility depends on dose and timing, rather than simply whether the prescription says twice daily.<sup>[[13]](#ref13)[[6]](#ref6)</sup>

## Oral Factor Xa Inhibitors

### Apixaban

Apixaban is a direct factor Xa inhibitor. 2.5 mg orally twice daily is the studied prophylactic dose in the gynecologic cancer trial below. Use after urologic cancer surgery is off-label in the US; FDA postoperative approval concerns hip/knee replacement. ASCO supports it as a selected extended-prophylaxis option after initial heparin, not the preferred drug for all pelvic reconstruction.<sup>[[8]](#ref8)[[14]](#ref14)[[2]](#ref2)</sup>

Renal excretion accounts for approximately 27% of clearance, which does not establish superior safety in renal failure. US labeled adult VTE indications have no renal dose adjustment, but efficacy/safety trials excluded dialysis and CrCl &lt;15 mL/min; this does not validate postoperative urologic use in those patients. Reassess acute kidney injury, hepatic disease, bleeding and oral absorption. Avoid combined strong CYP3A4/P-gp inhibitors when already using 2.5 mg twice daily, and avoid strong combined inducers. Active pathological bleeding and severe hypersensitivity are formal contraindications. Neuraxial hematoma is a boxed-warning risk.<sup>[[8]](#ref8)</sup>

| Setting | Apixaban dose |
|---|---|
| Postoperative prophylaxis after cystectomy or gynecologic cancer surgery (extended course) | 2.5 mg twice daily for 28 days in the gynecologic trial; off-label in urology.<sup>[[14]](#ref14)</sup> |
| Hip or knee replacement (US label) | 2.5 mg twice daily starting 12–24 hours after surgery: 12 days for knee, 35 days for hip.<sup>[[8]](#ref8)</sup> |

### What the Main Studies Establish

| Study | Result | Limit |
|---|---|---|
| **Guntupalli 2020**, 400 women randomized after surgery for suspected/confirmed gynecologic cancer | Apixaban 2.5 mg twice daily versus enoxaparin 40 mg daily for 28 days. Major bleeding was 1/204 versus 1/196; VTE 2/204 versus 3/196. Oral administration was easier and less painful.<sup>[[14]](#ref14)</sup> | Primarily a safety trial with few events and wide confidence intervals; no proof of equivalent efficacy for every urologic procedure. |
| **Westerman 2022**, prospective single-center quality-improvement study | Sequential enoxaparin (161 patients) and apixaban (154) periods: compliance barriers 33.5% versus 14.3%; 30-day postdischarge symptomatic VTE/major-bleeding composite 3.1% versus 0%.<sup>[[9]](#ref9)</sup> | Met the prespecified noninferiority threshold and favored apixaban statistically, but **not randomized**; temporal/selection confounding and rare events limit causal claims of superior safety. |
| **SUO survey conducted in 2023**, published online 2025 | Of **62 respondents, a 6% response rate**, 98% reported extended prophylaxis after cystectomy and 70% prescribed apixaban.<sup>[[7]](#ref7)</sup> | Practice-pattern evidence from respondents; not 70% of all urologic oncologists and not an efficacy recommendation. |

### Rivaroxaban

PROLAPS II randomized 582 patients after laparoscopic colorectal cancer surgery. Following LMWH from surgery to randomization at day 7±2, rivaroxaban 10 mg daily or placebo was given for three weeks. The day-28 composite of symptomatic VTE, screening-detected DVT or VTE-related death occurred in 3/287 versus 11/282 patients (1.0% versus 3.9%); major bleeding occurred in two rivaroxaban patients and none receiving placebo. Recruitment ended early because the study drug expired.<sup>[[15]](#ref15)</sup>

Compared with apixaban, rivaroxaban is once daily, was started after a week of LMWH in that trial, and has less urologic evidence. For adults, its only US-labeled indication for DVT prophylaxis following surgery is hip or knee replacement (10 mg daily for 35 or 12 days, started 6–10 hours after surgery once hemostasis is established), and the label advises avoiding it when CrCl is below 15 mL/min.<sup>[[28]](#ref28)</sup>

This supports the ASCO option in an appropriate cancer-surgery setting. It is not a urologic diversion trial or a universal requirement for seven days of “bridging” before any rivaroxaban indication. Verify the current agent-specific label, kidney/liver function and interactions when selecting it; do not substitute apixaban's renal rules.<sup>[[2]](#ref2)[[15]](#ref15)</sup>

### Bowel Resection and Oral Intake

Ileus, vomiting, inability to take oral medication or extensive intestinal resection can make oral prophylaxis unreliable. Consider a parenteral agent until absorption is dependable. An ileal conduit alone does not establish DOAC malabsorption; cystectomy observational outcomes cannot prove adequate absorption for every diversion anatomy.<sup>[[12]](#ref12)[[9]](#ref9)</sup>

## Neuraxial Safety — Confirm Both Timing and Dose

**ASRA fifth edition (2025):** the following are selected adult intervals for standard low-dose regimens. Renal impairment, traumatic placement, other antithrombotics or higher doses require the full anesthesia plan. Surgical hemostasis requires assessment beyond minimum catheter intervals.<sup>[[6]](#ref6)</sup>

| Regimen | Selected neuraxial requirements |
|---|---|
| **Enoxaparin once-daily low dose** | Placement ≥12 hours after last dose. First postoperative dose ≥12 hours after placement; second ≥24 hours later. Catheter may remain with appropriate precautions; remove ≥12 hours after last dose, then wait ≥4 hours before dosing. |
| **Enoxaparin twice-daily low dose** | First dose the following day and ≥12 hours after placement. Remove catheter **before starting** this regimen; wait ≥4 hours after removal. |
| **UFH 5,000 units SC twice/three times daily** | Placement and removal generally ≥4–6 hours after a dose. Catheter may remain; subsequent low-dose UFH may follow removal immediately. |
| **Low-dose apixaban** | Hold ≥36 hours before placement; placement/removal ≥6 hours before first postoperative dose. If inadvertently given with a catheter in place, withhold ≥36 hours or follow ASRA drug-level guidance before removal. |
| **Low-dose rivaroxaban** | Hold ≥24 hours before placement (≥30 hours if CrCl &lt;30 mL/min); placement/removal ≥6 hours before first postoperative dose. A residual level &lt;30 ng/mL (anti-Xa ≤0.1 IU/mL) is acceptable if placement is planned sooner. All ASRA grade IIC.<sup>[[6]](#ref6)</sup> |

The product labels give their own catheter rules. Apixaban: do not remove an indwelling epidural or intrathecal catheter earlier than 24 hours after the last dose, give the next dose no earlier than 5 hours after removal, and delay dosing 48 hours after a traumatic puncture. Rivaroxaban: do not remove a catheter before two half-lives have elapsed (18 hours at ages 20 to 45, 26 hours at ages 60 to 76), give the next dose no earlier than 6 hours after removal, and delay dosing 24 hours after a traumatic puncture. Both carry a boxed warning for spinal or epidural hematoma.<sup>[[8]](#ref8)[[28]](#ref28)</sup>

High-dose apixaban uses different intervals: ≥72 hours before placement and ≥24 hours from placement/removal to the first postoperative dose. Reduced renal clearance can prolong LMWH activity (half-life up to 16 hours in severe renal insufficiency, per ASRA); the usual 12-hour rule is insufficient as a blanket instruction in severe renal impairment, and ASRA describes the ESAIC/ESRA approach of doubling the interval or halving the dose when CrCl is below 30 mL/min, with an anti-Xa level of ≤0.1 IU/mL as the suggested acceptable residual. An urgent new neurologic deficit after neuraxial anesthesia requires immediate assessment for hematoma.<sup>[[6]](#ref6)[[5]](#ref5)</sup>

## Intermittent Pneumatic Compression

IPC reduces venous stasis without adding an anticoagulant drug. It is a useful adjunct in selected high-risk surgical patients and an alternative while pharmacologic prophylaxis is unsafe. Proper fit, actual wear and skin/limb assessment matter; assess arterial disease, wounds and suspected/known DVT against the device's instructions rather than treating compression as universally safe.<sup>[[12]](#ref12)[[1]](#ref1)</sup>

The 2022 Cochrane review included 34 studies and 14,931 participants, mostly surgery or trauma. Adding IPC to pharmacologic prophylaxis reduced DVT (OR 0.38, 95% CI 0.21–0.70; high certainty) and PE (OR 0.46, 0.30–0.71; low certainty). Bleeding estimates were very uncertain. Adding an anticoagulant to IPC alone also reduced VTE but increased bleeding; the two comparisons must not be conflated.<sup>[[10]](#ref10)</sup>

In immobile stroke patients (CLOTS 3, 2,876 patients), IPC reduced DVT, which supports its use where pharmacologic prophylaxis is unsafe; the population is not surgical.<sup>[[27]](#ref27)</sup>

In contrast, PREVENT randomized 2,003 ICU patients already receiving pharmacologic prophylaxis and found no significant reduction in new proximal DVT with adjunctive IPC among those evaluable for the primary outcome: 37/957 (3.9%) versus 41/985 (4.2%), RR 0.93 (95% CI 0.60–1.44). This does not settle every elective surgical setting.<sup>[[11]](#ref11)</sup>

Ambulation alone is not a substitute for formal prophylaxis in a high-risk patient; ACCP 2012 limits ambulation-only management to very-low-risk patients.<sup>[[25]](#ref25)</sup>

## Bleeding and Contraindications

Active significant bleeding may require withholding pharmacologic prophylaxis and using appropriate mechanical measures until reassessment. Platelet count, trend, cause of thrombocytopenia, recent bleeding, kidney/liver function and neuraxial procedures all affect that decision. INR above 1.5 is assessed with bleeding risk; HIT and hypersensitivity contraindications are agent-specific. Reassess daily rather than leaving an initial hold in place indefinitely.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[5]](#ref5)[[8]](#ref8)[[13]](#ref13)</sup>

A prophylactic IVC filter should not be used as a substitute for pharmacologic prophylaxis in a patient who cannot receive it. ASCO guidance does not recommend prophylactic filter placement for primary VTE prevention in major cancer surgery, because no randomized trial establishes benefit and the device carries long-term harms. Use mechanical prophylaxis and reassess for pharmacologic prophylaxis as bleeding risk falls instead; this is distinct from the ASCO option of a filter for acute VTE diagnosed within four weeks when anticoagulation is absolutely contraindicated and the thrombus burden is considered life-threatening.<sup>[[2]](#ref2)</sup>

## Procedure Summary

| Procedure | Practical approach |
|---|---|
| Cystectomy with diversion | Pharmacologic prophylaxis plus IPC; extended course of about 4 weeks with LMWH, or apixaban after an initial LMWH or UFH period in selected cancer patients (ASCO 2023: weak recommendation, low-quality evidence). Total duration and stop date named at discharge.<sup>[[12]](#ref12)[[1]](#ref1)[[2]](#ref2)</sup> |
| Open radical prostatectomy with extended lymphadenectomy | Pharmacologic prophylaxis per ESAIC 2024; extended course individualized.<sup>[[12]](#ref12)</sup> |
| Robotic prostatectomy, nephrectomy, RPLND | Individualize by risk; only about 30% of surveyed SUO respondents used extended prophylaxis after RALP or RPLND.<sup>[[7]](#ref7)[[21]](#ref21)</sup> |
| TURP | Not routine; ASH 2019 suggests against pharmacologic prophylaxis.<sup>[[3]](#ref3)</sup> |

## Renal Impairment After Diversion

Patients having cystectomy often have reduced renal function from obstruction, chemotherapy or a solitary kidney. Choose the agent for the current CrCl.

| Agent | CrCl below 30 mL/min |
|---|---|
| Enoxaparin | 30 mg once daily for abdominal-surgery prophylaxis.<sup>[[5]](#ref5)</sup> |
| UFH | No renal adjustment; usual choice.<sup>[[13]](#ref13)</sup> |
| Apixaban | Trials excluded CrCl below 15 mL/min; individualize.<sup>[[8]](#ref8)</sup> |
| Rivaroxaban | Avoid below 15 mL/min.<sup>[[28]](#ref28)</sup> |

## Practical Pearls

- Radical cystectomy carries the highest urologic VTE risk, and more than half of events occur after discharge.<sup>[[19]](#ref19)[[22]](#ref22)</sup>
- LMWH remains the established extended agent.<sup>[[2]](#ref2)</sup> Among surveyed SUO respondents, apixaban was the most-prescribed option; they reported injection-adherence barriers for enoxaparin and cost/insurance barriers for apixaban.<sup>[[7]](#ref7)</sup> A review of four nonrandomized comparative urologic studies (856 patients, mostly cystectomy), including Westerman’s prospective study, found no statistically significant VTE or bleeding difference between DOACs and LMWH at study-specific follow-up of 30 to 90 days; evidence is insufficient to determine equivalent efficacy.<sup>[[9]](#ref9)[[32]](#ref32)</sup> A gynecologic-oncology randomized trial also informs selected apixaban use but does not establish efficacy across urologic procedures.<sup>[[14]](#ref14)</sup>
- When CrCl is below 30 mL/min, UFH is an option; the labeled enoxaparin dose for abdominal-surgery prophylaxis is 30 mg SC daily.<sup>[[5]](#ref5)[[13]](#ref13)</sup>
- Add IPC for high-risk cancer surgery; use it alone while pharmacologic prophylaxis is contraindicated.<sup>[[10]](#ref10)[[25]](#ref25)</sup>
- Consider absorption before prescribing a DOAC after long bowel resection or with ileus.
- Coordinate enoxaparin with neuraxial catheter timing by regimen: for once-daily low-dose use, place or remove a catheter at least 12 hours after the last dose and give the next dose at least 4 hours after removal; remove the catheter before starting twice-daily low-dose use. With CrCl &lt;30 mL/min, the label suggests waiting at least 24 hours before catheter removal after 30 mg once daily.<sup>[[5]](#ref5)[[6]](#ref6)</sup>
