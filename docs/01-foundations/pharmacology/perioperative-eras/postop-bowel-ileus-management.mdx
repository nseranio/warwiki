# Postoperative Bowel &amp; Ileus Management — Drug-Class Hub

Postoperative ileus (POI) can delay recovery after GU operations involving bowel — urinary diversion (ileal conduit, orthotopic neobladder, Mainz, Indiana), augmentation cystoplasty, continent catheterizable channels, and large pelvic reconstruction with bowel resection or interposition. Reported POI rates vary with definition, procedure, approach and ERAS pathway; it is an important cause of prolonged admission and readmission.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[2]](#ref2)[[3]](#ref3)</sup> Different bowel segments recover at different rates, but clinical ileus is multifactorial. Do not use fixed segment-recovery times to dismiss persistent vomiting, pain or distention; obstruction, leak, infection and metabolic disturbances need consideration.<sup>[[5]](#ref5)</sup>

This article is the drug-class pharmacology hub, covering agents, mechanisms, doses, indication-by-indication evidence and safety ceilings. The clinical workflow for post-op constipation and ileus management (bowel prep, ERAS prophylactic regimens, stepped escalation and the PAMORA framework) lives at [Postoperative constipation &amp; ileus](/docs/foundations/perioperative-care/postoperative-management/constipation) and should not be re-derived here. Use the two together: workflow on one page, pharmacology on this one.

For adjacent topics see [NSAIDs &amp; analgesics](/docs/foundations/pharmacology/neuropathic-pelvic-pain/nsaids-analgesics) (opioid-sparing analgesia), [ERAS](/docs/foundations/perioperative-care/perioperative-protocols/eras), and [Nausea &amp; vomiting](/docs/foundations/perioperative-care/intraoperative-care/nausea-vomiting).

|---|---|---|---|
| **PAMORA — Alvimopan** | Peripheral μ-opioid receptor antagonist (blocks GI opioid effect without crossing BBB) | Accelerate GI recovery after surgery including bowel resection / primary anastomosis | Moderate-certainty cystectomy recovery evidence; strongest for predominantly open cohorts<sup>[[6]](#ref6)[[7]](#ref7)[[8]](#ref8)</sup> |
| **Prokinetic — Metoclopramide** | D₂ antagonist; sensitizes upper GI to ACh | Antiemetic with modest upper-GI prokinetic effect | Low for POI; moderate for PONV<sup>[[10]](#ref10)[[12]](#ref12)[[13]](#ref13)</sup> |
| **Prokinetic — Neostigmine** | AChE inhibitor | **Acute colonic pseudo-obstruction (Ogilvie)** — a guideline-supported role after failed supportive care | **High** for ACPO; low for routine POI<sup>[[21]](#ref21)[[22]](#ref22)[[23]](#ref23)</sup> |
| **Stimulant laxative — Bisacodyl** | Prodrug → antiabsorptive, secretory, prokinetic on colon | Selected postoperative constipation regimen; not treatment of obstruction | Moderate in colorectal; extrapolated<sup>[[17]](#ref17)</sup> |

## 1. Alvimopan — Selected Perioperative GI Recovery

Alvimopan is the best-studied pharmacologic agent for POI prevention after open bowel-segment GU surgery with a placebo-controlled cystectomy trial demonstrating faster GI recovery and shorter admission. The 2024 AUA/ASCO/SUO guideline recommends μ-opioid antagonist therapy after radical cystectomy unless contraindicated (Strong Recommendation; Evidence Level: Grade B).<sup>[[37]](#ref37)</sup> The US indication is recovery after surgery that includes partial bowel resection with primary anastomosis, not every operation near bowel.<sup>[[4]](#ref4)</sup><sup>[[6]](#ref6)</sup>

### Mechanism

**Peripherally acting μ-opioid receptor antagonist (PAMORA)**: K_i 0.4 nM at the μ receptor, with slower dissociation kinetics than other μ ligands. Has limited central penetration, so it blocks opioid-induced GI dysmotility while preserving central analgesia from perioperative opioids.<sup>[[4]](#ref4)</sup>

### Dosing

- **12 mg PO 30 min – 5 h before surgery**, then **12 mg PO twice daily** starting POD 1 until discharge
- **Maximum 15 total doses or 7 days** — whichever comes first
- **Inpatient use only** through the **Alvimopan REMS Program**<sup>[[4]](#ref4)</sup>

### Efficacy in radical cystectomy with urinary diversion

**Lee 2014 multicenter RCT** (280 randomized; 277 modified intention-to-treat):<sup>[[6]](#ref6)</sup>

- **GI-2 recovery** (first solid food and BM): 5.5 vs 6.8 days (HR 1.8; p &lt; 0.0001)<sup>[[6]](#ref6)</sup>
- **Length of stay:** reduced by approximately 2.7 days
- **POI-related morbidity:** 8.4% versus 29.1%; this was a defined morbidity outcome, not simply any delayed bowel movement<sup>[[6]](#ref6)</sup>
- **Cochrane 2017** found moderate-certainty evidence for faster GI recovery / discharge, based on this single eligible trial, not independent randomized replication<sup>[[7]](#ref7)</sup>

### Real-world and ERAS-integrated data

- **Belle 2019** Vizient database (n = 7,472 cystectomies 2014–2016): alvimopan utilization increased from 35% to 59%; associated with decreased perioperative morbidity (10.5% vs 19.2%; p = 0.027)<sup>[[8]](#ref8)</sup>
- **Hanna 2021:** in sequential retrospective cystectomy cohorts, ERAS plus alvimopan (n = 48) versus ERAS alone (n = 102) was associated with shorter median LOS (7 vs 9 days; p = 0.012) and less POI (14.6% vs 36.3%; p = 0.005). Median time to first BM was 4 days in both ERAS groups, versus 5 days before ERAS (overall p = 0.018).<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup>
- **Robotic applicability is less certain.** Small retrospective subgroups with no detected difference cannot establish absence of benefit or a universal open-versus-robotic POI rate<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[3]](#ref3)</sup>

**Alhashemi 2021 systematic review:** low-to-moderate certainty evidence for open bowel resection and open cystectomy with diversion; very-low-certainty for MIS. This is the framework for patient selection; the strongest direct cystectomy evidence comes from predominantly open surgery with postoperative IV opioid analgesia; contemporary robotic / opioid-sparing pathways may have different absolute benefit.<sup>[[9]](#ref9)</sup>

### Safety and the FDA boxed warning

**FDA Boxed Warning.** A 12-month trial of alvimopan **0.5 mg BID for chronic non-cancer pain** showed a greater incidence of myocardial infarction vs placebo. A causal relationship has not been established, and **this imbalance was not observed in short-term trials of surgery that included bowel resection** (alvimopan 12 mg for up to 7 days, n = 1,142 vs placebo, n = 1,120).<sup>[[4]](#ref4)</sup> Alvimopan is therefore restricted to **short-term inpatient use only** (≤ 15 doses, ≤ 7 days) through the REMS program.

### Contraindication versus populations where use is not recommended

The label contraindicates alvimopan after therapeutic opioid doses for more than seven consecutive days immediately before treatment. Patients given more than three opioid doses in the week before surgery were not studied in the postoperative-ileus trials; if treated, monitor for abdominal pain, nausea, vomiting and diarrhea.<sup>[[4]](#ref4)</sup> It separately says use is not recommended in severe hepatic impairment, ESRD, complete GI obstruction / surgery to correct it, and pancreatic or gastric anastomoses. These distinctions matter; no category implies established safety for an unstudied setting. Monitor for GI adverse effects with lesser renal / hepatic impairment.<sup>[[4]](#ref4)</sup>

In trials of surgery that included bowel resection, the most common adverse reaction occurring more often with alvimopan was dyspepsia (1.5% vs 0.8% with placebo).<sup>[[4]](#ref4)</sup>

### Other PAMORAs

For opioid-induced constipation outside the immediate perioperative window, especially in patients on chronic opioids for pelvic pain, IC/BPS flares or oncologic survivorship, methylnaltrexone, naloxegol and naldemedine are the relevant agents. See the PAMORA framework on [Postoperative constipation &amp; ileus](/docs/foundations/perioperative-care/postoperative-management/constipation) for indication-by-indication detail.

## 2. Metoclopramide — Antiemetic with Modest Upper-GI Prokinetic Effect

### Mechanism

**D₂-receptor antagonist** that sensitizes GI tissues to ACh, with increased gastric antral contractions, pyloric relaxation and duodenal or jejunal peristalsis. Has little effect on colonic motility; upper-GI pharmacology does not establish efficacy for postoperative ileus.<sup>[[10]](#ref10)</sup>

### Evidence in urinary-diversion surgery

**Donat 1999** (81 after cystectomy with diversion; 27 intervention, 54 concurrent controls) combined IV metoclopramide with NG removal planned before 24 hours (achieved in 78%):<sup>[[12]](#ref12)</sup>

- Faster return of bowel sounds: 2.9 vs 4.0 days (p = 0.0002)
- Earlier solid food tolerance: 6.7 vs 7.9 days (p = 0.04)
- Lower atelectasis: 15% versus 33% in the combined intervention; individual contributions cannot be isolated<sup>[[12]](#ref12)</sup>

The older review and EAST 2019 systematic guideline did not find evidence that metoclopramide hastens ileus resolution. EAST could not recommend for or against it because evidence was limited; this is not an established POI treatment.<sup>[[11]](#ref11)[[13]](#ref13)</sup>

### Current ERAS role

- Included in approximately 44% of protocols in a 2020 review; frequency of use does not prove anti-ileus efficacy<sup>[[14]](#ref14)</sup>
- Primary role is PONV prophylaxis with modest prokinetic adjunct, not primary POI prevention
- Do not schedule repeated doses solely because bowel function has not returned; identify the cause

### Dosing and safety

| Element | Detail |
|---|---|
| **Adult PONV label example** | 10 or 20 mg **IM once near the end of surgery**; this is not a scheduled IV ileus regimen |
| **Renal adjustment for repeated injection doses** | Current cited label: CrCl ≤60 mL/min → half the normal dose; ESRD → one quarter. Follow the exact indication / product |
| **FDA boxed warning** | Potentially irreversible tardive dyskinesia; risk increases with cumulative exposure and duration (the label advises avoiding more than 12 weeks in diabetic gastroparesis, and that limit is not relevant to postoperative use); a single dose can still cause acute dystonic or other extrapyramidal reactions |
| **Important exclusions** | Prior TD / dystonic reaction, GI hemorrhage / obstruction / perforation, pheochromocytoma / catecholamine-releasing paraganglioma, epilepsy or hypersensitivity |
| **Other precautions** | Avoid in Parkinson disease and with interacting dopamine blockers; assess sedation and neurologic reactions. For repeated doses in Child-Pugh B or C, reduce the dose by half. After bowel anastomosis, weigh the label's theoretical suture-line pressure concern before use. |

These dose and safety points come from the April 2026 injection label, not from proof of postoperative-ileus efficacy.<sup>[[10]](#ref10)</sup>

## 3. Neostigmine — For Acute Colonic Pseudo-Obstruction, Not Routine POI

Neostigmine has a specific, defined role in urology: treatment of acute colonic pseudo-obstruction (ACPO, or Ogilvie syndrome), which can complicate major pelvic GU surgery. It is not appropriate for routine POI prophylaxis.<sup>[[23]](#ref23)</sup>

### Mechanism

Acetylcholinesterase inhibition increases ACh at muscarinic receptors throughout the GI tract and stimulates colonic motility.

### Evidence in ACPO — strong

ASCRS 2021 gives neostigmine after failed supportive treatment a strong 1B recommendation. ASGE 2020 recommends neostigmine for eligible patients after failed or unsuitable conservative care, or when perforation risk warrants escalation.<sup>[[21]](#ref21)[[22]](#ref22)</sup>

- **Ponec 1999 small placebo-controlled RCT:** 10/11 patients receiving 2 mg IV neostigmine initially responded, versus 0/10 with placebo; median response time was four minutes.<sup>[[22]](#ref22)</sup> Recurrence and need for further decompression remained possible
- **Meta-analysis:** single IV 2–5 mg dose, 60–94% success; recurrence 0–31%; overall long-term response 69–100%<sup>[[22]](#ref22)</sup>
- **Second dose** for initial nonresponders: effective in 40–100%<sup>[[22]](#ref22)</sup>

### ACPO treatment boundaries

Exclude mechanical obstruction and assess for ischemia or perforation before pharmacologic decompression. For ACPO with cecal diameter &lt;12 cm and no fever, leukocytosis, significant pain, peritonitis or signs of ischemia or perforation, initial care includes correcting electrolytes and precipitating medications, bowel rest, and appropriate decompression; avoid oral osmotic and stimulant laxatives while the colon is dilated.<sup>[[21]](#ref21)[[22]](#ref22)</sup> Persistent symptoms after approximately 48–72 hours or increasing dilation require gastroenterology / surgical reassessment; clinical deterioration, peritonitis or suspected ischemia or perforation is not an automatic neostigmine indication.<sup>[[21]](#ref21)[[22]](#ref22)</sup>

For eligible ACPO after conservative treatment fails, ASGE recommends 2 mg IV over 3–5 minutes, with continuous cardiac-rhythm and respiratory monitoring and immediate access to atropine. Bronchospasm, bradycardia, vomiting, salivation and cramping can occur. Repeat dosing or infusion / alternative routes require specialist-directed selection. ASGE also offers colonoscopic decompression with a decompression tube as an alternative after failed conservative care, and ASCRS recommends considering it when neostigmine is contraindicated or ineffective.<sup>[[21]](#ref21)[[22]](#ref22)</sup>

Intestinal or urinary obstruction and hypersensitivity are contraindications. Bradycardia, asthma, renal insufficiency, recent MI, acidosis and peptic ulcer disease are important relative contraindications in the ASGE guidance; assess the individual risk rather than treating them all as equivalent absolute exclusions.<sup>[[21]](#ref21)</sup>

### Routine POI — not indicated

The Traut 2008 Cochrane review of systemic prokinetics for POI found neostigmine "might show effects" on recovery time, but the evidence was limited to small trials of moderate-to-poor quality. Neostigmine is not recommended for routine POI prophylaxis — its role is reserved for established ACPO or refractory ileus after expert assessment.<sup>[[23]](#ref23)</sup>

## 3a. Other Agents — Methylnaltrexone, Erythromycin, Intravenous Lidocaine, and Nasogastric Decompression

| Agent | What to know | Status |
|---|---|---|
| **Methylnaltrexone** | Peripheral μ-antagonist labeled only for opioid-induced constipation (chronic non-cancer pain; advanced illness). **Contraindicated in known or suspected GI obstruction or increased risk of recurrent obstruction** (perforation risk); the Relistor label also reports GI perforation in patients with opioid-induced constipation and advanced illness who had vulnerable bowel conditions including Ogilvie syndrome.<sup>[[31]](#ref31)</sup> A retrospective cohort of 110 patients with established POI (28 treated) found no difference in time to resolution (9.9 vs 11.4 days), LOS or decompression time.<sup>[[32]](#ref32)</sup> | Off-label for POI; use only after obstruction is excluded |
| **Erythromycin** | Motilin agonist. EAST 2019 (low-certainty evidence) found no effect on ileus resolution and made no recommendation for or against it.<sup>[[11]](#ref11)</sup> | Not established for POI |
| **IV lidocaine infusion** | Opioid-sparing multimodal adjunct. The Cochrane review (68 trials, 2018) graded effects on ileus and time to first bowel movement as very-low-certainty and dose and duration varied widely (1–5 mg/kg/h).<sup>[[33]](#ref33)</sup> The pragmatic ALLEGRO trial (557 analyzed, minimally invasive colectomy; 1.5 mg/kg/h for 6–12 h) found no difference in GI-3 recovery at 72 hours (57.3% vs 59%).<sup>[[34]](#ref34)</sup> | Do not rely on it for ileus prevention |
| **Nasogastric tube** | Routine postoperative NG decompression after cystectomy is unnecessary in uncomplicated recovery. A 43-patient cystectomy RCT summarized in the 2013 ERAS guidance found no difference in morbidity, bowel transit or LOS with removal at 12 hours versus after first flatus.<sup>[[35]](#ref35)</sup> That guidance also discusses gum chewing, PONV prophylaxis and minimally invasive surgery as components of multimodal ileus prevention.<sup>[[35]](#ref35)</sup> A 2025 cystectomy surgeon consensus advises removal at surgery's end unless an intraoperative complication warrants retention.<sup>[[36]](#ref36)</sup> Reinsert for persistent vomiting or distention. | Selective use |

Opioid-sparing analgesia (scheduled non-opioid agents, regional or local techniques) reduces the opioid exposure that drives POI and is the foundation on which the agents above operate; see [NSAIDs &amp; analgesics](/docs/foundations/pharmacology/neuropathic-pelvic-pain/nsaids-analgesics).

## 4. Stimulant Laxatives — Bisacodyl and Senna

**Bisacodyl** is one stimulant option for postoperative constipation once oral / rectal administration is appropriate:

### Mechanism

Prodrug hydrolyzed by intestinal brush-border enzymes into an active metabolite with antiabsorptive, secretory and prokinetic effects on the colon.<sup>[[16]](#ref16)</sup>

### Evidence

**Zingg 2008** colorectal RCT (200 randomized; 169 analyzed: bisacodyl 83, placebo 86):<sup>[[17]](#ref17)</sup>

- **GI-3 index** (mean of times to first flatus, defecation and solid-food tolerance): median 3.0 vs 3.7 days (p = 0.007)<sup>[[17]](#ref17)</sup>
- **First defecation:** 3.0 vs 4.0 days (p = 0.001)
- No difference in first flatus or solid-food tolerance
- No difference in morbidity / mortality

Wallström 2014 systematic review classified bisacodyl's effect on overall bowel motility as "uncertain" in colorectal surgery, with accelerated defecation but no consistent improvement in other GI recovery parameters.<sup>[[18]](#ref18)</sup>

ASCRS 2024 chronic-constipation guidance, not an ERAS-specific drug trial, recommends osmotic laxatives as first-line medical therapy (strong recommendation, moderate-quality evidence) and allows stimulants such as bisacodyl for rescue or second-line use. It notes a lack of randomized trials of the anthraquinone stimulants (senna, cascara, aloe) in chronic constipation. Chronic-constipation rankings at four weeks do not establish a postoperative ileus regimen.<sup>[[16]](#ref16)</sup>

### Dose and use

Zingg's colorectal RCT used 10 mg oral bisacodyl twice daily from the day before surgery through POD 3; a separate 20-patient colectomy RCT randomized patients without spontaneous defecation by POD 3 to a 10 mg rectal suppository or placebo, with a second dose after 12 hours if needed.<sup>[[17]](#ref17)[[38]](#ref38)</sup> Route and timing depend on the operation, oral tolerance and exclusion of obstruction or acute abdominal pathology. Do not automatically give rectal agents after anorectal reconstruction without the operative team’s plan.<sup>[[16]](#ref16)[[17]](#ref17)</sup>

**Senna:** alternative anthraquinone stimulant. **Patel 2010** compared senna plus docusate (48 patients) with placebo (45 patients) after pelvic reconstructive surgery; mean time to first BM was 3.00 vs 4.05 days (p &lt; 0.002).<sup>[[20]](#ref20)</sup> The combination result does not isolate a benefit from docusate; senna alone may avoid an unnecessary additional agent.

## 5. Docusate — De-prescribe as Monotherapy

### Mechanism

Anionic surfactant; emulsifies stool. No prokinetic activity.

### Evidence — negative

Pasay et al. summarized earlier docusate literature but studied a prescribing education intervention, not drug efficacy.<sup>[[19]](#ref19)</sup> A 2023 urogynecologic review found that multiple-agent laxative regimens may reduce bothersome constipation more than docusate alone; it did not establish docusate monotherapy efficacy.<sup>[[39]](#ref39)</sup> It does not lessen associated symptoms (abdominal cramps) or improve stool-evacuation perception.

### The inertia problem

A single-facility study by Lee et al., cited by Pasay et al., found that docusate accounted for 64% of laxative administrations there; Pasay et al. discussed low cost and historical practice as possible drivers of continued use.<sup>[[19]](#ref19)</sup> An educational intervention successfully reduced docusate use.<sup>[[19]](#ref19)</sup>

### Practical recommendation

Docusate has little supporting efficacy evidence. Prefer an effective osmotic or stimulant laxative when indicated; adding docusate to senna is not automatically beneficial and can add pill burden.

## 6. Simethicone — Comfort Measure, Not Prokinetic

### Mechanism

Non-absorbed surfactant reducing surface tension of GI gas bubbles and facilitating coalescence and passage. Does not stimulate motility.

### Evidence

**SPOT trial** (n = 118, colorectal surgery), a randomized POI study:<sup>[[15]](#ref15)</sup>

- First flatus: 25.2 vs 26.7 h (p = 0.98)
- First BM: 41.1 vs 42.9 h (p = 0.91)
- LOS: 4.5 vs 4.0 days (p = 0.63)

### Practical role

Symptomatic relief of bloating and gas pain only. It is generally well tolerated, but should not delay evaluation of worsening distention or pain.

## 7. Osmotic Laxatives and Prophylactic Laxative Use

**Wahafu 2025** real-world cohort (n = 1,724 urologic-cancer surgery patients):<sup>[[29]](#ref29)</sup>

- Within seven postoperative days, constipation occurred in 92/748 (12.3%) patients given prophylactic laxatives versus 319/748 (42.7%) managed after symptoms developed in the matched analysis (HR 0.24; 95% CI 0.19–0.30; p &lt; 0.001).<sup>[[29]](#ref29)</sup>

When ordinary postoperative constipation is anticipated and oral intake is appropriate, PEG 3350 17 g daily is one common osmotic option; a single stimulant can be selected or added if needed. Evidence does not establish routine stacking of senna and bisacodyl from POD 1, nor does constipation prevention prove treatment of ileus. See the workflow at [Postoperative constipation &amp; ileus](/docs/foundations/perioperative-care/postoperative-management/constipation).

## 8. Non-Pharmacologic Adjuncts — Gum Chewing and Coffee

Optional adjuncts for patients able to chew / swallow safely and whose postoperative diet permits them. Consider tolerance, aspiration risk and bladder-symptom triggers.

### Gum chewing ("sham feeding")

Vagal cholinergic stimulation and increased saliva production.

- **Kouba 2007** (102 cystectomy patients, sequential cohorts rather than randomization): flatus 2.4 versus 2.9 days; BM 3.2 versus 3.9 days; LOS difference was not statistically significant<sup>[[24]](#ref24)</sup>
- **Choi 2011** (cystectomy n = 60, open and robotic): flatus 57.1 vs 69.5 h; BM 76.7 vs 93.3 h<sup>[[25]](#ref25)</sup>
- **Sinz 2023 NMA** (32 RCTs, n = 4,999 across gum, coffee and caffeine comparisons; 26 trials studied gum): gum chewing reduced time to flatus by 11 h, time to defecation by 18 h and LOS by 0.9 d<sup>[[26]](#ref26)</sup>
- **ASCRS/SAGES ERAS** gives gum chewing a strong recommendation (1B): sugar-free, ≥ 10 min, 3–4× daily<sup>[[27]](#ref27)</sup>

### Coffee

Coffee may stimulate bowel activity through several mechanisms. Clinical comparisons do not establish a single causal ingredient.

- **Sinz 2023 NMA** — coffee reduced time to defecation by 13 h and LOS by 1.5 d<sup>[[26]](#ref26)</sup>
- **Zheng 2025 meta** reported favorable decaffeinated-coffee rankings in colorectal studies. Indirect rankings do not establish superiority for cystectomy or prove the mechanism<sup>[[28]](#ref28)</sup>

## Evidence Summary — Interpretation Across Studies

| Component | What the evidence supports | Main limitation |
|---|---|---|
| **Alvimopan** | Faster GI recovery and shorter admission in the cystectomy RCT<sup>[[6]](#ref6)[[7]](#ref7)</sup> | Predominantly open, opioid-PCA setting; Cochrane contained the same single trial |
| **ERAS and NG avoidance** | Williams IPD analysis associated ERAS with shorter LOS; NG avoidance was one associated component<sup>[[30]](#ref30)</sup> | The −8.7-day NG coefficient is **not a randomized treatment effect**, a promise to an individual patient, or a comparison against drugs |
| **Gum / coffee** | Possible small recovery gains; gum is included in colorectal ERAS guidance<sup>[[24]](#ref24)[[25]](#ref25)[[26]](#ref26)[[27]](#ref27)[[28]](#ref28)</sup> | Mixed procedures and study quality; optional when clinically appropriate |
| **Laxatives** | Useful for selected postoperative constipation; a colorectal bisacodyl RCT shortened time to defecation<sup>[[17]](#ref17)[[29]](#ref29)</sup> | Constipation is not ileus; nonrandomized prevention data cannot establish causality |
| **Metoclopramide / simethicone / docusate** | May have other symptom roles, but no established routine ileus-resolution benefit<sup>[[11]](#ref11)[[15]](#ref15)[[19]](#ref19)</sup> | Do not substitute symptom treatment for evaluation |
| **Neostigmine** | Effective for selected ACPO after supportive measures fail<sup>[[21]](#ref21)[[22]](#ref22)</sup> | Requires the correct diagnosis, monitored administration and assessment for complications |

## Practical Pearls

- **Distinguish constipation, postoperative ileus and ACPO.** Escalating laxatives or prokinetics without reassessing the diagnosis can delay recognition of obstruction, leak or ischemia.
- **Use alvimopan within its inpatient label / REMS limits.** The strongest cystectomy evidence comes from a predominantly open cohort; evaluate the likely absolute benefit within the current pathway.<sup>[[4]](#ref4)[[6]](#ref6)[[7]](#ref7)</sup>
- **Neostigmine requires appropriate monitoring and rescue capability.** Worsening pain, peritonitis or clinical deterioration requires urgent reassessment rather than reflex dosing.<sup>[[21]](#ref21)[[22]](#ref22)</sup>
- **Choose effective constipation agents and reassess tolerance.** Docusate does not need to be added automatically to a stimulant; a standard stack is not a substitute for a procedure-specific plan.<sup>[[16]](#ref16)[[19]](#ref19)[[20]](#ref20)</sup>
- **ERAS is a coordinated pathway.** Associations with individual components do not show that one intervention outperforms another; selective NG use remains appropriate for established indications.<sup>[[30]](#ref30)</sup>
