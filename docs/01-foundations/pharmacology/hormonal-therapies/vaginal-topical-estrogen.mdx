# Vaginal and Topical Estrogen

Vaginal and topical estrogen is a cornerstone of **genitourinary syndrome of menopause (GSM)** management, with its most evidence-based urologic applications being **prevention of recurrent UTIs** (AUA Moderate Recommendation; Grade B) and treatment of vulvovaginal symptoms. For coexisting OAB, local estrogen is an optional adjunct under AUA/SUFU/AUGS 2025 expert opinion<sup>[[31]](#ref31)</sup>.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[2]](#ref2)</sup> FDA labeling revisions began with a November 2025 request and the first six product approvals on **February 12, 2026**; check the current label of the prescribed product.<sup>[[4]](#ref4)</sup>

For related topics, see [Vaginal DHEA (prasterone)](/docs/foundations/pharmacology/hormonal-therapies/vaginal-dhea), [Ospemifene](/docs/foundations/pharmacology/hormonal-therapies/ospemifene), [Preoperative hormonal priming](/docs/foundations/pharmacology/hormonal-therapies/preoperative-hormonal-priming), the clinical [GSM](/docs/special-populations/05e-womens-health/gsm) article, [Urethral Bulking Agents](/docs/surgical-techniques/04f-incontinence-procedures/procedures/urethral-bulking-agents), and [UTI suppressive &amp; prophylactic therapy](/docs/foundations/pharmacology/infection-prophylaxis/uti-suppressive-prophylactic).

## Why vaginal estrogen matters in urology

The vagina and lower urinary tract are estrogen-responsive tissues; their embryologic origins are not identical. ERα and ERβ are expressed in vaginal epithelium, urethra, trigone, detrusor, pelvic-floor musculature, and endopelvic fascia.<sup>[[5]](#ref5)[[6]](#ref6)</sup>

Menopausal hypoestrogenism is associated with:

- Vaginal epithelial thinning (loss of superficial cells, rise in parabasal cells)
- **Vaginal pH** 3.5–4.5 → **5.0–7.0** with loss of *Lactobacillus* dominance → uropathogen colonization (*E. coli*, Enterobacteriaceae)
- Urethral mucosal atrophy, which may contribute to urinary symptoms; SUI remains multifactorial
- Decreased periurethral blood flow and mucosal-barrier impairment
- Urgency, frequency, and nocturia, which require assessment for other causes
- Increased susceptibility to UTI<sup>[[5]](#ref5)[[7]](#ref7)[[8]](#ref8)</sup>

Vaginal estrogen can improve local estrogenization — restoring epithelial thickness, normalizing pH, re-establishing Lactobacillus dominance, and improving periurethral tissue quality.<sup>[[9]](#ref9)[[10]](#ref10)</sup>

## Formulations and dosing

All FDA-approved formulations; systemic absorption varies substantially by product and dose.

| **Vaginal cream** | Premarin | Conjugated estrogen 0.625 mg/g | 0.5–2 g for atrophic vaginitis (dose individualized) | Daily for 21 days, then 7 days off; dyspareunia: **0.5 g twice weekly** or 21 days on/7 off. | **Vaginal ring** | Estring | Estradiol 2 mg ring releasing 7.5 µg/day | — | **Insert; replace every 90 days** | Very low |
| **Vaginal insert** | Intrarosa | **Prasterone (DHEA) 6.5 mg** | — | Nightly | Minimal (intracrine) — see [Vaginal DHEA](/docs/foundations/pharmacology/hormonal-therapies/vaginal-dhea) |
| **Oral SERM** | Osphena | **Ospemifene 60 mg** | — | Daily with food | Systemic (oral) — see [Ospemifene](/docs/foundations/pharmacology/hormonal-therapies/ospemifene) |

**Systemic absorption principles:**<sup>[[10]](#ref10)[[11]](#ref11)</sup>

- Serum estradiol with the 10 µg tablet (about 3–11 pg/mL), 4 µg / 10 µg inserts (3.6 and 4.6 pg/mL versus 4.3 with placebo at day 14) and the 7.5 µg/day ring (5–10 pg/mL) stays within or near the postmenopausal range (NAMS 2020); ACOG 2021 groups the 4 µg insert, 7.5 µg ring and 10 µg tablet/insert as associated with levels below 20 pg/mL<sup>[[13]](#ref13)[[30]](#ref30)</sup>
- Circulating estrogen rises **transiently** with the first application on atrophic tissues, then normalizes within 2–12 weeks as epithelium thickens and absorption decreases
- **Cream absorption is variable and less well characterized**: the user measures the dose, and the available serum data come from older, higher-dose studies with less sensitive assays; inserts and rings deliver a fixed low dose<sup>[[13]](#ref13)</sup>
- Labeled steady-state estradiol is about 5.5 pg/mL (Vagifem 10 µg, day 83) and 7–8 pg/mL (Estring, weeks 12–48); Imvexxy 4 µg and 10 µg averaged 3.6 and 4.6 pg/mL on day 14 versus 4.3 pg/mL with placebo<sup>[[40]](#ref40)[[41]](#ref41)[[42]](#ref42)</sup>
- The 4 µg insert has not been studied for rUTI prevention (AUA rUTI 2025 Table 5 footnote)<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup>

## Recurrent UTI prevention — the strongest urologic indication

### Guidelines

- **AUA/CUA/SUFU 2025 (Moderate Recommendation; Grade B):** recommend vaginal estrogen for peri- and postmenopausal patients with recurrent UTI when appropriate and without a contraindication.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup> The panel found no formulation clearly superior and advises using the patient's preferred product; it notes that a Cochrane review suggested cream may prevent UTI better than the ring. Systemic estrogen is not recommended for rUTI, and women already on systemic estrogen should still receive vaginal estrogen.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup>
- **AUA/SUFU/AUGS GSM 2025:** recommends local low-dose vaginal estrogen for GSM with recurrent UTI (Moderate Recommendation; Grade B); for GSM with comorbid conditions such as OAB it is an option (Expert Opinion).<sup>[[31]](#ref31)</sup>
- **AGS Beers Criteria 2023:** "First-line preventive therapy for recurrent UTIs in most older women is vaginal estrogen" — while explicitly recommending **against** systemic estrogen for this indication.<sup>[[12]](#ref12)</sup>
- **NAMS 2020** GSM Position Statement — small RCTs of vaginal estrogen (conjugated estrogen cream, estriol cream, low-dose estradiol ring) reduced recurrent UTI frequency in postmenopausal women.<sup>[[13]](#ref13)</sup>

### Evidence

| Study | n | Design | Finding |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Chen 2021 meta-analysis** | 1,936 (vaginal E) | 5 RCTs | **RR 0.42** (95% CI 0.30–0.59) for rUTI reduction with vaginal estrogen; **oral estrogen showed no benefit** (RR 1.11; 95% CI 0.92–1.35)<sup>[[14]](#ref14)</sup> |
| **AUA rUTI 2025 evidence review** | 4 RCTs (n=313); 5 with an added 35-woman trial | Guideline systematic review | Four earlier trials: RR 0.59 (95% CI 0.35–1.01; I² 76%), not significant. Adding a 2024 high-risk-of-bias trial: RR 0.58 (95% CI 0.39–0.87). Strength of evidence low<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup> |
| **Tan-Kim 2023** | 5,638 | Retrospective before-after cohort, Kaiser Permanente (no control group) | UTI frequency **3.9 → 1.8/year** (**51.9% reduction**, p &lt;0.001); **31.4% had zero UTIs** in the following year<sup>[[15]](#ref15)</sup> |

**Predictors of persistent UTIs despite vaginal estrogen** (Tan-Kim 2023): age ≥75, higher baseline UTI frequency, urinary incontinence, urinary retention, diabetes.<sup>[[15]](#ref15)</sup>

**TAPER trial (July 2026).** In 114 postmenopausal participants, twice-weekly periurethral estradiol cream (0.5 g) was noninferior to intravaginal applicator delivery (1 g) for six-month UTI-free status: 50.9% versus 52.6% (difference −1.75 percentage points, 95% CI −20.0 to 17.0). This single-center, unblinded trial used a **25-percentage-point noninferiority margin**. It supports discussing an application alternative when applicator use is difficult, but does not establish precise equivalence, long-term benefit, or applicability to other formulations.<sup>[[35]](#ref35)</sup>

### Mechanism — microbiome restoration

**Srinivasan 2022** secondary analysis of an RCT (*JAMA Netw Open*) — after 12 weeks of vaginal estradiol tablets, **80%** of women had *Lactobacillus* / *Bifidobacterium*-dominant vaginal communities vs **36%** with a low-pH moisturizer and **26%** with placebo (p &lt;0.001); median pH was 5 vs 6 with placebo.<sup>[[10]](#ref10)</sup>

**Shen 2016** (low-dose estrogen in atrophic vaginitis) showed rising *Lactobacillus* and falling *Gardnerella* with a lower vaginal pH.<sup>[[16]](#ref16)</sup> **Moore 2024** (22 women with SUI on estriol cream, before-after, no control) found that women with a Lactobacillus-deficient baseline improved most (pH fell about 1.3 points; p = 0.004), while Lactobacillus-dominant women stayed stable.<sup>[[8]](#ref8)</sup> Microbiome shift is the proposed mechanism; these studies did not measure UTI outcomes.

See [UTI suppressive &amp; prophylactic therapy](/docs/foundations/pharmacology/infection-prophylaxis/uti-suppressive-prophylactic) and [Non-antibiotic UTI prevention](/docs/foundations/pharmacology/infection-prophylaxis/non-antibiotic-uti-prevention) for the broader rUTI framework.

## Urgency / urge urinary incontinence

Vaginal estrogen **may improve some urinary symptoms when GSM coexists**, but benefit is inconsistent across outcomes. It is not a replacement for standard OAB or SUI care. Systemic estrogen can worsen incontinence.<sup>[[31]](#ref31)</sup><sup>[[11]](#ref11)[[17]](#ref17)</sup>

- **Christmas 2023 systematic review** — vaginal estrogen improves dysuria, frequency, urge incontinence, and SUI; systemic HT may cause or worsen UI.<sup>[[17]](#ref17)</sup>
- **IUGA Committee Opinion** — vaginal estrogen has beneficial effects on UI symptoms vs placebo (evidence level 2C).<sup>[[6]](#ref6)</sup>
- **EAU non-neurogenic female LUTS 2026:** offer vaginal oestrogen to women with LUTS and associated GSM symptoms (weak recommendation; LE 1a). The evidence is mainly from SUI and mixed populations and is hard to generalize to predominant OAB/UUI.<sup>[[43]](#ref43)</sup>
- **NICE NG123:** offer vaginal oestrogen to women with OAB symptoms and genitourinary symptoms or signs of menopause (1.4.37), and do not offer systemic hormone replacement therapy to treat urinary incontinence (1.4.36).<sup>[[44]](#ref44)</sup>
- **Harncharoenkul 2026 RCT** (n = 86, 17β-estradiol 10 µg vs placebo) — overall storage symptoms did not differ at 12 wk, but **UUI showed sustained significant improvement** at 4 and 12 wk (p = 0.013); urethral maturation index and vaginal pH improved significantly.<sup>[[18]](#ref18)</sup>

**The WHI vs vaginal contrast:** WHI oral CEE **increased** incontinence. Systemic and local routes have fundamentally different effects on the lower urinary tract — systemic appears to act via counter-productive effects on collagen metabolism and vascular tone.<sup>[[11]](#ref11)[[17]](#ref17)</sup>

## Stress urinary incontinence

Evidence for vaginal estrogen in SUI is **less robust** than for urgency symptoms.<sup>[[19]](#ref19)</sup> EAU 2026 recommends offering vaginal oestrogen to postmenopausal women with SUI and vulvovaginal atrophy symptoms (strong recommendation), and rates it as improving SUI in the short term (LE 1a).<sup>[[43]](#ref43)</sup> Weber 2015 systematic review: topical estrogen appears to decrease both OAB and UI complaints, but signal is stronger for urgency-type symptoms. **Rahn 2023 preoperative RCT** in prolapse patients showed trends toward SUI improvement with preop vaginal estrogen (50% vs 43%; p = 0.78 — **not statistically significant**).<sup>[[20]](#ref20)</sup>

## Pelvic organ prolapse

Vaginal estrogen has not established improved anatomical repair durability. The 2026 EVA multicenter trial adds a patient-reported recovery benefit; see the [priming hub](/docs/foundations/pharmacology/hormonal-therapies/preoperative-hormonal-priming) for results and limitations.

- **Taithongchai 2023 Cochrane review** — little to no difference in subjective or objective POP improvement when estrogen was added to surgery; possible fewer postoperative UTIs, with heterogeneous contributing studies and uncertain overall conclusions.<sup>[[21]](#ref21)</sup>
- **IUGA Committee Opinion** — definitive evidence on local estrogen and POP prevention or treatment is lacking.<sup>[[6]](#ref6)</sup>
- **NICE NG123:** consider vaginal oestrogen for women with prolapse and genitourinary symptoms or signs of menopause (1.7.3), and an oestrogen-releasing ring when cognitive or physical impairment makes pessaries or creams hard to use (1.7.4). NICE found no evidence on oestrogen for treating prolapse itself.<sup>[[44]](#ref44)</sup>

**Perioperative use** is covered in [Preoperative hormonal priming](/docs/foundations/pharmacology/hormonal-therapies/preoperative-hormonal-priming). The **IMPROVE trial** (Rahn 2023) showed 7 weeks of preop vaginal estrogen improved objective estrogenization signs but subjective symptom improvement was inconclusive.<sup>[[20]](#ref20)</sup>

## Perioperative use in urologic / gynecologic surgery

Commonly prescribed preoperatively before:

- Pelvic reconstructive surgery (prolapse repair, slings)
- Vaginal hysterectomy
- Anti-incontinence procedures

Rationale: improve tissue quality, vascularity, and wound healing — though high-quality evidence for improved surgical outcomes is limited.<sup>[[20]](#ref20)[[21]](#ref21)</sup> EAU 2026 rates neoadjuvant or adjuvant local oestrogen as ineffective for improving outcomes of mid-urethral sling surgery (LE 2b).<sup>[[43]](#ref43)</sup> NICE suggests topical oestrogen cream as non-surgical management of a single vaginal mesh exposure smaller than 1 cm², with follow-up within 3 months.<sup>[[44]](#ref44)</sup> See [Preoperative hormonal priming](/docs/foundations/pharmacology/hormonal-therapies/preoperative-hormonal-priming).

## Safety

### Endometrial safety

- **Low-dose vaginal estrogen generally does not require added progestogen**, but this differs from systemic unopposed estrogen and does not establish lifetime absence of endometrial risk. AUA 2025 advises against surveillance solely because of local low-dose estrogen, DHEA, or ospemifene use; symptoms and other risk factors still require evaluation.<sup>[[11]](#ref11)[[31]](#ref31)</sup>
- **Long-term (&gt;1 year) endometrial safety data are limited** for vaginal estrogen, vaginal DHEA, and ospemifene
- **Postmenopausal bleeding always warrants evaluation** with endometrial biopsy and/or TVUS<sup>[[11]](#ref11)</sup>

### Breast cancer safety — reassuring observational data with remaining uncertainty

| Source | n | Finding |
|---|---|---|
| **Beste 2025 meta-analysis** | 8 observational studies; 24,060 for recurrence | Vaginal estrogen **not associated** with increased breast cancer recurrence (pooled unadjusted OR 0.48; 95% CI 0.23–0.98), BC mortality (OR 0.60; 95% CI 0.18–1.95) or overall mortality (OR 0.46; 95% CI 0.42–0.49)<sup>[[26]](#ref26)</sup> |
| **McVicker 2024 *JAMA Oncology*** | 49,237 | **No increase** in breast-cancer-specific mortality versus HRT nonusers (HR 0.77; 95% CI 0.63–0.94)<sup>[[27]](#ref27)</sup> |
| **Agrawal 2023 US claims analysis** | 42,113 | Comparable recurrence risk (RR 1.03; 95% CI 0.91–1.18); also comparable in ER-positive disease (RR 0.94; 95% CI 0.77–1.15)<sup>[[28]](#ref28)</sup> |
| **Cold 2022 Danish cohort** | Early breast cancer | **Small increased recurrence risk in the aromatase-inhibitor subgroup** (RR 1.39; 95% CI 1.04–1.85); survival not worse<sup>[[29]](#ref29)</sup> |

For the July 2026 VEMORA randomized symptom study and its limitations, see [GSM in breast cancer survivors](/docs/special-populations/05e-womens-health/gsm#special-population-breast-cancer-survivors). Symptom improvement and serum estradiol measurements do not establish long-term recurrence safety.

**Guideline positions in breast cancer survivors:**

- **ACOG 2021 clinical consensus** — nonhormonal methods are first-line; if they fail, low-dose vaginal estrogen may be used after risk-benefit discussion in breast cancer survivors, including those on tamoxifen, with **shared decision-making with the patient and oncologist** for AI users<sup>[[30]](#ref30)</sup>
- **AUA/SUFU/AUGS 2025** — low-dose vaginal estrogen may be recommended in the context of multidisciplinary shared decision-making (Expert Opinion), and clinicians should tell patients there is no evidence linking it to breast cancer development. Data on aromatase-inhibitor users are scarce; the 10 µg tablet, 4 µg insert or low-dose ring may have lower absorption and be preferable<sup>[[31]](#ref31)</sup>
- **AUA rUTI 2025** — vaginal estrogen has not been shown to increase cancer recurrence; consider it for UTI prevention in women with a breast cancer history in coordination with the oncologist<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup>
- **Product labeling** — FDA warnings changed through product-specific approvals; these do not replace individualized breast-cancer counseling.<sup>[[4]](#ref4)</sup>

### Cardiovascular and thromboembolic safety

**No evidence** that low-dose vaginal estrogen increases CHD, stroke, or VTE. The WHI findings of increased CV risk applied to **oral systemic CEE**, not to vaginal preparations.<sup>[[11]](#ref11)[[32]](#ref32)</sup>

### FDA regulatory update — November 2025 to February 2026

FDA requested changes in November 2025, then approved the first **six products** on February 12, 2026, removing cardiovascular disease, breast cancer, and probable dementia statements from their boxed warnings. This was not simultaneous removal of every warning from every estrogen product. Use the actual product label for contraindications and counseling.<sup>[[4]](#ref4)</sup>

**Labeled contraindications** for vaginal estradiol and conjugated-estrogen products include undiagnosed abnormal genital bleeding; breast cancer or a history of it; other estrogen-dependent neoplasia; active or prior DVT/PE; active or prior arterial thromboembolic disease (stroke, MI); hepatic impairment or disease; known thrombophilia (protein C, protein S or antithrombin deficiency); and hypersensitivity.<sup>[[36]](#ref36)[[40]](#ref40)[[42]](#ref42)</sup> These sit alongside the reassuring observational breast-cancer data and the guideline positions above, which is why shared decision-making with oncology, rather than a blanket rule, is the current approach.

Contemporary commentaries discuss how these changes may improve prescribing, but they are interpretations of the regulatory action, not substitutes for product labeling or individualized assessment.<sup>[[3]](#ref3)[[33]](#ref33)[[34]](#ref34)</sup>

## Practical prescribing

### Choosing a formulation

| Situation | Preferred |
|---|---|
| **UTI prevention** (general) | Any low-dose vaginal estrogen formulation; AUA does not specify a preferred product<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup> |
| **Patient convenience / adherence** | **Vaginal ring (Estring)** — 90-day replacement; useful when applicator or cream use is difficult (NICE suggests the ring for women with cognitive or physical impairment)<sup>[[44]](#ref44)</sup> |
| **Lowest systemic absorption** | **4 µg insert (Imvexxy)** or **7.5 µg/day ring (Estring)**<sup>[[11]](#ref11)</sup> |
| **Breast cancer survivor (on AI)** | Individualized low-dose product and shared decision with oncology; no universal safety ranking<sup>[[29]](#ref29)[[31]](#ref31)</sup> |
| **Prefers oral therapy** | Ospemifene 60 mg daily<sup>[[24]](#ref24)</sup> |
| **Prefers non-estrogen vaginal therapy** | Prasterone 6.5 mg nightly<sup>[[37]](#ref37)</sup> |

### Duration of therapy

GSM is a **chronic progressive condition** — symptoms recur on discontinuation. NAMS advises continuing therapy as long as needed for symptom control, absent contraindications; trial safety data extend to 1 year and observational data support longer use. Reassess benefit and tolerability periodically and follow the product-specific labeling.<sup>[[13]](#ref13)[[4]](#ref4)</sup>

### Monitoring

- **No routine endometrial surveillance** is required with low-dose vaginal estrogen<sup>[[11]](#ref11)[[25]](#ref25)</sup>
- **Evaluate any postmenopausal bleeding** with endometrial biopsy and/or TVUS<sup>[[11]](#ref11)</sup>
- Routine serum estradiol monitoring is not needed; levels with low-dose products remain in the postmenopausal range<sup>[[13]](#ref13)</sup>
- Reassess symptoms periodically to confirm ongoing benefit<sup>[[22]](#ref22)</sup>

## Systemic estrogen — what NOT to do

- **Systemic estrogen does NOT prevent UTIs** (RR 1.11; 95% CI 0.92–1.35 vs placebo)<sup>[[14]](#ref14)</sup>
- **Systemic estrogen WORSENS urinary incontinence** (WHI data)<sup>[[11]](#ref11)[[17]](#ref17)</sup>
- **AGS Beers Criteria alternatives (2025)** state: "Do not initiate systemic estrogen (e.g., oral, transdermal); consider deprescribing among older women already taking this medication; do not use systemic estrogen to manage incontinence (all types)"<sup>[[12]](#ref12)</sup>
- Women on systemic HT for vasomotor symptoms may **still need additional vaginal estrogen** for GSM symptoms<sup>[[22]](#ref22)</sup>

## Clinical Positioning

- **Vaginal estrogen is recommended for rUTI prevention in peri- and postmenopausal women** (AUA/CUA/SUFU 2025 Moderate Recommendation, Grade B; AGS Beers calls it first-line in older women) — RR 0.42 in the Chen meta-analysis and a 51.9% reduction in the uncontrolled Tan-Kim cohort; AUA's own pooled evidence is low strength.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[14]](#ref14)[[15]](#ref15)</sup>
- **Route matters — vaginal works, oral does not.** Oral estrogen has RR 1.11 for rUTI (no benefit) and worsens incontinence per WHI. Do not substitute.<sup>[[14]](#ref14)[[17]](#ref17)</sup>
- **Check the current product label.** FDA revisions were product-specific.<sup>[[4]](#ref4)</sup>
- **Formulation choice follows patient preference and clinical context.** Inserts and rings offer more predictable low-dose exposure; consider dexterity, cost, local symptoms, and oncology input when relevant.<sup>[[13]](#ref13)[[31]](#ref31)</sup>
- **In breast cancer survivors, vaginal estrogen is no longer reflexively contraindicated.** Recurrence studies and mortality studies address different outcomes: McVicker evaluated breast-cancer mortality, not recurrence. Observational associations do not prove protection. Shared decision-making with oncology is appropriate — particularly in AI users, where the Cold 2022 Danish cohort showed a small RR 1.39 recurrence signal without survival difference.<sup>[[26]](#ref26)[[27]](#ref27)[[28]](#ref28)[[29]](#ref29)</sup>
- **No progestogen is routinely needed** for endometrial protection with low-dose vaginal estrogen in short-term trials (AUA reviewed 8–36 weeks); long-term data remain limited. Always evaluate postmenopausal bleeding.<sup>[[11]](#ref11)[[25]](#ref25)</sup>
- **Microbiome restoration is the proposed mechanism** — 80% of vaginal-estradiol users had *Lactobacillus*-dominant microbiota at 12 weeks versus 26% with placebo in the Srinivasan 2022 RCT. Women with baseline Lactobacillus-deficient microbiomes show the greatest benefit.<sup>[[8]](#ref8)[[10]](#ref10)</sup>
- **For GSM with OAB, consider vaginal estrogen as an adjunct.** Do not delay appropriate bladder training, pelvic-floor therapy, or OAB medication solely to complete an estrogen trial.<sup>[[31]](#ref31)</sup>
- **SUI evidence is weaker.** Vaginal estrogen may modestly improve SUI as part of GSM management but should not substitute for pelvic-floor PT, pessary, or surgical therapy.<sup>[[19]](#ref19)[[20]](#ref20)</sup>
- **Counsel predictors of non-response** — age ≥75, higher baseline UTI frequency, incontinence, retention, and diabetes predict persistent UTIs despite vaginal estrogen.<sup>[[15]](#ref15)</sup>
