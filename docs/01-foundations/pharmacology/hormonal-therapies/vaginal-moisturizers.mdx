# Vaginal Moisturizers & Lubricants

**Vaginal moisturizers and lubricants** are nonhormonal options for **genitourinary syndrome of menopause (GSM)** and vulvovaginal dryness. They sit alongside [vaginal estrogen](/docs/foundations/pharmacology/hormonal-therapies/vaginal-topical-estrogen), [vaginal DHEA](/docs/foundations/pharmacology/hormonal-therapies/vaginal-dhea), and [ospemifene](/docs/foundations/pharmacology/hormonal-therapies/ospemifene) among the GSM treatment options, and are the default starting point for women who prefer to avoid hormones or for whom hormones are relatively contraindicated, especially survivors of estrogen-dependent breast cancer.<sup>[[3]](#ref3)</sup> For the reconstructive surgeon and urogynecologist, they matter as a low-risk adjunct for tissue comfort before and after pelvic surgery, for pessary tolerance, and for symptom relief alone or alongside other GSM treatment.

---

## Moisturizers Versus Lubricants

The two product classes are frequently conflated but serve distinct roles.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[2]](#ref2)</sup>

| | Vaginal moisturizer | Vaginal lubricant |
|---|---|---|
| **Purpose** | Trap moisture, restore mucosal hydration, longer-term symptom control | Reduce friction and dyspareunia at intercourse |
| **Site** | Applied intravaginally | Applied at the introitus / vaginal opening |
| **Schedule** | Regular use, most often **2–3 times per week** (NAMS)<sup>[[21]](#ref21)</sup>; product-specific regimens differ | As-needed, immediately before intercourse |
| **Effect on tissue** | Sustained improvement in dryness, pH, elasticity, itching, irritation | Transient lubrication only |

Prospective studies of regular moisturizer use show measurable improvement in vaginal dryness, pH balance, and elasticity, with reductions in itching, irritation, and dyspareunia.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[2]](#ref2)</sup>

## Vaginal Moisturizers

### Formulations

- Hyaluronic acid–based and polycarbophil-based products are the most commonly used moisturizers.<sup>[[2]](#ref2)</sup>
- Polyacrylic acid and vitamin E / vitamin D vaginal suppositories are additional nonhormonal options with reported efficacy.<sup>[[2]](#ref2)[[3]](#ref3)</sup> The ACOG 2021 consensus table lists example regimens from the trials: hyaluronic acid 5 mg daily for 2 weeks then 3–5 times per week; polycarbophil gel 2.5 g three times per week; polyacrylic acid 3 g daily; vitamin E 30–200 IU and vitamin D 1,000 IU suppositories. Evidence is from small trials, and ACOG found insufficient data to say one approach is superior.<sup>[[3]](#ref3)</sup>
- Regulatory classification is product-specific: many US personal lubricants and moisturizers are medical devices, including products cleared through FDA 510(k), rather than cosmetics or prescription hormone drugs.<sup>[[15]](#ref15)</sup> Very little data exist on the safety of products containing flavors, warming agents, or preservatives such as parabens and propylene glycol; avoiding them is reasonable in patients with vulvar dermatoses or sensitive tissue. Moisturizers and lubricants can themselves irritate thin vulvovaginal tissue, and adding local vaginal hormone often improves tolerance.<sup>[[14]](#ref14)[[21]](#ref21)</sup>

### Evidence

The evidence base is meaningful but of low certainty.

- A **2024 systematic review** (*Annals of Internal Medicine*, 46 RCTs, of which only 4 tested moisturizers) found that vaginal moisturizers may improve dryness versus placebo, with low certainty of evidence. Most trials lasted 12 weeks or less. The same review rated vaginal estrogen, vaginal DHEA, and oral ospemifene as also improving GSM symptoms with similarly low certainty.<sup>[[4]](#ref4)</sup>
- The **Mitchell 2018 RCT** (*JAMA Internal Medicine*, n = 302) compared vaginal estradiol, a polycarbophil-based moisturizer, and dual placebo in women with moderate-to-severe vulvovaginal symptoms. All three groups improved similarly, with no significant difference between either active treatment and placebo.<sup>[[5]](#ref5)</sup> This is interpreted either as a strong placebo effect from regular vaginal gel application, or as evidence that the moisturizing properties of the placebo gel were themselves therapeutic.
- A **2023 systematic review** of nonestrogen GSM therapies (*Obstetrics & Gynecology*) reinforced that moisturizers are an evidence-supported nonhormonal option without a clear best-in-class formulation.<sup>[[8]](#ref8)</sup>

### Moisturizers Versus Vaginal Estrogen

The **Garcia de Arriba 2022 RCT** (n = 172) found a hormone-free moisturizing cream non-inferior to 0.1% estriol cream for mild-to-moderate vulvovaginal dryness over 43 days. In women whose dryness caused severe impairment of daily life, estriol cream was significantly superior (p = 0.0032).<sup>[[6]](#ref6)</sup> The trial was open-label and manufacturer-sponsored, so it does not establish a universal treatment hierarchy. The 2026 EAU guideline, drawing on a systematic review, notes that vaginal estrogen outperforms lubricants and moisturizers on objective measures of vulvovaginal atrophy but not on subjective symptoms.<sup>[[20]](#ref20)</sup> AUA/SUFU/AUGS 2025 recommends moisturizers and/or lubricants, alone or with other therapies, for vaginal dryness and dyspareunia (moderate recommendation, Grade C) and separately recommends offering low-dose vaginal estrogen (strong recommendation, Grade C). General GSM care does not require failure of moisturizers before low-dose vaginal estrogen. For urinary symptoms of GSM (frequency, urgency, leakage) and rUTI prevention, no studies show benefit from moisturizers or lubricants, so the guideline does not treat them as first-line for those symptoms and pairs them with hormonal therapy.<sup>[[14]](#ref14)</sup>

## Vaginal Lubricants

Lubricants are applied to the introitus before intercourse to reduce friction and dyspareunia, on an as-needed basis. ACOG endorses them as a nonhormonal option for friction-related dyspareunia.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup> NAMS 2020 lists nonhormonal lubricants with sexual activity as first-line therapy for less severe GSM (Level A).<sup>[[21]](#ref21)</sup>

### Lubricant Types

| Type | Advantages | Disadvantages | Condom-compatible |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Water-based** | Widely available, easy cleanup | Dries out quickly causing friction; many are hyperosmolar | Check product and condom labeling |
| **Silicone-based** | Long-lasting, does not dry out, formulation-specific tolerability | Incompatible with silicone toys; more expensive | Check product and condom labeling |
| **Oil-based (food-grade)** | Inexpensive, long-lasting | Degrades latex condoms; petroleum jelly was associated with more bacterial vaginosis and oils with more *Candida* colonization in one study<sup>[[21]](#ref21)</sup> | No (latex) |
| **Hyaluronic acid–based** | Possible mucosal benefit | Limited comparative data | Varies by product |

No lubricant type has proven superior overall; selection should account for condom compatibility, patient sensitivities, and preference.<sup>[[2]](#ref2)</sup>

### Osmolality and pH — An Underappreciated Selection Criterion

The osmolality and pH of a lubricant materially affect vaginal mucosal health. The WHO/UNFPA/FHI360 advisory distinguished an ideal osmolality ≤380 mOsm/kg from an interim procurement ceiling ≤1200 mOsm/kg; the latter is not an iso-osmolar definition. Select pH and condom/fertility compatibility for the intended use rather than applying one range to every product; AUA/SUFU/AUGS 2025 cites the WHO 1,200 mOsm/kg limit and a pH of 5.0–7.0.<sup>[[16]](#ref16)</sup><sup>[[9]](#ref9)[[10]](#ref10)[[14]](#ref14)</sup> Many commercial lubricants are markedly hyperosmolar.

- **Dezzutti 2012** evaluated 14 over-the-counter lubricants: hyperosmolar products (including KY Jelly and Astroglide) caused loss of epithelial viability and epithelial stripping in explant tissue. KY Jelly, Replens (a polycarbophil moisturizer) and a nonoxynol-9 spermicidal gel were also toxic to Lactobacillus in vitro. By contrast, nearly iso-osmolar products (Good Clean Love, PRÉ) and silicone-based lubricants showed no significant epithelial damage.<sup>[[11]](#ref11)</sup> These are laboratory findings; clinical consequences have not been shown.
- **Wilkinson 2019** confirmed in 3-dimensional vaginal epithelial-cell models that hyperosmolar lubricants alter cell morphology and are selectively cytotoxic, inflammatory, and barrier-disrupting.<sup>[[12]](#ref12)</sup>
- **Palacios 2023 RCT** (n = 174; open-label, 4 weeks) compared five water-based lubricants, four formulated to meet the WHO osmolality ceiling (≤1200 mOsm/kg) and one of higher osmolality. All five improved FSFI scores and were well tolerated, so efficacy is not compromised by meeting the WHO criterion. The trial did not test mucosal safety.<sup>[[9]](#ref9)</sup>

### Irritant and compatibility assessment

Review formulation and concentration, patient irritation history, and condom/fertility compatibility. Avoid products that cause symptoms; high osmolality, spermicides, fragrances, and warming additives can be problematic. A named preservative alone does not establish clinical toxicity.<sup>[[13]](#ref13)[[14]](#ref14)</sup>

## Breast-Cancer Survivors

ACOG's Clinical Consensus on urogenital symptoms in women with a history of estrogen-dependent breast cancer states that nonhormonal methods (moisturizers and lubricants) should be considered first-line treatment. Low-dose vaginal estrogen may follow if these fail, after shared decision-making.<sup>[[3]](#ref3)</sup>

- In a double-blind crossover RCT of breast-cancer survivors (n = 45 assessable), a polycarbophil-based moisturizer (Replens) and a placebo lubricating product both reduced dryness by about 60% (no difference). Dyspareunia improved 60% with the moisturizer versus 41% with placebo (P = .05).<sup>[[17]](#ref17)</sup>
- In a randomized double-blind crossover trial (n = 38 analyzed), silicone-based and water-based lubricants did not differ significantly on the primary measure of sexual discomfort (P = .06). A post hoc analysis favored silicone for penetration pain, and more women preferred silicone (65% versus 35%). Most women remained distressed with either product.<sup>[[18]](#ref18)</sup>
- **Topical 4% aqueous lidocaine** applied to the vulvar vestibule for 3 minutes before penetration targets introital dyspareunia: in a double-blind trial of 46 breast-cancer survivors with severe penetrative dyspareunia, median pain during intercourse was 1.0 with lidocaine versus 5.3 with saline (0–10 scale, P = .007).<sup>[[19]](#ref19)</sup>

## Guideline Recommendations

The **American College of Obstetricians and Gynecologists (ACOG)** endorses over-the-counter nonhormonal moisturizers and lubricants as reasonable options for vaginal dryness and friction-related dyspareunia, particularly for women who prefer to avoid hormonal therapy.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[2]](#ref2)</sup> The **American Academy of Family Physicians** similarly identifies over-the-counter hormone-free vaginal products as reasonable first-line therapy for GSM, with lubricants used as-needed for dyspareunia.<sup>[[7]](#ref7)</sup> When lubricants or moisturizers are insufficient, escalation to vaginal estrogen, vaginal DHEA (prasterone), or oral ospemifene is guided by symptom severity and patient preference. For breast-cancer survivors, AUA/SUFU/AUGS 2025 states that clinicians may recommend low-dose vaginal estrogen within multidisciplinary shared decision-making (expert opinion), so hormonal escalation should involve the oncologist.<sup>[[3]](#ref3)[[7]](#ref7)[[14]](#ref14)</sup>

## Reconstructive Relevance

Moisturizers and lubricants may improve dryness and comfort around pelvic care, but they have not established improved surgical healing, prolapse-repair durability, or prevention of pessary erosion. Lubricant laboratory-toxicity studies do not prove postoperative infection prevention. Resume vaginal products and intercourse after reconstruction according to the operating team's healing assessment. See [priming evidence](/docs/foundations/pharmacology/hormonal-therapies/preoperative-hormonal-priming).

---

## Practical Use

- **Moisturizers:** intravaginal application, most often 2–3 times per week, for ongoing symptom control; follow the product's own schedule.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[7]](#ref7)[[21]](#ref21)</sup>
- **Lubricants:** applied at the introitus immediately before intercourse, as needed.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[2]](#ref2)</sup>
- **Product selection:** choose by comfort, cost, sensitivities, intended use, and product-specific condom compatibility. Lower-osmolality options may be preferable when available; 1200 mOsm/kg is an upper procurement limit, not a physiological target.<sup>[[14]](#ref14)[[16]](#ref16)</sup>
- **Escalation:** if nonhormonal products give insufficient relief, move to vaginal estrogen, vaginal DHEA (prasterone), or oral ospemifene.<sup>[[7]](#ref7)</sup>
