# Vaginal DHEA (Prasterone)

**Vaginal DHEA (prasterone / Intrarosa)** is an FDA-approved intravaginal steroid for moderate-to-severe dyspareunia due to menopause. Its defining pharmacologic feature, intracrine conversion to androgens and estrogens within vaginal tissue, distinguishes it from vaginal estrogen by providing local androgenic and estrogenic effects; systemic exposure is low but not zero.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[2]](#ref2)[[3]](#ref3)</sup> A placebo-controlled analysis reported improvement across all six FSFI domains, including desire and arousal; this does not establish superior sexual-function benefit over vaginal estrogen.<sup>[[4]](#ref4)</sup>

For related agents, see [Vaginal and topical estrogen](/docs/foundations/pharmacology/hormonal-therapies/vaginal-topical-estrogen), [Ospemifene](/docs/foundations/pharmacology/hormonal-therapies/ospemifene), and [Preoperative hormonal priming](/docs/foundations/pharmacology/hormonal-therapies/preoperative-hormonal-priming). For the clinical framework, see [GSM](/docs/special-populations/05e-womens-health/gsm).

## Mechanism — the science of intracrinology

Prasterone (synthetic DHEA) exploits **intracrinology**: inactive precursor hormones are converted into active sex steroids within target cells, act locally, and are then inactivated before release into circulation.<sup>[[2]](#ref2)</sup>

**Enzymatic cascade within vaginal tissue:**<sup>[[5]](#ref5)[[2]](#ref2)</sup>

1. **DHEA to androstenedione** (3β-HSD1/HSD2)
2. **Androstenedione to testosterone** (17β-HSD3/HSD5)
3. **Testosterone to DHT** (5α-reductase isoforms)
4. **Androstenedione to estrone to estradiol** (aromatase and 17β-HSD)
5. **Active steroids to glucuronide or sulfate conjugates** (UGT and SULT), released as inactive metabolites

**Cellai 2021** confirmed the human vagina as an androgen-target organ: DHEA supplementation of human vaginal smooth-muscle cells increased androstenedione, testosterone, and DHT secretion and activated AR-dependent gene expression.<sup>[[5]](#ref5)</sup>

**Clinical consequence:** after menopause, ovarian estrogen production ceases, and DHEA (the remaining endogenous androgen-estrogen precursor) declines progressively from about age 30. Delivering DHEA directly to the vagina restores both the estrogenic effects (epithelial maturation, lubrication, pH normalization) and the androgenic effects (muscularis support, improved blood flow, enhanced sexual function) of a premenopausal hormonal milieu; the mechanistic distinction does not establish superior clinical outcomes over vaginal estrogen.<sup>[[2]](#ref2)[[6]](#ref6)</sup>

## FDA-approved indication and dosing

| Element | Details |
|---|---|
| **Brand** | Intrarosa<sup>[[3]](#ref3)</sup> |
| **US indication** | Moderate-to-severe dyspareunia — a symptom of vulvar and vaginal atrophy due to menopause |
| **EU indication** | Treatment of VVA in postmenopausal women with moderate-to-severe symptoms (broader than US)<sup>[[8]](#ref8)</sup> |
| **Dose** | **One vaginal insert (6.5 mg prasterone) once daily at bedtime** via the provided applicator |
| **Loading / taper** | None |
| **Progestogen** | **Not required** for endometrial protection; endometrial data extend to about 1 year<sup>[[7]](#ref7)</sup> |
| **Absolute contraindication** | Undiagnosed abnormal genital bleeding |
| **Label warning** | Estrogen is a metabolite of prasterone; exogenous estrogen is contraindicated in women with a known or suspected history of breast cancer; Intrarosa has **not been studied** in women with a history of breast cancer |
| **Pediatric** | Safety not established |
| **Hepatic / renal impairment** | Effect on PK not studied; 19% (12-week trials) and 11% (52-week trial) of participants were ≥65 y |

## Pivotal clinical trials

Approval rested on two Phase III placebo-controlled RCTs (ERC-231, ERC-238) plus the 52-week open-label safety trial ERC-230.<sup>[[3]](#ref3)[[8]](#ref8)</sup> Both RCTs met all four coprimary endpoints.

### ERC-231 coprimary endpoints (n = 482 ITT, 12 weeks)<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup>

| Endpoint | Effect over placebo | p |
|---|---|---|
| **Parabasal cells (%)** | ↓ 27.7% | &lt;0.0001 |
| **Superficial cells (%)** | ↑ 8.44% | &lt;0.0001 |
| **Vaginal pH** | ↓ 0.66 units | &lt;0.0001 |
| **Dyspareunia severity** | ↓ 0.36 units over placebo (−1.42 from baseline) | 0.0002 |
| **Vaginal dryness** (key secondary) | ↓ 0.27 units over placebo (−1.44 from baseline) | 0.004 |

On gynecologic evaluation, vaginal secretions, epithelial integrity, surface thickness, and color all improved 86–121% over the placebo effect (p &lt; 0.0001).<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup>

**ERC-238** (Archer 2015, 12 weeks) was smaller and showed a different effect profile: parabasal cells ↓45.8%, superficial cells ↑4.7%, pH ↓0.83, dyspareunia severity ↓46% over placebo (p = 0.013), dryness ↓0.43 units (p = 0.013).<sup>[[23]](#ref23)</sup>

### 2026 meta-analysis — Lemos

Systematic review and meta-analysis of 6 RCTs (5 unique; n = 1,611):<sup>[[9]](#ref9)</sup>

- Vaginal dryness: MD −0.23 (95% CI −0.35 to −0.11) versus placebo
- Dyspareunia: MD −0.40 (95% CI −0.66 to −0.15) versus placebo
- No major safety concerns; AEs mild and infrequent
- Low-to-moderate heterogeneity

## Sexual function — placebo-controlled FSFI findings

These are placebo-controlled findings, not a head-to-head comparison with vaginal estrogen. **Labrie 2015** Phase III FSFI analysis (n = 482): all six FSFI domains improved significantly over placebo at 12 weeks:<sup>[[4]](#ref4)</sup>

| FSFI domain | Improvement vs placebo | p |
|---|---|---|
| Desire | +49.0% | 0.0105 |
| Arousal | +56.8% | 0.0022 |
| Lubrication | +36.1% | 0.0005 |
| Orgasm | +33.0% | 0.047 |
| Satisfaction | +48.3% | 0.0012 |
| Pain | +39.2% | 0.001 |
| **Total FSFI** | **+41.3%** | 0.0006 |

An earlier dose-finding Phase III (Labrie 2009, n = 216) showed dose-dependent improvements in arousal or sensation (+68%, p = 0.006), arousal or lubrication (+39%, p = 0.0014), orgasm (+75%, p = 0.047), and dryness during intercourse (−57%, p = 0.0001) with the 1.0% DHEA dose.<sup>[[6]](#ref6)</sup>

Local androgenic activity is a proposed explanation for the desire and arousal findings. The trials do not isolate that mechanism or establish superiority to vaginal estrogen.<sup>[[4]](#ref4)[[6]](#ref6)</sup>

## Systemic exposure and its limits

**LC-MS/MS serum steroid analyses** after 12 weeks of 6.5 mg daily (Ke 2015, one trial; Martel 2016, integrated n = 723 DHEA + n = 266 placebo):<sup>[[10]](#ref10)[[11]](#ref11)</sup>

| Steroid | 12-wk level on DHEA | Normal postmenopausal value | Interpretation |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Estradiol (E2)** | 3.26–3.36 pg/mL | 4.17 pg/mL | **19–22% BELOW** normal postmenopausal |
| Estrone sulfate (E1-S) | 209–219 pg/mL | 220 pg/mL | Superimposable |
| Testosterone | Slightly ↑ | Within postmenopausal range | Lowest half of the range |
| DHEA-S | ↑ (dose-dependent) | Within postmenopausal range | — |
| ADT-G (androgen metabolite) | Within range | Normal postmenopausal | No excess androgenic activity |

Comparison with an average postmenopausal reference value does not show absence of absorption or absence of oncologic risk. The current label reports mean trough increases from baseline of 47% for prasterone, 21% for testosterone and 19% for estradiol after 12 weeks; these relative increases do not by themselves establish clinical harm or safety.<sup>[[3]](#ref3)</sup><sup>[[10]](#ref10)[[11]](#ref11)</sup>

### In cancer survivors — NCCTG N10C1 (Alliance)

**Barton 2018** (n = 345 blood samples in women with breast or gynecologic cancer history): 3.25 mg versus 6.5 mg DHEA versus plain moisturizer:<sup>[[12]](#ref12)</sup>

- DHEA-S and testosterone rose dose-dependently but stayed in the lowest half or quartile of the postmenopausal range
- Estradiol increased with 6.5 mg but not with 3.25 mg (p = 0.05)
- Estrogen concentrations did not change in the aromatase-inhibitor subgroup during this study; this short-term biochemical finding does not establish long-term breast-cancer safety
- Biomarkers of bone formation unchanged in all arms
- Vaginal maturation improved: 100% (3.25 mg), 86% (6.5 mg), 64% (moisturizer)

These exploratory biochemical findings may inform an oncology discussion, but they do not override the label warning or establish recurrence safety in AI users.

## Comparison with vaginal estrogen

**Archer 2017** indirect cross-trial comparison of prasterone 6.5 mg versus CEE 0.3 mg versus estradiol 10 µg:<sup>[[13]](#ref13)</sup>

| Outcome | Prasterone 6.5 mg | CEE 0.3 mg | Estradiol 10 µg |
|---|---|---|---|
| Dyspareunia — total Δ from baseline | 1.27–1.63 | 1.4 | 1.23 |
| Dyspareunia — Δ over placebo | 0.35–1.21 | 0.7–1.0 | 0.33 |
| Dryness — total Δ from baseline | 1.44–1.58 | 1.1 | 1.23 |
| Dryness — Δ over placebo | 0.30–0.43 | 0.40 | 0.33 |

Authors: prasterone "appears to be at least as efficacious as 0.3 mg CEE or 10 µg estradiol" for VVA symptoms.<sup>[[13]](#ref13)</sup> A 2023 *JAMA* review estimated symptom-improvement ranges of 60–80% (vaginal estrogen) versus 40–80% (prasterone) versus 30–50% (oral ospemifene).<sup>[[14]](#ref14)</sup>

### Features to discuss when choosing between treatments

- **All six FSFI domains** improve including desire and arousal<sup>[[4]](#ref4)</sup>
- **Androgenic effects** on muscularis and lamina propria (deeper tissue layers)<sup>[[6]](#ref6)</sup>
- Serum E2 below normal postmenopausal values<sup>[[10]](#ref10)</sup>
- **Comparative safety in AI users is unproven**; unchanged short-term E2 in one study does not establish recurrence safety<sup>[[12]](#ref12)</sup>

### Disadvantages vs vaginal estrogen

- **Daily** dosing (versus 2×/wk inserts or 90-day ring)
- **Vaginal discharge** from melting vehicle (approximately 6–14%)<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[3]](#ref3)</sup>
- More expensive; no generic
- **No UTI-prevention evidence.** Vaginal estrogen is the AUA-endorsed rUTI agent (RR 0.42 in randomized trials)<sup>[[21]](#ref21)</sup>
- **No head-to-head RCT** versus vaginal estrogen<sup>[[15]](#ref15)</sup>

See [Vaginal and topical estrogen](/docs/foundations/pharmacology/hormonal-therapies/vaginal-topical-estrogen) for the rUTI data.

## Adverse effects and safety

### Short-term (pooled 4 RCTs, n = 665 DHEA + n = 464 placebo)<sup>[[3]](#ref3)</sup>

Only AE with ≥2% incidence and greater than placebo: vaginal discharge 5.71% (DHEA) versus 3.66% (placebo), from melting of the suppository vehicle at body temperature, not pathologic.

### Long-term (52-wk open-label ERC-230, n = 521)<sup>[[8]](#ref8)[[3]](#ref3)</sup>

- Vaginal discharge: 14.2% (74/521)
- Abnormal Pap: 2.1% (11/521); 10 ASCUS, 1 LSIL
- **No endometrial hyperplasia or carcinoma**; in the 422 women with endometrial sampling, all had inactive or atrophic endometrium (NAMS 2020)<sup>[[7]](#ref7)</sup>
- No clinically significant serum-steroid changes beyond normal postmenopausal ranges
- No drug-related serious AEs

### Androgenic side effects

Cochrane review of DHEA (all routes) found an association with androgenic AEs, mainly acne (OR 3.77; 95% CI 1.36–10.4; p = 0.01), but the pooled trials used oral or transdermal DHEA; none tested the intravaginal insert, and the label lists no androgenic adverse reaction for it.<sup>[[16]](#ref16)[[3]](#ref3)</sup>

### Endometrial safety

No progestogen required; no hyperplasia in 52-wk data. The cited 52-week safety study was open-label, and long-term safety beyond this window is not established. Patients must be counseled to report any vaginal bleeding promptly.<sup>[[14]](#ref14)</sup>

## Use in breast cancer survivors

The most nuanced clinical question. The FDA label carries a warning; guideline positions are cautious but allow use.

| Source | Position |
|---|---|
| **FDA label** | Estrogen is a metabolite; use of exogenous estrogen is contraindicated in women with known/suspected breast cancer; Intrarosa **has not been studied** in this population<sup>[[3]](#ref3)</sup> |
| **ACOG 2021 clinical consensus** | DHEA or vaginal testosterone may be considered **when vaginal estrogen is not an option**; this is not a requirement to try estrogen first<sup>[[17]](#ref17)</sup> |
| **AUA/SUFU/AUGS 2025** | Clinicians should counsel that vaginal DHEA does not increase breast-cancer risk (Expert Opinion). The guideline text says clinicians may recommend vaginal DHEA in multidisciplinary shared decision-making for patients with a breast-cancer history, noting little data in this group; it states DHEA is not associated with increased circulating estradiol, which is a narrower claim than the label's<sup>[[22]](#ref22)</sup> |
| **NAMS 2020** | Vaginal DHEA is not contraindicated in breast-cancer survivors, but US labeling advises caution because estrogen is a metabolite; it has not been studied in this group, and estradiol and testosterone stay within postmenopausal ranges. Vaginal estrogen or DHEA may be considered when GSM persists after nonhormonal trials (Level C)<sup>[[7]](#ref7)</sup> |
| **NCCN Survivorship 2026** | Listed among "other topical hormones" for vaginal dryness; "limited data regarding the use of androgen therapy in survivors of hormonally mediated cancers"; "more information needed … particularly for breast cancer survivors on AIs"<sup>[[18]](#ref18)</sup> |
| **NCCN Breast Cancer Risk Reduction 2026** | **"Consider avoiding bioidentical hormones or DHEA"** in women on risk-reducing agents<sup>[[19]](#ref19)</sup> |

### VIBRA pilot — Mension 2022

**10 breast cancer survivors on AIs**, 6 months of vaginal prasterone (nightly for month 1, then every second night):<sup>[[20]](#ref20)</sup>

- Mean serum E2 3.4 to 4.3 pg/mL (p = 0.91, not significant)
- Dyspareunia VAS 8.5 to 0.4 (p = 0.018)
- VHI 9.75 to 15.8 (p = 0.028)
- FSFI 11.2 to 20.6 (p = 0.028)
- Vaginal pH 8.1 to 6.5 (p = 0.033)

### Pharmacologic rationale in AI users

Aromatase inhibition offers a mechanistic rationale for lower estrogen conversion, but it does not prove that conversion is absent in every patient or that DHEA is oncologically safe. NCCTG and the 10-patient VIBRA pilot provide short-term biochemical and symptom observations, not breast-cancer recurrence endpoints.<sup>[[12]](#ref12)[[20]](#ref20)</sup>

### Clinical implication

The 2025 AUA/SUFU/AUGS GSM guideline finds local low-dose vaginal estrogen has the strongest evidence, but too little evidence to favor one hormonal therapy over another and does not support a required stepwise sequence; AUA/SUFU/AUGS 2025 recommends that clinicians offer vaginal DHEA to patients with GSM for dryness and/or dyspareunia (Moderate Recommendation; Grade C); the 2020 NAMS position statement lists vaginal DHEA among effective treatments for moderate-to-severe GSM (Level A).<sup>[[22]](#ref22)[[7]](#ref7)</sup> After nonhormonal options, vaginal DHEA may be discussed when vaginal estrogen is not an option. Explain the FDA warning, limited long-term cancer data and lack of proven comparative safety in AI users; involve oncology in the decision.<sup>[[3]](#ref3)[[17]](#ref17)</sup>

## Practical prescribing

| Parameter | Recommendation |
|---|---|
| **Ideal candidate** | Postmenopausal woman with moderate-to-severe dyspareunia who prefers a daily vaginal option; prasterone is a hormone precursor that forms estrogen and androgen metabolites<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup> |
| **Dose** | 6.5 mg vaginal insert nightly at bedtime<sup>[[3]](#ref3)</sup> |
| **Onset** | Significant improvement by 6 weeks; full effect by 12 weeks<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup> |
| **Duration** | Indefinite — GSM is chronic; symptoms recur on discontinuation<sup>[[3]](#ref3)</sup> |
| **Progestogen** | **Not required**<sup>[[3]](#ref3)</sup> |
| **Monitoring** | No routine serum monitoring; evaluate any postmenopausal bleeding<sup>[[14]](#ref14)</sup> |
| **Patient counseling** | ~6–14% vaginal discharge from melting vehicle — not a sign of infection; panty liner if bothersome<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[3]](#ref3)</sup> |
| **Breast cancer survivors** | May be discussed when vaginal estrogen is not an option; oncology involvement and explicit cancer-safety uncertainty<sup>[[17]](#ref17)[[20]](#ref20)</sup> |
| **Cost** | Brand-name only (~$200–300/month without insurance); no generic |
| **Storage** | Store at **5–30 °C**; room temperature or refrigeration are permitted<sup>[[3]](#ref3)</sup> |

## Clinical Positioning

- **Vaginal DHEA 6.5 mg nightly is FDA-approved for moderate-to-severe dyspareunia due to menopause** and is supported by two Phase III placebo-controlled RCTs meeting all four coprimary endpoints.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[3]](#ref3)</sup>
- **FSFI improvement is supported by placebo-controlled data**; superiority over vaginal estrogen or a specific androgen-mediated clinical mechanism has not been established.<sup>[[4]](#ref4)</sup>
- **Systemic exposure is low, not absent.** Reference-range hormone levels do not establish safety in hormone-sensitive cancer.<sup>[[10]](#ref10)[[11]](#ref11)</sup>
- **No progestogen is required** and no endometrial hyperplasia was seen at 52 weeks; long-term (&gt;1 y) endometrial data are absent. Evaluate any postmenopausal bleeding.<sup>[[3]](#ref3)[[8]](#ref8)[[14]](#ref14)</sup>
- **Prasterone is not an established UTI-prevention treatment.** Vaginal estrogen remains the AUA-endorsed agent for rUTI prevention (RR 0.42). Do not substitute DHEA for UTI-driven indications.<sup>[[21]](#ref21)</sup>
- **In breast cancer survivors, DHEA may be discussed when vaginal estrogen is not an option**; ACOG does not require an estrogen trial first. Small AI-user studies cannot establish recurrence safety.<sup>[[17]](#ref17)[[20]](#ref20)</sup>
- **Counsel about vaginal discharge** from melting vehicle: 6% short-term, 14% long-term; not infectious; panty liner usually sufficient.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[3]](#ref3)</sup>
- **Cost and daily dosing are the practical downsides:** no generic, approximately $200–300/month; nightly application compared to 2×/wk or 90-day estrogen-ring alternatives.
- **Onset is gradual.** 6-week partial benefit, 12-week full effect; match expectations accordingly.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup>
- **Storage is 5–30 °C**, at room temperature or refrigerated; the current US label does not impose the former seven-day room-temperature rule.<sup>[[3]](#ref3)</sup>
