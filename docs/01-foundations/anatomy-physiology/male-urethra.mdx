# Male Urethra

The male urethra is a **tubular channel approximately 18–20 cm in length** that serves as both a **urinary conduit and reproductive passage**, extending from the bladder neck to the external urethral meatus at the glans penis.<sup>[[2]](#ref2)</sup> It is divided into anatomic segments and has complex neurovascular supply and sphincteric mechanisms that enable both continence and micturition.

![Gray's Anatomy figure of the male urethra laid open on its anterior surface](/img/anatomy/gray1142-urethra.png)
*Gray's Anatomy Fig. 1142 — The male urethra laid open on its anterior (upper) surface, showing the prostatic, membranous, and penile segments.*

## Anatomic Divisions

The male urethra can be divided into both anatomic and functional segments.<sup>[[2]](#ref2)</sup>

### Posterior Urethra (approx. 3–4 cm)

- **Prostatic Urethra (3–4 cm):** Traverses the prostate gland and receives the openings of the **ejaculatory ducts**. Its walls are intimately attached to the prostatic lobes.<sup>[[2]](#ref2)[[3]](#ref3)[[4]](#ref4)</sup>
- **Membranous Urethra (2–3.5 cm):** Passes through the urogenital diaphragm and represents the **narrowest portion** of the channel. It is surrounded by areolar tissue and the voluntary fibers of the external urethral sphincter.<sup>[[2]](#ref2)[[3]](#ref3)[[4]](#ref4)</sup>

### Anterior Urethra (approx. 15–16 cm)

- **Bulbar Urethra:** Contained within the bulb of the **corpus spongiosum**. Its lumen widens to form the urethral bulb (intrabulbar fossa), where semen collects before expulsion.<sup>[[2]](#ref2)[[3]](#ref3)</sup>
- **Penile (Pendulous) Urethra:** Extends through the penile shaft, lying within the corpus spongiosum, terminating at the **fossa navicularis** — a dilation in the glans penis.<sup>[[2]](#ref2)[[3]](#ref3)</sup>

Functionally, the urethra is divided into **posterior** (prostatic and membranous) and **anterior** segments, with the anterior urethra contained within the corpus spongiosum and penis.<sup>[[2]](#ref2)</sup>

## Histologic Structure

The urethral lining transitions throughout its length:

| Segment | Epithelium |
|---|---|
| Prostatic | Transitional (urothelium) |
| Membranous & bulbar | Stratified columnar |
| Penile | Pseudostratified columnar |
| Fossa navicularis / meatus | Stratified squamous |

The urethral wall contains both **smooth and striated muscle** components organized in circular and longitudinal layers.<sup>[[5]](#ref5)</sup> The urethra is equipped with secretory glands:

- **Glands of Littré:** Mucus-secreting cells distributed along the penile urethra; ducts open toward the meatus providing pre-ejaculatory lubrication.
- **Bulbourethral (Cowper's) Glands:** Located lateral to the membranous urethra; ducts empty into the bulbar urethra.
- **Lacuna Magna:** Large dorsal pocket in the fossa navicularis.

## Sphincteric Mechanisms and Continence

The male urethral sphincter complex consists of two components that work synergistically.<sup>[[6]](#ref6)</sup>

**Lissosphincter (smooth muscle / internal sphincter):** Extends from the bladder neck through the prostatic urethra. Primarily responsible for **passive continence at rest**, under autonomic control, generating myogenic tone through L-type calcium channels independent of action potentials.<sup>[[5]](#ref5)</sup>

**Rhabdosphincter (striated muscle / external sphincter):** Forms an **omega-shaped loop** extending from the membranous urethra over the base of the prostate, with fibers predominantly anterior and lateral to the urethra.<sup>[[4]](#ref4)[[6]](#ref6)[[7]](#ref7)</sup> Contains nearly equal numbers of slow- and fast-twitch fibers. Provides **active continence during stress** (coughing, straining) and contributes to antegrade semen propulsion during ejaculation.<sup>[[5]](#ref5)[[6]](#ref6)</sup>

## Physiologic Functions

The urethra performs multiple integrated functions.<sup>[[5]](#ref5)</sup>

**Continence maintenance:** The urethra generates sustained tone (peak urethral pressure >100 cmH₂O) to prevent urinary leakage during bladder filling, and produces transient reflex pressure increases when abdominal pressure rises.<sup>[[5]](#ref5)</sup>

**Micturition control:** Recent evidence challenges the traditional view of the urethra as a passive conduit. The urethra **actively opens from the very beginning of micturition** through contraction of the anterior and lateral urethral muscle unit, suggesting the urethra controls the entire micturition process rather than the bladder being primarily responsible.<sup>[[8]](#ref8)</sup>

**Urethro-vesical reflex:** The proximal urethra contains specialized paraneuronal cells in the urothelium expressing acetylcholine and serotonin. These cells are in close proximity to nerve fibers and can rapidly induce detrusor contractions, supporting **bidirectional communication** between the urethra and bladder.<sup>[[9]](#ref9)</sup>

**Reproductive function:** The urethra serves as the conduit for semen during ejaculation, with the rhabdosphincter contributing to antegrade semen propulsion.<sup>[[6]](#ref6)[[10]](#ref10)</sup>

## Innervation

The male urethra receives dual innervation from both autonomic and somatic nervous systems.<sup>[[7]](#ref7)[[11]](#ref11)[[12]](#ref12)</sup>

**Autonomic innervation:** The **inferior hypogastric plexus** provides sympathetic and parasympathetic innervation to the smooth muscle components. Nerves reach the prostate and proximal urethra from dorsolateral and dorsal approaches, following the ejaculatory duct and seminal vesicle. Both smooth muscle layers receive sympathetic and parasympathetic excitatory innervation as well as nitrergic (nitric oxide) inhibitory innervation.<sup>[[5]](#ref5)[[11]](#ref11)</sup>

**Somatic innervation:** The **pudendal nerve** provides somatic motor innervation to the rhabdosphincter, with branches also originating from S4 as the lowest branch of the pelvic splanchnic nerve. The nerve to the levator ani frequently forms a common trunk with branches from the pelvic plexus (94% of cases), and various connections exist between the pudendal nerve and branches medial to the levator ani.<sup>[[7]](#ref7)[[12]](#ref12)</sup>

The innervation of the urethra and corpora cavernosa derives from **two origins**: one follows the ejaculatory duct reaching the proximal urethra dorsally, while the other follows the inferior vesical artery to reach the prostate laterally, forming the **neurovascular bundle** that spreads to the pelvic floor muscles and corpora cavernosa.<sup>[[11]](#ref11)</sup>

## Vascular Supply

![Lateral cross-section of the penis showing the corpus spongiosum containing the urethra](/img/anatomy/penis-lateral-cross-section.jpg)
*Lateral cross-section of the penis demonstrating the urethra within the corpus spongiosum, flanked by the paired corpora cavernosa.*

**Arterial supply:** The **internal pudendal artery** — typically from the anterior division of the internal iliac artery — provides the primary supply. Distally it gives rise to the **penile artery**, which branches into the **bulbar** and **urethral arteries**. The urethral artery supplies the corpus spongiosum and anastomoses with the dorsal artery at the glans.<sup>[[13]](#ref13)</sup> Accessory pudendal arteries may arise above the pelvic diaphragm as anatomical variants.<sup>[[13]](#ref13)</sup>

**Vascular regulation:** Normal urethral pressure requires adequate blood flow to the urothelium and lamina propria.<sup>[[5]](#ref5)</sup> In urethral arterioles, nitric oxide released from parasympathetic nerves counteracts sympathetic vasoconstriction both pre-synaptically (inhibiting sympathetic transmission) and post-synaptically (causing smooth muscle relaxation), ensuring adequate blood supply to the metabolically active urethral musculature.<sup>[[14]](#ref14)</sup>

| Segment | Arterial Supply |
|---|---|
| Posterior urethra | Inferior vesical artery, middle rectal artery |
| Bulbar urethra | Bulbar artery (branch of internal pudendal) |
| Penile urethra | Urethral artery (branch of internal pudendal) |
| Glans / fossa navicularis | Dorsal artery anastomosis |

## Clinical Significance

:::note Stricture Disease
The bulbar segment is most susceptible to strictures: inflammation originating in the glands of Littré triggers dense fibrous proliferation (spongiofibrosis) in the surrounding corpus spongiosum. Depth of spongiofibrosis determines surgical approach — superficial fibrosis may allow non-transecting repair; deep fibrosis requires excision and anastomosis or graft augmentation.
:::

:::note Lymphatic Drainage & Oncology
**Anterior urethral** cancers drain to **superficial inguinal** lymph nodes. **Posterior urethral** cancers (prostatic, membranous, and bulbar segments) drain to pelvic lymphatic channels (external iliac, obturator groups).<sup>[[2]](#ref2)</sup>
:::

:::note Nerve-Sparing Surgery
Understanding the dual neurovascular origin — dorsal (following ejaculatory duct) and lateral (following inferior vesical artery, forming the NVB) — is critical for nerve-sparing prostatectomy and PFUI repair to preserve erectile and continence function.<sup>[[11]](#ref11)</sup>
:::

The complex anatomy of the male urethra makes it **more susceptible to injury and stricture disease than the female urethra**,<sup>[[15]](#ref15)</sup> and mastery of its segmental anatomy, sphincteric mechanisms, and neurovascular supply is foundational for all urethral reconstructive procedures.
