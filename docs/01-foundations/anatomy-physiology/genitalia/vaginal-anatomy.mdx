# The Vagina

The vagina is an elastic fibromuscular canal extending from the vulvar vestibule to the cervix. It serves as an outflow tract for menstrual fluid, a part of the reproductive tract, and a component of sexual function. This page is a gross-anatomy orientation, not guidance for prolapse, fistula, infection, pain, malignancy, congenital conditions, or vaginal reconstruction.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[2]](#ref2)</sup>

## Gross structure and relations

The vagina extends from the introitus to the cervix, where it forms fornices around the cervix. Its anterior wall is related to the urethra distally and bladder more proximally. Posteriorly, it is related to the perineal body, rectum, and—at its upper end—the rectouterine pouch. Laterally, it is related to pelvic-floor muscles, pelvic connective tissues, vessels, and nerves.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[2]](#ref2)</sup>

The vaginal wall has mucosal, muscular, and adventitial connective-tissue components. The nonkeratinized squamous mucosa is hormone responsive. Vaginal secretions derive from epithelial transudation, cervical mucus, vestibular gland secretions, and desquamated cells; the vagina itself has no discrete secretory glands.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup>

## Support and pelvic-floor relationships

Vaginal support depends on the interaction of pelvic-floor muscles with connective-tissue attachments to the pelvic sidewall. At the upper vagina and cervix, cardinal and uterosacral connective-tissue regions contribute apical support. Mid-vaginal support includes lateral attachments often described as paravaginal; distal support includes the perineal body, perineal membrane, and pelvic-floor musculature. This framework describes anatomic relationships, not a treatment plan for pelvic organ prolapse.<sup>[[3]](#ref3)</sup>

## Blood, lymphatic, and nerve supply

Vaginal blood supply includes branches of the vaginal and uterine arteries from the internal iliac system, with distal contributions from internal pudendal branches. Venous drainage generally follows these vessels toward internal iliac veins. The uterine artery travels near the lateral fornix while crossing above the ureter; this is an important pelvic relationship, but no procedural instruction is implied here.<sup>[[2]](#ref2)</sup>

Lymphatic drainage from the vagina primarily reaches internal and external iliac nodal basins, while the distal introitus has drainage patterns shared with the external genitalia. Innervation includes autonomic fibers through the inferior hypogastric plexus and somatic sensory contribution to the lower vagina from the pudendal nerve.<sup>[[2]](#ref2)[[4]](#ref4)</sup>

## Functional orientation

Estrogen influences epithelial thickness and glycogen availability, which in turn affects the vaginal environment across the lifespan. The upper vagina is predominantly associated with visceral/autonomic pathways and the lower vagina with greater somatic sensory input. These general physiologic relationships do not establish a diagnosis or treatment indication for an individual patient.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[2]](#ref2)</sup>
