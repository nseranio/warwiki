# The Vulva

The vulva is the external female genital complex. It includes the mons pubis, labia majora and minora, clitoris, vestibule, vestibular bulbs, glandular structures, urethral opening, and vaginal opening.[[1]](#ref1)[[2]](#ref2)

This page is an anatomy orientation, not a protocol for vulvar surgery, trauma, infection, dermatologic disease, pain, malignancy, female genital cutting reconstruction, gender-affirming care, labiaplasty, fistula repair, or obstetric injury.

See [Vaginal Anatomy](/docs/foundations/anatomy-physiology/genitalia/vaginal-anatomy), [Female Urethra](/docs/foundations/anatomy-physiology/urinary-tract/female-urethra), and [The Perineum](/docs/foundations/anatomy-physiology/pelvis-support/perineum).

## Surface structures

The mons pubis is fatty tissue over the pubic symphysis. The labia majora are paired outer folds, and the labia minora are thinner folds that border the vestibule and contribute anteriorly to the clitoral hood and frenulum. Labial appearance varies widely between individuals and across the lifespan.[[1]](#ref1)[[2]](#ref2)

The vestibule lies between the labia minora and contains the external urethral opening, vaginal opening, and glandular openings. Bartholin glands are associated with the posterolateral vestibular region; paraurethral (Skene) glands lie near the urethral opening.[[1]](#ref1)[[2]](#ref2)

## Clitoris, nerves, and vessels

The clitoris includes a visible glans and a body with paired crura. It contains erectile tissue and is highly innervated. The vestibular bulbs are adjacent erectile structures around the vestibular region.[[1]](#ref1)

The pudendal nerve gives terminal branches to the vulva, including the dorsal nerve of the clitoris, perineal branches, and inferior rectal branches. Additional anterior cutaneous contribution comes from ilioinguinal and genitofemoral nerves. The internal pudendal arterial system supplies much of the external genitalia, with external pudendal contributions to anterior structures.[[1]](#ref1)[[3]](#ref3)

The perineal body is a central fibromuscular attachment point posterior to the vestibule. It has close anatomic relationships to the superficial perineal musculature and pelvic-floor support structures.[[1]](#ref1)

## Clinical boundaries

- Do not use this page to plan a dissection, flap, nerve block, injection, biopsy, reconstructive procedure, treatment of a vulvar condition, or cancer evaluation.
- Prior surgery, trauma, radiation, scarring, inflammatory disease, pelvic-floor injury, pregnancy, and individual anatomic variation can alter the usual relationships.
- New vulvar pain, swelling, bleeding, ulceration, color change, mass, fever, urinary symptoms, or postoperative concerns should receive clinical assessment appropriate to the presentation.
