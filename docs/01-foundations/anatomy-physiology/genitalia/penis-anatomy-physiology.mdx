# The Penis

The **penis** is a tri-compartment erectile organ whose structural integrity (tunica albuginea), vascular inflow (cavernosal arteries), veno-occlusive mechanism (subtunical plexus compressed against the tunica), and neural input (cavernous and dorsal nerves) must all function for normal erection, micturition, and ejaculation.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[2]](#ref2)[[19]](#ref19)</sup> For the reconstructive urologist, each of those substrates is an operative target or pitfall: Peyronie's plaque surgery depends on tunica mechanics, penile prosthesis on tunica and corporal capacity, post-prostatectomy ED on cavernous-nerve preservation, priapism management on corporal physiology, and urethral reconstruction on Buck's-layer dissection planes. Erectile physiology is covered only to the depth required to reason about ED, priapism and nerve-sparing surgery.

---

## Gross Anatomy and Fascial Layers

The penis is composed of three erectile bodies: the paired corpora cavernosa (the rigidity engines) and the single midline corpus spongiosum (which surrounds the urethra and expands distally into the glans). Proximal attachments include the crura of the corpora cavernosa to the ischiopubic rami, the bulb of the corpus spongiosum to the perineal membrane, and the ischiocavernosus and bulbospongiosus muscles over each.<sup>[[5]](#ref5)</sup>

![Transverse mid-shaft penile cross-section showing the corpora, spongiosum, tunica, Buck's fascia, and dorsal neurovascular bundle](/img/diagrams/penile-cross-section.svg)

*Transverse mid-shaft section (dorsal up). The paired **corpora cavernosa**, each wrapped in **tunica albuginea** and carrying a central **deep cavernosal artery**, meet at the **intercavernosal septum**; the ventral **corpus spongiosum** carries the **urethra**. The **dorsal neurovascular bundle** (deep dorsal vein, paired dorsal arteries, dorsal nerves) runs at the 12-o'clock position beneath **Buck's fascia** — the relationships that define the dissection planes for Peyronie's plaque surgery, prosthesis placement, and nerve-sparing. (Original WARWIKI schematic)*

### External layers (superficial to deep)

| Layer | Composition | Operative relevance |
|---|---|---|
| **Skin** | Thin, mobile, hairless on the shaft | The mobile skin tube is the plane of degloving for circumcision, hypospadias repair, Peyronie's curvature repair, and skin-substitution urethroplasty |
| **Dartos fascia** | Loose subcutaneous tissue with smooth-muscle fibers and **axial-pattern vessels from the external pudendal artery**<sup>[[4]](#ref4)[[6]](#ref6)</sup> | The vascular plane for axial and island skin flaps (Orandi, Quartey, McAninch, Blandy) in substitution urethroplasty |
| **Buck's fascia** | Dense fibroelastic envelope encircling all three erectile bodies; splits into two leaves ventrally around the corpus spongiosum<sup>[[5]](#ref5)[[6]](#ref6)[[7]](#ref7)</sup> | Carries the **dorsal neurovascular bundle** (dorsal artery, dorsal nerves, deep dorsal vein). Dissection under Buck's is the Peyronie's / curvature plane; dissection above Buck's is the circumcision / degloving plane |
| **Tunica albuginea** | Bilayered collagen jacket around each corpus cavernosum<sup>[[8]](#ref8)[[9]](#ref9)</sup> | The rigidity-bearing structure; the plane of corporotomy for penile prosthesis and of plication/incision for Peyronie's correction |

### The tunica albuginea — the key structural layer

The tunica is bilayered:<sup>[[8]](#ref8)[[9]](#ref9)</sup>

- **Inner circular layer.** A complete 360° lamina with radiating intracavernous pillars (struts) that augment the septum and confer internal structural support.
- **Outer longitudinal layer.** Incomplete, approximately 300° coat; absent ventrally between the 5- and 7-o'clock positions where the corpus spongiosum and urethra lie.<sup>[[9]](#ref9)</sup> A Peyronie's plaque reduces local tunical extensibility and can bend the erect penis toward the involved side; the normal ventral gap alone does not establish the direction of a patient's curvature.<sup>[[27]](#ref27)</sup>

**Mechanical properties.** The tunica is collagen-dominant with elastic fibers and is anisotropic. In porcine tunica, type I collagen made up 95.5% of total protein, and Young's modulus was 60 MPa longitudinally vs 8 MPa circumferentially (six pigs); human values have not been reported in the same way.<sup>[[10]](#ref10)</sup> The tunica has regional thickness differences in the cited developmental-anatomy work.<sup>[[11]](#ref11)</sup> Plication shortens the convex side to balance the shorter plaque side; selection depends on erectile function, length and deformity rather than a presumed universal plaque direction.<sup>[[27]](#ref27)</sup>

**Why dissection planes matter.** Three parallel dissection planes drive most open penile surgery:
1. **Above Buck's (subcutaneous / dartos).** Degloving for circumcision, phalloplasty skin mobilization, hypospadias.
2. **Under Buck's, on the tunica.** Peyronie's plication / incision, curvature correction, venous ligation.
3. **Into the tunica (corporotomy).** Penile prosthesis insertion, evacuation of ischemic priapism, Peyronie's plaque excision with graft.
Misidentifying these planes can injure the dorsal neurovascular structures or lead to implant malposition; this anatomy overview does not establish the frequency of either mechanism.

### Ligamentous support

The penis is suspended by two ligaments relevant to reconstructive surgery:

- **Fundiform ligament.** Superficial, from the linea alba to the penile dartos.
- **Suspensory ligament.** Deeper triangular condensation from the pubic symphysis to the dorsal tunica at the penopubic junction. Partial division is the anatomic step of lengthening procedures; complete division destabilizes the penis and is generally avoided.

## Erectile Tissue and Corporal Architecture

The corpora cavernosa contain a three-dimensional network of endothelium-lined sinusoidal spaces separated by bundles of trabecular smooth muscle that insert at multiple points into the fibrous skeleton.<sup>[[5]](#ref5)[[12]](#ref12)</sup> The two cavernosa communicate through the midline septum, which is fenestrated proximally and complete distally. This is exploited by the corporal cross-over technique for unilateral dilator impasse during prosthesis insertion, and it underlies the clinical presentation of unilateral stuttering priapism with bilateral rigidity. The corpus spongiosum has the same sinusoidal architecture but thinner walls and larger sinuses, so it remains compressible during erection and does not occlude the urethra.

The subtunical venular plexus lies between the sinusoids and the tunica albuginea and is the mechanical substrate of veno-occlusion.<sup>[[14]](#ref14)</sup>

## Vascular Anatomy

### Arterial inflow

The dominant supply is from the internal pudendal artery (a branch of the anterior division of the internal iliac). As it enters Alcock's canal it becomes the common penile artery, which divides into three terminal branches on each side:<sup>[[3]](#ref3)[[5]](#ref5)[[13]](#ref13)</sup>

| Artery | Course | Territory |
|---|---|---|
| **Cavernosal (deep) artery** | Enters the corpus cavernosum at the hilum; gives off **helicine arteries** that open into the sinusoids | Fills the corpus cavernosum during erection |
| **Dorsal penile artery** | Runs under Buck's fascia on the dorsum, bracketed by the paired dorsal nerves and the deep dorsal vein | Supplies skin, Buck's, and contributes to glans engorgement |
| **Bulbourethral artery** | Perineal; enters the bulb of the corpus spongiosum | Supplies the corpus spongiosum, urethra, and glans |

The three paired vessels have extensive anastomoses, which is the anatomic basis of glans survival after urethroplasty even when the urethral artery is sacrificed, and of partial-penectomy flap design.<sup>[[5]](#ref5)</sup> The external pudendal arteries (from the femoral system) supply penile skin and dartos and provide the axial vessels for distal-based skin flaps.<sup>[[4]](#ref4)[[6]](#ref6)</sup>

Accessory pudendal arteries run above the pelvic diaphragm and can be dominant suppliers. A meta-analysis of 23 studies (4,945 patients) found an accessory pudendal artery in a pooled 28.5% of patients, with penile supply from the internal pudendal artery alone in 61.9%, from both in 32.8% and from the accessory artery alone in 5.4%.<sup>[[34]](#ref34)</sup> The authors advocate preserving the vessel during radical prostatectomy when it is present, because its sacrifice could reduce penile arterial inflow.

### Venous drainage

Three drainage systems in parallel:<sup>[[5]](#ref5)[[8]](#ref8)[[14]](#ref14)</sup>

- **Superficial.** Superficial dorsal veins in the dartos, drain to the external pudendal / saphenous system.
- **Intermediate.** Deep dorsal vein in the midline beneath Buck's fascia, fed by circumflex veins from the spongiosum and emissary veins piercing the tunica from the cavernosa.
- **Deep / proximal.** Cavernosal and crural veins drain the proximal corpora into the internal pudendal venous system.

The subtunical venular plexus interposed between the sinusoids and the tunica is the hinge of the veno-occlusive mechanism (see below). Venous-ligation / dorsal-vein procedures for venogenic ED target the deep dorsal and circumflex drainage; long-term outcomes are poor, so the operation has largely been abandoned.

## Innervation

Penile innervation follows three systems, arriving in two functional compartments:<sup>[[5]](#ref5)[[13]](#ref13)[[15]](#ref15)[[16]](#ref16)[[17]](#ref17)[[18]](#ref18)</sup>

| System | Origin | Nerve | Destination | Function |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Parasympathetic** | S2–S4 | Pelvic → pelvic plexus → **cavernous nerves** | Helicine arteries and trabecular smooth muscle | **Erection** (NO release) |
| **Sympathetic** | T10–L2 | Hypogastric → pelvic plexus → cavernous nerves | Trabecular smooth muscle | **Detumescence and ejaculation** (α-adrenergic) |
| **Somatic** | S2–S4 | Pudendal → **dorsal nerves of the penis** (sensory) and **perineal branches** (sensory and motor) | Penile skin and glans; ischiocavernosus and bulbospongiosus | Sensation and muscular **expulsion** of semen; emission is predominantly sympathetic<sup>[[31]](#ref31)</sup> |

### The cavernous nerves — the clinically critical bundle

The cavernous nerves are the largest branches of the inferior hypogastric (pelvic) plexus. They run along the posterolateral aspect of the seminal vesicles and prostate, cross the urogenital diaphragm with the membranous urethra, and ascend to the 1- and 11-o'clock positions on the bulbous urethra before piercing the tunica albuginea alongside the cavernosal artery.<sup>[[13]](#ref13)</sup> They terminate on helicine arterioles and trabecular smooth muscle.

Two clinical implications:

1. **Nerve-sparing radical prostatectomy.** Cavernous nerve pathways run close to the prostate and can be affected by thermal, traction and resection injury. Nerve preservation is relevant to erectile recovery, but outcomes vary with baseline function, age, cancer extent and technique, and this anatomy does not support a single recovery rate.<sup>[[26]](#ref26)[[30]](#ref30)</sup>
2. **Dual route to the corpora.** Cavernous nerves reach the urethra and corpora along *both* a dorsal (along the seminal-vesicle / ejaculatory-duct axis) and a lateral (along the inferior vesical artery, forming the NVB) pathway.<sup>[[17]](#ref17)</sup> Preserving only the lateral NVB does not preserve full erectile innervation. The cadaveric authors advise saving both the dorsal and lateral roots, which were connected in about one third of specimens.

### The dorsal nerves — anatomy of the DPN block

The paired dorsal nerves course with the dorsal vessels near Buck's fascia. Landmark-based dorsal penile nerve blocks may use 10- and 2-o'clock infrapubic approaches, but techniques are not standardized, and the nearby vessels and corpora create injection hazards. Ultrasound can identify the target compartment just deep to Buck's fascia. This description is not a needle-placement guide.<sup>[[32]](#ref32)</sup>

### Neurochemical organization

Proximally, the intracavernous compartment is predominantly autonomic (nitrergic); distally, both extra- and intracavernous compartments are predominantly somatic, with intrapenile anastomoses carrying mixed fibers.<sup>[[18]](#ref18)</sup> In a fetal immunohistochemical study, nNOS-positive cavernous nerve fibers joined the dorsal nerve at the penile hilum.<sup>[[16]](#ref16)</sup> This communication may explain why priapism can coexist with preserved somatic sensation, but the clinical consequences have not been established.

## Physiology of Erection

Erection is a neurovascular event coordinating neural input, smooth-muscle relaxation, and a mechanical veno-occlusive switch.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[2]](#ref2)[[19]](#ref19)[[22]](#ref22)[[23]](#ref23)[[24]](#ref24)</sup>

### Flaccid state

Tonic α-adrenergic sympathetic output contracts trabecular smooth muscle; cavernosal arteries are constricted, sinusoids are collapsed, and intracavernosal PₒO₂ is low (approximately 35 mmHg).

### Tumescence

Sexual stimulus activates pelvic-nerve parasympathetic output and withdraws sympathetic tone:

1. **NO release** from nonadrenergic-noncholinergic (NANC) nerve terminals (neurogenic NO, from nNOS) and endothelium (endothelial NO, from eNOS).
2. **sGC activation** raises **cGMP** in trabecular smooth muscle.
3. **Cytosolic Ca²⁺ falls**, producing smooth-muscle **relaxation** of helicine arterioles and trabeculae.
4. **Sinusoids engorge** with high-flow arterial blood; the corpora expand against the tunica albuginea.

Neurogenic NO initiates erection; endothelial NO sustains it.<sup>[[20]](#ref20)</sup>

### Veno-occlusion — the mechanical switch

As the sinusoids balloon, the subtunical venular plexus is compressed between the expanded sinusoids and the noncompliant tunica albuginea, shutting off emissary venous outflow. Intracavernosal pressure rises toward, and briefly above, systolic blood pressure; intracavernosal PₒO₂ approaches arterial values.<sup>[[14]](#ref14)[[19]](#ref19)</sup>

### Rigid-erection phase

Reflex contraction of the ischiocavernosus (and, transiently, bulbospongiosus) muscles at the crura drives intracavernosal pressure above systolic pressure, producing maximal axial rigidity during penetrative activity.

### Detumescence

Sympathetic activation (orgasm, ejaculation, or loss of stimulus) releases norepinephrine, constricting arterioles and contracting trabeculae. PDE5 hydrolyzes cGMP, terminating smooth-muscle relaxation. The subtunical venous channels reopen and venous drainage resumes in three phases (initial pressure rise, slow decline, rapid decline).<sup>[[19]](#ref19)[[21]](#ref21)[[25]](#ref25)</sup> PDE5 inhibitors act by potentiating this pathway; they do not independently trigger erection, and sexual stimulation and intact neurogenic / endothelial NO release are still required.

## Clinical Correlations for the Reconstructive Urologist

- **Erectile dysfunction mechanisms (treatment drivers).** Clinically useful ED phenotyping distinguishes arteriogenic (atherosclerotic inflow disease, often co-present with CAD), venogenic (venous leak) (inadequate veno-occlusion from tunical failure, large venous channels, or Peyronie's), neurogenic (post-prostatectomy, diabetes, SCI), smooth-muscle / endothelial (age-related or systemic vascular disease), and psychogenic. PDE5i efficacy decreases in venogenic and severe neurogenic ED; intracavernosal injection, VED, and penile prosthesis populate the escalation ladder.
- **Peyronie's disease.** Focal tunica-albuginea fibrosis that may bend the penis toward the plaque. Surgery is considered for stable disease with intercourse-limiting deformity, after assessing length, curvature, rigidity and patient goals:<sup>[[27]](#ref27)</sup>
  - **Plication** (Nesbit excision, 16-dot, or tunica plication) shortens the convex side. It is simple and preserves erectile function, but shortens the penis.
  - **Tunical incision with grafting** can minimize shortening for severe or complex deformity in patients with adequate erectile function; postoperative ED remains a material risk. A &gt;60° threshold has been proposed but not validated as an absolute rule, and impaired erections are not an indication for grafting alone.<sup>[[27]](#ref27)</sup>
  - **Penile prosthesis** with straightening if needed (modeling, plication, incision with or without graft), for Peyronie's with ED that does not respond to pharmacotherapy.<sup>[[27]](#ref27)</sup>
- **Penile prosthesis.** Corporotomies at the 2- and 10-o'clock position on each side spare the dorsal neurovascular bundle and the ventral urethra / spongiosum (5- to 7-o'clock). Crural perforation is the most dangerous error during dilation; cross-over at the fenestrated proximal septum is the salvage for unilateral impasse.
- **Priapism: ischemic versus nonischemic.**
  - **Ischemic (low-flow).** Rigid painful corpora, with corporal blood gas showing hypoxia and acidosis; a urologic emergency. Urgent aspiration or irrigation and intracavernosal phenylephrine precede surgical escalation. Distal shunting, sometimes with tunneling, is an option when initial measures fail; a proximal shunt is not the mandatory next step. Early prosthesis may be discussed for prolonged or refractory cases with likely smooth-muscle injury, and it is not automatic at &gt;24 hours. Reassess persistent rigidity after shunting with blood gas or Doppler before reoperation.<sup>[[28]](#ref28)[[33]](#ref33)</sup>
  - **Nonischemic (high-flow).** Often a post-traumatic arteriocavernosal fistula with nonischemic corporal blood gas; specialist evaluation may lead to initial perineal compression or selective embolization if conservative management fails. It is not managed on the ischemic emergency pathway, although prolonged untreated cases can affect erectile function.<sup>[[28]](#ref28)</sup>
- **Post-prostatectomy ED.** Cavernous-nerve injury and vascular factors may contribute; baseline function, age and nerve preservation influence recovery. Pro-erectile treatment may start early for symptoms. Available evidence does not establish that any particular PDE5-inhibitor rehabilitation regimen restores spontaneous erectile function.<sup>[[30]](#ref30)</sup>
- **Dorsal penile nerve block.** Paired dorsal nerves are targets for infrapubic landmark or ultrasound-guided techniques, and the fascial compartment and the proximity of vessels and corpora matter. The 10- and 2-o'clock approach is one described option. It does not define a fixed injection depth, and the midline is not uniformly nerve-free.<sup>[[32]](#ref32)</sup>
- **Penile trauma: fracture of the penis.** Forcible bending of an erect penis may rupture the tunica albuginea, often with a popping sound, rapid detumescence and swelling; presentation varies. Urethral injury accompanies a subset (about 15% in the EAU summary, with a reported range of 1–38%). Blood at the meatus, hematuria or impaired voiding heighten concern, but their absence does not exclude urethral injury. Assess the urethra and surgically close a confirmed tunical tear; delayed presentation alone does not preclude exploration.<sup>[[29]](#ref29)</sup>
- **Urethral reconstruction and flap design.** The axial dartos vessels (off the external pudendal) supply skin flaps for substitution urethroplasty; the subcoronal and penile shaft skin are reliable donor territories. Harvest must preserve the dorsal neurovascular bundle and the Buck's-fascia plane.
- **Hypospadias and epispadias.** Corporal and glandular asymmetry, urethral-plate location, and tunical thickness drive operation choice (TIP / Snodgrass, Koyanagi, Duckett onlay); Buck's-fascia-plane dissection and axial skin-flap design govern reliable coverage.
- **Lymphatic drainage.** Penile skin and prepuce drain to superficial inguinal nodes; glans and corpora drain to deep inguinal and external iliac nodes. This split dictates the inguinal-dissection template in penile squamous-cell carcinoma.
