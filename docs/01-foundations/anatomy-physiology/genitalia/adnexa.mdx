# The Adnexa

The **adnexa** (ovaries, fallopian tubes, their suspensory mesenteric ligaments, and their vascular and lymphatic pedicles) are the lateralmost structures of the female reproductive tract. To the reconstructive pelvic surgeon they are the posterolateral neighbor of the ureter, the source of the ovarian vascular pedicle (IP ligament), and the territory of opportunistic salpingectomy, adnexal torsion and risk-reducing surgery. They generate three reconstructive problems: (1) iatrogenic ureteric injury in the ovarian fossa and IP ligament, (2) the pelvic-congestion and ovarian-vein thrombophlebitis presentations that a pelvic reconstructive specialist sees, and (3) the long-term pelvic-floor and urogenital consequences of surgical menopause when both adnexa are removed. This article covers surgical anatomy and perioperative issues, and compresses folliculogenesis, steroidogenesis, tubal cell biology and reproductive cycle detail to the depth the reconstructive urologist or urogynecologist needs.

See also [The Uterus](/docs/foundations/anatomy-physiology/genitalia/uterus) for the shared vascular and ligamentous pedicles; [The Ureters](/docs/foundations/anatomy-physiology/urinary-tract/ureter-anatomy-physiology) for ureter-in-the-ovarian-fossa anatomy; and [Pelvic Neuroanatomy](/docs/foundations/anatomy-physiology/pelvis-support/pelvic-neuroanatomy) for the superior hypogastric plexus through which adnexal autonomic fibers travel.

## The Ovary

### Location and surgical relations

The ovary lies in the ovarian fossa (Waldeyer's fossa) on the lateral pelvic sidewall, a shallow peritoneal recess. Standard anatomic descriptions bound it as follows:

| Boundary | Structure |
|---|---|
| **Superior** | **External iliac vessels** (the fossa lies in the angle between the external and internal iliac arteries) |
| **Anterior** | Obliterated umbilical artery and the broad ligament |
| **Posterior** | **Internal iliac artery and the ureter**. The ureter runs directly posterior to the ovary. |
| **Floor (lateral)** | Obturator vessels and nerve and the internal obturator muscle, covered by parietal peritoneum |

In adnexal surgery (oophorectomy, cystectomy, salpingo-oophorectomy, excision of endometrioma) the ureter is the closest significant structure to the ovary. Before transecting the infundibulopelvic (suspensory) ligament laterally or mobilizing a fixed mass, positively identify the ureter on the pelvic sidewall. Fixed endometriomas, ruptured dermoids, tubo-ovarian abscesses and radiation obliterate this plane, and distorted anatomy of this kind is the setting for many iatrogenic ureteric injuries in benign gynecologic surgery.

Typical dimensions are approximately 3–4 cm × 2–2.5 cm × 1–1.5 cm in reproductive-age women. The ovaries shrink to almond size after menopause.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[2]](#ref2)[[3]](#ref3)</sup>

### Ligamentous attachments

Three connective-tissue attachments anchor the ovary, each a vessel-carrying mesentery rather than a fibrous cord:<sup>[[4]](#ref4)[[5]](#ref5)[[6]](#ref6)</sup>

| Structure | Connects | Contents |
|---|---|---|
| **Infundibulopelvic (IP) / suspensory ligament of the ovary** | Ovary to pelvic sidewall | **Ovarian artery and vein, lymphatics, autonomic nerves**; the pedicle secured at oophorectomy |
| **Utero-ovarian (ovarian proper) ligament** | Ovary to uterine cornua | Utero-ovarian arterial anastomosis |
| **Mesovarium** | Ovary to posterior broad ligament | Hilar vessels, nerves, lymphatics |

The IP ligament is the operative pedicle for oophorectomy; the utero-ovarian ligament is the pedicle that remains attached to the uterus after salpingo-oophorectomy. When the ovary is preserved at hysterectomy, the utero-ovarian and IP ligament vessels remain the ovarian blood supply; when it is removed, both pedicles must be secured.

### Histology and tumor origin

The ovary contains three functional tissues, each the origin of a distinct tumor family, which is relevant when an adnexal mass is encountered intraoperatively:<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup>

| Tissue | Origin of |
|---|---|
| **Surface epithelium** (coelomic) | **Epithelial ovarian tumors** (the large majority), including high-grade serous tumors, many of which appear to originate from the fallopian-tube fimbria rather than the ovarian surface |
| **Sex cords / stroma** (granulosa, theca, fibroblasts) | **Sex-cord / stromal tumors**, often hormonally active (granulosa-cell, Sertoli–Leydig, fibroma / thecoma) |
| **Germ cells** (oocytes within follicles) | **Germ-cell tumors** — mature teratoma, dysgerminoma, yolk-sac, choriocarcinoma |

### Vascular supply and the right-vs-left asymmetry

- **Ovarian artery.** Arises directly from the aorta below the renal arteries, traverses the retroperitoneum in the IP ligament, and enters the ovary at the mesovarium.
- **Utero-ovarian arterial anastomosis** at the cornua: the uterine and ovarian arteries communicate. This anastomosis is the anatomic basis of uterine artery embolization failure in fibroids with dominant ovarian artery supply, and of persistent bleeding after unilateral ligation of either source.

Venous drainage has the same asymmetry seen with the testis:

- Right ovarian vein drains directly into the IVC
- Left ovarian vein drains into the left renal vein (at approximately 90°)

The left-sided drainage into the renal vein, with the same nutcracker geometry, explains two clinical patterns:

- **Pelvic congestion syndrome** and ovarian-vein reflux are more common on the left.
- **Postpartum ovarian-vein thrombophlebitis** is more common on the right, where the dextrorotated gravid uterus compresses the vein and the vein enters the IVC directly.

### Lymphatic drainage — paraaortic and pelvic pathways

Ovarian lymph commonly follows the IP ligament cephalad with the ovarian vessels to the paraaortic and paracaval nodes at the level of the renal vessels. Pelvic drainage also occurs.<sup>[[7]](#ref7)[[8]](#ref8)[[9]](#ref9)[[10]](#ref10)</sup>

- Right ovary drains to paracaval / interaortocaval nodes
- Left ovary drains to preaortic / para-aortic nodes

A secondary pathway drains to pelvic (external iliac, internal iliac) nodes. In a cadaveric dye-injection study of 20 ovaries, the pelvic territory was stained in 50% of cases and the lumbo-aortic territory in 65%.<sup>[[7]](#ref7)</sup> In a small isotopic lymphography series (8 premenopausal and 6 postmenopausal women), premenopausal ovaries drained to both lumbo-aortic and pelvic nodes, whereas after menopause lymphatic flow decreased and drainage was essentially aorto-lumbar.<sup>[[8]](#ref8)</sup>

Ovarian cancer can involve paraaortic and pelvic basins, but whether and how to sample nodes depends on stage, histology and the oncologic operation. In advanced disease after complete macroscopic resection with non-suspicious nodes on imaging and at surgery, systematic pelvic and paraaortic lymphadenectomy is not recommended (LION randomized trial, 2019; ESGO/ESMO/ESP 2025). Staging decisions in apparent early disease differ.<sup>[[26]](#ref26)[[27]](#ref27)</sup>

## The Fallopian Tube

### Segments and surgical landmarks

Four segments, each with distinct operative relevance:<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[16]](#ref16)[[17]](#ref17)</sup>

| Segment | Length | Key feature |
|---|---|---|
| **Infundibulum + fimbriae** | Distal trumpet with finger-like fimbriae; the ovarian fimbria touches the ovary | Open communication with the peritoneal cavity; probable origin of many high-grade serous carcinomas (via serous tubal intraepithelial carcinoma, STIC); target of risk-reducing salpingectomy |
| **Ampulla** | 5–8 cm; widest; thin-walled | **Site of fertilisation** and of most ectopic pregnancies |
| **Isthmus** | 2–3 cm; narrow, thick-walled | Site of tubal sterilisation (ligation, fulguration, clips / rings, Essure) |
| **Interstitial (intramural) portion** | Traverses the uterine wall at the cornua | Site of cornual/interstitial ectopic pregnancy, which carries a high risk of catastrophic rupture |

### Histology and vascular supply

Mucosa contains ciliated, secretory (peg), and intercalated cells on highly folded epithelium; muscularis has inner circular and outer longitudinal smooth muscle; serosa continuous with the mesosalpinx.<sup>[[15]](#ref15)[[16]](#ref16)</sup>

The tube is perfused from the ovarian and uterine arteries via anastomoses within the mesosalpinx; its supply is segmental rather than marginal, which is compatible with ovary-preserving hysterectomy and salpingectomy without ovarian devascularization.<sup>[[18]](#ref18)[[19]](#ref19)</sup>

### Cyclic function — at surgical depth

The tube picks up the oocyte at the fimbria via a combination of fimbrial motion and ciliary current, transports it to the ampulla for fertilization, nurtures the early embryo in a secretion-rich environment, and delivers the blastocyst to the uterus over 3–4 days. In mice lacking oviductal motile cilia, the infundibulum failed to pick up ovulated oocytes, whereas sperm still reached the ampulla and embryos still reached the uterus, with reduced efficiency; myosalpinx contractions contribute to egg transport along the oviduct.<sup>[[20]](#ref20)[[21]](#ref21)</sup> Disruption of any of these steps, such as scarring from PID (chlamydia / gonorrhea), endometriosis, or surgery, produces tubal-factor infertility.

## Adnexal Physiology — Kept to Operative Depth

### The hypothalamic-pituitary-ovarian axis

A brief summary sufficient for counseling:<sup>[[11]](#ref11)[[12]](#ref12)</sup>

- **GnRH** from the hypothalamus pulses LH/FSH from the anterior pituitary.
- FSH drives follicular recruitment and granulosa-cell proliferation; LH drives theca-cell androgen production and triggers ovulation.
- Ovarian steroids (estradiol, progesterone, inhibin) feed back on the hypothalamus and pituitary.
- A mid-cycle LH surge triggered by rising estradiol ovulates the dominant follicle; the collapsed follicle becomes the corpus luteum producing progesterone until luteolysis or pregnancy rescue by hCG.

### The two-cell, two-gonadotropin model of steroidogenesis

This underpins PCOS, adrenal-androgen differential diagnosis, and hormone-suppressive therapy:<sup>[[13]](#ref13)[[14]](#ref14)</sup>

- Theca cells (LH-responsive) produce androstenedione from cholesterol.
- Granulosa cells (FSH-responsive) express aromatase (CYP19) and convert theca-derived androstenedione into estradiol.
- Postovulation the corpus luteum produces progesterone under LH / hCG drive; progesterone withdrawal drives menstruation.

### Menopause and its consequences for the pelvic floor

Ovarian estrogen production falls sharply at menopause. The urogenital consequences make ovariectomy decisions relevant to the reconstructive surgeon:

- **Urogenital atrophy.** Thinned vaginal and urethral epithelium, reduced lubrication and submucosal vascular seal, raised vaginal pH.
- **Urinary symptoms** (including urgency and recurrent UTI) may coexist with genitourinary syndrome of menopause. Estrogen loss alone does not explain every stress or urgency incontinence symptom.
- **Native-tissue prolapse repair.** Vaginal estrogen may improve atrophy symptoms and tissue appearance. In IMPROVE (n = 199 randomized, conjugated estrogen cream), 12-month failure was 19% with estrogen and 9% with placebo (adjusted HR 1.97, 95% CI 0.92–4.22), so routine perioperative use did not improve surgical success.<sup>[[24]](#ref24)</sup> In EVA (n = 293, oestriol cream), anatomic outcomes and reintervention did not differ, but 92% vs 80% of women reported improvement on the PGI-I at 12 months (p = 0.02).<sup>[[25]](#ref25)</sup>
- **Surgical menopause** (bilateral oophorectomy in premenopausal women) produces the same spectrum abruptly and often more severely. The tradeoff against cancer prevention and ovarian-vein pathology is discussed explicitly.

## Clinical Correlations for the Reconstructive Surgeon

- **Ureteric injury during adnexectomy.** The ureter lies on the floor of the ovarian fossa, immediately posterior to the ovary, and passes medial to the IP ligament as the ligament crosses the pelvic brim. Identifying the ureter before IP-pedicle clamping is the main injury-prevention step, particularly in endometrioma excision, tubo-ovarian abscess drainage, and any reoperation.
- **Opportunistic salpingectomy.** The tubal origin of many high-grade serous carcinomas supports offering and discussing bilateral salpingectomy during benign hysterectomy or when permanent sterilization is desired; it reduces but does not eliminate ovarian-cancer risk. Technique: remove the tube to the uterotubal junction while preserving the ovarian blood supply when ovaries remain. It should not change the intended hysterectomy route solely to accomplish salpingectomy.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[22]](#ref22)[[23]](#ref23)</sup>
- **Risk-reducing salpingo-oophorectomy (RRSO).** The indication and timing require gene-, age- and patient-specific counseling for BRCA1/2, Lynch syndrome and other inherited risks. The operation removes both tubes and ovaries, with detailed fimbrial examination (SEE-FIM) when indicated. Discuss surgical-menopause and urogenital symptoms; vaginal estrogen can address appropriate atrophy symptoms, but should not be promised to improve later prolapse-repair durability.<sup>[[23]](#ref23)[[24]](#ref24)[[25]](#ref25)</sup>
- **Adnexal torsion.** The ovary and tube can twist on the IP-ligament / utero-ovarian ligament axis, causing venous engorgement and ischemia. Urgent evaluation is needed. In adolescents especially, detorsion and ovarian preservation are preferred regardless of a blue-black appearance, because Doppler flow and gross color do not reliably establish viability. Associated cyst management and oophoropexy are individualized.<sup>[[28]](#ref28)</sup>
- **Ectopic pregnancy.** About 95% implant in the fallopian tube, dominantly in the ampulla. Management is methotrexate (medical) for small, unruptured, stable ectopics and salpingostomy (linear incision, evacuation, tube preservation) or salpingectomy (complete tube removal) for surgical cases. Interstitial (cornual) ectopics require wedge resection or cornuostomy with attention to the uterine-cornual vascular pedicle.
- **Pelvic inflammatory disease (PID) and tubo-ovarian abscess (TOA).** Ascending infection (gonorrhea, chlamydia) leads to salpingitis and then TOA. Sequelae: tubal scarring, adhesions, tubal infertility, chronic pelvic pain, 6–10× risk of ectopic pregnancy. TOA is a reconstructive problem: drainage, antibiotics, and, when disease is extensive, adnexectomy in a potentially hostile pelvis with distorted ureteric anatomy.
- **Ovarian vein thrombophlebitis.** Postpartum septic thrombophlebitis is more common on the right (the dextrorotated gravid uterus compresses the right ovarian vein against the pelvic brim). It presents as persistent fever after negative infectious workup. CT shows a dilated thrombosed ovarian vein that may extend to the IVC or renal vein. Management is anticoagulation and broad-spectrum antibiotics.
- **Pelvic congestion syndrome.** Chronic pelvic pain worsened by standing, improved by recumbency; associated with ovarian-vein varicosities (more often left than right) and sometimes with nutcracker-syndrome left-renal-vein compression. Treatment is ovarian or internal iliac vein embolization or surgical ligation, with the reconstructive surgeon increasingly involved as part of an interdisciplinary team.
- **Ovarian remnant syndrome.** Recurrent cyclic pelvic pain and a palpable or imaging mass after prior oophorectomy, from residual ovarian tissue. Common after difficult oophorectomy through dense endometriotic or TOA-related adhesions. Reoperation in the lateral pelvis carries substantial ureteric-injury risk, and preoperative imaging and ureteric stents are commonly used.
- **Ovarian cancer operations.** Staging and cytoreduction are stage-, histology- and patient-specific gynecologic-oncology decisions; possible components include hysterectomy, bilateral salpingo-oophorectomy, omentectomy, washings/biopsies and selected node assessment. A blanket pelvic/paraaortic lymphadenectomy rule does not apply to completely resected advanced disease with clinically non-suspicious nodes.<sup>[[26]](#ref26)[[27]](#ref27)</sup> Urologic involvement can include ureterolysis or urinary-tract reconstruction in extensive cases.
- **Incidental adnexal mass at pelvic reconstructive surgery.** Discovered adnexal masses during prolapse or fistula surgery should be evaluated preoperatively with TV-US and CA-125; if malignancy cannot be excluded, refer to gynecologic oncology before proceeding with the reconstructive step.
- **Sterilization options and reversal.** Tubal ligation (Pomeroy, Parkland), fulguration, and mechanical (Filshie clip, Hulka clip, Falope ring) remain in use; Essure hysteroscopic devices have been withdrawn, and their removal (salpingectomy with cornual resection) is a common referral. Reversal depends on residual tube length, prior method, and female and male fertility factors.
- **Ectopic pregnancy after tubal ligation.** In the prospective U.S. Collaborative Review of Sterilization (10,685 women), the 10-year cumulative probability of ectopic pregnancy was 7.3 per 1000 procedures. It varied with method and age (31.9 per 1000 after bipolar coagulation before age 30 years vs 1.2 per 1000 after postpartum partial salpingectomy) and did not fall after the third year, so it belongs in the informed-consent discussion.<sup>[[29]](#ref29)</sup>
- **Müllerian anomalies of the tube and ovary.** Tubal agenesis, absence of a fimbriated end, accessory tubes or ostia, and supernumerary / ectopic ovaries are uncommon but can be encountered at reconstructive surgery. Associated genitourinary anomalies (renal agenesis, ureteral duplication) are found in about 30% and warrant upper-tract imaging.
- **Oophoropexy for pediatric and adolescent patients undergoing pelvic radiation.** The reconstructive team is occasionally asked to reposition the ovaries out of a planned radiation field to preserve endocrine function. The repositioning affects subsequent surgical access and fertility planning.
