# The Testicles & Scrotum

Each **testis** is an endocrine (testosterone) and exocrine (sperm) organ suspended in the scrotum by the spermatic cord and enveloped in a concentric sleeve of fascia derived from the abdominal wall.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[13]](#ref13)</sup> The operative orientation is layers, blood supply and lymphatic drainage: the tunica-vaginalis plane matters in hydrocele and hernia repair, collateral arterial supply and pampiniform venous anatomy matter in varicocelectomy and orchiopexy, retroperitoneal versus scrotal lymphatic drainage matters in tumor management, and the dartos–Scarpa–Colles' continuum helps explain Fournier's spread.

## Testis — Gross and Surgical Anatomy

The adult testis is an ovoid organ of approximately 4–5 cm and 15–25 mL, suspended in the scrotum with its long axis vertical and slightly tilted forward. It is surrounded by a tough fibrous **tunica albuginea**, which sends **septula** inward to divide the parenchyma into approximately 250–300 lobules, each housing 1–4 seminiferous tubules.<sup>[[2]](#ref2)</sup> The tubules converge posteriorly at the mediastinum testis (a thickening of the tunica) to form the **rete testis**, which drains through approximately 10–20 **efferent ductules** into the caput epididymis.

### Tunical layers — the operative plane for hydrocele, torsion, and trauma

Surrounding the testis, from deep to superficial:

| Layer | Origin | Operative relevance |
|---|---|---|
| **Tunica albuginea** | Dense fibrous capsule of the testis itself | The plane of **testis-sparing surgery**, biopsy, and **micro-TESE** |
| **Tunica vaginalis — visceral layer** | Lines the tunica albuginea | The tunica-vaginalis space is where **hydroceles** accumulate |
| **Tunica vaginalis — parietal layer** | Lines the inside of the scrotal wall | The closure / resection plane in hydrocele repair (Jaboulay, Lord, or excisional) |
| **Internal spermatic fascia** | Transversalis fascia | Spermatic-cord investment |
| **Cremasteric fascia + cremaster muscle** | Internal oblique | **Cremasteric reflex**; incised during orchiopexy |
| **External spermatic fascia** | External oblique aponeurosis | Superficial cord investment |
| **Dartos fascia** | Scarpa's → Colles' fascia continuum | Superficial hematoma / urinoma / Fournier plane; midline septum of the scrotum |
| **Scrotal skin** | Thin, rugated, hair-bearing | Sweat glands and hair follicles; site of epidermal inclusion cysts, hidradenitis suppurativa<sup>[[16]](#ref16)</sup> |

### Spermatic cord contents

Within the cord, from anterior to posterior:

- **Testicular artery** (from aorta)
- **Cremasteric artery** (from inferior epigastric)
- **Deferential artery** (from inferior vesical)
- **Pampiniform venous plexus.** The three arteries are bracketed by the venous plexus, a geometry that matters for microsurgical varicocelectomy.
- **Lymphatics** to para-aortic nodes
- **Vas deferens.** Posteromedial and palpably firm; the landmark when the surgeon "loses" the cord.
- **Genital branch of the genitofemoral nerve** (motor to cremaster; sensory to anterior scrotum)
- **Ilioinguinal nerve** (sensory only; runs *on* the cord, not inside the internal spermatic fascia)
- **Sympathetic and parasympathetic autonomic fibers**

## Vascular Supply and Drainage

### Arterial supply — threefold and redundant

Three paired arteries perfuse the testis and epididymis, with extensive anastomoses along the cord and at the tunica:<sup>[[18]](#ref18)</sup>

| Artery | Origin | Territory |
|---|---|---|
| **Testicular artery** | Abdominal aorta (below the renal arteries) | Dominant supply; enters the testis at the mediastinum; bifurcates into polar branches |
| **Cremasteric artery** | Inferior epigastric (external iliac) | Cord, cremaster, and lower-pole anastomosis |
| **Deferential artery** | Inferior vesical (internal iliac) | Vas deferens and epididymis; anastomoses at the mediastinum |

The cremasteric and deferential supplies are the basis for testicular salvage after testicular-artery injury. The testis can survive ligation of the main testicular artery at the internal ring if the collaterals are intact (a principle exploited in the *Fowler–Stephens orchiopexy* for high intra-abdominal undescended testes).

### Venous drainage — the varicocele anatomy

Veins emerge from the testis at the mediastinum as the **pampiniform plexus** and ascend through variable venous channels that usually coalesce into a dominant testicular (gonadal) vein:<sup>[[19]](#ref19)[[31]](#ref31)</sup>

- **Left testicular vein.** Usually drains into the left renal vein.
- **Right testicular vein.** Usually drains into the inferior vena cava.
- Venous valves, competence, duplications and collateral channels vary. One cadaveric series found absent valves in about one-third of left veins at pelvic or lumbar levels, not all veins; an earlier dissection series described occasional abdominal cross-communication. Neither supports an invariant valveless, side-isolated system.<sup>[[19]](#ref19)[[31]](#ref31)</sup>

Three consequences follow:

1. **Left-side predominance of varicoceles** reflects the longer venous course and left-renal-vein drainage; renal-vein compression is a possible contributor, not a universal mechanism.
2. **Isolated clinical right varicocele** warrants further assessment for abdominal, retroperitoneal or congenital causes; imaging is selected to the clinical setting and is not inferred from side alone.<sup>[[27]](#ref27)</sup>
3. **Pampiniform countercurrent heat exchange** helps keep the testis cooler than core temperature. Impaired thermoregulation is one proposed contributor to varicocele-associated infertility, not a complete explanation.<sup>[[27]](#ref27)</sup>

### Lymphatic drainage — why orchiectomy is inguinal, not scrotal

Testicular and intratunical lymph drains with the cord upward to the para-aortic and interaortocaval nodes at the L1–L3 level:<sup>[[20]](#ref20)[[21]](#ref21)[[22]](#ref22)</sup>

- **Right testis.** Drains primarily to the interaortocaval and precaval nodes.
- **Left testis.** Drains primarily to the left para-aortic and preaortic nodes.
- **Scrotal skin.** Drains to the superficial inguinal nodes, a completely separate lymphatic territory.

**Inguinal, not scrotal, orchiectomy for suspected testicular cancer.** Radical inguinal orchiectomy with high cord control is standard; a scrotal approach should be avoided because it is associated with higher local recurrence. A prior scrotal violation needs individualized specialist review; it does not automatically change TNM stage or require inguinal radiotherapy or hemiscrotectomy.<sup>[[26]](#ref26)</sup>

### Innervation

- **Autonomic.** Testicular nerves travel with the testicular artery from the T10–T11 segments via the celiac and intermesenteric plexuses; sympathetic dominance.
- **Referred pain** from testicular pathology is classically to the flank or ipsilateral lower abdomen (T10–T11 dermatome), paralleling ureteric pain.
- **Scrotal sensation.** The anterior hemiscrotum is supplied by the genital branch of the genitofemoral nerve (L1–L2) and the ilioinguinal nerve (L1); the posterior hemiscrotum by the posterior scrotal nerves from the pudendal (S2–S4). The interscrotal septum is densely innervated, and each hemiscrotum is largely innervated separately, which is the anatomic basis for unilateral scrotal blocks (the septal innervation data come from fetal specimens).<sup>[[23]](#ref23)[[24]](#ref24)</sup>
- **Post-vasectomy pain and chronic orchialgia** are thought to involve ilioinguinal, genitofemoral, and spermatic-cord autonomic fibers. Microsurgical spermatic-cord denervation selectively transects these while preserving vas, testicular artery, and lymphatics.

## Testicular Physiology — Operative Depth Only

### Endocrine function

Leydig cells in the interstitium produce approximately 3–10 mg of testosterone daily under LH drive, with morning peaks in circulating concentration.<sup>[[4]](#ref4)[[5]](#ref5)</sup> Intratesticular testosterone is 50–100× circulating levels, a detail that matters because:

- Exogenous testosterone suppresses LH and FSH and collapses intratesticular testosterone. This is the mechanism by which TRT produces oligospermia or azoospermia, and the reason fertility counseling must precede its initiation.
- Recovery of spermatogenesis after stopping exogenous testosterone varies and should not be promised within 3–6 months. Men seeking fertility need individualized evaluation; gonadotropin therapy has a role in selected secondary hypogonadism, while SERMs are off-label with limited evidence in that setting.<sup>[[28]](#ref28)</sup>

### Spermatogenesis — counseling numbers

Spermatogenesis takes approximately 74 days from spermatogonial stem cell to mature spermatozoon in the tubular lumen, with an additional 10–14 days of epididymal transit and maturation.<sup>[[6]](#ref6)[[7]](#ref7)</sup> Daily sperm production is approximately 45 million per testis, or about 1,000 sperm per second in total.<sup>[[6]](#ref6)</sup> Clinical translations:

- **Post-vasectomy semen analysis** is needed to confirm clearance; a first test is commonly scheduled at 8–16 weeks. Ejaculation count alone is unreliable, and alternative contraception is required until the result meets the treating service's clearance criteria.<sup>[[30]](#ref30)</sup>
- **Gonadotoxic exposures** (chemotherapy, radiation) damage the stem / differentiating pool; recovery timelines parallel the 74-day cycle plus stem-cell regeneration.
- **Fertility preservation** should be offered before potentially gonadotoxic therapy when feasible and desired; it should be coordinated without delaying urgent cancer care.<sup>[[26]](#ref26)</sup>

### Blood–testis barrier

Sertoli–Sertoli tight junctions create a blood–testis barrier (BTB) separating basal from adluminal germ-cell compartments. Antisperm antibodies may follow reproductive-tract injury or obstruction, but their presence alone does not establish infertility or predict an individual vasovasostomy outcome.<sup>[[3]](#ref3)</sup>

### Thermoregulation

Normal testicular temperature is approximately 33°C, 2–4°C below core temperature. Four mechanisms cooperate:<sup>[[12]](#ref12)[[14]](#ref14)[[15]](#ref15)[[17]](#ref17)</sup>

- **Pampiniform plexus.** Countercurrent heat exchange (the main mechanism).
- **Cremaster muscle.** Retracts the testis toward the body in cold.
- **Dartos muscle.** Wrinkles and shortens the scrotal skin in cold; thins and elongates in heat.
- **Scrotal sweating.** Thin skin with dense sweat glands dissipates heat.

Elevated scrotal temperature (varicocele, cryptorchidism, chronic heat exposure, sedentary position) is associated with impaired spermatogenesis. Germ-cell apoptosis and BTB disruption have been described as mechanisms, and this is the pathophysiologic rationale for varicocelectomy in infertility.

## The Epididymis and Vas Deferens

The epididymis runs along the posterolateral surface of the testis and has three operatively useful regions:<sup>[[8]](#ref8)[[9]](#ref9)</sup>

| Segment | Function | Clinical corollary |
|---|---|---|
| **Caput (head)** | Receives sperm from efferent ductules; initial maturation | Proximal epididymal segment considered during assessment of obstruction and selected sperm-retrieval procedures |
| **Corpus (body)** | Primary maturation zone; sperm acquire progressive motility and fertilizing capacity | Site of post-infectious (chlamydia, gonorrhea, tuberculosis) stricture |
| **Cauda (tail)** | Sperm storage in quiescent state | Source of stored ejaculated sperm. After vasectomy, residual sperm in the vas distal to the occlusion persist for weeks |

The epididymis terminates as the vas deferens, which ascends through the cord, enters the internal ring, and runs retroperitoneally to the bladder base, joining the seminal-vesicle duct to form the ejaculatory duct (see [The Prostate & Seminal Vesicles](/docs/foundations/anatomy-physiology/genitalia/prostate-seminal-vesicle)).

Epididymal sperm maturation is androgen-dependent and is influenced by segment-specific secretions and **epididymosomes** (epithelial extracellular vesicles delivering cargo to spermatozoa).<sup>[[10]](#ref10)[[11]](#ref11)</sup> Retrieval site and technique depend on the cause of azoospermia and assisted-reproduction plan; duration of obstruction alone does not impose a fixed testicular-versus-epididymal hierarchy.<sup>[[27]](#ref27)</sup>

## The Scrotum

### Layers (superficial → deep) — the abdominal-wall homologues

| Scrotal layer | Abdominal-wall homologue | Operative note |
|---|---|---|
| **Skin** | Skin | Thin, no subcutaneous fat, rugated under dartos tone |
| **Dartos fascia + dartos muscle** | Scarpa's fascia / Colles' fascia | Continuous with the perineum and anterior abdominal wall; the fascial route of Fournier's gangrene spread |
| **External spermatic fascia** | External oblique aponeurosis | |
| **Cremasteric fascia + muscle** | Internal oblique | Cremasteric reflex (L1–L2 genitofemoral) |
| **Internal spermatic fascia** | Transversalis fascia | Deepest investment of the cord |
| **Tunica vaginalis (parietal)** | Parietal peritoneum (processus vaginalis) | Site of hydrocele; communicating hydrocele = patent processus vaginalis |

### Arterial supply

- **Anterior hemiscrotum.** Branches of the external pudendal arteries (from the femoral).
- **Posterior hemiscrotum.** Branches of the internal pudendal arteries (from the internal iliac).

This split supports scrotal flap design for reconstruction and explains the robustness of scrotal viability even with extensive debridement.

### Dartos-fascia continuity and Fournier's gangrene

Dartos fascia is continuous superiorly with Scarpa's fascia of the anterior abdominal wall and posteriorly with Colles' (perineal superficial) fascia. Fournier's gangrene can spread along this plane and beyond the initially visible skin changes; the testis is often spared because of its separate arterial supply, but viability of all involved tissues must be assessed during urgent debridement. Viable testes and cord are preserved whenever possible.

## Clinical Correlations for the Reconstructive Urologist

- **Testicular torsion.** A surgical emergency: urgent exploration is required, ideally within 4–6 hours, but later presentation is not a reason to withhold assessment. Salvage is not guaranteed even after prompt treatment, and an apparently viable testis can later atrophy. Manual detorsion may be attempted while awaiting surgery but does not replace exploration; contralateral fixation is commonly performed at that operation, with perinatal cases assessed separately.<sup>[[25]](#ref25)</sup>
- **Cryptorchidism and orchiopexy.** Undescended testes carry increased fertility and tumor risks; timely specialist management is recommended. A low-lying palpable testis may be approached inguinally or scrotally; a high intra-abdominal testis may require a staged procedure, including Fowler–Stephens or another technique selected for the anatomy. Collateral flow is the rationale for Fowler–Stephens, not a guarantee of survival.<sup>[[29]](#ref29)</sup>
- **Hydrocele and hernia.** Communicating hydrocele and indirect inguinal hernia reflect a patent processus vaginalis. Non-communicating adult hydrocele is managed by Jaboulay (eversion), Lord (plication), or excisional technique, addressing the tunica-vaginalis parietal layer.
- **Varicocele.** Dilation of the pampiniform plexus, usually left-sided. Isolated clinical right-sided or sudden-onset findings merit assessment for secondary causes, with imaging individualized to age and examination. Indications for repair depend on fertility context, symptoms and testicular development. Microsurgical inguinal or subinguinal repair is an established option with generally lower recurrence and complication rates than non-microsurgical approaches, but the evidence does not make one route obligatory for every patient.<sup>[[27]](#ref27)</sup>
- **Testicular germ-cell tumor.** Most postpubertal germ-cell tumors arise through a GCNIS-associated pathway, but not all testicular tumors do. Radical inguinal orchiectomy with high cord control is standard for suspected cancer; avoid a scrotal approach because of local-recurrence risk. Para-aortic and interaortocaval drainage informs retroperitoneal staging and selected RPLND templates; nerve-sparing aims to preserve ejaculatory function where oncologically feasible.<sup>[[26]](#ref26)</sup>
- **Fournier's gangrene.** Necrotizing fasciitis of the dartos / Scarpa's / Colles' plane. Emergent wide debridement with urinary and fecal diversion as needed; testes, cord, and glans penis usually preserved. Reconstruction later with split-thickness grafts, fasciocutaneous flaps, or muscle flaps (gracilis) depending on defect.
- **Sperm retrieval.** MESA and PESA are used for obstructive azoospermia (proximal ducts dilated with mature sperm), TESE for non-obstructive azoospermia (focal spermatogenesis), and micro-TESE (operating microscope to identify dilated opaque seminiferous tubules containing active spermatogenesis) is widely preferred for non-obstructive azoospermia.
- **Vasectomy and reversal.** Vas occlusion techniques vary; a post-vasectomy semen analysis, not ejaculation count, establishes clearance before contraception is stopped.<sup>[[30]](#ref30)</sup> Vasovasostomy or vasoepididymostomy is selected from operative findings and fertility goals. Longer obstructive interval can increase the chance that epididymal reconstruction is needed, but fixed patency promises and automatic procedure selection are not justified here.
- **Testicular prosthesis.** Offered at the time of orchiectomy or later; silicone implant in the scrotal compartment. Counsel about size options, palpation, and the risk of infection or extrusion.
- **Scrotal reconstruction.** After Fournier's, penile cancer en bloc, or burn or avulsion. Options progress from primary closure or split-thickness graft to local fasciocutaneous flaps to musculocutaneous flaps (gracilis, medial thigh). The testes can be temporized in medial-thigh pouches if the scrotum is not immediately reconstructable.
- **Chronic orchialgia.** Medical management first (NSAIDs, neuropathic agents, pelvic-floor PT). Diagnostic spermatic-cord block is the gateway to microsurgical spermatic-cord denervation, which ligates cord autonomic fibers while preserving vas, testicular and deferential arteries, and lymphatics.
- **Referred pain, the crossovers.** Testicular pathology refers to the flank via T10–T11; ureteric pathology refers to the scrotum and groin via the same dermatomes. A patient with acute flank and testicular pain needs both ureter and testis evaluated.
