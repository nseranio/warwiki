# The Cervix

The cervix is the inferior fibromuscular cylinder of the uterus that projects into the vaginal vault and contains the endocervical canal linking the uterine cavity to the vagina. For the reconstructive pelvic surgeon it matters in four ways: (1) it is part of **Level-I apical support** through the cardinal and uterosacral complexes; (2) it is an **anatomic neighbor of the ureter** at the uterine-artery crossing, a recognized site of operative ureteric injury; (3) autonomic nerves in the paracervical tissues matter during radical hysterectomy; and (4) cervical disease and its treatment may leave fistula, stenosis or bladder dysfunction. This article focuses on surgical anatomy, structural support and selected reconstructive consequences. Screening and cancer-treatment decisions require their own current guidance.

See also [The Vagina](/docs/foundations/anatomy-physiology/genitalia/vaginal-anatomy) for the surrounding fornices; [The Ureters](/docs/foundations/anatomy-physiology/urinary-tract/ureter-anatomy-physiology) for the "water under the bridge" geometry; and [Pelvic Neuroanatomy](/docs/foundations/anatomy-physiology/pelvis-support/pelvic-neuroanatomy) for the pelvic plexus.

## Gross Anatomy

The cervix is a ~3–4 cm cylinder that divides anatomically into two portions:<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[2]](#ref2)</sup>

- **Supravaginal portion** — above the vaginal attachment, surrounded by the cardinal-ligament complex and paracervical tissues.
- **Vaginal portion (portio vaginalis)** — protrudes into the vaginal vault, visible at speculum examination; demarcated from the vault by the anterior, posterior, and lateral **fornices**.

The canal extends from the **internal os** (communication with the uterine cavity) to the **external os** (communication with the vaginal vault). Normal external os is slit-like in parous women and round in nulliparas — a classically-examined morphology at pessary fitting and at colposcopy.

### Three mucosal regions

| Region | Epithelium | Surface landmarks |
|---|---|---|
| **Ectocervix (portio)** | Non-keratinized stratified squamous — continuous with vaginal epithelium | Smooth, pink at speculum |
| **Endocervix** | Simple columnar mucus-secreting; surface thrown into **plicae palmatae** that increase surface area | Red, granular at speculum; lined by glands |
| **Transformation zone (TZ)** | Zone of ongoing **squamous metaplasia** between the original and current squamocolumnar junctions (SCJ) | A major site of HPV-associated cervical precancer and cancer; visibility varies by age and individual anatomy<sup>[[3]](#ref3)[[4]](#ref4)[[5]](#ref5)</sup> |

The TZ commonly becomes more visible on the ectocervix around puberty and may recede into the canal after menopause. **Postmenopause alone does not make endocervical curettage a universal screening step.** Cervical screening follows current age/risk-based guidance; at **colposcopy after an abnormal result**, the ASCCP recommends endocervical sampling when the squamocolumnar junction is not fully visible and for several higher-risk findings, and prefers it after age 40, subject to stated exceptions. It is **unacceptable in pregnancy**.<sup>[[21]](#ref21)</sup>

## Structural Supports — The Apex of Pelvic-Floor Reconstruction

The cervix is the anchoring point of **Level-I pelvic support** in DeLancey's model. Two ligamentous / connective-tissue complexes suspend it:<sup>[[11]](#ref11)</sup>

| Complex | Origin | Insertion | Contents |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Cardinal ligament (Mackenrodt)** | Pelvic sidewall at the obturator internus fascia | Lateral cervix and upper vagina | **Uterine artery and vein, ureter** (at its midpoint), pelvic plexus autonomic fibers, lymphatics |
| **Uterosacral ligament (USL)** | Presacral fascia at the level of S2–S4 | Posterior cervix and upper vagina | Sympathetic fibers from the pelvic plexus; lymphatic channels |

Both are more accurately described as **condensations of endopelvic fascia and smooth muscle** than as true ligaments. Clinically:

- **Apical prolapse (uterine / vault)** represents failure of the cardinal-uterosacral (CUL) complex.
- **Sacrocolpopexy, USLS, Manchester (Fothergill), and vaginal hysterectomy with McCall culdoplasty** all aim to re-establish or reinforce this complex.
- **Cervical elongation** (disproportionate overgrowth of the cervix without true descent) is a masquerader of apical prolapse — recognition is important because treatment (cervical amputation ± reinforcement, or the Manchester procedure) differs from simple apical suspension.

## Anatomical Relations — Why the Ureter Lives Under the Uterine Artery

| Aspect | Relation | Operative note |
|---|---|---|
| **Anterior** | Bladder base and trigone, separated by the **vesicocervical/vesicouterine space** | The plane of anterior dissection in hysterectomy, cervical cancer surgery, and vesicovaginal fistula repair |
| **Posterior** | Rectum and the **rectouterine pouch (Douglas')** | Approached via the posterior colpotomy; enterocele sits in this peritoneal pocket |
| **Lateral** | Broad ligament, cardinal ligament, uterine vessels, and **ureter** near the lateral cervix (distance varies) | Site of potential iatrogenic ureteric injury |

**Water under the bridge — the ureteric injury pattern.** The **uterine artery crosses anterior (over) the ureter** near the cervix, but the often-quoted ~1.5–2-cm distance varies with anatomy and pathology. This is a recognized danger zone in hysterectomy and deep paracervical dissection. Identify the ureter and adapt the dissection to the actual anatomy; visualization is an important safeguard, not a guarantee against every mechanism of injury.

## Vascular Supply

Arterial supply is overwhelmingly from the **uterine artery** (a branch of the internal iliac, anterior division), which gives off a **cervicovaginal branch** at the level of the isthmus.<sup>[[7]](#ref7)[[8]](#ref8)[[9]](#ref9)</sup> Additional contributions come from the **vaginal artery** and, in limited fashion, the middle rectal artery.

The cervical stroma contains a distinctive **high-density mucosal capillary plexus** in the endocervical lamina propria, arranged in near-parallel bundles with few interconnections — an architecture suited to the vascular remodeling of pregnancy.<sup>[[7]](#ref7)</sup> In the non-pregnant cervix this vascularity is the reason even superficial biopsies and LEEP excisions bleed.

Venous drainage parallels the arteries to the uterine and vaginal plexuses and thence the internal iliac vein.

Lymphatic drainage of the cervix runs to **parametrial, obturator, internal iliac, external iliac, and common iliac nodes**, with presacral drainage from the posterior lip — the anatomic basis of the **parametrial and pelvic lymphadenectomy template in radical hysterectomy (Wertheim / Okabayashi / Querleu-Morrow classifications)**.

## Innervation — Nerve-Sparing and Cervical Cancer Surgery

The cervix is richly innervated — in fact more densely than the uterine corpus and fundus, with the highest neurotransmitter density in the reproductive tract.<sup>[[10]](#ref10)[[11]](#ref11)[[12]](#ref12)</sup> This is the anatomic substrate of **nerve-sparing radical hysterectomy**.

| Source | Pathway | Function |
|---|---|---|
| **Parasympathetic** | S2–S4 pelvic nerve → inferior hypogastric plexus → cardinal ligament (lateral and inferior) | Smooth muscle tone and vasodilation; afferent pain fibers |
| **Sympathetic** | T10–L2 hypogastric nerve → inferior hypogastric plexus → **uterosacral ligament (posterior)** | Contraction, vasoconstriction; afferent pain fibers |

The **inferior hypogastric plexus and its branches to the bladder, rectum and vagina** are at risk during paracervical resection. In the 2017 Querleu–Morrow classification, **type C1 is nerve-preserving**: the hypogastric nerve, inferior hypogastric plexus and bladder branches are identified and preserved while uterine branches are divided. **Type C2 does not preserve the autonomic nerves.** These are distinct surgical extents, not interchangeable names for a single ventral-versus-dorsal cut. Functional and oncologic outcomes depend on tumor selection and operative execution; anatomy alone does not establish equivalent cancer control.<sup>[[10]](#ref10)[[11]](#ref11)[[22]](#ref22)</sup>

## Cervical Mucus — Compressed to What's Clinically Useful

Endocervical columnar cells produce a **hydrated mucin gel** that changes cyclically under estrogen and progesterone control:<sup>[[13]](#ref13)[[14]](#ref14)[[15]](#ref15)[[16]](#ref16)</sup>

- **Periovulatory (estrogen-dominant)** — thin, copious, low-viscosity, high-spinnbarkeit mucus with a "ferning" pattern when dried — permissive to sperm transit and partially protective to vaginal flora.
- **Luteal and pregnant (progesterone-dominant)** — thick, scant, highly viscous, sperm-impermeable mucus that forms the **cervical mucus plug** of pregnancy — a mechanical and antimicrobial barrier to ascending infection.
- **Postmenopausal (hypoestrogenic)** — atrophic, thin, reduced mucus, higher vaginal pH (as with vaginal atrophy; see [The Vagina](/docs/foundations/anatomy-physiology/genitalia/vaginal-anatomy)).

The clinical translations worth knowing at the reconstructive level are: **vaginal estrogen may be considered for genitourinary symptoms/signs of menopause** in a patient with prolapse, but improved surgical repair or pessary retention is not established by that recommendation; **cervical mucus penetrability testing** (historical post-coital test) has limited modern clinical utility; and **cervical ectopy (visible columnar epithelium on the ectocervix)** is commonly physiological in younger women, oral-contraceptive users and pregnancy.<sup>[[26]](#ref26)[[27]](#ref27)</sup>

## Cervical Remodeling in Pregnancy (in broad strokes)

The non-pregnant cervix is a stiff collagen-dominant organ; pregnancy transforms it into a compliant, extensible one through four sequential processes:<sup>[[6]](#ref6)[[17]](#ref17)[[18]](#ref18)[[19]](#ref19)[[20]](#ref20)</sup>

1. **Softening** — slow decrease in tensile strength from early pregnancy, with increasing tissue hydration and hydrophilic glycosaminoglycans.
2. **Ripening** — **increased compliance/distensibility** and reduced tissue stiffness and tensile strength, with greater hydration and collagen-matrix remodeling.<sup>[[23]](#ref23)</sup>
3. **Dilation** — active labor opens the cervix under uterine contraction.
4. **Postpartum repair** — rapid return toward baseline structure.

**Cervical insufficiency** describes a clinical pattern of painless cervical dilation and pregnancy loss or very early birth in the absence of labor; it should not be diagnosed simply as early entry into a normal remodeling phase. Cerclage is considered for selected patients according to obstetric history, examination and current guidance, not for every prior LEEP/cone or cervical trauma. The obstetric pathway is outside this anatomy page's scope.

## Clinical Correlations for the Reconstructive Surgeon

- **Hysterectomy and ureteric injury.** The ureter passes close to the cervix and beneath the uterine artery. Identify and protect it during dissection in this region, especially when anatomy is distorted; recognition of its course does not eliminate injury risk. Cystoscopy can help evaluate suspected urinary-tract injury, but its use and interpretation depend on the operation and clinical circumstances.<sup>[[25]](#ref25)</sup>
- **Nerve-sparing radical hysterectomy.** Querleu–Morrow **type C1**, not C2, aims to preserve the hypogastric nerve, inferior hypogastric plexus and bladder branches. The extent of parametrial resection and cancer-treatment context determine whether nerve preservation is appropriate; do not infer a fixed functional benefit or oncologic equivalence from the classification alone.<sup>[[22]](#ref22)</sup>
- **Apical prolapse and the cervix.** Uterine prolapse is failure of the CUL complex. Options: **vaginal hysterectomy** with apical suspension (USL suspension or McCall culdoplasty), **sacrospinous hysteropexy**, **sacrohysteropexy** (mesh), and — when uterine preservation with cervical elongation is desired — the **Manchester (Fothergill) procedure** (cervical amputation + cardinal-ligament plication + anterior colporrhaphy).
- **Cervical elongation.** Disproportionate cervical overgrowth mimics apical prolapse; at examination the uterine body may remain within the pelvis despite a cervix below the hymen. Treatment is **trachelectomy or Manchester** rather than apical suspension alone.
- **Conization and LEEP.** Outpatient excisional procedures for CIN 2/3. Clinically relevant to obstetric outcomes (excision depth is associated with preterm delivery risk) and to the later reconstructive surgeon (cervical stenosis can cause hematometra or dysmenorrhea; cervical shortening affects fertility-preserving cerclage planning).<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup>
- **Trachelectomy.** Fertility-sparing cervical surgery may include conization, simple or radical trachelectomy after specialist staging and multidisciplinary selection. The **ESGO/ESHRE/ESGE fertility-sparing guideline** distinguishes favorable tumors around **≤2 cm** from selected **2–4-cm** cases and requires assessment of histology, nodal status, margins and internal-os involvement; a stage label alone does not determine whether radical trachelectomy is appropriate. Bladder, ureter and pelvic-plexus anatomy still matter.<sup>[[24]](#ref24)</sup>
- **Supracervical (subtotal) hysterectomy and the retained cervix.** Leaves the cervical stump in situ; the patient must continue cervical cancer screening. Complications include persistent cyclical bleeding, stump infection, and, rarely, stump carcinoma — each of which can present to a reconstructive surgeon years later.
- **Sequelae of pelvic radiation.** Radiation can cause **vesicovaginal or rectovaginal fistula, cystitis, vaginal stenosis, ureteric stricture and bladder dysfunction**. Repair planning depends on tissue quality, fistula location, prior treatment and patient goals. **Well-vascularized tissue interposition is often considered for radiated or complex fistulas**, but flap selection and repair feasibility are case-specific; neither universal flap use nor inevitable failure of every local repair follows from anatomy alone.<sup>[[25]](#ref25)</sup>
- **Vesicocervical space and vesicovaginal fistula.** A post-hysterectomy VVF may involve the vaginal cuff and adjacent bladder dissection plane. Repair may use transvaginal or abdominal access, layered closure and, for selected complex or radiated defects, vascularized tissue interposition; no one plane, flap or approach is required for every fistula.<sup>[[25]](#ref25)</sup> See [The Vagina](/docs/foundations/anatomy-physiology/genitalia/vaginal-anatomy).
- **Cervical cancer and the reconstructive burden.** Prior radical surgery or chemoradiation can complicate fistula repair, urinary diversion, vaginal reconstruction and bladder-function care. Evaluation is tailored to the presenting problem and may include direct examination, cystoscopy and upper-tract imaging when anatomy or ureteric involvement is uncertain.<sup>[[25]](#ref25)</sup>
- **Cerclage.** McDonald (transvaginal, easy), Shirodkar (transvaginal, subcutaneous submucosal), transabdominal (laparoscopic / open, for failed vaginal cerclage or after trachelectomy). Primarily the obstetrician's territory; relevant to the reconstructive surgeon when prior bladder / urethral surgery complicates access.
- **Postmenopausal tissue and pessary care.** Vaginal estrogen may be considered when prolapse coexists with genitourinary symptoms/signs of menopause; it is **not established as a way to improve pessary retention**. In a 2025 placebo-controlled trial among women already fitted with ring pessaries, 12-month continuation **with patient-reported improvement** was 181/208 (87.0%) with estrogen versus 176/203 (86.7%) with placebo (risk difference 0.3%, 95% CI −6.2 to 6.9). This composite does not mean estrogen has no possible effect on local vaginal symptoms.<sup>[[26]](#ref26)[[27]](#ref27)</sup>
