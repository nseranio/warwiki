# The Cervix

The cervix is the fibromuscular lower portion of the uterus that connects the uterine cavity to the vagina. It lies posterior to the bladder and anterior to the rectum; its lateral relationships include the uterine vessels, supporting connective tissue, and ureter.[[1]](#ref1)[[2]](#ref2)

This page is an anatomic orientation. It does not provide a protocol for screening, abnormal-test management, hysterectomy, radical surgery, prolapse repair, cervical insufficiency, pregnancy care, cancer treatment, fistula repair, radiation sequelae, or hormone treatment.

See [The Vagina](/docs/foundations/anatomy-physiology/genitalia/vaginal-anatomy), [The Ureters](/docs/foundations/anatomy-physiology/urinary-tract/ureter-anatomy-physiology), [The Uterus](/docs/foundations/anatomy-physiology/genitalia/uterus), and [Pelvic Neuroanatomy](/docs/foundations/anatomy-physiology/pelvis-support/pelvic-neuroanatomy).

## Basic organization

The cervical canal extends from the internal os, which opens to the uterine cavity, to the external os, which opens to the vagina. The ectocervix projects into the upper vagina and is visible at speculum examination; the endocervical canal is lined by columnar epithelium. The transformation zone is the region where squamous and columnar epithelia meet and change over time.[[1]](#ref1)[[3]](#ref3)

The cervix is collagen-rich connective tissue with smooth muscle, proteoglycans, and glycosaminoglycans. These components contribute to its mechanical behavior across reproductive states.[[1]](#ref1)

## Support, vessels, and adjacent structures

The cardinal and uterosacral connective-tissue complexes contribute to support of the cervix and upper vagina. Their tissue planes also contain vessels, nerves, and lymphatics. The named structures do not replace direct identification of patient-specific anatomy during pelvic surgery.[[2]](#ref2)

The uterine artery is an internal-iliac branch, with a descending branch contributing to cervical and vaginal blood supply. The ureter passes near the uterine vessels in the lateral pelvis; this relationship is relevant when working in the paracervical or cardinal-ligament region.[[2]](#ref2)[[4]](#ref4)

## Clinical boundaries

- Do not use this page to determine cervical cancer screening or follow-up, operative approach, apical-support procedure, nerve-sparing technique, ureteral-safety strategy, or pregnancy management.
- Prior surgery, cancer, radiation, endometriosis, prolapse, pregnancy, congenital variation, and pelvic disease can alter the usual tissue planes and risk.
- Pelvic pain, bleeding, pregnancy-related symptoms, urinary obstruction, fever, new mass symptoms, or postoperative concerns require clinical assessment appropriate to the presentation.
