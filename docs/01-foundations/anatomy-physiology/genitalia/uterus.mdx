# The Uterus

The uterus is a muscular organ whose function (implantation, gestation, parturition and menstrual shedding) is almost entirely reproductive. For the reconstructive pelvic surgeon, however, it matters as a structural and anatomical entity: the uterus anchors the Level-I apical support through the cardinal and uterosacral complex, shares a posterolateral vascular–neural pedicle with the cervix that contains the ureter and pelvic plexus, and is the operative neighbor whose manipulation (hysterectomy, myomectomy, cesarean section, arterial embolization) generates a large fraction of the urogynecologic workload, from vesicovaginal fistula to apical vault prolapse to radiation and surgical neurogenic bladder. This article concentrates on the surgical anatomy, ligamentous support, vascular pedicle, and innervation that matter for the reconstructive surgeon, and condenses menstrual-cycle endocrinology and molecular parturition biology to the minimum useful depth.

See also [The Cervix](/docs/foundations/anatomy-physiology/genitalia/cervix) (contiguous organ sharing most of these supports); [The Vagina](/docs/foundations/anatomy-physiology/genitalia/vaginal-anatomy) for DeLancey's support model; and [The Ureters](/docs/foundations/anatomy-physiology/urinary-tract/ureter-anatomy-physiology) for the "water under the bridge" anatomy at the cardinal ligament.

## Gross Anatomy

The uterus is a pear-shaped fibromuscular organ sitting between bladder (anterior) and rectum (posterior), suspended in the pelvis by four paired ligamentous attachments and the peritoneal broad ligament. Typical dimensions in nulliparous reproductive-age women are 7–8 cm long, 4–5 cm wide and 2–3 cm thick, with substantial variation by age and parity.<sup>[[5]](#ref5)[[6]](#ref6)[[7]](#ref7)</sup> Parous uterine volume is approximately 40% greater than nulliparous.

Three anatomic regions:

| Region | Location | Operative relevance |
|---|---|---|
| **Fundus** | Above the insertion of the fallopian tubes | Palpable landmark at delivery and during laparoscopy; cornual-region pregnancy is classically "interstitial" here |
| **Corpus (body)** | Main muscular mass | Site of fibroids, adenomyosis, endometrial cancer; the operative field of myomectomy and hysterectomy |
| **Cervix** | See [The Cervix](/docs/foundations/anatomy-physiology/genitalia/cervix) | Level-I support anchor; ureteric neighbor |

The junction between corpus and cervix (the **isthmus**) is anatomically where the uterus narrows and where cesarean hysterotomy is performed (low transverse), where cesarean scar defects (isthmocele) form, and where supracervical hysterectomy transects the uterus.

### Position and relations

- **Anteverted and anteflexed** is the common position: anteversion describes the cervix–vagina angle, while anteflexion describes the body–cervix angle, both directed forward.<sup>[[27]](#ref27)</sup>
- **Retroverted or retroflexed** in approximately 20%; a normal variant, but relevant to pessary fit, IUD placement, and surgical orientation
- Anterior: bladder, via the vesicouterine pouch (anterior cul-de-sac)
- Posterior: rectum, via the rectouterine pouch of Douglas (posterior cul-de-sac), the deepest peritoneal recess in the pelvis and the site of enterocele and of posterior culdotomy access
- Lateral: broad ligament with its contents (see below); pelvic sidewall further laterally

## Wall Structure

Three layers, with a clinically distinct **junctional zone** visible on MRI:<sup>[[3]](#ref3)[[4]](#ref4)[[5]](#ref5)</sup>

| Layer | Composition | Operative relevance |
|---|---|---|
| **Endometrium** | Functionalis (proliferates and sheds) + basalis (regenerative) | Site of cyclic bleeding, endometrial hyperplasia and cancer, menstrual dysfunction — the target of endometrial ablation; sampling via pipelle / D&C |
| **Junctional zone** | Inner myometrial band with tightly packed smooth muscle; MRI-visible | Thickening or irregularity can contribute to an **adenomyosis** assessment, but a single &gt;12 mm cutoff is not diagnostic by itself<sup>[[29]](#ref29)</sup> |
| **Myometrium** | >95% smooth muscle in interlacing bundles; intrinsic pacemaker activity | Site of leiomyomas (submucosal / intramural / subserosal); the dissected territory of myomectomy; the contracting organ at labor and in postpartum hemorrhage |
| **Perimetrium (serosa)** | Peritoneal covering on most of the outer surface; continuous with broad ligament laterally | The serosal plane of adhesiolysis; **absent over the lower anterior segment** where the bladder reflects, which is why cesarean and hysterectomy bladder-peritoneal dissection is done in this avascular plane |

## Ligamentous Support — The Apical Framework

The uterus is "suspended in air" by four paired attachments, not all of which are true ligaments. For the reconstructive surgeon only two are load-bearing; the others function as conduits or positional tethers.<sup>[[18]](#ref18)[[19]](#ref19)[[20]](#ref20)[[21]](#ref21)[[22]](#ref22)</sup>

| Structure | Function | Contents |
|---|---|---|
| **Cardinal ligament (Mackenrodt / transverse cervical)** | **Primary load-bearing Level-I support**; suspends the cervix/upper vagina from the pelvic sidewall | Uterine artery and veins; **ureter at its base**; pelvic plexus autonomic fibers; lymphatics |
| **Uterosacral ligament** | Posterior Level-I support; also the site of USL suspension procedures | Sympathetic fibers of the pelvic plexus, lymphatics |
| **Round ligament** | Positional only — maintains anteversion; **not load-bearing** | From uterine cornua → inguinal canal → labia majora; contains Sampson's artery (branch of uterine or ovarian) |
| **Broad ligament** | Peritoneal drape; carries vessels and tubes | Two layers of peritoneum enclosing fallopian tubes, ovarian ligaments, round ligaments, and the uterine vessels laterally |

The cardinal ligament is a visceral mesentery. Cadaveric and MRI studies demonstrate that it is not a discrete fibrous band but a perivascular or perineural sheath, essentially a visceral mesentery of vessels, lymphatics, autonomic nerves, and fatty connective tissue extending between the pelvic sidewall and the cervix. Radical-hysterectomy nomenclature (Piver / Querleu-Morrow) is about *how laterally* in this sheath the dissection extends, which determines how much of the pelvic plexus is sacrificed.<sup>[[19]](#ref19)</sup>

## Vascular Supply

Arterial supply is dominantly from the uterine artery (a branch of the internal iliac anterior division), with ovarian-artery anastomoses providing a second axis of supply that is clinically exploited and sometimes clinically surprising:<sup>[[12]](#ref12)[[13]](#ref13)[[14]](#ref14)[[15]](#ref15)</sup>

| Source | Course | Territory |
|---|---|---|
| **Uterine artery** | Internal iliac → crosses over the ureter 1.5–2 cm lateral to the cervix → ascends in the broad ligament along the lateral uterus | Dominant supply; gives arcuate → radial → spiral arteries into endometrium |
| **Ovarian artery** | From aorta → infundibulopelvic (suspensory) ligament → ovary → anastomoses with the uterine artery at the cornua | Upper uterus, fundus, tubes |
| **Contralateral uterine** | Across-midline anastomoses with opposite uterine artery | Collateral supply; **why unilateral uterine-artery ligation does not devascularize the uterus** |

### Ovarian-uterine territorial shift — functional, not fixed

The border between uterine and ovarian arterial territories may vary with menstrual phase: a small physiologic study reported greater ovarian-artery contribution to the cornual region in the follicular phase and greater uterine-artery contribution in the luteal phase.<sup>[[15]](#ref15)</sup> Ovarian–uterine collaterals matter during uterine-artery embolization (UAE), but that study does not establish them as the most common reason for fibroid or postpartum-hemorrhage treatment failure.

### Veno-arterial countercurrent exchange

Uterine veins drain to the internal iliac venous plexus; some join the ovarian venous outlet. Close apposition of vessels permits proposed countercurrent transfer of locally released substances between ovary, tube, and uterus.<sup>[[12]](#ref12)</sup> This anatomic hypothesis should not be treated as proof that vaginal progesterone or hCG acts only through local, rather than systemic, transfer.

## Innervation

Autonomic, with sparse somatic contribution:<sup>[[11]](#ref11)[[16]](#ref16)[[17]](#ref17)</sup>

- **Sympathetic.** T10–L2, then the superior hypogastric plexus (at the sacral promontory), paired hypogastric nerves and the inferior hypogastric (pelvic) plexus, reaching the uterus via the cardinal and uterosacral ligaments. Mediates uterine smooth-muscle contraction and vasoconstriction.
- **Parasympathetic.** S2–S4 pelvic splanchnic nerves, then the pelvic plexus, reaching the uterus via the same ligamentous routes. Vasodilation and relaxation.
- **Afferent pain.** Visceral afferents track with the sympathetic input; labor pain below the cervix is parasympathetic-S2-S4, above via T10–L2. This is the distribution targeted by neuraxial labor analgesia and by superior hypogastric plexus block.

The pelvic plexus sits between the cardinal ligament ventrally and the uterosacral ligament dorsally. Preserving bladder, rectal and vaginal-sexual function during radical hysterectomy turns on sparing the posterolateral component of this plexus. See [The Cervix](/docs/foundations/anatomy-physiology/genitalia/cervix) for the nerve-sparing radical-hysterectomy details.

## Physiology (kept brief)

### Menstrual cycle — the endometrial framework

The approximately 28-day cycle under the hypothalamic–pituitary–ovarian axis produces three functional phases:<sup>[[2]](#ref2)[[8]](#ref8)[[9]](#ref9)[[10]](#ref10)</sup>

- **Menstrual (days 1–5).** Progesterone withdrawal is followed by spiral-artery vasoconstriction and ischemic shedding of the functionalis.
- **Proliferative (days 6–14, estrogen-dominant).** Basalis-driven regeneration, gland formation, stromal proliferation, spiral-artery growth. Endometrial thickness rises from 2–4 mm to 8–12 mm.
- **Secretory (days 15–28, progesterone-dominant).** Glandular coiling and secretion, stromal decidualization, and the window of implantation (days 20–24).

Endometrial thickness is one part of evaluating abnormal bleeding, not a stand-alone rule-out test. ACOG's 2026 focused update recommends both transvaginal ultrasound and endometrial tissue sampling for most patients with postmenopausal bleeding, replacing routine reliance on an initial ≤4 mm ultrasound result alone; any ultrasound-only exception requires careful individual selection.<sup>[[28]](#ref28)</sup> Persistent or recurrent bleeding still requires evaluation regardless of thickness. An incidental &gt;4 mm measurement without bleeding does not automatically require sampling under the prior committee opinion.<sup>[[35]](#ref35)</sup>

### Myometrial quiescence and labor

During pregnancy the myometrium is held quiescent by progesterone-dominant signaling (PR suppression of inflammatory transcription factors, repression of contraction-associated proteins, potassium-channel-mediated hyperpolarization). At labor, functional progesterone withdrawal (decline in PR activity rather than in circulating progesterone in humans) lifts this suppression: oxytocin-receptor, connexin-43, and COX-2 expression rise, calcium-channel activity rises, and coordinated contraction becomes possible.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[23]](#ref23)[[24]](#ref24)[[25]](#ref25)[[26]](#ref26)</sup>

Clinical translations for the reconstructive surgeon: oxytocin and prostaglandins are uterotonics for postpartum hemorrhage, while selected agents can be used as tocolytics in obstetric care. Hemorrhage response is concurrent and cause-directed, not a fixed wait-through-each-step ladder: current WHO guidance calls for immediate uterine massage, an oxytocic, tranexamic acid, IV fluids, and examination for the bleeding source, with tamponade, surgery, transfusion or other escalation when needed.<sup>[[32]](#ref32)[[36]](#ref36)</sup>

### Pregnancy remodeling

The uterus undergoes marked smooth-muscle hypertrophy and hyperplasia, vascular expansion, and decidualization of the stroma. It returns toward baseline over the puerperium through apoptosis and matrix remodeling. Placenta accreta spectrum is a distinct disorder of placental attachment and invasion, not simply disordered postpartum involution.

## Clinical Correlations for the Reconstructive Surgeon

- **Hysterectomy approach selection.** Vaginal and other minimally invasive routes are generally preferred when feasible for benign disease, but route depends on anatomy, concurrent procedures, surgeon expertise, available resources and informed patient preference.<sup>[[33]](#ref33)</sup> The uterine-artery pedicle sits lateral to the cervix with the ureter inferior (see [The Cervix](/docs/foundations/anatomy-physiology/genitalia/cervix)); deliberate ureter identification is an important injury-prevention step.
- **Ureteric injury.** The most common iatrogenic ureteric injury is at the uterine-artery crossing. Risk is concentrated in radical hysterectomy, deep paravaginal dissection, and management of post-cesarean hematoma. See [The Ureters](/docs/foundations/anatomy-physiology/urinary-tract/ureter-anatomy-physiology).
- **Supracervical (subtotal) hysterectomy.** Preserves the cervix and some support attachments. Continuing cervical screening and possible cyclical bleeding require counseling; randomized evidence has not shown better urinary, bowel or sexual function than total hysterectomy.<sup>[[30]](#ref30)</sup>
- **Myomectomy and fibroid mapping.** Fibroids are mapped as submucosal (FIGO type 0–2), intramural (3–4), subserosal (5–7), or other (8, e.g. cervical, parasitic). Submucosal fibroids are removed hysteroscopically; the rest are approached laparoscopically or openly depending on size, number, and fertility plans. The reconstructive surgeon should understand this language because myomectomy scar is a risk factor for rupture in subsequent pregnancy and for intraoperative findings of dense pelvic adhesions at later urogynecologic operation.
- **Uterine artery embolization (UAE).** An option for selected symptomatic fibroids and certain bleeding emergencies; ovarian–uterine collateral supply is a technical consideration, not an established dominant cause of failure. For people contemplating pregnancy, effects on fertility and pregnancy remain uncertain and should be discussed alongside alternatives such as myomectomy.<sup>[[31]](#ref31)</sup>
- **Adenomyosis.** Endometrial-type tissue within the myometrium can be associated with heavy bleeding and pain. Junctional-zone thickening on MRI is one finding, not a stand-alone &gt;12 mm diagnosis; imaging and management depend on the full clinical picture and fertility goals.<sup>[[29]](#ref29)</sup>
- **Cesarean section and its downstream reconstruction.** Cesarean surgery can cause bladder or ureteric injury and rarely urogenital fistula, but it should not be called the leading worldwide cause of vesicovaginal fistula; prolonged obstructed labor remains a major cause where emergency obstetric care is limited.<sup>[[34]](#ref34)</sup> Placenta accreta spectrum can require planned multidisciplinary surgery and urologic assistance with complex bladder and ureter dissection. A cesarean scar defect (isthmocele) may cause postmenstrual spotting and can be evaluated for repair when clinically appropriate.
- **Obstetric fistula.** Prolonged obstructed labor can cause pressure necrosis of pelvic tissues and vesicovaginal or rectovaginal fistula; iatrogenic injury during obstetric or gynecologic surgery is another pathway. Evaluation and repair timing, route, and need for tissue interposition depend on the defect and surrounding tissue; see [The Vagina](/docs/foundations/anatomy-physiology/genitalia/vaginal-anatomy).<sup>[[34]](#ref34)</sup>
- **Apical prolapse with uterine descent.** Options preserve or remove the uterus: vaginal hysterectomy with USLS or SSLF, sacrospinous hysteropexy, sacrohysteropexy, or Manchester (Fothergill) procedure (cervical amputation and CUL plication). Patient counseling turns on fertility plans, cervix-cancer risk, and prior operations.
- **Postoperative bladder, bowel, and sexual dysfunction.** Mechanisms can include neural injury, altered support, pain and other perioperative factors; the page does not establish a universal neural cause. Pelvic-plexus preservation remains an important consideration in appropriately selected radical procedures.
- **Uterine rupture.** Usually at the site of a prior scar (C-section, myomectomy). Surgical emergency with fetal and maternal risk.
- **Placental anomalies and hysterectomy.** Complex placenta accreta spectrum cases may involve urologic support for bladder or ureter dissection and repair; prophylactic ureteric stents are individualized, not routine for every case. Knowing the uterine-artery–ureter geometry matters.
- **Endometrial cancer.** Most common gynecologic malignancy in high-resource settings. Staging hysterectomy and bilateral salpingo-oophorectomy ± lymphadenectomy. Radiation for higher stage or recurrence produces radiation cystitis, fistula, ureteric stricture, and bladder atony, the familiar reconstructive late-morbidity profile.
- **Gender-affirming hysterectomy.** An option for some gender-diverse patients based on their goals, not a required component of masculinizing care. Route, whether to remove tubes or ovaries, and any preoperative evaluation are individualized.
- **Müllerian anomalies.** Septate, bicornuate, didelphys, unicornuate uterus, and MRKH (Müllerian agenesis) have implications for fertility, obstetric outcomes, and reconstructive options (hysteroscopic septal resection, neovagina creation). Cross-link to [The Vagina](/docs/foundations/anatomy-physiology/genitalia/vaginal-anatomy) for neovaginal options in MRKH.
- **Uterine transplantation.** Investigational-to-emerging option for uterine-factor infertility (MRKH, prior hysterectomy). Technically complex vascular anastomoses; urologists are typically involved for ureteric planning and surveillance.
- **IUD perforation.** Uncommon but clinically important. The device can migrate to bladder, bowel, or peritoneum. Retrieval is hysteroscopic, laparoscopic, or open depending on location.
