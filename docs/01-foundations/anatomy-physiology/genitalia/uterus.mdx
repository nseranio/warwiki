# The Uterus

The uterus is a muscular pelvic organ involved in menstruation, implantation, pregnancy, and parturition. It lies between the bladder and rectum and is continuous inferiorly with the cervix. This page is a gross-anatomy orientation, not guidance for abnormal bleeding, pregnancy, fibroids, adenomyosis, malignancy, prolapse, hysterectomy, or other gynecologic care.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[2]](#ref2)</sup>

## Gross structure and relations

The uterus has a fundus, body, isthmus, and cervix. Its cavity is lined by endometrium and surrounded by myometrium, which is covered externally by peritoneum over much of the organ. Uterine position varies between individuals; anteverted, anteflexed, retroverted, retroflexed, and midline orientations are recognized.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup>

The anterior relationship is to the bladder, with the vesicouterine peritoneal reflection between them. Posteriorly, the uterus is related to the rectum through the rectouterine pouch. Laterally, it is associated with the broad ligament and the pelvic sidewall. The uterine tubes enter near the fundus, and the cervix projects toward the upper vagina.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[2]](#ref2)</sup>

## Supporting structures

Support comes from the pelvic floor and connective tissue around the cervix and upper vagina. The cardinal (transverse cervical) and uterosacral ligaments are important components of this support. The cardinal ligament extends from the lateral cervix toward the pelvic wall and contains uterine vessels; the uterosacral ligament extends posteriorly from the cervix toward the sacrum. These are complex connective-tissue and neurovascular regions rather than simple isolated cords.<sup>[[2]](#ref2)[[3]](#ref3)</sup>

The broad ligament is a double layer of peritoneum related to the uterus, uterine tubes, and ovaries. The round ligament runs from the uterine cornu through the inguinal canal toward the labia majora. The ovarian ligament connects the ovary to the uterus.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[2]](#ref2)</sup>

## Blood, lymphatic, and nerve supply

The uterine arteries are the principal arterial supply and arise from the internal iliac system. They ascend along the lateral uterus and anastomose with ovarian-artery branches near the uterine cornu. Within the uterine wall, arterial branches give rise to arcuate, radial, basal, and spiral vessels. Venous drainage is through the uterine venous plexus to the internal iliac veins.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[4]](#ref4)</sup>

Lymphatic drainage is segmental: the fundus drains principally toward para-aortic nodes, while lower uterine segments drain along the uterine vessels toward internal and external iliac nodes. Some channels accompanying the round ligament reach superficial inguinal nodes.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup>

Autonomic innervation reaches the uterus through the inferior hypogastric (uterovaginal) plexus. Pelvic splanchnic nerves contribute parasympathetic fibers. The proximity of these pathways to the cardinal and uterosacral regions is clinically important, but does not itself prescribe a surgical approach or predict an individual functional outcome.<sup>[[4]](#ref4)</sup>

## Functional orientation

The endometrium undergoes cyclical change in response to ovarian hormones. The myometrium is composed of smooth muscle and expands substantially during pregnancy; coordinated contraction supports labor. These physiologic summaries do not establish the cause of bleeding, pelvic pain, infertility, or pregnancy complications in an individual patient.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[4]](#ref4)</sup>
