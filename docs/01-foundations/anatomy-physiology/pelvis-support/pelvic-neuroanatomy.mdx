# Pelvic Neuroanatomy

Pelvic neuroanatomy is the single most consequential substrate in reconstructive pelvic surgery: the cavernous nerves whose preservation makes nerve-sparing prostatectomy possible, the pelvic plexus in the cardinal / uterosacral ligaments whose preservation governs bladder, bowel, and sexual outcomes after radical hysterectomy, the superior hypogastric plexus at risk during sacrocolpopexy, the pudendal nerve traversing Alcock's canal for every perineal operation, and the somatic nerves whose injury patterns appear as flank, groin, medial-thigh, or labial pain. This article consolidates that anatomy into a single reference, with explicit cross-links to the organ articles that use it.

See also [The Perineum](/docs/foundations/anatomy-physiology/pelvis-support/perineum) for pudendal-branch territory; [The Presacral Space](/docs/foundations/anatomy-physiology/pelvis-support/presacral-anatomy) for the superior hypogastric plexus geometry; [The Cervix](/docs/foundations/anatomy-physiology/genitalia/cervix) and [The Uterus](/docs/foundations/anatomy-physiology/genitalia/uterus) for nerve-sparing radical hysterectomy; [The Penis](/docs/foundations/anatomy-physiology/genitalia/penis-anatomy-physiology), [The Prostate](/docs/foundations/anatomy-physiology/genitalia/prostate-seminal-vesicle), [The Bladder](/docs/foundations/anatomy-physiology/urinary-tract/bladder-anatomy-physiology) for organ-specific innervation.

## Organizing Framework — Three Parallel Systems

The pelvic viscera receive three systems acting in concert:

| System | Origin | Output |
|---|---|---|
| **Sympathetic** | T10–L2 | Storage, continence, vasoconstriction, **ejaculation (emission)** |
| **Parasympathetic** | S2–S4 | Voiding, defecation, **erection / clitoral engorgement** |
| **Somatic** | S2–S4 (pudendal) + lumbosacral plexus (iliohypogastric, ilioinguinal, genitofemoral, femoral, obturator, sciatic) | Voluntary sphincter control; perineal and genital sensation; lower-limb motor/sensory |

Autonomic fibers converge on the **inferior hypogastric (pelvic) plexus** on each side of the rectum, upper vagina and prostate, the terminal relay station for bladder, rectum, vagina, prostate, and erectile innervation. Somatic fibers travel independently via named nerves.

---

## Sympathetic Pathway

### Thoracolumbar origin and abdominal traversal

1. **Preganglionic cell bodies**: T10–L2 intermediolateral gray column.
2. **Exit** via ventral roots, then white rami communicantes, then the sympathetic trunk.
3. **Lumbar splanchnic nerves** leave the lumbar sympathetic trunk and contribute, directly or through preaortic plexuses, to the **superior hypogastric plexus** and hypogastric nerves. The greater, lesser and least **thoracic splanchnic nerves** primarily serve abdominal prevertebral plexuses and should not be taught as the direct pelvic route ([anatomy chapter](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/books/NBK560504/)).

### Superior hypogastric plexus ("presacral nerve")

- Begins at the aortic bifurcation; descends across the sacral promontory in a connective-tissue sheet just below peritoneum.
- Its exact position, side-to-side deviation and width vary between specimens; published cadaveric measurements are not a transferable operative safe distance.
- **Predominantly sympathetic**, but carries some postganglionic parasympathetic and afferent fibers.
- Splits into paired **hypogastric nerves**.
- Transmits visceral afferents for midline pelvic pain; it is the target of superior hypogastric plexus block and presacral neurectomy for refractory dysmenorrhea or endometriosis.

### Hypogastric nerves

- Paired; descend from the superior hypogastric plexus across the pelvic inlet.
- Run within the parietal pelvic fascia posterolaterally along the pelvic sidewall.
- They can run close to the uterosacral complex, so they are vulnerable during uterosacral suspension, radical hysterectomy and ventral rectopexy; a fixed millimeter separation is not a safe operative landmark.
- Merge with pelvic splanchnic nerves to form the **inferior hypogastric plexus**.

### Sacral sympathetic trunk

Continues the sympathetic trunk into the pelvis medial to the anterior sacral foramina. **Sacral splanchnic branches** can contribute to the inferior hypogastric plexus, with variable identification across dissection series.

See [The Presacral Space](/docs/foundations/anatomy-physiology/pelvis-support/presacral-anatomy) for further sacrocolpopexy-relevant relationships and the callout on preserving the superior hypogastric plexus; reported distances are not universal safe zones.

## Parasympathetic Pathway

### Sacral origin

- Preganglionic fibers arise from S2–S4 ventral horn (intermediolateral cell column).
- Exit as **pelvic splanchnic nerves (nervi erigentes)** from the ventral rami of S2–S4.
- Merge directly into the inferior hypogastric plexus from behind, joining the hypogastric nerves (sympathetic) and sacral splanchnic contributions.

### Clinical significance

- Parasympathetic drive produces voiding (detrusor contraction), defecation (rectal contraction, IAS relaxation), and erection or clitoral engorgement (cavernosal NO release).
- Injury to the pelvic splanchnic nerves or inferior hypogastric plexus may contribute to impaired bladder emptying or sensation, bowel dysfunction and sexual dysfunction; the phenotype depends on injury extent and other preserved pathways.
- Suprasacral spinal and sacral or cauda-equina lesions have different typical bladder patterns, but lesion level alone does not establish a patient's urodynamic phenotype.

## The Inferior Hypogastric (Pelvic) Plexus — Where It All Converges

A dense fan-like plexus beside the rectum, with branches toward the upper vagina or prostate. Its relationship to the pelvic fascia and the cardinal-uterosacral complex or lateral prostatic pedicle varies across individuals and dissection planes; a single 1–3 cm offset is not a reliable surgical boundary.

### Inputs
- Hypogastric nerve (sympathetic from T10–L2)
- Pelvic splanchnic nerves (parasympathetic from S2–S4)
- Sacral splanchnic nerves (sympathetic from sacral trunk, approximately 47%)
- Sensory afferents

### Outputs (branches)
- **Bladder branches.** Travel toward the bladder base, with branches near the distal ureter.
- **Uterine branches.** Accompany the uterine vascular and support complex; distinguish them from bladder-directed fibers during dissection.
- **Rectal branches.** Reach the lateral rectal wall, sometimes with direct sacral contributions.
- **Vaginal branches.** Pass to the upper vagina.
- **Cavernous nerves.** Pass to the corpora cavernosa (M) or clitoral body (F); see below.

### Why nerve-sparing surgery turns on the inferior hypogastric plexus

- **Radical hysterectomy.** Nerve-sparing dissection aims to identify and preserve appropriate hypogastric, pelvic-splanchnic and bladder branches when oncologically feasible; functional outcomes are not guaranteed by a named plane.
- **Radical prostatectomy.** Cavernous fibers commonly course posterolateral to the prostate, but their distribution is variable. Nerve preservation must be balanced with cancer control, rather than inferred from a fixed 5/7-o'clock line.
- **Total mesorectal excision.** Dissection along the mesorectal plane aims to reduce hypogastric-nerve and plexus injury; it does not make injury impossible.

## Cavernous Nerves — Male Cadaveric Dorsal and Lateral Contributions

Autonomic cavernous fibers arise from the inferior hypogastric plexus and contribute to erectile responses; neighboring urethral innervation also matters for continence. A [male cadaveric microdissection](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/29577446/) described dorsal and lateral contributions to the proximal urethra and erectile tissue:<br/>

1. **Dorsal route.** Follows the seminal-vesicle and ejaculatory-duct axis toward the male proximal urethra and prostate base.
2. **Lateral route.** Follows the inferior vesical artery toward posterolateral periprostatic neurovascular tissue.

Female clitoral and vaginal autonomic pathways are related but cannot be mapped one-to-one from that male dissection. Distal branches reach erectile tissue through variable courses. See [The Penis](/docs/foundations/anatomy-physiology/genitalia/penis-anatomy-physiology), [The Prostate](/docs/foundations/anatomy-physiology/genitalia/prostate-seminal-vesicle) and [The Vulva](/docs/foundations/anatomy-physiology/genitalia/female-external-genitalia).

**The two routes are an anatomic observation, not a guaranteed outcome rule.** A male cadaveric microdissection described dorsal and lateral contributions to the proximal urethra and corpora, with communication in only a subset of specimens; surgical preservation may help function but must remain oncologically appropriate. Seminal-vesicle-tip sparing cannot be assumed to preserve potency or continence in every patient.

## Somatic Lumbosacral Plexus — What Reconstructive Surgery Touches

### From the lumbar plexus (L1–L4)

| Nerve | Roots | Territory | Operative risk |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Iliohypogastric** | T12–L1 | Anterior abdominal wall / mons | Pfannenstiel incision entrapment, trocar injury |
| **Ilioinguinal** | L1 | Inguinal region, anterior scrotum / labia majora | Inguinal herniorrhaphy neuralgia; laparoscopic trocar injury |
| **Genitofemoral** | L1–L2 | **Genital branch**: cremaster (M), anterior scrotum / labia majora. **Femoral branch**: skin over femoral triangle | Retroperitoneal dissection, psoas abscess drainage |
| **Lateral femoral cutaneous** | L2–L3 | Lateral thigh skin | **Lithotomy-positioning neuropathy** ("meralgia paresthetica"); retractor injury |
| **Femoral** | L2–L4 | Anterior thigh, quadriceps, medial leg (saphenous) | Deep retractor injury (Bookwalter / Balfour); lithotomy; psoas dissection |
| **Obturator** | L2–L4 | Medial thigh, adductors | Obturator-canal / obturator-LND injury; **TOT sling** |

### From the sacral plexus (L4–S4)

| Nerve | Roots | Territory | Operative risk |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Sciatic** | L4–S3 | Posterior thigh, entire leg below knee | Lithotomy, prone positioning, retroperitoneal SSLF mispositioning |
| **Posterior femoral cutaneous** | S1–S3 | Posterior thigh + perineal branch to lateral perineum | SSLF and Alcock's-canal dissection |
| **Superior gluteal** | L4–S1 | Gluteus medius/minimus | Transgluteal approaches |
| **Inferior gluteal** | L5–S2 | Gluteus maximus | Transgluteal, deep pelvic work |
| **Pudendal** | **S2–S4 (Onuf's nucleus)** | See below — **the perineal nerve** | SSLF adjacent; **Alcock's-canal entrapment**; pudendal-nerve block landmark |

### The pudendal nerve (S2–S4)

![Course of the pudendal nerve from the sacral roots through Alcock's canal to its three terminal branches](/img/diagrams/pudendal-nerve.svg)

*Schematic course of the pudendal nerve (S2–S4): through the greater sciatic foramen below piriformis, around the ischial spine/sacrospinous ligament, through the lesser sciatic foramen and along Alcock's canal before branching toward the anal sphincter, perineum and external genitalia. The ischial spine and canal are clinically relevant relationships, not a procedural needle or suture-placement map. (Original WARWIKI schematic; branch variation is not depicted.)*

- Exits pelvis through the greater sciatic foramen below piriformis.
- Wraps around the ischial spine and sacrospinous ligament.
- Re-enters through the lesser sciatic foramen into Alcock's (pudendal) canal on the medial surface of the obturator internus.
- Three terminal branches:
  - **Inferior rectal nerve.** External anal sphincter, perianal sensation.
  - **Perineal nerve.** Deep: bulbospongiosus, ischiocavernosus, transverse perinei and external urethral sphincter (via Onuf's nucleus); superficial: posterior scrotal or labial sensation.
  - **Dorsal nerve of the penis or clitoris.** Dominant sensory input to the glans; runs periosteal on the inferior pubic ramus.

**Onuf's nucleus** (sacral ventral horn) contains motor neurons supplying the striated external urethral and external anal sphincters. Relative preservation has been reported in ALS, but individual continence cannot be predicted from that histologic observation alone.

See [The Perineum](/docs/foundations/anatomy-physiology/pelvis-support/perineum) for the full pudendal-branch territory and [The Vulva](/docs/foundations/anatomy-physiology/genitalia/female-external-genitalia) for the clitoral-NVB callout.

## Enteric Nervous System

The **intrinsic nervous system of the gut** regulates the rectum and anal canal through two plexuses:

- **Auerbach's (myenteric) plexus.** Lies between circular and longitudinal muscle; coordinates motility.
- **Meissner's (submucosal) plexus.** Lies in the submucosa; coordinates secretion and local reflexes.

Ganglion cells in the anal canal are mostly restricted to the zone above the dentate line. Absence of ganglion cells (Hirschsprung's disease) produces functional obstruction; this is the anatomic substrate of the recto-anal inhibitory reflex (RAIR) and the pathophysiologic basis for aganglionosis.

## Reference Zones for Reconstructive Surgery

### The cardinal ligament geometry
- The cardinal ligament is a visceral perivascular or perineural mesentery carrying uterine vessels, ureter (at its base), pelvic plexus fibers, and fatty connective tissue.
- Radical-hysterectomy nomenclature (Piver / Querleu-Morrow) is fundamentally about how laterally the dissection extends through the cardinal, determining how much of the plexus is sacrificed.

### The 5-7 o'clock prostatic pedicle
- Prostatic pedicle and NVB run posterolaterally at 5 and 7 o'clock along the capsule.
- **Intrafascial, interfascial and extrafascial** describe different tissue planes and often different amounts of neural preservation; neither the clock position nor plane name guarantees functional recovery or oncologic suitability.

### The 2 and 10 o'clock dorsal penile / clitoral neurovascular geometry
- Dorsal penile / clitoral nerves course in the dorsal neurovascular tissues toward the glans; branching and communicating fibers vary.
- Do not infer a universally nerve-free 12-o'clock line or use this schematic alone to plan a dorsal nerve block.

### S1 foramen proximity in sacrocolpopexy
- A [cadaveric study](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/28709584/) placed the medial-superior S1 foramen at a median 26 mm caudal (range 22–37) and 19 mm lateral (range 13–23) to its defined promontory and midline landmarks; these are orientation data, not a 2.5 cm × 2 cm safety boundary.
- Sacral fixation requires direct anatomic identification and attention to nearby nerves, vessels and the disc; a fixed measurement cannot exclude radiculopathy or other injury.

### Alcock's canal and pudendal entrapment
- The original [**Nantes criteria**](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/17828787/) for suspected pudendal nerve entrapment require pain in pudendal territory, worse when sitting, not waking the patient at night, no objective sensory loss, and relief with a correctly performed diagnostic pudendal block. They are diagnostic aids, not proof of entrapment in every pelvic-pain patient.
- Decompression is a selected specialist option after careful diagnosis and treatment discussion; this anatomy page does not establish its effectiveness for an individual.

## Clinical Correlations for the Reconstructive Surgeon

- **Nerve-sparing radical prostatectomy.** Identify variable posterolateral and seminal-vesicle-adjacent fibers when oncologically appropriate; do not equate one fascial plane with guaranteed potency or continence.
- **Nerve-sparing radical hysterectomy.** Selected approaches aim to preserve pelvic autonomic branches while meeting oncologic margins; the extent of resection and function are individualized.
- **Nerve-preserving sacrocolpopexy.** A [six-cadaver study](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/20542624/) described a right common-iliac and peritoneal approach above the promontory to expose fixation anatomy while protecting presacral nerves. It did not establish a universal 30 × 20 mm safe mesh zone below the promontory.
- **Ureteric-injury prevention at hysterectomy and USLS.** The ureter crosses beneath the uterine artery near the cervix, with variable spacing; identify the actual ureteral course before clamping or placing deep sutures.
- **Post-hysterectomy bladder dysfunction.** Autonomic nerve injury is one possible contributor, especially after radical surgery; evaluate other causes rather than attributing every case to the inferior hypogastric plexus.
- **Post-APR / exenteration sexual dysfunction.** Pelvic autonomic nerve injury may contribute, but treatment extent and other vascular, hormonal and psychosocial factors also matter; no dissection plane guarantees prevention.
- **Pudendal nerve block.** The ischial-spine region is an anatomic landmark, but route, imaging and needle placement require a trained procedural protocol, not this overview.
- **Obturator-nerve injury** during TOT, paravaginal repair or obturator LND produces medial-thigh sensory loss and adductor weakness.
- **Lithotomy-position neuropathy.** Common peroneal, femoral and lateral femoral cutaneous nerves may be injured by positioning or retractors; use pressure avoidance and individualized positioning checks rather than treating one angle or time interval as universally protective.
- **Superior hypogastric plexus block / presacral neurectomy.** These are selected pain procedures with distinct indications and uncertain benefit for a given patient; anatomic location alone does not establish effectiveness or exclude other pain generators.
- **Ganglion impar block.** The ganglion impar lies near the sacrococcygeal junction and is not the inferior hypogastric or pelvic plexus; it may be considered in selected perineal or coccygeal pain care.
- **Sacral neuromodulation (SNM).** Usually accesses a sacral foramen, often S3, and is an option for selected refractory OAB, non-obstructive urinary retention and fecal incontinence ([AUA OAB guidance](https://www.auanet.org/documents/Guidelines/PDF/2024%20Guidelines/OAB%202024%20Unabridgedv2.pdf); [AUA retention statement](https://www.auanet.org/advocacy/best-practice-statements-and-whitepapers/chronic-urinary-retention); [ASCRS fecal-incontinence guidance](https://www.ascrsu.com/ascrs/view/ASCRS-Evidence-Based-Guidelines-and-Expert-Consensus/3982020/all/Management%20of%20Fecal%20Incontinence%20%282023%29)). Its mechanism is incompletely resolved; chronic pelvic pain is not a routine equivalent indication.
- **Neurogenic bladder phenotypes by lesion level.** Suprapontine, suprasacral spinal and sacral or cauda-equina lesions have useful *typical* patterns, but actual storage and emptying behavior requires patient-specific evaluation and often urodynamics.
