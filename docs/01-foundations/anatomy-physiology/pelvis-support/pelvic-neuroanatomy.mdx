# Pelvic Neuroanatomy

**Pelvic neuroanatomy** determines functional outcome in reconstructive pelvic surgery. The cavernous nerves are the basis of nerve-sparing prostatectomy. The pelvic plexus in the cardinal and uterosacral ligaments is associated with bladder, bowel and sexual function after radical hysterectomy. The superior hypogastric plexus lies at risk during sacrocolpopexy, and the pudendal nerve traverses Alcock's canal in every perineal operation. Somatic nerve injuries present as flank, groin, medial-thigh or labial pain. This article consolidates that anatomy and links to the organ articles that use it.

See also [The Perineum](/docs/foundations/anatomy-physiology/pelvis-support/perineum) for pudendal-branch territory; [The Presacral Space](/docs/foundations/anatomy-physiology/pelvis-support/presacral-anatomy) for the superior hypogastric plexus geometry; [The Cervix](/docs/foundations/anatomy-physiology/genitalia/cervix) and [The Uterus](/docs/foundations/anatomy-physiology/genitalia/uterus) for nerve-sparing radical hysterectomy; [The Penis](/docs/foundations/anatomy-physiology/genitalia/penis-anatomy-physiology), [The Prostate](/docs/foundations/anatomy-physiology/genitalia/prostate-seminal-vesicle), [The Bladder](/docs/foundations/anatomy-physiology/urinary-tract/bladder-anatomy-physiology) for organ-specific innervation.

## Organizing Framework — Three Parallel Systems

The pelvic viscera receive three systems acting in concert:

| System | Origin | Output |
|---|---|---|
| **Sympathetic** | T10–L2 | Storage, continence, vasoconstriction, **ejaculation (emission)** |
| **Parasympathetic** | S2–S4 | Voiding, defecation, **erection / clitoral engorgement** |
| **Somatic** | S2–S4 (pudendal) + lumbosacral plexus (iliohypogastric, ilioinguinal, genitofemoral, femoral, obturator, sciatic) | Voluntary sphincter control; perineal and genital sensation; lower-limb motor/sensory |

Autonomic fibers converge on the **inferior hypogastric (pelvic) plexus** on each side of the rectum, upper vagina and prostate, the terminal relay station for bladder, rectum, vagina, prostate, and erectile innervation. Somatic fibers travel independently via named nerves.

## Sympathetic Pathway

### Thoracolumbar origin and abdominal traversal

1. **Preganglionic cell bodies**: T10–L2 intermediolateral gray column.
2. **Exit** via ventral roots, then white rami communicantes, then the sympathetic trunk.
3. **Lumbar splanchnic nerves** leave the lumbar sympathetic trunk and contribute, directly or through preaortic plexuses, to the **superior hypogastric plexus** and hypogastric nerves. The greater, lesser and least **thoracic splanchnic nerves** primarily serve abdominal prevertebral plexuses and are not the direct pelvic route ([anatomy chapter](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/books/NBK560504/)).

### Superior hypogastric plexus ("presacral nerve")

- Begins at the aortic bifurcation; descends across the sacral promontory in a connective-tissue sheet just below peritoneum.
- In 17 female cadavers, the plexus lay a median 21.3 mm (range 9–40 mm) below the aortic bifurcation in 14 specimens (82.4%) and a median 25.3 mm above it in the rest. It lay left of midline in 58.8%, with median length 39.5 mm and width 9 mm, a flat band rather than a cord.<sup>[[Ripperda 2017]](#nref-ripperda)</sup> Position varies between specimens, so these values orient the dissection and do not define a safe distance.
- **Predominantly sympathetic**. Immunohistochemistry in four cadavers showed sympathetic and most likely postganglionic parasympathetic fibers; afferent fibers also travel in it.
- Splits into paired **hypogastric nerves**.
- Transmits visceral afferents for midline pelvic pain; it is the target of superior hypogastric plexus block and presacral neurectomy for refractory dysmenorrhea or endometriosis.

### Hypogastric nerves

- Paired; descend from the superior hypogastric plexus across the pelvic inlet.
- Run within the parietal pelvic fascia posterolaterally along the pelvic sidewall.
- In the same cadaver series they lay a median 23 mm (range 5–32 mm) below the promontory, and the nearest nerve branch lay a median 0–0.5 mm from the midportion of the uterosacral ligament. This proximity makes them vulnerable during uterosacral suspension, radical hysterectomy and ventral rectopexy.
- Merge with pelvic splanchnic nerves to form the **inferior hypogastric plexus**.

### Sacral sympathetic trunk

Continues the sympathetic trunk into the pelvis medial to the anterior sacral foramina. **Sacral splanchnic branches** contributed to the inferior hypogastric plexus in 16 of 34 hemipelvises (47%) where the trunk was identified, and in 2 of 6 cadavers in another dissection series.

See [The Presacral Space](/docs/foundations/anatomy-physiology/pelvis-support/presacral-anatomy) for further sacrocolpopexy-relevant relationships and the callout on preserving the superior hypogastric plexus.

## Parasympathetic Pathway

### Sacral origin

- Preganglionic fibers arise from S2–S4 ventral horn (intermediolateral cell column).
- Exit as **pelvic splanchnic nerves (nervi erigentes)** from the ventral rami of S2–S4.
- Merge directly into the inferior hypogastric plexus from behind, joining the hypogastric nerves (sympathetic) and sacral splanchnic contributions.

### Clinical significance

- Parasympathetic drive produces voiding (detrusor contraction), defecation (rectal contraction, IAS relaxation), and erection or clitoral engorgement (cavernosal NO release).
- Injury to the pelvic splanchnic nerves or inferior hypogastric plexus may contribute to impaired bladder emptying or sensation, bowel dysfunction and sexual dysfunction. The phenotype depends on injury extent and on the pathways that remain.
- Suprasacral spinal lesions spare the sacral reflex arc and typically produce a hyperreflexic bladder. Sacral and cauda-equina lesions interrupt the arc and typically produce an underactive or acontractile bladder. Urodynamics defines the individual phenotype.

## The Inferior Hypogastric (Pelvic) Plexus — Where It All Converges

A dense fan-like plexus beside the rectum, with branches toward the upper vagina or prostate. In female cadavers it formed 1–3 cm lateral to the rectum and upper third of the vagina.<sup>[[Ripperda 2017]](#nref-ripperda)</sup> It lies in the cardinal and uterosacral ligaments (female) or the lateral prostatic pedicle (male). Its relationship to the pelvic fascia varies between individuals, so the offset is not a fixed surgical boundary.

### Inputs
- Hypogastric nerve (sympathetic from T10–L2)
- Pelvic splanchnic nerves (parasympathetic from S2–S4)
- Sacral splanchnic nerves (sympathetic from sacral trunk, in about 47% of hemipelvises)
- Sensory afferents

### Outputs (branches)
- **Bladder branches.** One to three discrete branches pass deep to the ureter toward the bladder base.
- **Uterine branches.** Follow the ascending uterine artery superficial to the ureter, which distinguishes them from the bladder branches during dissection.
- **Rectal branches.** Reach the lateral rectal wall; an S4 branch attached directly to it in 53% of specimens.
- **Vaginal branches.** Pass to the upper vagina.
- **Cavernous nerves.** Pass to the corpora cavernosa (M) or clitoral body (F); see below.

### Why nerve-sparing surgery turns on the inferior hypogastric plexus

- **Radical hysterectomy.** Nerve-sparing dissection aims to preserve the hypogastric, pelvic-splanchnic and bladder branches, including the dorsolateral parasympathetic part of the cardinal ligament, when oncologically feasible. A Cochrane review compares nerve-sparing with standard radical hysterectomy for early cervical cancer.
- **Radical prostatectomy.** Cavernous fibers commonly course posterolateral to the prostate at about 5 and 7 o'clock, but their distribution is variable. Nerve preservation is balanced against cancer control.
- **Total mesorectal excision.** Dissection along the mesorectal plane aims to spare the hypogastric nerves and plexus, though injury still occurs.

## Cavernous Nerves — Male Cadaveric Dorsal and Lateral Contributions

Cavernous fibers arise from the inferior hypogastric plexus and contribute to erectile responses, and neighboring urethral innervation contributes to continence. A [male cadaveric microdissection](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/29577446/) (three formalin-embalmed and 16 Thiel-embalmed hemipelves) described two origins for the nerve supply to the proximal urethra and corpora:<br/>

1. **Dorsal route.** Follows the seminal-vesicle and ejaculatory-duct axis toward the male proximal urethra and prostate base.
2. **Lateral route.** Follows the inferior vesical artery toward posterolateral periprostatic neurovascular tissue.

Female clitoral and vaginal autonomic pathways are related but have not been mapped one-to-one from that male dissection. See [The Penis](/docs/foundations/anatomy-physiology/genitalia/penis-anatomy-physiology), [The Prostate](/docs/foundations/anatomy-physiology/genitalia/prostate-seminal-vesicle) and [The Vulva](/docs/foundations/anatomy-physiology/genitalia/female-external-genitalia).

The two routes were connected in about one third of samples, and the authors concluded that saving both the dorsal and lateral roots is important to maintain erectile and continence function. Sparing only the lateral neurovascular bundle does not preserve the dorsal contribution. Seminal-vesicle-tip sparing, when oncologically safe, preserves dorsal fibers; whether it improves potency or continence has not been established by this anatomic study.

## Somatic Lumbosacral Plexus — What Reconstructive Surgery Touches

### From the lumbar plexus (L1–L4)

| Nerve | Roots | Territory | Operative risk |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Iliohypogastric** | T12–L1 | Anterior abdominal wall / mons | Pfannenstiel incision entrapment, trocar injury |
| **Ilioinguinal** | L1 | Inguinal region, anterior scrotum / labia majora | Inguinal herniorrhaphy neuralgia; laparoscopic trocar injury |
| **Genitofemoral** | L1–L2 | **Genital branch**: cremaster (M), anterior scrotum / labia majora. **Femoral branch**: skin over femoral triangle | Retroperitoneal dissection, psoas abscess drainage |
| **Lateral femoral cutaneous** | L2–L3 | Lateral thigh skin | **Lithotomy-positioning neuropathy** ("meralgia paresthetica"); retractor injury |
| **Femoral** | L2–L4 | Anterior thigh, quadriceps, medial leg (saphenous) | Deep retractor injury (Bookwalter / Balfour); lithotomy; psoas dissection |
| **Obturator** | L2–L4 | Medial thigh, adductors | Obturator-canal / obturator-LND injury; **TOT sling** |

### From the sacral plexus (L4–S4)

| Nerve | Roots | Territory | Operative risk |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Sciatic** | L4–S3 | Posterior thigh, entire leg below knee | Lithotomy, prone positioning, retroperitoneal SSLF mispositioning |
| **Posterior femoral cutaneous** | S1–S3 | Posterior thigh + perineal branch to lateral perineum | SSLF and Alcock's-canal dissection |
| **Superior gluteal** | L4–S1 | Gluteus medius/minimus | Transgluteal approaches |
| **Inferior gluteal** | L5–S2 | Gluteus maximus | Transgluteal, deep pelvic work |
| **Pudendal** | **S2–S4 (Onuf's nucleus)** | See below — **the perineal nerve** | SSLF adjacent; **Alcock's-canal entrapment**; pudendal-nerve block landmark |

### The pudendal nerve (S2–S4)

![Course of the pudendal nerve from the sacral roots through Alcock's canal to its three terminal branches](/img/diagrams/pudendal-nerve.svg)

*Schematic course of the pudendal nerve (S2–S4): through the greater sciatic foramen below piriformis, around the ischial spine/sacrospinous ligament, through the lesser sciatic foramen and along Alcock's canal before branching toward the anal sphincter, perineum and external genitalia. The ischial spine and canal are clinically relevant relationships, not a procedural needle or suture-placement map. (Original WARWIKI schematic; branch variation is not depicted.)*

- Exits pelvis through the greater sciatic foramen below piriformis.
- Wraps around the ischial spine and sacrospinous ligament.
- Re-enters through the lesser sciatic foramen into Alcock's (pudendal) canal on the medial surface of the obturator internus.
- Three terminal branches:
  - **Inferior rectal nerve.** External anal sphincter, perianal sensation.
  - **Perineal nerve.** Deep: bulbospongiosus, ischiocavernosus, transverse perinei and external urethral sphincter (via Onuf's nucleus); superficial: posterior scrotal or labial sensation.
  - **Dorsal nerve of the penis or clitoris.** Dominant sensory input to the glans; runs periosteal on the inferior pubic ramus.

**Onuf's nucleus** (sacral ventral horn) contains motor neurons supplying the striated external urethral and external anal sphincters. Relative preservation has been reported in ALS, which is consistent with the continence typically retained until late disease.

See [The Perineum](/docs/foundations/anatomy-physiology/pelvis-support/perineum) for the full pudendal-branch territory and [The Vulva](/docs/foundations/anatomy-physiology/genitalia/female-external-genitalia) for the clitoral-NVB callout.

## Enteric Nervous System

The **intrinsic nervous system of the gut** regulates the rectum and anal canal through two plexuses:

- **Auerbach's (myenteric) plexus.** Lies between circular and longitudinal muscle; coordinates motility.
- **Meissner's (submucosal) plexus.** Lies in the submucosa; coordinates secretion and local reflexes.

Ganglion cells in the anal canal are mostly restricted to the zone above the dentate line. Absence of ganglion cells (Hirschsprung's disease) produces functional obstruction; this is the anatomic substrate of the recto-anal inhibitory reflex (RAIR) and the pathophysiologic basis for aganglionosis.

## Reference Zones for Reconstructive Surgery

### The cardinal ligament geometry
- The cardinal ligament is a visceral perivascular or perineural mesentery carrying uterine vessels, ureter (at its base), pelvic plexus fibers, and fatty connective tissue.
- Radical-hysterectomy nomenclature (Piver / Querleu-Morrow) is fundamentally about how laterally the dissection extends through the cardinal, determining how much of the plexus is sacrificed.

### The 5-7 o'clock prostatic pedicle
- The prostatic pedicle and NVB run posterolaterally at 5 and 7 o'clock along the capsule.
- **Intrafascial** dissection, between the capsule and prostatic fascia, preserves the most nerve tissue. **Interfascial** and **extrafascial** dissection sacrifice progressively more. Neither the clock position nor the plane name guarantees functional recovery or oncologic suitability.

### The 2 and 10 o'clock dorsal penile / clitoral neurovascular geometry
- Dorsal penile / clitoral nerves course in the dorsal neurovascular tissues toward the glans; branching and communicating fibers vary.
- The dorsal penile nerve block is classically placed at the 10 and 2 o'clock positions; branching and communicating fibers vary, so a nerve-free 12 o'clock line is not assured.

### S1 foramen proximity in sacrocolpopexy
- A [cadaveric study](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/28709584/) placed the medial-superior S1 foramen at a median 26 mm caudal (range 22–37) and 19 mm lateral (range 13–23) to its defined promontory and midline landmarks. The authors advise anticipating the first sacral nerve about 2.5 cm below the promontory midpoint and 2 cm to the right of midline. Fixation below the promontory risks S1 radiculopathy.
- Sacral fixation depends on direct identification of nearby nerves, vessels and the disc.

### Alcock's canal and pudendal entrapment
- The original [**Nantes criteria**](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/17828787/) for suspected pudendal nerve entrapment require pain in pudendal territory, worse when sitting, not waking the patient at night, no objective sensory loss, and relief with a correctly performed diagnostic pudendal block. The criteria are clinical, and imaging abnormalities that explain the symptoms exclude the diagnosis.
- Decompression is reserved for refractory cases after a diagnostic pudendal block.

## Clinical Correlations for the Reconstructive Surgeon

- **Nerve-sparing radical prostatectomy.** Preserve both the lateral neurovascular bundle and the dorsal route along the seminal vesicles when oncologically appropriate. No single fascial plane guarantees potency or continence.
- **Nerve-sparing radical hysterectomy.** The aim is to preserve pelvic autonomic branches while meeting oncologic margins.
- **Nerve-preserving sacrocolpopexy.** A [six-cadaver study](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/20542624/) described a longitudinal peritoneal incision along the right common iliac artery and above the promontory. This approach exposes the anterior longitudinal ligament while protecting the superior hypogastric plexus and the presacral area below the promontory.
- **Ureteric-injury prevention at hysterectomy and USLS.** The ureter crosses beneath the uterine artery near the cervix, with variable spacing. Identify the ureter before clamping or placing deep sutures.
- **Post-hysterectomy bladder dysfunction.** Autonomic nerve injury is one contributor, especially after radical surgery, and other causes are evaluated.
- **Post-APR / exenteration sexual dysfunction.** Pelvic autonomic nerve injury may contribute, alongside vascular, hormonal and psychosocial factors.
- **Pudendal nerve block.** The needle is directed medial to the ischial spine through the sacrospinous ligament, transvaginally or transperineally, with the spine palpated as the landmark.
- **Obturator-nerve injury** during TOT, paravaginal repair or obturator LND produces medial-thigh sensory loss and adductor weakness.
- **Lithotomy-position neuropathy.** Common peroneal, femoral and lateral femoral cutaneous nerves may be injured by stirrup pressure, deep retractors or hip positioning. Padding and periodic positioning checks are used to prevent it.
- **Superior hypogastric plexus block / presacral neurectomy.** These are selected procedures for central midline pelvic pain such as dysmenorrhea or central endometriosis, and they do not address lateral or vulvar pain.
- **Ganglion impar block.** The ganglion impar lies near the sacrococcygeal junction and is not the inferior hypogastric or pelvic plexus; a ganglion impar block is used in selected perineal or coccygeal pain.
- **Sacral neuromodulation (SNM).** Usually accesses a sacral foramen, often S3, and is an option for selected refractory OAB, non-obstructive urinary retention and fecal incontinence ([AUA OAB guidance](https://www.auanet.org/documents/Guidelines/PDF/2024%20Guidelines/OAB%202024%20Unabridgedv2.pdf); [AUA retention statement](https://www.auanet.org/advocacy/best-practice-statements-and-whitepapers/chronic-urinary-retention); [ASCRS fecal-incontinence guidance](https://www.ascrsu.com/ascrs/view/ASCRS-Evidence-Based-Guidelines-and-Expert-Consensus/3982020/all/Management%20of%20Fecal%20Incontinence%20%282023%29)). Its mechanism is incompletely resolved, and chronic pelvic pain is not a routine indication.
- **Neurogenic bladder phenotypes by lesion level.** Suprapontine lesions typically produce overactivity with preserved coordination, suprasacral spinal lesions add detrusor-sphincter dyssynergia, and sacral or cauda-equina lesions produce an acontractile bladder. Actual behavior needs urodynamic evaluation.
