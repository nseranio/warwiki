# The Perineum

The perineum is the region inferior to the pelvic diaphragm at the pelvic outlet. This is a source-bounded anatomy orientation, not an operative guide, diagnostic framework, or management protocol. Anatomy and neurovascular branching vary; use procedure-specific references and current specialty guidance for clinical decisions.

## Regional organization

The perineum is commonly described as a diamond-shaped region bounded anterolaterally by the ischiopubic rami and posterolaterally by the sacrotuberous ligaments. A line joining the ischial tuberosities divides it into an anterior **urogenital triangle** and posterior **anal triangle**.

The urogenital triangle contains structures related to the urethra and external genitalia. The anal triangle contains the anal canal and adjacent ischioanal spaces. The perineal body is a midline fibromuscular convergence point between these regions and provides attachment for multiple pelvic-floor and perineal structures.

## Perineal spaces and membrane

The perineal membrane separates the urogenital triangle into superficial and deep perineal spaces. The **superficial perineal space** is inferior to the membrane; the **deep perineal space** is superior to it and inferior to the pelvic diaphragm. These are useful orientation terms, but classical depictions of a discrete, planar “urogenital diaphragm” do not adequately represent the three-dimensional anatomy.

In both sexes, the deep perineal space contains the urethra, components of the external urethral sphincter complex, and neurovascular structures supplying the external genitalia. The male deep space also includes the bulbourethral glands; the female deep space includes the vagina. The superficial space includes muscles associated with erectile structures, with sex-specific external-genital relationships.

## Muscular and fascial relationships

The pelvic diaphragm, chiefly levator ani and coccygeus, forms the superior muscular boundary of the perineal region. Superficial perineal muscles include bulbospongiosus, ischiocavernosus, and superficial transverse perineal muscles. The external anal and urethral sphincter complexes have important regional relationships but should not be reduced to a single flat layer.

The perineal body serves as an attachment point for several muscles and connective-tissue structures. Its detailed composition and its relationship to pelvic support are best considered with sex-specific genital anatomy pages and procedure-specific sources.

## Neurovascular orientation

The internal pudendal vessels and pudendal nerve are important regional structures. The pudendal nerve arises principally from S2–S4, leaves the pelvis through the greater sciatic foramen, returns through the lesser sciatic foramen, and travels in the pudendal canal. Its commonly described branches include inferior rectal, perineal, and dorsal genital branches. Branching patterns and relationships vary.

For a source-bounded overview of the autonomic and somatic pathways, see [Pelvic Neuroanatomy](/docs/foundations/anatomy-physiology/pelvis-support/pelvic-neuroanatomy). Organ-specific vascular anatomy belongs on the relevant genital and urinary-tract pages.
