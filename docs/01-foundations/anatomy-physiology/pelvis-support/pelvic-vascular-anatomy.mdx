# Pelvic Vascular Anatomy

**Pelvic vascular anatomy** covers the arterial pedicles that are controlled in pelvic surgery, the thin-walled venous plexuses that bleed heavily, and the collateral networks that determine ischemic risk after ligation or embolization. Branching patterns, venous connections and the relationship of vessels to the ureter vary, so an average cadaveric distance orients the surgeon and direct identification decides where to dissect, ligate or fix.

For bleeding and repair decisions, see [Vascular Injury & Damage Control](/docs/foundations/surgical-principles/vascular-damage-control); for access, see [Operative Exposure](/docs/foundations/surgical-principles/exposure). Organ-specific companions include [Bladder](/docs/foundations/anatomy-physiology/urinary-tract/bladder-anatomy-physiology), [Prostate](/docs/foundations/anatomy-physiology/genitalia/prostate-seminal-vesicle), [Uterus](/docs/foundations/anatomy-physiology/genitalia/uterus) and [Vagina](/docs/foundations/anatomy-physiology/genitalia/vaginal-anatomy).

## Arterial supply

The common iliac artery divides near the pelvic brim into the external and internal iliac arteries; its vertebral level and relation to the ureter are variable. The external iliac passes beneath the inguinal ligament to become the femoral artery. The internal iliac supplies much of the pelvic viscera, pelvic wall, buttock and perineum.

The familiar anterior and posterior division pattern is a useful starting map, but shared trunks, absent branches and origins from another named artery occur. Anterior branches supply both visceral and parietal territories. The origins of the prostatic, vesical, vaginal, uterine and obturator arteries are confirmed at surgery rather than assumed from their names.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[2]](#ref2)</sup>

### Usual internal iliac branches

| Branch or group | Main territory and operative relationship |
|---|---|
| **Superior vesical** | Usually arises from the patent proximal umbilical artery and supplies the superior bladder. The superior vesical pedicle is controlled early in cystectomy. |
| **Inferior vesical / prostatic branches** | Supply the bladder base, prostate and adjacent structures in men. Origins and pedicle relationships vary. The cavernous nerves run beside the prostatic pedicle, not within the artery. |
| **Vaginal branches** | Supply the vagina and contribute to adjacent bladder/urethral circulation; communicate with uterine and pudendal branches. |
| **Uterine** | Supplies the uterus and cervix with ascending, descending and adnexal connections. Crosses above the ureter near the cervix. |
| **Middle rectal** | Variable contribution to the rectum, sometimes absent; communicates with superior and inferior rectal territories. |
| **Obturator** | Reaches the medial thigh through the obturator canal. It may arise from, or communicate with, the external iliac/inferior epigastric system over the superior pubic ramus. |
| **Internal pudendal** | Leaves below piriformis through the greater sciatic foramen, passes around the ischial spine/sacrospinous ligament and re-enters through the lesser sciatic foramen. Its branches supply the perineum, erectile tissues and lower anal canal. |
| **Inferior gluteal** | Usually an anterior-division branch, exiting below piriformis to the buttock. |
| **Iliolumbar** | Usually posterior division; supplies iliacus, psoas and adjacent lumbar structures. |
| **Lateral sacral** | Usually posterior division; supplies sacral and foraminal structures. |
| **Superior gluteal** | Usually posterior division; exits above piriformis to the gluteal region. |

The pudendal course and vulvovaginal anastomoses are described in the reconstructive anatomy review; the corona mortis requires separate attention below.<sup>[[2]](#ref2)[[3]](#ref3)</sup>

### Arteries entering from outside the internal iliac system

- **Gonadal arteries:** usually arise from the abdominal aorta. The ovarian vessels reach the adnexa in the infundibulopelvic ligament; the testicular vessels descend toward the deep inguinal ring and spermatic cord. The ovarian artery communicates with the uterine artery near the adnexa and uterine cornua.
- **Superior rectal artery:** continuation of the inferior mesenteric artery into the pelvis; supplies the rectum and contributes to the anal cushions through distal branches.
- **Median sacral artery:** usually arises from the posterior distal aorta and descends in front of the sacrum.
- **External pudendal arteries:** arise from the femoral system and supply anterior external genital tissues, with connections to internal pudendal branches. The labial fat pad has both superior external-pudendal and inferior internal-pudendal contributions. The commonly used inferiorly based Martius flap preserves the inferior pedicle and is not an exclusively external-pudendal flap.<sup>[[2]](#ref2)[[4]](#ref4)</sup>

## Relationships that change the dissection

### Ureter and pelvic pedicles

At the pelvic brim, the ureter crosses anterior to the iliac vessels, often around the common iliac bifurcation or external iliac artery. Trace it into the operative field, because the crossing differs between sides. Near the cervix, the uterine artery crosses over the ureter ("water under the bridge"), classically about 1.5–2 cm lateral to the cervix. The distance changes with anatomy, traction, prolapse, masses and previous surgery, so the ureter is identified before the uterine artery is clamped in hysterectomy, uterosacral suspension and deep paravaginal dissection.

Around the prostate, the vascular pedicles and cavernous nerve pathways are close and variable. Division close to the prostate favors nerve preservation but does not guarantee it. The organ-specific dissection plane is used, adjusted for scarring and oncologic requirements.

### Corona mortis

**Corona mortis** describes arterial and/or venous connections between the obturator and external iliac or inferior epigastric systems over the superior pubic ramus. Venous connections are particularly important. The term is not limited to an accessory obturator artery.<sup>[[3]](#ref3)</sup>

A 2020 systematic review of 13 anatomical, imaging and operative studies, comprising 1,455 hemipelves, estimated an overall prevalence near 46%, with marked between-study heterogeneity. The venous type was more frequent than the arterial type (42% vs 25%). A crossing vessel should be anticipated. Bleeding can be rapid and hard to expose in hernia repair, pelvic-fracture packing and Burch colposuspension. Inspect the retropubic surface during Burch, hernia, fracture and other dissections near Cooper's ligament; a vessel that must be divided is clipped and divided before traction tears it.<sup>[[3]](#ref3)</sup>

### Collateral arterial circulation

| Connection | Systems linked |
|---|---|
| Superior ↔ middle/inferior rectal | Inferior mesenteric ↔ internal iliac/pudendal |
| Ovarian ↔ uterine | Aortic gonadal ↔ internal iliac |
| Lumbar ↔ iliolumbar | Aorta ↔ internal iliac |
| Median ↔ lateral sacral | Aorta ↔ internal iliac |
| Gluteal ↔ femoral/circumflex branches | Internal iliac ↔ femoral/profunda circulation |
| Obturator ↔ inferior epigastric/external iliac | Internal ↔ external iliac systems |

This redundancy is why bilateral internal iliac ligation rarely causes pelvic visceral necrosis, and why embolization is sometimes selective rather than proximal. Collateral flow does not make ligation or embolization free of ischemic risk. Shock, atherosclerosis, prior embolization, radiation and previous surgery can reduce the available circulation. Hemorrhage-control decisions are covered in the [vascular management pathway](/docs/foundations/surgical-principles/vascular-damage-control).<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup>

The uterine–ovarian connection matters during embolization, because residual ovarian supply and non-target passage depend on the patient's vascular map. A thermal study in 20 women found that the boundary between uterine and ovarian supply to the cornual area shifts across the cycle, with ovarian supply predominating in the follicular phase and uterine supply in the luteal phase.<sup>[[10]](#ref10)</sup> Whether this affects embolization outcome has not been established.

Related embolization procedures use the same map. Prostatic artery embolization targets prostatic branches, and collateral supply from the inferior vesical, accessory internal pudendal and anterior rectal arteries is a recognized cause of technical failure. Ovarian vein embolization for pelvic congestion targets the left ovarian vein, sometimes with internal iliac tributaries.

## Venous anatomy

Pelvic visceral plexuses communicate extensively with one another, the iliac systems, gonadal veins and vertebral and paravertebral channels. Pelvic veins are thin-walled and plexiform, and many channels lack effective valves, although not every pelvic or gonadal vein is valveless. Venous variants and collateral recruitment can be substantial, especially with obstruction or reflux.<sup>[[5]](#ref5)</sup>

### Vesical and prostatic plexuses

The vesical and prostatic venous plexuses communicate around the bladder and prostate and drain toward the internal iliac system. The deep dorsal penile vein connects with the prostatic plexus; the dorsal venous complex is the dominant bleeding source during anterior and apical prostate dissection. Suture ligation, stapler or athermal division and bulldog clamping have all been used, and timing varies by technique, each with tradeoffs for the urethra and continence.<sup>[[5]](#ref5)</sup>

### Uterine, vaginal and ovarian veins

The uterine and vaginal plexuses communicate across the pelvis and drain through uterine and vaginal veins into the internal iliac systems, with additional connections to the ovarian veins. The right ovarian vein usually drains to the IVC and the left to the left renal vein; duplicated trunks and variant junctions occur. Valves may be absent or incompetent, but cross-pelvic and gonadal–iliac communications are present.<sup>[[5]](#ref5)</sup>

Dilated pelvic veins or a compression on imaging do not alone establish the cause of pelvic pain. Symptoms are correlated with reflux, obstruction and collateral pathways. The left ovarian vein and its drainage into the left renal vein at a near right angle are the anatomic substrate of left-dominant pelvic congestion syndrome. Venous embolization addresses selected abnormal pathways; uterine artery embolization is an arterial treatment.<sup>[[5]](#ref5)</sup>

### Pampiniform and testicular drainage

The pampiniform plexus surrounds the testicular artery within the spermatic cord and participates in heat exchange. The right testicular vein usually drains to the IVC and the left to the left renal vein. These asymmetrical outflow routes, valve function and venous pressure help explain the predominance of left varicocele. The junction angle alone does not diagnose nutcracker syndrome or determine treatment. See [Testicles & Scrotum](/docs/foundations/anatomy-physiology/genitalia/testicles-scrotum).<sup>[[5]](#ref5)</sup>

### Presacral and vertebral plexuses

The presacral network includes median and lateral sacral veins and connections with veins entering the sacral foramina and vertebral system. Injury can cause major hemorrhage, and traction or blind attempts to clamp a retracted vessel can enlarge the defect. In 52 unembalmed female cadavers, the left common iliac vein was the closest vessel cephalad to the sacral promontory midpoint (mean 27 mm, range 9–52 mm) and laterally (mean 22 mm, range 9–35 mm). The middle sacral artery and vein lay a mean 4 mm and 7 mm from the midpoint, and the sacral venous plexuses a mean 34 mm (range 4–86 mm). The authors describe the location of these vessels as highly variable and advise exposing the anterior longitudinal ligament before placing sutures.<sup>[[5]](#ref5)[[6]](#ref6)</sup>

Control starts with directed pressure and exposure, followed by a source-specific repair or hemostatic strategy with help. Instruments are not driven blindly into a sacral foramen. Detailed relationships are in [The Presacral Space](/docs/foundations/anatomy-physiology/pelvis-support/presacral-anatomy); active bleeding management is in [Vascular Injury & Damage Control](/docs/foundations/surgical-principles/vascular-damage-control).

The valveless vertebral venous plexus (Batson's plexus) communicates with pelvic veins and allows bidirectional flow. It offers a potential route for tumor or infection spread, although vertebral metastases need not all follow it.<sup>[[5]](#ref5)</sup>

### Iliac veins

The external iliac vein is generally medial to its artery in the distal pelvis, while more proximal relationships and tributaries vary. The left common iliac vein passes beneath the right common iliac artery; this is the anatomic substrate of May–Thurner syndrome, although an imaging appearance alone does not establish symptomatic disease.<sup>[[5]](#ref5)</sup>

Unexpected tributaries can cross a lateral or presacral dissection. One descriptive study examined 40 iliac venous systems in 20 cadavers and found presacral external-iliac tributaries in 20 systems from 15 cadavers. Its 75% figure refers to cadavers, not to all venous systems or a proven population prevalence. These branches are anticipated during exenteration, sacrectomy, sacrocolpopexy and lateral pelvic surgery.<sup>[[7]](#ref7)</sup>

## Lymphatic orientation

Lymphatic drainage is regional, overlapping and sometimes bilateral. Cancer-specific staging, mapping and previous treatment determine a lymphadenectomy template, and the nodal basins below are orientation only.

| Territory | Useful orientation |
|---|---|
| **Bladder and prostate** | Obturator, external iliac and internal iliac nodes, then common iliac and presacral nodes in extended drainage. |
| **Cervix and uterus** | Obturator, external iliac, internal iliac and parametrial nodes, then common iliac and para-aortic nodes. |
| **Upper vagina** | Internal and external iliac nodes. |
| **Lower vagina and vulva** | Superficial inguinal nodes, then deep inguinal (Cloquet's) and external iliac nodes. |
| **Testis and ovary** | Para-aortic and interaortocaval nodes, following the gonadal vessels. |
| **Rectum and anus** | Above the dentate line, superior rectal and mesorectal nodes toward the inferior mesenteric system; below it, inguinal nodes. |
| **Penis** | Inguinal drainage can be bilateral. In penile cancer, the usual progression is superficial then deep inguinal nodes, followed by ipsilateral pelvic nodes; the glans does not routinely bypass the groin. |
| **Male anterior urethra** | Superficial/deep inguinal drainage, then pelvic nodes; use the lesion's exact location in staging. |
| **Male posterior urethra** | Pelvic nodal drainage. |
| **Female urethra** | Proximal third drains toward pelvic nodes; distal two-thirds toward superficial/deep inguinal nodes. |

The penile and urethral rows follow the current EAU guidelines.<sup>[[8]](#ref8)[[9]](#ref9)</sup> The remaining rows are standard anatomy-text descriptions. Gonadal drainage toward retroperitoneal nodes differs from the predominantly inguinal drainage of external genital skin. See the organ-specific anatomy pages for further detail.
