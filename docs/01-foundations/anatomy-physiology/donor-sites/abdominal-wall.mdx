# The Abdominal Wall

The **abdominal wall** is the access path to every intra-abdominal pelvic operation, the donor site of the major pelvic reconstructive flaps, and the site of the commonest postoperative complications: wound infection, fascial dehiscence, incisional hernia and trocar-site hernia. The layered anatomy determines how to enter the abdomen safely (midline, Pfannenstiel, Gibson or Cherney), how to avoid the ilioinguinal and iliohypogastric nerves during Pfannenstiel incision or laparoscopic port placement, how to design and pedicle a VRAM or SIEA flap for perineal reconstruction, where to site a stoma, and how to reconstruct the wall after large defects.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup>

See also [The Retropubic Space](/docs/foundations/anatomy-physiology/pelvis-support/retropubic-anatomy) for the preperitoneal space behind the pubis; [Pelvic Vascular Anatomy](/docs/foundations/anatomy-physiology/pelvis-support/pelvic-vascular-anatomy) for the inferior epigastric and iliac systems; [Pelvic Neuroanatomy](/docs/foundations/anatomy-physiology/pelvis-support/pelvic-neuroanatomy) for ilioinguinal / iliohypogastric / genitofemoral nerves; and [The Perineum](/docs/foundations/anatomy-physiology/pelvis-support/perineum) for the VRAM-flap recipient bed.

## Regional Divisions

The abdominal wall consists of three continuous regions that behave as one mechanical unit:

- **Anterior abdominal wall.** Costal margin superiorly, iliac crest inferiorly, linea alba medially, linea semilunaris laterally
- **Lateral abdominal wall.** The three flat muscles (external oblique, internal oblique, transversus abdominis)
- **Posterior abdominal wall.** Psoas major, quadratus lumborum, iliacus; bounded medially by lumbar vertebrae, laterally by the transverse abdominal aponeurosis

## Layered Anatomy of the Anterior Wall

From superficial to deep there are nine layers, operatively organized into two "containers" (superficial fascia and muscle complex) separated by the rectus sheath:

| Layer | Notes | Operative relevance |
|---|---|---|
| **Skin** | Thin, mobile | Langer-line orientation dictates scar quality |
| **Camper's fascia** | Fatty superficial layer | Haematoma plane; lipoabdominoplasty territory |
| **Scarpa's fascia** | Membranous; continuous with dartos and Colles' | A potential plane of **Fournier's gangrene** spread; whether to reapproximate it at closure depends on the incision and wound |
| **External oblique aponeurosis** | Anterior rectus sheath | Pfannenstiel, lateral trocar sites |
| **Internal oblique aponeurosis** | Bilaminar — contributes to anterior + posterior sheath above arcuate line | TAP-plane layer |
| **Transversus abdominis aponeurosis** | Posterior rectus sheath above arcuate line; anterior only below | **TAR** (transversus abdominis release) target in posterior component separation |
| **Transversalis fascia** | Innermost endoabdominal fascia | The layer that **contains the myopectineal orifice of Fruchaud** — failure here produces groin hernias<sup>[[2]](#ref2)</sup> |
| **Preperitoneal fat** | With "fatty trident" geometry anteriorly | Space of Retzius + spaces of Bogros — preperitoneal mesh placement<sup>[[3]](#ref3)</sup> |
| **Peritoneum** | Serosa | Intraperitoneal plane |

### The rectus sheath and the arcuate line

The rectus sheath encloses the rectus abdominis. In the conventional model its composition changes at the arcuate line (linea semicircularis), often described in the lower third between the umbilicus and pubis. Its level and appearance vary: the transition can be gradual or doubled, so a fixed surface measurement should not substitute for identifying the actual fascial planes:<sup>[[4]](#ref4)[[5]](#ref5)[[6]](#ref6)</sup>

| Level | Anterior rectus sheath | Posterior rectus sheath |
|---|---|---|
| **Above arcuate line** | External oblique + anterior lamina of internal oblique | Posterior lamina of internal oblique + transversus abdominis |
| **Below arcuate line, in the conventional pattern** | External + internal oblique + transversus (all three pass anteriorly) | Posterior aponeurotic sheath attenuates or ends; transversalis fascia and peritoneum remain behind rectus |

Clinical consequences:

- **Below the usual arcuate transition, the posterior aponeurotic sheath is reduced or absent**; the transversalis fascia remains, but individual anatomy varies. Secure fascial closure matters for suprapubic incisions.
- The retromuscular (retrorectus) plane is available above the arcuate line for the Rives-Stoppa repair and related hernia operations.
- The inferior epigastric artery ascends from the external iliac system toward the deep surface of rectus and enters the sheath near the arcuate region; its course should be identified rather than inferred from a fixed arcuate-line level.<sup>[[6]](#ref6)[[7]](#ref7)</sup>

### Linea alba

Midline fibrous raphe formed by the decussation of the aponeuroses from xiphoid to pubic symphysis. Widest at the umbilicus (approximately 1–2 cm). Disruption produces rectus diastasis (postpartum, obesity-related) and midline incisional hernia.

### Linea semilunaris

Lateral border of the rectus sheath. It is the landmark for component separation (both anterior oblique release and posterior TAR) and for Spigelian hernia (at the junction of the semilunar and arcuate lines).<sup>[[8]](#ref8)</sup>

## Muscles

| Muscle | Fiber direction | Function (besides core) |
|---|---|---|
| **Rectus abdominis** | Vertical | Trunk flexion; vascular pedicle for **VRAM flap** |
| **External oblique** | Inferomedially ("hands in pockets") | Contralateral trunk rotation |
| **Internal oblique** | Superomedially | Ipsilateral rotation |
| **Transversus abdominis** | Horizontal | Contributes to active expiration and intra-abdominal pressure; forms the deep boundary of the TAP-block plane; target of TAR |

The three lateral muscles act as a composite laminate: oblique fiber orientations in adjacent layers allow force transfer and increase passive load-bearing. This is the mechanical basis of intra-abdominal pressure generation during Valsalva, cough, and parturition.

### Transversus abdominis plane (TAP)

Between internal oblique and transversus abdominis. It contains the segmental nerves (T6–L1) and a rich vascular plexus.<sup>[[9]](#ref9)</sup> It is the target of the TAP block, a regional anesthetic technique widely used after cesarean section, open urologic surgery, and abdominal wall reconstruction.

## Vascular Supply — Reconstructive Pedicles

### Deep inferior epigastric artery (DIEA)

**The dominant vessel for abdominal-wall flaps:**<sup>[[10]](#ref10)</sup>
- Branch of the external iliac artery.
- Ascends deep to rectus and enters the sheath near the variable arcuate region.
- Gives musculocutaneous perforators to the overlying skin. In a 20-cadaver microdissection study, the large perforators (>0.5 mm; mean 5.4 per specimen) lay mostly lateral and inferior to the umbilicus, at a mean distance of 4 cm from it, and the lateral branch was dominant in 80% of specimens (El-Mrakby and Milner 2002).<sup>[[11]](#ref11)</sup>
- Dominant vascular pedicle of:
  - **VRAM (vertical rectus abdominis myocutaneous) flap.** Used for perineal reconstruction after APR, pelvic exenteration, radical vulvectomy, or extensive Fournier's debridement.
  - **Free TRAM (transverse rectus abdominis myocutaneous) flap.** The free flap uses the DIEA for breast reconstruction; the conventional pedicled TRAM uses the superior epigastric vessels.<sup>[[29]](#ref29)</sup>
  - **DIEP (deep inferior epigastric perforator) flap.** Muscle-sparing perforator-based free flap.

### Superficial inferior epigastric artery (SIEA)

- Branch of the femoral artery.
- Supplies the subcutaneous tissue of the lower abdomen without entering the muscle.
- Pedicle of the SIEA flap for perineal or groin reconstruction when a muscle-sparing approach is preferred. SIEA suitability varies because the artery may be absent or too small. An older series reported the SIEA absent in 35%; a cadaver study found it in 20 of 22 dissections with a mean caliber of 1.9 mm; and in a prospective breast-reconstruction series an SIEA flap could be used in only 30% of cases because the artery was absent or too small (Reardon 2004; Chevray 2004).<sup>[[12]](#ref12)[[13]](#ref13)</sup>

### Superior epigastric artery

- Terminal branch of the internal thoracic artery.
- Supplies the upper rectus; anastomoses within the rectus muscle with the DIEA above the umbilicus, which is the physiologic basis of TRAM-flap survival.<sup>[[10]](#ref10)</sup>

### Deep circumflex iliac and intercostal / lumbar arteries

- Supply the lateral abdominal wall.
- The deep circumflex iliac artery ascends laterally and contributes to flank skin. It is the pedicle of the DCIA osteocutaneous flap (iliac-crest bone grafts).

### Venous and lymphatic drainage

Parallels arteries. Paraumbilical veins connect to the portal system via the ligamentum teres and are enlarged in portal hypertension as caput medusae. Lymphatic drainage of the lower abdominal wall follows the inguinal basin; the upper wall drains to axillary and parasternal chains.

## Innervation

### Thoracolumbar segmental nerves (T6–L1)

- Enter the TAP plane between internal oblique and transversus abdominis.
- Branch extensively within the TAP, with lateral perforators to skin and anterior branches to the rectus sheath.<sup>[[9]](#ref9)</sup>
- **T10 innervates the umbilicus.** It is the constant dermatomal landmark.
- **T6–T9** supply upper abdominal wall.
- **T11–L1** supply the lower abdominal wall and rectus muscle.

### Ilioinguinal and iliohypogastric nerves (L1)

Although often labeled L1 nerves, spinal contributions vary: in a 200-side cadaver study, the ilioinguinal nerve arose from L1 alone on 130 sides (65%), and the iliohypogastric nerve also received T11 or T12 contributions.<sup>[[36]](#ref36)</sup> Both traverse the lower lateral abdominal wall, but their course relative to the ASIS and muscle layers varies:<sup>[[7]](#ref7)</sup><br/>
- **Iliohypogastric.** Lateral cutaneous branch to the gluteal skin and anterior cutaneous branch to the suprapubic skin.
- **Ilioinguinal.** Traverses the inguinal canal anterior to the cord / round ligament; supplies the upper medial thigh, mons, anterior scrotum / labia majora.

**Avoiding ilioinguinal / iliohypogastric injury.** Both nerves are at risk during Pfannenstiel incisions (especially lateral extension), lateral trocar placement for laparoscopic urology, and low transverse gynecologic incisions. Their courses vary: map the planned incision or port against the ASIS, rectus border, inferior epigastric vessels, prior scars, and visible structures rather than treating one distance from the midline as a universal safe zone.<sup>[[7]](#ref7)</sup> Avoid unnecessary lateral fascial extension. Injury can contribute to chronic groin pain; persistent pain after hernia repair needs assessment of neuropathic and non-neuropathic causes before any selected neurectomy.<sup>[[14]](#ref14)</sup>

### Genitofemoral nerve (L1–L2)

Travels on the surface of the psoas; gives the genital branch (cremaster motor in men, sensation to anterior scrotum / labia) that traverses the internal ring into the inguinal canal, and the femoral branch to skin over the upper anterior thigh.

### Subcostal nerve (T12)

Supplies the most inferior flank skin and the lowest rectus fibers.

## Incisions — The Urologic and Urogynecologic Menu

| Incision | Trajectory | Reconstructive uses | Comments |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Lower midline** | Xiphoid-to-pubis midline through linea alba | Open radical prostatectomy, cystectomy, complex ureteral reconstruction, exploratory | Simple, extensile, through the relatively avascular linea alba; higher incisional-hernia rate than transverse |
| **Pfannenstiel** | Transverse ~2 fingerbreadths above pubis; skin incision transverse, fascia opened transversely with rectus sheaths separated off the underlying rectus muscle | Cesarean, hysterectomy, open abdominal sacrocolpopexy, Burch, pelvic LN dissection, retropubic prostatectomy (alternative) | Cosmetically superior; risk of ilioinguinal / iliohypogastric nerve injury with lateral extension |
| **Cherney** | Transverse with **division of the rectus tendons** from the pubis | Larger cesarean or gynecologic cancer exposure; wide access to bladder and lateral pelvis | Better pelvic exposure than Pfannenstiel; tendons must be reattached at closure |
| **Maylard** | Transverse with **transection of the rectus muscles** | Wide pelvic exposure, radical pelvic surgery | Must identify and ligate the inferior epigastric vessels before muscle transection |
| **Gibson** | Oblique, parallel to inguinal ligament | Renal transplant, ureteric reimplantation, distal ureterolithotomy | Muscle-splitting extraperitoneal approach to iliac vessels and distal ureter |
| **Flank (modified Foley, 11th or 12th rib)** | Subcostal, obliquely through three flat muscles | Open nephrectomy, nephroureterectomy, large retroperitoneal mass | Can produce flank bulge from iliohypogastric / subcostal nerve denervation ("post-nephrectomy flank deformity") |
| **Chevron / Rooftop** | Bilateral subcostal | Major retroperitoneal / transplant access | Rare in urology; used when massive retroperitoneal exposure is required |

## Port Placement for Laparoscopic / Robotic Urology

A few recurring principles:

- **Camera entry** is often umbilical, but prior surgery, hernia, abdominal distension, scars and expected adhesions may require a different entry site or technique; left-upper-quadrant entry (Palmer's point) also has contraindications and requires its own safety assessment.
- **Working ports** should support instrument reach and triangulation for the planned procedure; spacing depends on patient habitus, platform and target anatomy.
- **Plan lateral ports around the patient's visible anatomy**, avoiding the ilioinguinal / iliohypogastric nerve courses and the inferior epigastric vessels; no fixed midline distance is a universal safe zone.<sup>[[7]](#ref7)</sup>
- **Avoid the inferior epigastric vessels.** Use direct visualization and, when useful, transillumination to identify their course rather than assuming it from a fixed surface landmark. Trocar injury can cause bleeding or rectus-sheath hematoma.
- **Consider fascial closure for ports ≥10 mm**, especially umbilical or single-incision sites, to reduce trocar-site hernia risk.<sup>[[15]](#ref15)</sup> The European/American Hernia Societies rate this a weak suggestion because direct closure-outcome evidence is very limited.<sup>[[16]](#ref16)</sup>

## Inguinal Region — A Quick Reference

### Inguinal canal
Approximately 4 cm oblique passage; deep (internal) ring lateral to the inferior epigastric vessels, superficial (external) ring superior to the pubic tubercle.<sup>[[2]](#ref2)</sup>

Contents:
- Men: spermatic cord (vas, testicular artery, pampiniform plexus, cremasteric vessels, genital branch of genitofemoral nerve)
- Women: round ligament of the uterus
- Both: ilioinguinal nerve (anterior to the cord or round ligament)

### Hesselbach's triangle
Site of direct inguinal hernia. Boundaries:
- Medial: lateral border of rectus abdominis
- Lateral: inferior epigastric vessels
- Inferior: inguinal ligament

### Myopectineal orifice of Fruchaud
The entire aperture in the lower abdominal wall through which all groin hernias (indirect inguinal, direct inguinal, and femoral) traverse. It is contained only by transversalis fascia; the principle of preperitoneal mesh coverage (Stoppa, TEP, TAPP) is to reinforce the entire orifice.<sup>[[2]](#ref2)[[3]](#ref3)[[17]](#ref17)</sup>

### Hernia subtypes at a glance

| Type | Relationship to inferior epigastric vessels |
|---|---|
| **Indirect inguinal** | Lateral; follows the path of testicular descent through the deep ring |
| **Direct inguinal** | Medial (through Hesselbach's triangle) |
| **Femoral** | Below the inguinal ligament; medial to the femoral vein; strangulates readily due to rigid boundaries |

## Posterior Abdominal Wall (Brief)

Relevant mostly for retroperitoneal access and psoas-hitch reconstruction.

- **Psoas major.** Origins from T12–L5; inserts on lesser trochanter. When needed for a tension-free distal ureteral reimplant, a psoas hitch secures the mobilized bladder toward the ipsilateral psoas tendon.<sup>[[18]](#ref18)</sup> Avoid entrapment of the genitofemoral nerve on the muscle's anterior surface; see the dedicated reconstruction technique for operative details.
- **Quadratus lumborum.** Lateral to psoas; flank stabilizer; target of the QL block for post-op analgesia in urologic surgery.
- **Iliacus.** Iliac fossa; joins psoas as iliopsoas.

Named lumbar triangles are rare hernia sites:

- **Superior (Grynfeltt-Lesshaft).** The most common; bounded by 12th rib, quadratus lumborum, and internal oblique.
- **Inferior (Petit).** Bounded by iliac crest, latissimus dorsi, and external oblique.

## Physiology — The Core, Breathing, and IAP

### Respiration

In six seated volunteers, transversus abdominis showed phasic expiratory activity at ventilations of 10–18 L/min during hyperoxic hypercapnia before external oblique or rectus abdominis activity was recorded. During inspiratory elastic loading, it was active in six of six subjects, compared with one of six for external oblique and two of six for rectus abdominis; internal oblique was not recorded.<sup>[[19]](#ref19)</sup>

Transversus abdominis contraction contributes to active expiration by raising intra-abdominal pressure and displacing the diaphragm cephalad. Its recruitment varies with respiratory load.<sup>[[19]](#ref19)</sup>

### Intra-abdominal pressure

Modulated by the abdominal-wall complex for:
- Defecation and micturition (Valsalva)
- Parturition (expulsive efforts)
- Cough, sneeze, vomit
- **Core stability and spinal protection** during lifting

### Abdominal compartment syndrome

Sustained intra-abdominal pressure >20 mmHg with new organ dysfunction.<sup>[[20]](#ref20)</sup> It occurs in the urologic context after:
- Extensive pelvic packing for hemorrhage
- Massive transfusion / resuscitation in polytrauma
- Retroperitoneal hematoma (pelvic fracture, post-biopsy, ruptured AAA)

Management includes protocolized intra-abdominal-pressure monitoring in at-risk critically ill patients and decompressive laparotomy for overt abdominal compartment syndrome when indicated (WSACS 2013).<sup>[[20]](#ref20)</sup>

## Abdominal-Wall Planes for Mesh / Reconstruction (ICAP Terminology)

The International Classification of Abdominal Wall Planes (ICAP), a 20-surgeon Delphi consensus, standardizes the terms for mesh placement. The table lists the commonly used planes; ICAP also names inlay (within the defect) and retromuscular planes.<sup>[[21]](#ref21)</sup> For midline incisional hernia, the 2023 EHS guideline strongly recommends retromuscular mesh (very low-certainty evidence) and advises caution with mesh exposed to viscera because it can complicate later abdominal surgery.<sup>[[33]](#ref33)</sup>

| Plane | Location | Common uses |
|---|---|---|
| **Onlay** | Superficial to anterior rectus sheath | Small ventral hernias |
| **Anterectus** | Between anterior sheath and rectus | Rarely used |
| **Retrorectus (Rives-Stoppa)** | Between rectus and posterior sheath | Midline ventral hernias |
| **Preperitoneal** | Between transversalis fascia and peritoneum | TEP / TAPP inguinal repair; Stoppa |
| **Intraperitoneal (IPOM)** | Inside peritoneum | Laparoscopic ventral-hernia repair<sup>[[22]](#ref22)</sup> |
| **Interoblique** | Between external and internal oblique | Rarely used |
| **Retro-oblique (TAP)** | Between internal oblique and transversus; neurovascular plane | Selected mesh placement, distinct from the retromuscular TAR plane |
| **Transversalis fascial** | Between transversus or posterior sheath and transversalis fascia | Distinct from the retromuscular TAR plane |

### Component separation

- **Anterior component separation (Ramirez).** Release of the external oblique aponeurosis lateral to the linea semilunaris; allows medial advancement of the rectus, internal oblique and transversus complex.<sup>[[8]](#ref8)[[23]](#ref23)</sup>
- **Posterior component separation, TAR (Novitsky).** Division of the transversus abdominis muscle medial to the linea semilunaris, then dissection between transversus and transversalis fascia; creates a large retromuscular / preperitoneal pocket for mesh. Used for large defects and after prior anterior release.<sup>[[8]](#ref8)</sup>

## Clinical Correlations for the Reconstructive Urologist

- **VRAM flap for perineal reconstruction.** After APR, pelvic exenteration, radical vulvectomy, radiated perineal wound, or Fournier's reconstruction. Pedicled on the DIEA; muscle and overlying skin rotated through a transabdominal or transpelvic tunnel to the perineum. Must preserve at least one rectus for core integrity; unilateral harvest is the norm. A systematic review of 65 studies (1,827 patients) after exenterative pelvic surgery reported mean VRAM donor-site morbidity of 15%, including abdominal dehiscence in 5.5% and incisional hernia in 3.3%.<sup>[[39]](#ref39)</sup>
- **SIEA / DIEP / TRAM.** SIEA and DIEP spare rectus muscle; conventional TRAM harvests rectus muscle, although muscle-sparing TRAM variants preserve more of it.<sup>[[30]](#ref30)</sup> Careful preoperative vascular mapping with CT angiography.
- **Stoma siting.** An ileal conduit is often placed in the right abdomen; a catheterizable channel may be umbilical or elsewhere, and a colostomy is often left-sided. The exact site is chosen with the patient and a qualified ostomy clinician or surgeon. For a pouching stoma, seek a flat, visible surface within the rectus borders when feasible; assess sitting and standing contours and avoid creases, scars, belt lines, and bony prominences. The anterior axillary line is not a default transrectus site.<sup>[[24]](#ref24)</sup>
- **Post-nephrectomy flank bulge.** Denervation of the subcostal or iliohypogastric nerves after a flank incision can produce a bulge without a fascial defect, but a true incisional hernia also needs consideration. In a 70-patient survey after flank or thoracoabdominal nephrectomy incision, 34/70 reported a bulge persisting more than 1 year and 24% reported persistent flank pain.<sup>[[31]](#ref31)</sup> CT features may help distinguish denervation from a hernia.<sup>[[32]](#ref32)</sup>
- **Rectus diastasis.** Postpartum or obesity-related separation of the rectus muscles at the linea alba (&gt;2 cm by the EHS definition) may be associated with core instability or impaired body image. The EHS weakly suggests considering physiotherapy before surgery; if surgery is chosen, it suggests linea alba plication (with or without mesh augmentation) without a concomitant midline hernia and mesh-based repair with one, although plication alone may suffice for a concomitant umbilical or epigastric hernia &lt;1 cm.<sup>[[35]](#ref35)</sup>
- **Incisional hernia after open cystectomy / transplant / major urologic surgery.** Risk is higher with midline than transverse incisions. In the STITCH randomized trial (545 patients followed to 1 year), small-bites fascial closure was followed by incisional hernia in 13% versus 21% with large bites (adjusted OR 0.52; 95% CI 0.31–0.87).<sup>[[25]](#ref25)</sup> Follow-up of the randomized cohort estimated 13-year cumulative incisional-hernia incidence of 34% versus 49% (HR 0.61; 95% CI 0.43–0.86), with median time to censoring of 8 years; repair rates were similar.<sup>[[34]](#ref34)</sup> Recurrent or large defects often require component separation.<sup>[[23]](#ref23)</sup>
- **Trocar-site hernia after laparoscopic urology.** Umbilical and ≥10-mm ports are higher-risk locations; fascial closure is suggested for ≥10-mm ports, especially umbilical or single-incision sites, while the direct evidence for prevention remains very limited.<sup>[[15]](#ref15)[[16]](#ref16)</sup>
- **Inguinal orchiectomy approach.** For a suspicious intratesticular mass, the standard oncologic operation uses an inguinal approach with high spermatic-cord control;<sup>[[26]](#ref26)</sup> the precise incision and dissection follow the patient's anatomy and the dedicated testicular-cancer guidance (see [The Testicles & Scrotum](/docs/foundations/anatomy-physiology/genitalia/testicles-scrotum)).
- **Post-herniorrhaphy neuropathic pain.** The ilioinguinal, iliohypogastric, and genital branch of genitofemoral nerves may be involved. Persistent pain warrants specialist, multidisciplinary assessment; selective or triple neurectomy is an option for carefully selected refractory patients, not an automatic definitive treatment.<sup>[[14]](#ref14)[[27]](#ref27)</sup>
- **Abdominal compartment syndrome after pelvic packing.** In at-risk critically ill patients, measure and manage intra-abdominal pressure; overt syndrome (sustained pressure >20 mmHg with new organ dysfunction) may require decompressive laparotomy.<sup>[[20]](#ref20)</sup>
- **Rectus sheath hematoma.** Injury to the inferior epigastric vessels during trocar placement or paracentesis, or bleeding within the rectus sheath in patients receiving anticoagulation<sup>[[38]](#ref38)</sup>; may present as a painful palpable mass below the umbilicus; Fothergill sign is persistence and fixation of the palpable rectus-sheath mass during rectus contraction.<sup>[[37]](#ref37)</sup> Usually managed conservatively; large or expanding hematomas may need angioembolization of the DIEA.
- **Groin bulge after appendectomy.** A later right groin bulge merits examination for inguinal hernia and other causes. A patent processus vaginalis may coexist in children; denervation after an open incision has also been proposed.<sup>[[28]](#ref28)</sup>
