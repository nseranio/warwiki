# Bowel Anatomy

Small and large bowel may be used in urinary diversion, augmentation, continent catheterizable channels, and selected genital reconstruction. Safe selection and reconstruction depend on the segment's mesentery and blood supply, its absorptive and secretory function, the length removed from gastrointestinal continuity, and the anticipated duration of urine contact.[[1]](#ref1)[[2]](#ref2)

This page is a general anatomic and physiologic orientation. It does not prescribe a bowel segment, length, vascular pedicle, bowel preparation, anastomosis, reservoir configuration, postoperative irrigation, surveillance interval, or management plan.

See also [Urinary Diversion Principles](/docs/surgical-techniques/04c-urinary-diversion/urinary-diversion-principles), [Augmentation Principles](/docs/surgical-techniques/04b-bladder-reconstruction/augmentation-principles), [Catheterizable Channels](/docs/surgical-techniques/04b-bladder-reconstruction/catheterizable-channels), [Mucus Management](/docs/foundations/pharmacology/urinary-diversion-specific/mucus-management), and [Vitamin B12 Supplementation](/docs/foundations/pharmacology/urinary-diversion-specific/vitamin-b12-supplementation).

## Basic organization

The small intestine extends from the pylorus to the ileocecal junction and includes duodenum, jejunum, and ileum. The colon continues from cecum through ascending, transverse, descending, and sigmoid portions to rectum. The terminal ileum meets the cecum at the ileocecal valve.[[1]](#ref1)[[3]](#ref3)

Jejunum and ileum are intraperitoneal and suspended by mesentery. Much of the duodenum and the ascending and descending colon are secondarily retroperitoneal; transverse and sigmoid colon have mesenteries. These relationships affect operative exposure and mobility but do not, by themselves, determine suitability for reconstruction.[[1]](#ref1)[[3]](#ref3)

The intestinal wall contains mucosa, submucosa, muscularis propria, and serosa or adventitia depending on location. The myenteric plexus lies between the circular and longitudinal muscle layers and participates in motility.[[1]](#ref1)

## Vascular and neural orientation

The superior mesenteric artery supplies jejunum, ileum, cecum, ascending colon, and proximal transverse colon; the inferior mesenteric artery supplies distal transverse colon, descending colon, sigmoid colon, and upper rectum. Mesenteric vascular patterns and collateral pathways vary. Any bowel reconstruction requires direct assessment of perfusion and the planned mesenteric reach rather than reliance on a generalized map.[[3]](#ref3)

The small bowel and proximal colon receive parasympathetic input through the vagus; distal colon receives pelvic splanchnic input. Enteric neural networks continue to coordinate local motility and secretion within bowel tissue.[[1]](#ref1)

## Segment physiology relevant to reconstruction

The ileum absorbs bile salts and vitamin B12. Small and large bowel retain absorptive and secretory properties when exposed to urine; metabolic effects vary with the segment used, surface area, urine-contact time, renal and liver function, and other patient factors. Ileal and colonic urinary segments can be associated with hyperchloremic metabolic acidosis and nutritional or electrolyte effects, while the risk profile is not uniform across diversion types or patients.[[1]](#ref1)[[2]](#ref2)

Bowel used in the urinary tract also continues to produce mucus. The clinical significance of mucus, bacteriuria, electrolyte changes, stone risk, and long-term malignancy risk depends on the reconstruction and patient context; this overview does not set treatment thresholds or surveillance schedules.[[2]](#ref2)

## Reconstructive context

Ileal, ileocolonic, and colonic segments have each been used for different urinary reconstructions. Segment choice must balance the required urinary function with residual bowel function, prior bowel operations, radiation or inflammatory disease, vascular anatomy, renal function, malignancy context, and the patient's ability to participate in long-term care.[[2]](#ref2)

Named procedures such as an ileal conduit, continent cutaneous reservoir, orthotopic neobladder, augmentation cystoplasty, and appendiceal or ileal catheterizable channel are covered in their dedicated pages. A general anatomy page cannot establish an indication or a technically appropriate reconstruction for an individual.

## Clinical boundaries

- Do not infer safe resection lengths, nutritional replacement needs, acid-base management, infection treatment, irrigation regimen, or cancer surveillance from this page.
- Prior radiation, inflammatory bowel disease, short-bowel risk, renal dysfunction, prior surgery, vascular disease, and the planned urinary reconstruction can materially change risk.
- New abdominal pain, obstructive symptoms, persistent vomiting, fever, altered stoma output, dehydration, or postoperative wound concerns need timely clinical assessment.
