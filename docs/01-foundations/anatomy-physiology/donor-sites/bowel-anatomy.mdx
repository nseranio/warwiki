# Bowel Anatomy

The **bowel** is the main reconstructive material for many urologic operations, more than it is an organ of digestion: ileal conduits and neobladders, continent cutaneous reservoirs built from the ileocecal segment or sigmoid, catheterizable channels from appendix or reconfigured ileum (Monti), augmentation cystoplasty, vaginal reconstruction with sigmoid or ileum, antegrade continence enema (MACE) stomas, and posterior perineal flaps from gracilis or VRAM.

See also [The Anal Canal](/docs/foundations/anatomy-physiology/pelvis-support/anal-anatomy); [The Presacral Space](/docs/foundations/anatomy-physiology/pelvis-support/presacral-anatomy) for mesorectum and TME plane; [Pelvic Vascular Anatomy](/docs/foundations/anatomy-physiology/pelvis-support/pelvic-vascular-anatomy) for SMA and IMA trunks; and the perioperative article on [Postoperative Constipation and Ileus](/docs/foundations/perioperative-care/postoperative-management/constipation) for ERAS bowel-function recovery.

## Gross Anatomy

### Small intestine (~6–7 m)

| Segment | Length | Key operative features |
|---|---|---|
| **Duodenum** | ~25 cm, mostly retroperitoneal (except D1) | Receives pancreatic duct and CBD at ampulla of Vater; the **ligament of Treitz** marks the DJ junction; rarely used for urinary reconstruction |
| **Jejunum** | ~2.5 m | Long vasa recta, few vascular arcades, wider lumen, thicker wall, prominent plicae circulares; rarely used reconstructively (metabolic complications when used for diversion) |
| **Ileum** | ~3.5 m | Short vasa recta, multiple vascular arcades, narrower lumen, thinner wall, fewer folds; **terminal ileum** absorbs vitamin B12 and bile salts; Peyer's patches; the most commonly used segment for urinary reconstruction<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup> |

### Large intestine (~1.5 m)

- **Cecum and terminal ileum.** Basis of the Indiana pouch and Mitrofanoff-via-appendix; the appendix is a length of small-caliber catheterizable channel with its own mesoappendix.
- **Ascending colon, hepatic flexure, transverse colon, splenic flexure, descending colon.** Territory of colonic conduits and some continent cutaneous reservoirs.
- **Sigmoid colon.** One option for intestinal neovaginal reconstruction, some continent reservoirs and selected colonic conduits; bowel selection depends on the patient's anatomy, prior treatment and reconstructive goal.
- **Rectum.** See [The Anal Canal](/docs/foundations/anatomy-physiology/pelvis-support/anal-anatomy) and [The Presacral Space](/docs/foundations/anatomy-physiology/pelvis-support/presacral-anatomy).

Surface landmarks of the colon:
- **Teniae coli.** Three longitudinal muscle bands running from the appendix base to the rectosigmoid junction (where they splay into a continuous longitudinal layer).
- **Haustra.** Sacculations between the teniae.
- **Epiploic appendages.** Fat-filled peritoneal tags along the colon.
- **Ileocecal valve.** Imperfect flap valve with tonic muscular thickening; preserved in the Indiana pouch for continence. Loss of the valve and adjacent terminal ileum after ileocecal resection can contribute to diarrhea and bacterial overgrowth.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup>

### Peritoneal relations

| Organ | Peritoneal relationship |
|---|---|
| Duodenum (D2–D4), ascending colon, descending colon | Retroperitoneal / secondarily retroperitoneal |
| Jejunum, ileum, cecum (usually), transverse colon, sigmoid colon | Intraperitoneal with mesentery |
| Upper, middle and lower rectum | Upper third: anterior and lateral peritoneal covering; middle third: anterior covering only; lower third: extraperitoneal. TME follows the mesorectal plane.<sup>[[40]](#ref40)</sup> |

## Vascular Supply — The Reconstructive Surgeon's Map

Most of the bowel is supplied by three unpaired aortic branches; the distal rectum and anal canal also receive pelvic arterial branches.<sup>[[2]](#ref2)[[21]](#ref21)</sup>

| Vessel | Territory | Reconstructive relevance |
|---|---|---|
| **Celiac trunk** | Foregut — stomach to D2 | Not typically used for urinary reconstruction |
| **SMA** | Midgut — D3 to proximal ⅔ transverse colon (ileum, cecum, ascending, hepatic flexure) | **Supplies every ileal-, ileocecal-, and right-colic-based urinary reservoir** |
| **IMA** | Hindgut — distal ⅓ transverse colon to upper rectum | Supplies **sigmoid conduits / neovaginas** and colonic reservoirs |

### SMA branches relevant to urinary reconstruction

- **Ileocolic artery.** Last SMA branch; supplies the terminal ileum and cecum. It is the pedicle of the Indiana pouch and of any ileocecal reconstruction, and must be preserved.
- **Jejunal and ileal arterial arcades.** Short, closely spaced arcades near the bowel wall. Ileum has 3–5 arcade levels; jejunum has 1–2. The ileum is more mobile on its mesentery than jejunum, with a longer arc of rotation for pelvic anastomosis.
- **Right colic artery.** Variable and absent in a minority of patients; ascending-colon supply.
- **Middle colic artery.** Transverse colon; at risk during extended colonic reservoirs.

### IMA branches

- **Left colic artery.** Descending colon.
- **Sigmoid arteries.** Typically 2–4; supply the sigmoid used for sigmoid vaginoplasty and sigmoid conduits.
- **Superior rectal artery.** Terminal branch; dominant supply to the upper rectum.

### Collateral circulation

- **Marginal artery of Drummond.** A mesenteric-border collateral route connecting SMA and IMA territories, but its caliber and continuity vary, especially near watershed regions; assess actual perfusion when planning resection or anastomosis.<sup>[[2]](#ref2)</sup>
- **Arc of Riolan (meandering mesenteric artery).** Inconstant proximal anastomosis between middle colic and left colic; hypertrophies when IMA is occluded.
- **Watershed zones.** The splenic flexure (SMA–IMA junction) and the rectosigmoid junction (Sudeck's critical point, between last sigmoid and superior rectal) are most vulnerable to ischemia;<sup>[[2]](#ref2)[[3]](#ref3)</sup> consider both when planning a bowel anastomosis in radiated or vasculopathic patients.

### Venous drainage

All mesenteric venous drainage ultimately joins the portal vein:
- **Superior mesenteric vein (SMV).** Drains small bowel and proximal colon; joins splenic vein to form portal.
- **Inferior mesenteric vein (IMV).** Drains distal colon and upper rectum; joins the splenic vein retropancreatic.
- **Superior rectal vein.** Drains to the IMV (portal).
- **Middle and inferior rectal veins.** Drain to the internal iliac (systemic).

The rectal portal–systemic venous connection is the anatomic basis of rectal varices in portal hypertension.<sup>[[35]](#ref35)</sup> Upper-rectal venous drainage reaches the liver through the portal system; lower-rectal drainage enters the systemic circulation and helps explain why low rectal tumors have relatively more pulmonary metastases without hepatic metastases than higher tumors.<sup>[[36]](#ref36)</sup>

## Wall Architecture

Four concentric layers, with subtle segment-specific differences that matter reconstructively:

| Layer | Small bowel features | Large bowel features | Reconstructive relevance |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Mucosa** | Villi and crypts; absorptive enterocytes predominate; goblet cells fewer than in colon; Paneth cells at crypt base; M cells over Peyer's patches | No villi, crypts only; absorptive colonocytes predominate; goblet cells more numerous; Paneth cells are normal in the right colon; their presence distal to the splenic flexure is conventionally termed metaplasia<sup>[[27]](#ref27)</sup> | Mucus output and mucus-lumen biology differ by segment; the **high mucus output of colonic reservoirs** is clinically bothersome after colonic neobladder / conduit |
| **Submucosa** | Meissner's plexus; Brunner's glands in duodenum; dense lymphatics (Peyer's patches in ileum) | Meissner's plexus; no Brunner's; sparse lymphoid tissue | The structural strength of any bowel anastomosis resides in the submucosa; suture purchase must include it |
| **Muscularis propria** | Inner circular + outer longitudinal | Outer longitudinal **condensed into three teniae** | Teniae are the landmark for detubularization in ileocecal and colonic reservoirs |
| **Serosa / adventitia** | Serosa on intraperitoneal, adventitia on retroperitoneal portions | Same | Serosal surface is the mesothelial plane for adhesion formation |

### Segment-specific absorptive specialisation

| Nutrient / solute | Dominant site | Clinical relevance after bowel reconstruction |
|---|---|---|
| **Iron, folate, calcium** | Duodenum / proximal jejunum<sup>[[4]](#ref4)</sup> | Preserved unless extensive proximal small-bowel resection |
| **Magnesium** | Jejunum and ileum<sup>[[4]](#ref4)</sup> | Extensive small-bowel loss can reduce absorptive reserve, particularly when distal small bowel and colon are absent.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup> |
| **Fat-soluble vitamins (A, D, E, K)** | Small intestine; vitamin K absorption also occurs in terminal ileum<sup>[[4]](#ref4)</sup> | Substantial ileal loss can impair bile-salt recycling and fat-soluble vitamin absorption<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup> |
| **Carbohydrates, proteins, water-soluble vitamins** | Jejunum and proximal ileum | Preserved with standard ileal conduit / neobladder |
| **Bile salts** | **Terminal ileum** (ASBT transporter) | Terminal-ileal loss can cause bile-acid diarrhea and, with more extensive loss, steatorrhea and fat-soluble vitamin deficiency; risk is not set by one universal length cutoff<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup> |
| **Vitamin B12** | **Terminal ileum** (intrinsic factor–cubam complex) | Ileal resection/reconfiguration can increase deficiency risk; length alone does not reliably exclude it.<sup>[[5]](#ref5)</sup> After cystectomy with bowel diversion, EAU guidance states that B12 is measured annually<sup>[[6]](#ref6)</sup> |
| **Water and Na⁺** | Small intestine, including terminal ileum, absorbs most of the daily luminal load; colon absorbs much of the remainder<sup>[[4]](#ref4)</sup> | Colonic urinary segments can contribute to hyperchloremic metabolic acidosis through urinary NH₄⁺ and Cl⁻ uptake with Na⁺ and bicarbonate secretion; rat perfusion linked ammonium to net sodium secretion and chloride absorption to bicarbonate secretion.<sup>[[7]](#ref7)[[28]](#ref28)</sup> |
| **Short-chain fatty acids (SCFAs)** | Colon | ~5–10% of daily energy; butyrate is colonocyte fuel<sup>[[8]](#ref8)</sup> |

## Innervation — Extrinsic Autonomic + Intrinsic Enteric

### Extrinsic autonomic

| System | Origin | Territory |
|---|---|---|
| **Parasympathetic — vagus (CN X)** | Brainstem | Stomach, small bowel and proximal colon (the distal limit is described as the distal transverse colon or the **splenic flexure**) |
| **Parasympathetic — pelvic splanchnics (S2–S4)** | Sacral cord | Distal colon (splenic flexure to rectum) |
| **Sympathetic** | T5–L2 via celiac, superior mesenteric, and inferior mesenteric ganglia | Entire bowel; reduces motility and secretion, causes vasoconstriction |

**Sympathetic block / dysautonomia.** Neuraxial anesthesia and perioperative vagotonic drugs favor bowel activity. Visceral pain afferents travel with the sympathetic routes, and referred pain follows embryologic origin: midgut structures (small bowel to the proximal transverse colon) refer to the periumbilical region and hindgut structures (distal colon and upper rectum) to the hypogastrium.

### Enteric nervous system (ENS) — the "second brain"

- **Myenteric (Auerbach's) plexus.** Between circular and longitudinal muscle; coordinates motility.<sup>[[9]](#ref9)[[10]](#ref10)</sup>
- **Submucosal (Meissner's) plexus.** In submucosa; coordinates secretion and local reflexes.
- **Hundreds of millions of neurons.** They can coordinate peristalsis independently of CNS input,<sup>[[9]](#ref9)[[10]](#ref10)</sup> which is why isolated bowel segments used for urinary reconstruction continue to peristalse after extrinsic (mesenteric) denervation. In a 68-patient human urodynamic series, 36 conduits with normal upper tracts emptied mainly by low-pressure to-and-fro activity; high-frequency, high-amplitude contractions were common among 32 patients with progressive upper-tract dilation and were consistent with distal conduit obstruction.<sup>[[26]](#ref26)</sup> Intrinsic bowel contractions can also raise pressure in non-detubularized reservoirs. This is the anatomical argument for detubularization and spherical reconfiguration of neobladders to maximize capacity and minimize pressure.

## Motility Patterns Relevant to Reconstruction

- **Segmentation.** Non-propulsive mixing.
- **Peristalsis.** Propulsive wave; ascending excitation (ACh, substance P) proximal and descending inhibition (NO, VIP) distal to bolus.
- **Migrating motor complex (MMC).** 90–120 minute fasting "housekeeper" wave; propagates from stomach to terminal ileum; driven by motilin.<sup>[[10]](#ref10)</sup>
- **High-amplitude propagating contractions (HAPCs).** Mass colonic movements; triggered by gastrocolic reflex.<sup>[[11]](#ref11)</sup>
- **Gastrocolic reflex.** Meal-stimulated colonic activity;<sup>[[11]](#ref11)</sup> part of the ERAS rationale for early feeding.<sup>[[12]](#ref12)</sup>

Clinical implications for bowel reconstruction:

- **Non-detubularized bowel used as a urinary reservoir** can retain coordinated contractile waves, raising storage pressure and potentially compromising continence or upper-tract protection.
- **Detubularization** (opening the segment on its antimesenteric border and reconfiguring spherically) dissipates these waves; it is the principle of Studer, Hautmann, and Indiana-pouch construction.
- **Postoperative ileus** after cystectomy has several contributors, including bowel manipulation, inflammatory and autonomic responses, opioids and electrolyte derangements.<sup>[[22]](#ref22)</sup> A randomized radical-cystectomy trial found that alvimopan shortened gastrointestinal recovery versus placebo, and ERAS guidance recommends multimodal perioperative care.<sup>[[12]](#ref12)[[13]](#ref13)</sup> See [Postoperative Constipation](/docs/foundations/perioperative-care/postoperative-management/constipation).

## Intestinal Barrier (Brief)

Three layers: mucus (MUC2-rich, two layers in colon), epithelial monolayer with tight junctions, and gut vascular barrier. Clinically relevant for the reconstructive urologist in three settings:

1. **Bowel-to-urine contact in reservoirs.** Bowel mucosa continues to secrete mucus after urinary reconstruction, while metabolic effects vary with bowel segment and length, urine contact time, and renal function.<sup>[[31]](#ref31)</sup> Malignancy risk is especially established at ureterocolonic anastomoses in diversions mixing urine and stool.<sup>[[18]](#ref18)</sup> After augmentation cystoplasty, the estimated cancer risk varies widely and the carcinogenesis pathway remains unclear.<sup>[[41]](#ref41)</sup>
2. **Postoperative ileus / AKI.** Severe ileus, splanchnic hypoperfusion, or sepsis disrupt barrier integrity and drive bacterial translocation.
3. **Inflammatory bowel disease.** For benign urinary reconstruction, an ICS working group recommends specialist IBD assessment and segment-specific planning. The ICS working group treats an acute UC flare or active Crohn disease as a contraindication to elective bowel-based urinary reconstruction and advises waiting at least three months after an acute flare resolves.<sup>[[23]](#ref23)</sup> Crohn disease remains high risk even in remission, and alternative diversion may be needed when safe bowel harvest is not feasible.<sup>[[14]](#ref14)[[23]](#ref23)</sup> The ICS working group also advises against elective bowel-based reconstruction during systemic steroid use in ulcerative colitis or Crohn disease and recommends an IBD-supervised withdrawal interval of at least six weeks before surgery.<sup>[[23]](#ref23)</sup>

## Microbiome — Operative-Relevant Points Only

- **Colonic load**: 10¹¹–10¹² bacteria/mL; small-bowel load much lower (10³–10⁸).<sup>[[8]](#ref8)</sup>
- Colonic SCFA production (acetate, propionate, butyrate) contributes approximately 5–10% of daily energy; butyrate fuels colonocytes.<sup>[[8]](#ref8)</sup>
- **Relevance to urinary reconstruction:** bowel used as a conduit / reservoir continues to produce mucus and is often bacteriuric. Bacteriuria is frequent: a systematic review cited by the EAU reported asymptomatic bacteriuria in 25–86% of intestinal conduits and 9.1–85% of orthotopic neobladders.<sup>[[15]](#ref15)</sup> The EAU Urological Infections guideline gives a strong recommendation not to screen for or treat asymptomatic bacteriuria in reconstructed lower urinary tracts and a strong recommendation to screen for and treat it before urologic procedures that breach the mucosa.<sup>[[15]](#ref15)[[16]](#ref16)</sup> Symptomatic infection is assessed and treated on its own merits. Stones or stoma problems require their own assessment; a positive urine culture does not automatically make an antibiotic indication.

## Clinical Correlations for the Reconstructive Urologist

### Urinary reconstruction using bowel

- **Ileal conduit (Bricker).** A short ileal segment is isolated proximal to the ileocecal valve, with ileoileal re-anastomosis, Wallace or Bricker ureteroileal anastomosis, and an end stoma. Segment length and distance from the valve are individualized to reach, blood supply and remaining bowel; preserving a specified 15–20 cm does not guarantee normal B12 absorption. In a 70-patient series (median follow-up 65 months), low serum B12 occurred in 5 patients in the 15–20 cm ileal-segment group and 1 in the ileocecal group; abnormal Schilling tests occurred in 1 and 6 patients, respectively.<sup>[[17]](#ref17)</sup> Neither abnormality was observed in the 50–60 cm ileal-segment group (Ganesan 2002).<sup>[[17]](#ref17)</sup> Monitor B12 after cystectomy with bowel diversion instead of prescribing lifelong replacement solely from a length cutoff.<sup>[[6]](#ref6)</sup>
- **Orthotopic neobladder (Studer, Hautmann, T-pouch).** An ileal segment is detubularized and reconfigured into a low-pressure reservoir; ureteroileal implantation and urethroileal anastomosis follow the chosen design. Bowel length and terminal-ileal preservation vary by technique and patient; preserving the ileocecal valve is useful but no fixed retained length guarantees normal absorption. The EAU states that continent diversion requires motivation and manual ability and lists debilitating neurological or psychiatric illness, limited life expectancy, and severe hepatic or renal impairment as contraindications. It gives a Strong recommendation not to offer orthotopic substitution to patients with invasive tumor in the urethra or at the urethral dissection level; high-dose preoperative radiotherapy, complex urethral stricture, and severe sphincter incontinence are relative contraindications.<sup>[[37]](#ref37)</sup>
- **Indiana pouch (ileocecal continent cutaneous).** Right colon and an ileal segment are reconfigured; tapering or plication helps create the catheterizable continence mechanism. Segment dimensions and the stoma site (umbilical or another suitable marked location) are individualized. Counsel about B12 surveillance.
- **Sigmoid conduit / colon conduit.** Colonic conduits have been described when ileum is unsuitable after extensive small-bowel resection or because of ileal Crohn disease. In Crohn disease, specialist assessment should map both small and large bowel, and any harvested segment should be away from current or previously active disease; active disease precludes elective bowel-based reconstruction.<sup>[[23]](#ref23)</sup> After pelvic irradiation, case series used transverse colon and ureters above the treated field; diversion with low-lying small bowel or descending colon was associated with greater morbidity.<sup>[[32]](#ref32)[[33]](#ref33)</sup> In a four-institution retrospective cohort of 179 colon-conduit patients, 63.7% had prior radiotherapy; 90-day surgical or procedural reintervention was 30.2%, and 90-day mortality was 4.5%.<sup>[[42]](#ref42)</sup> Colonic segments have a larger lumen and thicker wall. Hyperchloremic acidosis remains a risk, especially with impaired renal function or prolonged urine contact.<sup>[[31]](#ref31)</sup>
- **Augmentation cystoplasty.** In ileocystoplasty, a detubularized ileal patch is anastomosed to the bivalved bladder.<sup>[[38]](#ref38)</sup> In continent catheterizable ileocecal cecocystoplasty, the detubularized cecum is anastomosed to the bladder while tapered terminal ileum forms the catheterizable channel.<sup>[[39]](#ref39)</sup> Metabolic and cancer-surveillance considerations as for other bowel-bladder interfaces.
- **Monti / Mitrofanoff continent catheterizable channels.** Appendix (classic Mitrofanoff) or reconfigured ileum (Monti / Yang-Monti) tunneled submucosally into the bladder or reservoir, forming a one-way valve against a smooth muscle backing. Umbilical and non-umbilical stoma sites are both used according to reach and patient needs.
- **MACE (antegrade continence enema).** Antegrade irrigation for neurogenic or structural bowel dysfunction (including spina bifida, spinal cord injury and Hirschsprung disease) may use a continent catheterizable appendicostomy or a cecostomy tube. Tube cecostomy is a separate access technique and does not create an appendiceal channel.<sup>[[24]](#ref24)[[25]](#ref25)</sup>

### Vaginal / perineal reconstruction

- **Sigmoid neovagina (MRKH / post-exenteration).** About 12–15 cm of sigmoid is isolated on its vascular pedicle and anastomosed to the introitus. It provides mucosal lubrication; mucus output and diversion colitis can occur. Delayed adenocarcinoma has been reported, so new neovaginal bleeding warrants evaluation; no routine cancer-screening interval is established.<sup>[[30]](#ref30)</sup>
- **Ileal neovagina.** An alternative when sigmoid is unavailable. A retrospective 105-patient comparison reported 12–15 cm neovaginal lengths and mucus-like discharge with both ileal and sigmoid segments at 6–62 months of follow-up.<sup>[[29]](#ref29)</sup>
- **Gracilis / VRAM flaps.** For perineal reconstruction after exenteration or APR; derived from adjacent lower extremity or anterior abdominal wall and not from bowel. See [The Perineum](/docs/foundations/anatomy-physiology/pelvis-support/perineum).

### Metabolic sequelae of bowel-urine contact

- **Hyperchloremic metabolic acidosis.** It can occur after colonic urinary segments (sigmoid conduit, ureterosigmoidostomy, colonic neobladder) and after ileal reservoirs; colonic segments may be more prone to metabolic changes. Hypokalemia is a less frequent accompanying abnormality.<sup>[[7]](#ref7)[[31]](#ref31)</sup>
- **Hypokalemia, hypomagnesemia, hypocalcemia.** Potassium loss can be intestinal and renal, calcium depletion can involve renal wasting and bone buffering of acidosis, and magnesium depletion may involve renal wasting or poor nutrition. Correcting acidosis can worsen potassium depletion.<sup>[[31]](#ref31)</sup>
- **B12 deficiency.** Risk rises after terminal-ileal resection or bowel diversion, but segment length alone does not rule out low B12 levels<sup>[[17]](#ref17)</sup>; the EAU muscle-invasive bladder cancer guideline (section 7.4) states that B12 should be measured annually, citing low levels in 17% of patients with bowel diversion.<sup>[[5]](#ref5)[[6]](#ref6)</sup>
- **Bile-salt malabsorption.** Terminal-ileal loss can cause diarrhea; evaluate extent and mechanism before choosing treatment. Bile-acid sequestration may help selected patients, while extensive ileal loss can require a different nutritional approach.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup>
- **Osteopenia / osteomalacia.** Chronic acidosis and impaired calcium or vitamin D handling can demineralize bone. Treat documented acidosis; consider calcium/vitamin D supplementation and DXA according to individual risk, because routine supplementation and serial DXA for every diversion patient are not established.<sup>[[31]](#ref31)</sup>
- **Reservoir stones.** Stasis, mucus and UTI; managed with irrigations, stone clearance, and occasional revision.
- **Secondary malignancy after bowel urinary reconstruction.** Risk is especially established at ureterocolonic anastomoses after ureterosigmoidostomy.<sup>[[18]](#ref18)</sup> For ureterosigmoidostomy and related diversions that mix urine and stool, Woodhouse's guideline recommends annual flexible sigmoidoscopy from 10 years after surgery (Grade B), continuing after conversion unless the ureterocolonic anastomoses were removed.<sup>[[18]](#ref18)</sup> Augmented bladders and isolated reservoirs still need long-term clinical follow-up; spina bifida guidance calls for cystoscopy after augmentation when symptoms or clinical changes arise, and routine annual endoscopy of every conduit or reservoir is not established.<sup>[[18]](#ref18)[[19]](#ref19)</sup>

### Pelvic radiation and bowel

- **Radiation enteritis.** Early acute phase (mucositis, diarrhea) and late chronic phase (fibrotic strictures, fistulae, ischemic segments, malabsorption); the latter dominates the reconstructive workload.
- **Radiated ileum** is at risk for stricture and fistula; use bowel outside the treated field where feasible for conduit or reservoir. A retrospective series of 30 patients given at least 65 Gy used transverse colon for a conduit, allowing nonirradiated bowel and ureters.<sup>[[34]](#ref34)</sup>
- **Rectourethral and enterovaginal fistulae** after pelvic radiation often prompt consideration of well-vascularized tissue interposition, chosen for fistula location and tissue quality. Gracilis or omentum has been used in radiation-associated rectourethral repair; a Martius flap has been used for radiation-associated rectovaginal repair.<sup>[[43]](#ref43)[[44]](#ref44)[[45]](#ref45)</sup>

### Operative pitfalls rooted in bowel anatomy

- **Watershed ischemia at the splenic flexure and rectosigmoid.** Anastomotic leak risk; check perfusion (ICG angiography, clinical bleeding of cut edge) before committing.
- **Ligament of Treitz.** Structural landmark for feeding jejunostomy and for orientation after massive resection.
- **Ileocecal valve preservation.** Loss is associated with chronic diarrhea and small-bowel bacterial overgrowth; consider this in short-bowel decisions.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup>
- **Serosal adhesions.** Every prior abdominal operation increases reoperation difficulty, which is the rationale for minimal bowel manipulation.
- **Stoma siting.** Preoperative site marking is done with a qualified ostomy clinician or surgeon. Ileal conduits are often right-sided, colostomies often left-sided, and continent catheterizable channels may be umbilical or elsewhere. The final site should fit the patient's abdominal contour, visibility, reach and pouching or catheterization needs while avoiding folds, scars and belt lines.<sup>[[20]](#ref20)</sup>
