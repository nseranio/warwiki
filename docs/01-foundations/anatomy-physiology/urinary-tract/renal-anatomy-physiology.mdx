# The Kidneys

The kidneys are paired retroperitoneal organs that together receive roughly 20% of cardiac output and produce ~180 L of glomerular ultrafiltrate per day, of which ~1.5 L is excreted as urine.<sup>[[3]](#ref3)[[4]](#ref4)</sup> For the reconstructive urologist, the kidney matters as a **surgical territory** (the fascial compartments, vascular pedicle, and collecting system that dictate access and reconstruction), a **functional reserve** (the ability to lose parenchyma to obstruction, infarction, or resection without decompensation), and a **pressure-sensitive end-organ** whose performance depends on unobstructed outflow and perfusion pressure. This article focuses on the gross, vascular, and collecting-system anatomy most relevant to operative planning, and on the physiology needed to interpret obstruction, ischemia, and loss of nephron mass — not a full nephrology text.

## Surgical Anatomy

Each adult kidney is ~11 × 6 × 3 cm and 125–170 g. The right kidney sits 1–2 cm lower than the left because of the liver. Relations to know in the operating field:

| Aspect | Left kidney | Right kidney |
|---|---|---|
| Anterior | Stomach, pancreas (body/tail), spleen, splenic flexure, jejunum | Liver, duodenum (2nd portion), hepatic flexure |
| Posterior | Diaphragm, psoas, quadratus lumborum, transversus abdominis; 11th–12th ribs | Same; usually only 12th rib |
| Superior | Left adrenal | Right adrenal |

Upper pole relations explain the risk of **pleural injury** with posterior flank access above the 12th rib and **splenic / hepatic capsule injury** during mobilization. Pancreatic tail injury is a recognized pitfall on the left.

### Fascial compartments

Understanding the retroperitoneal envelope is essential for urinoma tracking, abscess drainage, and correctly developing planes during nephrectomy, ureterolysis, and auto-transplantation.<sup>[[2]](#ref2)</sup>

- **Renal (fibrous) capsule** — tightly adherent; stripped off during decapsulation during intraparenchymal dissection or partial nephrectomy.
- **Perirenal fat** — the immediate surround; harbors accessory polar vessels.
- **Gerota's (renal) fascia** — encloses kidney, perirenal fat, and adrenal. Fuses **superolaterally** and **medially across the midline in only a limited band**, and is **open inferiorly**, which is why urinomas and retroperitoneal hematomas preferentially track into the pelvis rather than across to the contralateral side.
- **Pararenal fat** — outside Gerota's; anterior and posterior compartments.
- **Transversalis fascia** — the lateral limit of the retroperitoneum.

### Collecting system

The papillae drain through 7–14 **minor calyces**, which coalesce into **2–3 major calyces** (typically upper, interpolar, lower), then the **renal pelvis**. Calyceal anatomy varies widely and is worth reviewing on preoperative imaging before endopyelotomy, UPJ reconstruction, or percutaneous access planning. The **ureteropelvic junction (UPJ)** lies on the medial aspect of the pelvis. A lower-pole crossing vessel is a frequent finding in selected adult UPJ-obstruction surgical series, but its presence alone does not prove that it is the cause of obstruction.<sup>[[6]](#ref6)</sup>

### Innervation and referred pain

Visceral sensory fibers travel with sympathetic roots **T10–L1**. Pain from ureteral obstruction, pyelonephritis, or parenchymal distension classically refers to the flank and radiates to the ipsilateral groin or testicle/labium majus, reflecting shared innervation with the genitofemoral and ilioinguinal nerves.

## Vascular Anatomy

The vascular pedicle is the single most consequential structure in any renal operation.

![Renal vascular tree from the renal artery to the glomerular and peritubular capillaries](/img/anatomy/openstax-blood-flow-kidney.jpg)

*The renal vascular tree. The renal artery divides into segmental → interlobar → arcuate → interlobular (cortical radiate) arteries, which feed the glomerular afferent arterioles; efferent arterioles then form the peritubular capillary network investing the tubules. (CC BY, OpenStax Anatomy & Physiology)*

- A **single renal artery** enters each hilum in most people; a systematic review estimated accessory arteries in about **21%**, with variation by population and imaging method.<sup>[[7]](#ref7)</sup> Segmental and polar branches often supply distinct territories, so inadvertent loss can cause segmental ischemia; the extent of infarction and whole-kidney functional effect depend on the artery and territory, rather than being inevitable in every case.<sup>[[8]](#ref8)</sup>
- Each renal artery divides at the hilum into **anterior and posterior divisions**; the avascular **Brödel's line** lies posterolaterally at the boundary between them and is the classical plane for intersegmental incision in open surgery and for access in endoscopic surgery.
- Segmental arteries → interlobar → arcuate (at the corticomedullary junction) → interlobular (cortical radial) → **afferent arterioles**. Efferent arterioles emerging from juxtamedullary glomeruli give rise to the **vasa recta** that supply the medulla — a detail that matters because the medulla is essentially supplied by *postglomerular* blood and therefore more vulnerable to ischemia than the cortex.
- **Venous drainage**: interlobular → arcuate → interlobar → segmental → main renal vein. The **left renal vein** receives the left gonadal vein, left adrenal vein, and lumbar branches, and passes anterior to the aorta between the SMA and aorta (vulnerable to nutcracker physiology). The **right renal vein** is short, drains directly, and usually has no tributaries.
- **Lymphatics**: right kidney → right paracaval/interaortocaval; left kidney → left paraaortic/preaortic. Cross-drainage is common on the right.

**Accessory arteries and reconstruction.** Unrecognized variant vessels can complicate renal hilar and UPJ surgery. Review available vascular-phase imaging and, when the operation requires it, map the arteries and veins on both sides, including lower-pole vessels near the UPJ. Preservation or sacrifice of a branch is individualized to its territory, procedure and available reconstruction options; neither CT urogram phase selection nor a brief clamp/Doppler test alone establishes that a segment is dispensable.<sup>[[7]](#ref7)[[8]](#ref8)</sup>

## Nephron Overview (Clinically Relevant Level)

Each kidney contains roughly a million **nephrons**, with substantial individual variation. After loss of renal mass, single-nephron filtration can rise in the remaining kidney — **compensatory hyperfiltration** — but the degree of compensation and long-term outcome vary. Hyperfiltration after donation is not by itself proof of progressive injury.<sup>[[9]](#ref9)</sup>

![The uriniferous tubule (nephron) and its course through the cortex and medulla](/img/anatomy/gray1128-kidney-section.png)

*The uriniferous tubule (nephron). The glomerular capsule and convoluted tubules sit in the cortical substance; the loop of Henle descends into the medulla, and the collecting tubules drain through the ducts of Bellini at the papilla. (Public domain, Gray's Anatomy)*

![Sites of solute reabsorption and secretion along the nephron](/img/anatomy/openstax-nephron-transport.jpg)

*Tubular handling along the nephron — the major solutes and water reabsorbed into the blood and secreted into the filtrate at each segment. This segmental division of labor underlies the kidney's clearance of drugs and metabolites and the electrolyte disturbances seen after bowel-segment urinary reconstruction. (CC BY, OpenStax Anatomy & Physiology)*

**Glomerular filtration** depends on three things:

1. The **ultrafiltration coefficient** (area × hydraulic permeability of the barrier) — falls in glomerulosclerosis, diabetic nephropathy, and with nephron loss.
2. The **hydrostatic pressure gradient** between glomerular capillary and Bowman's space — falls with ureteral obstruction (rising intra-Bowman pressure) and with renal vein obstruction or renal tamponade (rising venous pressure).
3. The **oncotic pressure** opposing filtration — modestly altered by plasma protein concentration.

Tubular function can be collapsed, for operative purposes, into three jobs:

- **Bulk reabsorption (proximal tubule)** — recovers 60–70% of filtered Na⁺ and water, ~80% of filtered bicarbonate, and nearly all filtered glucose, amino acids, and low-molecular-weight proteins. Substantial generalized proximal dysfunction can produce **low-molecular-weight proteinuria, glucosuria despite euglycemia, aminoaciduria, and phosphaturia** — a Fanconi phenotype. These findings are not a universal or earliest signature of every ischemic or drug-associated acute kidney injury; distinguish kidney injury that follows contrast from injury caused by contrast.<sup>[[5]](#ref5)[[10]](#ref10)</sup>
- **Concentration (loop of Henle + collecting duct)** — generates and exploits the medullary osmotic gradient under vasopressin control. Loop-of-Henle injury (as in chronic obstruction or medullary ischemia) produces **impaired concentration** and a fixed, dilute urine output — a common finding after relief of long-standing obstruction (post-obstructive diuresis).
- **Fine tuning (distal nephron)** — regulated final Na⁺, K⁺, H⁺, and water handling under aldosterone and vasopressin. Relevant in interpreting post-operative electrolyte and acid-base derangements.

**The medulla is a vulnerable zone.** Its relatively low oxygen tension reflects countercurrent vascular exchange while the thick ascending limb has high transport-related oxygen demand. This makes medullary tissue susceptible to hypoxic stress, but ischemic, drug-associated and post-contrast kidney injury have different mechanisms and distributions; the medulla is not invariably the first or only territory affected.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[10]](#ref10)</sup>

### Illustrative adult reference values

These approximate values describe healthy adult physiology, not universal normal limits or procedure thresholds. GFR, urine volume and concentrating ability vary with age, body size, fluid intake, illness and measurement method.

| Parameter | Typical adult value |
|---|---|
| Renal blood flow | ~1 L/min (20–25% of cardiac output) |
| GFR | ~100–120 mL/min/1.73 m² |
| Filtration fraction | ~20% |
| Daily ultrafiltrate | ~180 L |
| Daily urine output | ~1.0–2.0 L |
| Minimum urine osmolality | ~50 mOsm/kg (max dilution) |
| Maximum urine osmolality | ~1,200 mOsm/kg (max concentration) |

## Endocrine Output

Three hormonal roles matter operatively:

- **Renin** (juxtaglomerular cells) — triggered by reduced afferent-arteriolar perfusion pressure, sympathetic input, and reduced distal NaCl delivery. Clinically important in **renovascular hypertension**, post-clamp ischemic hypertension, and the hemodynamic swings after nephrectomy/revascularization.
- **Erythropoietin** (peritubular cortical fibroblasts) — stimulated by hypoxia. Reduced erythropoietin contributes to anemia in advanced CKD over time; an abrupt postoperative hemoglobin fall should not be attributed to new erythropoietin loss without assessing bleeding, dilution and other causes.<sup>[[11]](#ref11)</sup>
- **Active vitamin D** (proximal-tubule 1α-hydroxylase) — converts 25(OH)D to 1,25(OH)₂D. Failure drives the secondary hyperparathyroidism and bone disease of advanced CKD.

## Clinical Correlations for the Reconstructive Urologist

- **Medullary ischemic injury and warm ischemia time.** During partial nephrectomy, minimize ischemic exposure while preserving viable parenchyma and achieving safe tumor removal. Some series associate longer warm ischemia beyond about 25–30 minutes with worse operated-kidney function, but 25 minutes is not a universal safe/unsafe boundary; an off-clamp approach is not routinely superior to limited on-clamp ischemia in patients with two functioning kidneys.<sup>[[12]](#ref12)[[13]](#ref13)</sup>
- **Segmental arterial supply.** Injury to an accessory or polar artery can compromise the supplied territory during partial nephrectomy, donor procurement, transplant, or UPJ reconstruction. The actual infarct and functional consequence depend on branch caliber, territory and collateral/alternative perfusion; assess the anatomy rather than assuming every ligation has the same outcome.<sup>[[8]](#ref8)</sup>
- **Obstruction and backpressure nephropathy.** Elevated collecting-system pressure can reduce filtration and contribute to parenchymal injury. Recovery after drainage depends on duration, severity, infection and baseline function; monitor for post-obstructive diuresis when relevant. The separate renal-compression hypothesis should not be treated as proof that Gerota's fascia alone drives chronic obstruction injury.<sup>[[2]](#ref2)</sup>
- **Venous congestion and compartment pressure.** Elevated renal venous or intra-abdominal pressure may reduce renal perfusion and filtration; the contribution of a distinct "renal tamponade" mechanism in heart failure remains a hypothesis rather than a diagnosis of dysfunction without intrinsic disease.<sup>[[2]](#ref2)</sup>
- **Split renal function assessment.** MAG3 or DMSA studies can estimate each kidney's contribution when the result would change decisions about a poorly functioning obstructed unit, bilateral disease or salvage versus nephrectomy; they are not mandatory before every nephrectomy.
- **Hyperfiltration after nephrectomy.** The remaining kidney often compensates, so total GFR usually falls by less than half after donation, but the amount varies between people. Donor follow-up includes blood pressure, estimated GFR and albuminuria assessment; hyperfiltration does not inevitably cause later glomerulosclerosis.<sup>[[9]](#ref9)[[14]](#ref14)</sup>
- **Low-molecular-weight proteinuria as a tubular-injury marker.** Cystatin C, β₂-microglobulin, and retinol-binding protein are normally reabsorbed by the proximal tubule; their appearance in the urine signals proximal tubular injury rather than glomerular disease.<sup>[[5]](#ref5)</sup>
- **Gerota's fascia, urinoma, and abscess.** Urinomas from calyceal tears, pyeloplasty leak, or percutaneous-tract extravasation are contained within Gerota's and preferentially track **inferiorly** toward the pelvis; abscesses can cross into the pararenal spaces once Gerota's is violated. Imaging interpretation after any upper-tract reconstruction depends on knowing these compartments.
