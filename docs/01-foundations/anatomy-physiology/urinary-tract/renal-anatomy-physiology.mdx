# The Kidneys

The kidneys are paired retroperitoneal organs that receive roughly 20% of cardiac output and produce ~180 L of glomerular ultrafiltrate per day, of which only ~1.5 L is excreted as urine.<sup>[[5]](#ref5)[[10]](#ref10)</sup> This disproportionately large share of cardiac output, combined with the intricate countercurrent architecture of the nephron, is what makes the kidney uniquely capable of homeostatic fine-tuning — and uniquely vulnerable to ischemic, nephrotoxic, and obstructive insult.<sup>[[3]](#ref3)[[5]](#ref5)</sup> This article surveys the gross, vascular, and microscopic anatomy of the kidney and the core physiologic functions of each nephron segment relevant to reconstructive urology.

---

## Gross Anatomy

Each kidney is a bean-shaped retroperitoneal organ approximately 11 cm long, 6 cm wide, and 3 cm thick in the adult, weighing 125–170 g. The right kidney sits ~1–2 cm lower than the left due to displacement by the liver.<sup>[[4]](#ref4)</sup> Each is encased in a rigid fibrous capsule, surrounded by perirenal adipose tissue, and enveloped by **Gerota's (renal) fascia**, which fuses superolaterally and opens inferomedially — a detail directly relevant to urinoma propagation and retroperitoneal access.<sup>[[4]](#ref4)</sup>

*Boorsma EM, Ter Maaten JM, Voors AA, van Veldhuisen DJ.* JACC Heart Fail. 2022;10(3):175–183. [doi:10.1016/j.jchf.2021.12.005](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jchf.2021.12.005)
:::

The parenchyma is organized into three radial zones:<sup>[[3]](#ref3)[[6]](#ref6)</sup>

| Zone | Contents | Physiologic Role |
|---|---|---|
| **Cortex** | Glomeruli, proximal and distal convoluted tubules, cortical collecting ducts | Bulk filtration and regulated reabsorption |
| **Outer medulla** (outer and inner stripes) | Thick ascending limbs, medullary collecting ducts, vasa recta | Establishment of the medullary osmotic gradient |
| **Inner medulla / papilla** | Thin limbs of Henle, terminal collecting ducts | Countercurrent concentration; drains into minor calyces |

:::info[Figure — Anatomical and physiologic features of the renal cortex and medulla]

*Brezis M, Rosen S.* N Engl J Med. 1995;332(10):647–655. [doi:10.1056/NEJM199503093321006](https://doi.org/10.1056/NEJM199503093321006)
:::

The **hilum** on the medial aspect transmits, from anterior to posterior, the renal vein, renal artery, and renal pelvis. The pelvis narrows into the proximal ureter as it descends over the psoas muscle. Seven to 14 minor calyces coalesce into 2–3 major calyces, which merge to form the renal pelvis.

### Vascular Anatomy

The kidney receives a single **renal artery** from the aorta in ~70–75% of individuals; accessory renal arteries are present in 20–30% and are typically end-arteries with no collateral supply — a surgically critical fact during partial nephrectomy, transplant procurement, and ureteral reconstruction. Each renal artery branches into an anterior and a posterior division at the hilum (Brödel's bloodless line lies posterolaterally between them), giving rise to segmental → interlobar → arcuate → interlobular (cortical radial) → afferent arterioles. Each glomerulus receives one afferent arteriole and drains into an efferent arteriole, which then forms either the peritubular capillary network (cortical nephrons) or the **vasa recta** (juxtamedullary nephrons) — the latter being indispensable for countercurrent exchange.<sup>[[3]](#ref3)[[5]](#ref5)</sup>

### Lymphatics and Innervation

Renal lymphatics drain to lateral aortocaval, paraaortic, and interaortocaval nodes. Sympathetic fibers from the celiac and aorticorenal plexuses regulate vascular tone and renin release; visceral afferents carry pain via T10–L1 and refer to the flank and groin.

## The Nephron

Each kidney contains ~1 million nephrons, the functional units of the kidney.<sup>[[5]](#ref5)[[7]](#ref7)</sup> A nephron consists of a **glomerulus** (filtration apparatus) and a long tubule with approximately a dozen differentiated segments.<sup>[[5]](#ref5)</sup> Nephrons are classified by the depth of their loops of Henle:<sup>[[6]](#ref6)</sup>

| Nephron type | Proportion | Loop depth | Function |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Cortical (short-looped)** | ~85% | Outer medulla | Bulk reabsorption |
| **Juxtamedullary (long-looped)** | ~15% | Deep into inner medulla | Urinary concentration via countercurrent |

### The Glomerulus

Blood enters the glomerular capillary tuft via the afferent arteriole, undergoes ultrafiltration across a three-layered barrier, and exits through the efferent arteriole.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup> The filtration barrier comprises:<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[5]](#ref5)[[10]](#ref10)</sup>

1. **Fenestrated endothelium** — ~70–100 nm pores covered by a negatively charged glycocalyx
2. **Glomerular basement membrane (GBM)** — type IV collagen (α3α4α5), laminin, nidogen, and heparan sulfate proteoglycans
3. **Podocytes** — visceral epithelial cells with interdigitating foot processes bridged by slit diaphragms, whose central backbone is nephrin

The barrier is permeable to water, small solutes, and low-molecular-weight proteins but restricts proteins &gt;60–70 kDa — and, by virtue of the anionic glycocalyx and GBM, further restricts negatively charged macromolecules such as albumin.<sup>[[5]](#ref5)[[10]](#ref10)</sup>

*Tryggvason K, Patrakka J, Wartiovaara J.* N Engl J Med. 2006;354(13):1387–1401. [doi:10.1056/NEJMra052131](https://doi.org/10.1056/NEJMra052131)
:::

### The Tubule

The tubule is divided into functionally distinct segments, each with a characteristic epithelial phenotype and transporter repertoire.<sup>[[5]](#ref5)[[9]](#ref9)[[11]](#ref11)</sup>

| Segment | Key function |
|---|---|
| **Proximal tubule** (S1–S3) | Isosmotic reabsorption of 60–70% of filtered Na⁺ and water; ~80% of filtered bicarbonate; nearly all filtered glucose, amino acids, and low-molecular-weight proteins; organic anion/cation secretion |
| **Thin descending limb** | Water-permeable, solute-impermeable — passive water loss concentrates tubular fluid in the hypertonic medulla |
| **Thin ascending limb** | Passively NaCl-permeable; solute diffusion out into the interstitium |
| **Thick ascending limb (TAL)** | NKCC2-driven active NaCl reabsorption; impermeable to water — dilutes tubular fluid and builds the medullary gradient |
| **Distal convoluted tubule (DCT)** | Thiazide-sensitive NCC; fine control of Na⁺ and Mg²⁺ |
| **Connecting tubule / collecting duct** | Aldosterone-dependent ENaC Na⁺ reabsorption and K⁺ / H⁺ secretion; vasopressin-dependent AQP2 water reabsorption |

:::info[Figure — The renal tubule]
Segment-by-segment transporter map and the molecular basis of vasopressin-driven water reabsorption in the collecting duct.
*Knepper MA, Kwon TH, Nielsen S.* N Engl J Med. 2015;372(14):1349–1358. [doi:10.1056/NEJMra1404726](https://doi.org/10.1056/NEJMra1404726)
:::

### Juxtaglomerular Apparatus

At the vascular pole of each glomerulus, specialized cells of the distal nephron (the **macula densa**) sit against the afferent arteriole and modified smooth-muscle cells of that arteriole (**juxtaglomerular cells**). The macula densa senses luminal NaCl concentration and, via paracrine adenosine and prostaglandin signaling, drives **tubuloglomerular feedback** — constricting the afferent arteriole to reduce single-nephron GFR when distal Na⁺ delivery is high, and triggering **renin release** when it is low.<sup>[[5]](#ref5)</sup>

## Physiology

### Glomerular Filtration

GFR is governed by Starling forces across the filtration barrier:

> **GFR = K_f × (P_GC − P_BS − π_GC)**

where **K_f** is the product of hydraulic permeability and surface area, **P_GC** and **P_BS** are glomerular-capillary and Bowman's-space hydrostatic pressures, and **π_GC** is the glomerular-capillary oncotic pressure (Bowman's space is essentially protein-free).<sup>[[5]](#ref5)[[10]](#ref10)</sup> The kidneys autoregulate GFR across a wide mean arterial pressure range (~80–180 mmHg) through myogenic and tubuloglomerular-feedback mechanisms acting on afferent-arteriolar tone.

Typical reference values:

| Parameter | Adult value |
|---|---|
| Renal blood flow | ~1 L/min (20–25% of cardiac output) |
| Renal plasma flow | ~600 mL/min |
| GFR | ~100–120 mL/min/1.73 m² |
| Filtration fraction | ~20% |
| Daily ultrafiltrate | ~180 L |
| Daily urine output | ~1.0–2.0 L |

### Reabsorption and Secretion

The proximal tubule performs the bulk of reabsorption through **polarized transport** driven by the basolateral Na⁺/K⁺-ATPase.<sup>[[5]](#ref5)[[8]](#ref8)</sup> The low intracellular Na⁺ this generates powers apical Na⁺-coupled cotransporters for glucose (SGLT1/2), amino acids, phosphate (NaPi-2a), and organic solutes. Filtered low-molecular-weight proteins — hormones, vitamin-binding proteins, and small enzymes — are recovered by **megalin–cubilin** receptor-mediated endocytosis.<sup>[[5]](#ref5)[[11]](#ref11)</sup> Organic anions (urate, many drugs including β-lactams, NSAIDs, and contrast media) and organic cations are secreted into the tubular lumen by specific transporters (OATs, OCTs) in the late proximal tubule — a clinically important route of drug elimination and of nephrotoxicity.

### Concentration and Dilution

The loop of Henle functions as a **countercurrent multiplier**, generating a corticomedullary interstitial osmotic gradient that rises from ~300 mOsm/kg in the cortex to ~1200 mOsm/kg at the papilla.<sup>[[2]](#ref2)[[5]](#ref5)</sup> Under vasopressin (AVP/ADH) stimulation, collecting-duct principal cells insert **aquaporin-2 (AQP2)** water channels into their apical membrane, allowing osmotic water flow into the hypertonic interstitium and concentration of the final urine.<sup>[[2]](#ref2)</sup> In the absence of AVP, the collecting duct is water-impermeable and dilute urine (&lt;100 mOsm/kg) is excreted.

:::info[Medullary hypoxia]
The medulla operates at a chronic PₒO₂ of 10–20 mmHg (versus ~50 mmHg in the cortex) because countercurrent exchange shunts oxygen from descending to ascending vasa recta before it reaches the deep medulla, while NKCC2-driven Na⁺ reabsorption in the TAL is exquisitely oxygen-hungry. This precarious balance makes the outer medulla the dominant site of ischemic acute tubular injury.<sup>[[3]](#ref3)</sup>
:::

### Acid–Base Regulation

The kidney defends plasma pH through two processes:<sup>[[8]](#ref8)[[9]](#ref9)</sup>

1. **Bicarbonate reclamation** — the proximal tubule reabsorbs ~80% of filtered HCO₃⁻ via apical NHE3-mediated H⁺ secretion coupled to luminal and cytoplasmic carbonic anhydrase.
2. **New bicarbonate generation** — proximal-tubule **ammoniagenesis** from glutamine (yielding NH₄⁺ excreted in the urine and HCO₃⁻ returned to blood) and distal H⁺ secretion onto titratable buffers (phosphate) via α-intercalated H⁺-ATPase and H⁺/K⁺-ATPase.

During metabolic acidosis, proximal-tubule glutamine uptake and catabolism are upregulated, increasing ammoniagenesis and net bicarbonate production.<sup>[[8]](#ref8)</sup>

### Electrolyte Homeostasis

| Electrolyte | Dominant nephron site(s) | Key regulator |
|---|---|---|
| **Na⁺** | Proximal tubule (bulk), TAL, DCT, collecting duct (fine-tune) | Aldosterone (ENaC), ANP, RAS |
| **K⁺** | Proximal and TAL (reabsorption), principal cell (secretion) | Aldosterone, plasma K⁺, tubular flow |
| **Ca²⁺** | Proximal tubule (paracellular), TAL (paracellular via claudin-16/19), DCT (active via TRPV5) | PTH, 1,25-(OH)₂-vitamin D, CaSR |
| **Mg²⁺** | TAL (paracellular; dominant site), DCT (active via TRPM6) | Plasma Mg²⁺, CaSR |
| **Phosphate** | Proximal tubule (NaPi-2a/2c) | PTH ↓, FGF23 ↓, dietary load |
| **H⁺ / HCO₃⁻** | Proximal tubule (reclamation); α-intercalated cells (secretion) | Plasma pH, pCO₂, aldosterone |

### Endocrine Functions

The kidney synthesizes and releases:<sup>[[5]](#ref5)[[7]](#ref7)</sup>

- **Renin** — juxtaglomerular cells; initiates the renin–angiotensin–aldosterone system in response to reduced renal perfusion pressure, β1-adrenergic stimulation, or decreased macula-densa NaCl delivery.
- **Erythropoietin (EPO)** — peritubular fibroblasts of the cortex and outer medulla; released in response to hypoxia via HIF stabilization.
- **Active vitamin D (1,25-(OH)₂-D₃)** — proximal-tubule 1α-hydroxylase, upregulated by PTH and suppressed by FGF23.
- **Prostaglandins and kinins** — local vasoactive modulators of renal blood flow.

### Metabolic Functions

The proximal tubule is an important site of **gluconeogenesis** (accounting for ~20–25% of whole-body glucose production in the fasted state), and contributes significantly during metabolic acidosis and prolonged fasting.<sup>[[8]](#ref8)[[11]](#ref11)</sup> It also catabolizes reabsorbed peptides and small proteins, including insulin, glucagon, PTH, and β2-microglobulin — a major reason their plasma half-lives rise sharply in advanced CKD.

## Clinical Correlations for the Reconstructive Urologist

- **Medullary ischemic injury** — the outer medulla's chronic hypoxia explains why contrast, NSAIDs, nephrotoxic antibiotics (aminoglycosides), and prolonged hypotension preferentially injure the S3 proximal segment and TAL.<sup>[[3]](#ref3)</sup>
- **Obstruction and backpressure nephropathy** — the rigid capsule and encapsulating Gerota's fascia allow transmitted backpressure to compress the parenchyma; the cortex is more susceptible than the medulla to pressure-driven atrophy in chronic obstruction.<sup>[[4]](#ref4)</sup>
- **Renal tamponade in volume overload** — elevated venous pressure, ascites, or abdominal compartment syndrome reduce effective perfusion pressure across the encapsulated kidney and produce functional decline without intrinsic parenchymal disease.<sup>[[4]](#ref4)</sup>
- **End-arterial segmental supply** — accessory and polar renal arteries have no collaterals; inadvertent ligation during partial nephrectomy, transplant, or ureteral reconstruction produces a wedge of infarction.
- **Low-molecular-weight proteinuria** as a marker of proximal tubule injury — cystatin C, β2-microglobulin, and retinol-binding protein escape glomerular filtration but are normally fully reabsorbed by megalin–cubilin; their appearance in the urine signals tubular rather than glomerular disease.<sup>[[11]](#ref11)</sup>
