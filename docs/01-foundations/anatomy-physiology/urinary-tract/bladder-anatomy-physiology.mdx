# The Bladder

The bladder is a distensible muscular reservoir that spends most of its time storing urine and comparatively little time emptying it. Its value in reconstruction is its **low-pressure capacitance** during storage and its **coordinated emptying** when voluntary discharge is desired. Reconstructive decisions (continence procedures, outlet surgery, augmentation, diversion, ureteral reimplantation, neobladder) turn on preserving or rebuilding those two properties. This article focuses on the surgical anatomy, vascular and neural supply, and functional physiology that matter at the table, not a molecular account of urothelial sensory biology.

## Surgical Anatomy

The adult bladder is a pelvic organ when empty and becomes intra-abdominal as it fills, which matters for suprapubic access, Pfannenstiel incisions, and penetrating trauma interpretation. Capacity is approximately 400–600 mL; in a healthy-volunteer study, mean maximum cystometric capacity was 500 mL in men and 560 mL in women.<sup>[[23]](#ref23)</sup>

![Median sagittal section of the male pelvis showing the bladder, prostate, and rectum](/img/anatomy/gray1136-bladder-male.png)

*Median sagittal section of the male pelvis. The bladder lies behind the pubic symphysis and in front of the rectum; the prostate and seminal vesicle sit at the bladder base, and the urethra passes through the pelvic floor toward the penis. (Public domain, Gray's Anatomy)*

![Interior of the bladder showing the trigone and the ureteric and internal urethral orifices](/img/anatomy/gray1140-bladder-female.png)

*Interior of the bladder. The smooth-walled **trigone** is bounded by the two ureteric orifices — joined by the interureteric ridge (torus uretericus) — and the internal urethral orifice at its apex; it is the fixed landmark for cystoscopic orientation and ureteral reimplantation. (Public domain, Gray's Anatomy)*

### Position and relations

| Aspect | Relation |
|---|---|
| **Anterior** | Pubic symphysis, **retropubic space of Retzius** (areolar plane exploited for suprapubic access, retropubic prostatectomy, Burch colposuspension, and many sling procedures) |
| **Superior (dome)** | Peritoneum, small bowel, sigmoid colon; the peritoneal reflection can extend onto adjacent upper bladder surfaces, while blunt intraperitoneal rupture commonly occurs at the distended dome<sup>[[16]](#ref16)[[22]](#ref22)</sup> |
| **Posterior (M)** | Vas deferens ampullae, seminal vesicles, rectum — rectovesical (Denonvilliers') fascia between |
| **Posterior (F)** | Anterior vaginal wall, uterine cervix (vesicouterine pouch extends to mid-bladder; anterior dissection in hysterectomy enters here) |
| **Inferior** | Prostate (M) or pelvic floor / urethra (F); bladder neck fixed to the pelvic floor |
| **Lateral** | Obturator internus and levator ani; neurovascular pedicles enter posterolaterally |

### Gross structure

- **Dome and body.** The most distensible region; the distended dome is a common site of blunt intraperitoneal rupture.<sup>[[16]](#ref16)</sup>
- **Trigone.** Triangular muscular floor between the two ureteral orifices and the internal urethral meatus. It is relatively smooth and less distensible than the body, and helps orient surgery around the ureteral orifices; the intramural course of each ureter is important to the anti-reflux mechanism (see [The Ureters](/docs/foundations/anatomy-physiology/urinary-tract/ureter-anatomy-physiology)).
- **Bladder neck (vesical outlet).** Where detrusor fibers converge and are continuous with smooth muscle of the proximal urethra. Forms the **internal (involuntary) urethral sphincter**.

### Ligaments and the retropubic space

- **Median umbilical ligament.** Obliterated urachus running from the dome to the umbilicus. Persistent urachal remnants produce cysts, sinuses, and adenocarcinoma.
- **Medial umbilical ligaments.** Paired obliterated umbilical arteries; important landmarks entering the retropubic space laparoscopically or robotically.
- **Puboprostatic (M) / pubovesical (F) ligaments.** Anchor the bladder neck to the pubis; preservation is part of most contemporary continence-sparing prostatectomy techniques.
- **Lateral pedicles.** Vascular and neural pedicles attaching the bladder to the pelvic sidewall, carrying the inferior vesical vessels and bladder-branch pelvic plexus.

## Vascular Supply and Drainage

### Arterial supply

The bladder is supplied by branches of the internal iliac (hypogastric) artery:

| Vessel | Origin | Territory |
|---|---|---|
| **Superior vesical artery** | From the patent portion of the obliterated umbilical artery | Anterosuperior dome and lateral wall |
| **Inferior vesical artery** | Internal iliac anterior division (M; often gives prostatic branches) | Bladder base and trigone; seminal vesicles / prostate |
| **Vaginal (F) / middle rectal / obturator (accessory)** | Variable internal iliac branches | Bladder base in women; small accessory territories |

**Pedicle control in radical cystectomy.** Superior, lateral and posterior vesical pedicles are controlled according to the operative approach and oncologic extent. Pelvic plexus branches and sexual-function nerves lie close to the posterolateral dissection planes; pedicle control must account for their variable course when nerve preservation is oncologically appropriate. No single pedicle or clamping maneuver explains all postoperative sexual dysfunction.<sup>[[17]](#ref17)</sup>

### Venous drainage

The vesical venous plexus drains to the internal iliac veins. In men it communicates freely with the prostatic (Santorini's) plexus anterior to the bladder neck and prostate. The engorged veins of this communication are the dominant bleeding source at dorsal vein or apical prostatic dissection and at cystectomy.

### Lymphatic drainage

Lymphatic drainage follows the arteries to the obturator, external iliac, and internal iliac nodes, with secondary drainage to common iliac and presacral basins. Standard pelvic lymphadenectomy for muscle-invasive bladder cancer targets the obturator, external iliac, and internal iliac groups; extended templates add common iliac up to the aortic bifurcation and the presacral group.

## Bladder Wall and Compliance

The wall has four layers, but for operative reasoning only two behaviors matter: the mucosal barrier and detrusor compliance.

| Layer | Composition | What matters operatively |
|---|---|---|
| **Urothelium** | Stratified transitional epithelium (3–6 cell layers; basal, intermediate, umbrella cells) | High-resistance barrier to water and solutes; also an active sensory surface that releases ATP, NO, and prostanoids in response to stretch — the neurophysiologic substrate of urgency sensation<sup>[[3]](#ref3)[[4]](#ref4)</sup> |
| **Lamina propria** | ECM, elastic fibers, vessels, nerve endings, interstitial cells, muscularis mucosae | **T1** tumor invasion reaches this layer; **Ta** papillary tumors remain noninvasive. It also participates in signaling between urothelium and detrusor.<sup>[[2]](#ref2)[[20]](#ref20)</sup> |
| **Detrusor** | Heterogeneously oriented smooth-muscle bundles functioning as a syncytium via gap junctions | The **muscularis propria** — invasion of this layer (≥T2) defines muscle-invasive bladder cancer and changes the operative plan entirely<sup>[[20]](#ref20)</sup> |
| **Adventitia / serosa** | Fibrous coat where uncovered; peritoneum over the superior surface with variable reflections | Intraperitoneal vs. extraperitoneal injury informs management<sup>[[16]](#ref16)[[22]](#ref22)</sup> |

Even during storage, localized spontaneous bladder-wall activity can occur without a coordinated voiding contraction; larger pathological contractions are a different observation.<sup>[[5]](#ref5)</sup>

### Compliance

Compliance = ΔV / ΔPdet, the change in volume divided by the simultaneous change in detrusor pressure during filling, excluding contractions and technical artifacts. Vesical pressure alone is not the compliance denominator. Lower compliance and high storage pressures can signal upper-tract risk, particularly in neurogenic lower urinary tract dysfunction, but no single compliance or 40 cmH₂O cutoff certifies safety or danger across all patients. Interpret the pressure trace with reflux, imaging, renal function and the clinical context.<sup>[[12]](#ref12)[[13]](#ref13)[[14]](#ref14)</sup> See [Urodynamics](/docs/evaluation/ancillary-tests/urodynamics) and [Neurogenic Lower Urinary Tract Dysfunction](/docs/clinical-conditions/03d-nlutd/neurogenic-bladder).

Compliance is reduced by:

- Chronic bladder outlet obstruction (detrusor hypertrophy and collagen deposition)
- Radiation cystitis and chronic inflammation (interstitial fibrosis)
- Chronic indwelling catheters
- Neurogenic dysfunction, particularly suprasacral spinal cord injury with upper motor neuron detrusor overactivity

Restoring compliance is the core objective whenever the bladder is putting the kidneys at risk. The means are relieving obstruction, reducing overactivity, or surgically expanding the reservoir (augmentation cystoplasty, continent diversion).

## Innervation

The bladder is controlled by three peripheral neural systems acting in concert with the central switching circuits of the pontine micturition center (Barrington's nucleus).<sup>[[6]](#ref6)[[7]](#ref7)[[10]](#ref10)</sup>

| System | Spinal origin | Nerve | Effect |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Parasympathetic** | S2–S4 | Pelvic | Detrusor contraction (M3 ≫ M2 muscarinic) — the voiding effector |
| **Sympathetic** | T10–L2 | Hypogastric (from superior hypogastric plexus) | Detrusor relaxation (β3-adrenergic, dome); bladder-neck contraction (α1-adrenergic, trigone and neck) — the storage effector |
| **Somatic** | S2–S4, Onuf's nucleus | Pudendal | External urethral sphincter contraction (voluntary) |

### Clinical geometry of pelvic innervation

- The **pelvic plexus (inferior hypogastric plexus)** sits on the lateral rectum or posterolateral to the bladder base. Its bladder branches approach near the lateral vascular pedicles. A careful dissection plane may preserve relevant autonomic fibers during selected cystectomy and prostatectomy procedures, but proximity to the bladder wall alone does not guarantee nerve sparing.<sup>[[17]](#ref17)</sup>
- The pudendal nerve is usually outside standard cystectomy and prostatectomy resection planes, but postoperative sphincter function also depends on preservation of the sphincter complex and its local innervation; it is not guaranteed by pudendal-nerve preservation alone.
- Neurological lesion level suggests, but does not establish, a bladder behavior pattern: suprapontine disease may produce detrusor overactivity with preserved sphincter coordination; suprasacral spinal lesions may produce detrusor–sphincter dyssynergia; and sacral, cauda equina or peripheral lesions may produce underactivity or areflexia. Multiple sclerosis can involve several levels, and incomplete or evolving lesions produce mixed phenotypes; confirm the actual storage and emptying behavior clinically and, when indicated, urodynamically.<sup>[[14]](#ref14)</sup>

### Referred pain

Visceral afferents from the bladder travel with the pelvic and hypogastric nerves to T10–L2 (sympathetic) and S2–S4 (parasympathetic) roots, with bladder-base and trigone discomfort referred to the suprapubic region and perineum. Bladder pain is poorly localized compared with cutaneous pain, which makes interstitial cystitis and chronic pelvic pain syndromes hard to localize.

## Storage and Voiding Physiology

Storage and voiding operate as two distinct reflex patterns switched at the pons.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[6]](#ref6)[[10]](#ref10)</sup>

### Storage (the default state)

- **Sympathetic tone on.** β3-mediated detrusor relaxation; α1-mediated bladder-neck tone.
- **Somatic tone on.** EUS tonically contracted via pudendal input from Onuf's nucleus.
- **Parasympathetic tone off.** No detrusor contraction.
- **Guarding reflex.** Any transient rise in intravesical pressure (cough, sneeze, laugh, positional) triggers a spinal reflex that contracts the EUS more strongly. This is the mechanical basis of normal stress continence.<sup>[[11]](#ref11)</sup>

### Voiding (switched by the pons)

- Conscious decision and cortical permission release the tonic inhibition of the pontine micturition center.
- PMC output coordinates external-sphincter relaxation with parasympathetic pelvic-nerve output to contract the detrusor against an opening outlet.
- Sympathetic storage tone is simultaneously inhibited.
- The coordinated voiding reflex is modulated by sensory input and higher centers; incomplete or interrupted voiding can occur.<sup>[[10]](#ref10)</sup>

### When the switch fails

- **Detrusor–sphincter dyssynergia (DSD).** Impaired coordination associated with some suprasacral neurological lesions can produce detrusor contraction against an involuntarily contracting sphincter, incomplete emptying and potentially unsafe pressures. Management includes bladder-pressure reduction, reliable emptying and surveillance; surgery is reserved for selected patients when less invasive treatment is inadequate.<sup>[[14]](#ref14)</sup>
- **Detrusor overactivity.** Involuntary detrusor contractions during filling; neurogenic (suprapontine or suprasacral) or idiopathic. The target of antimuscarinics, β3-agonists, intradetrusor onabotulinumtoxinA, and sacral neuromodulation.
- **Underactive or acontractile detrusor.** Can result from sacral or peripheral lesions or non-neurological causes and may present with incomplete emptying or retention. Management depends on the cause, pressure and residual volume; intermittent catheterization is one option, with selected patients considered for other treatments.
- **Bladder outlet obstruction.** Mechanical resistance (BPH, urethral stricture, bladder-neck contracture) may raise voiding pressures and contribute to detrusor hypertrophy, trabeculation, diverticula or reduced compliance. Treatment ranges from observation and medication to outlet surgery according to cause, symptoms, complications and response.<sup>[[18]](#ref18)</sup>

### Illustrative adult observations

These are teaching examples, not diagnostic cutoffs or universal treatment targets. Age, sex, bladder sensation, measurement method and voided volume matter; interpret individual values with the complete assessment.<sup>[[12]](#ref12)[[18]](#ref18)</sup>

| Parameter | Illustrative value or interpretation |
|---|---|
| Functional bladder capacity | Often roughly 400–600 mL in teaching references; a 42-person healthy-volunteer study measured mean maximum cystometric capacity of 500 ± 165 mL in men and 560 ± 185 mL in women<sup>[[23]](#ref23)</sup> |
| First desire to void | In that cohort, 215 ± 105 mL in men and 235 ± 110 mL in women; not a diagnostic limit<sup>[[23]](#ref23)</sup> |
| Compliance | Report ΔV/ΔPdet and trace quality; thresholds depend on population and context |
| Post-void residual | Interpret with symptoms, repeated measurements and the clinical setting |
| Peak flow (Qmax) | Healthy-women pooled mean 28 mL/s (95% CI 27–30) across heterogeneous studies; interpret either sex with voided volume and pressure-flow findings when needed. Low flow alone does not distinguish obstruction from detrusor underactivity.<sup>[[19]](#ref19)[[24]](#ref24)</sup> |
| Voiding diary | Record volume and frequency over time rather than assuming one normal daily pattern |

## Clinical Correlations for the Reconstructive Urologist

- **Unsafe storage and upper-tract protection.** The historical 40 cmH₂O pressure-at-leak observation came from 42 patients with myelodysplasia, not a universal detrusor-pressure boundary. Identify high storage pressures, poor compliance, reflux and upper-tract change together; restore safe storage with medical or, when needed, surgical treatment.<sup>[[13]](#ref13)[[14]](#ref14)</sup>
- **Wall remodeling and diverticula.** Chronic outlet obstruction may cause detrusor hypertrophy, trabeculation and acquired pseudodiverticula, whose muscular wall is deficient. Congenital true diverticula can contain muscular fibers, often thin ones. Poor emptying or complications can prompt treatment; assess the outlet and individual anatomy before diverticulectomy.<sup>[[15]](#ref15)[[21]](#ref21)</sup>
- **Anti-reflux at the UVJ.** The intramural ureter and its bladder-wall course help limit reflux. Trigonal or ureteral-orifice resection can affect that junction, so reconstruction must account for the remaining anatomy; no one tunnel ratio applies to every repair. See [The Ureters](/docs/foundations/anatomy-physiology/urinary-tract/ureter-anatomy-physiology).
- **Nerve-sparing in cystectomy and prostatectomy.** Pelvic autonomic fibers lie near posterolateral dissection planes. Their preservation depends on anatomy, oncologic or other operative constraints, and the technique used; it cannot be promised by pedicle position alone.<sup>[[17]](#ref17)</sup>
- **Lymphadenectomy templates.** Obturator, external iliac, and internal iliac basins drain the bladder primarily; standard LND in muscle-invasive cancer covers these, with extended templates reaching the common iliac and presacral regions.
- **Neurogenic bladder by lesion level.** Lesion location suggests a possible phenotype but does not replace individual assessment. High storage pressure, low compliance, reflux and upper-tract findings are more actionable than lesion level alone.<sup>[[14]](#ref14)</sup>
- **Bladder-neck competence matters after prostatectomy.** The internal (involuntary) urethral sphincter is the bladder neck. In most radical prostatectomies it is incorporated into the urethrovesical anastomosis; continence after surgery therefore depends on the external sphincter and on anatomical preservation of the striated-sphincter complex and its innervation.
- **Urachal / umbilical abnormalities.** Urachal remnants (cysts, sinuses, patent urachus) present across the lifespan; urachal adenocarcinoma arises from the dome / anterior wall and typically requires partial cystectomy en bloc with the urachus and umbilicus.
- **Rupture and trauma.** Blunt intraperitoneal rupture, often at the distended dome, generally requires operative repair. Small uncomplicated intraperitoneal injuries during endoscopic procedures can be treated conservatively in selected patients. Uncomplicated blunt extraperitoneal injuries generally receive catheter drainage; bladder-neck involvement, intravesical bone fragments or associated injuries may require repair.<sup>[[16]](#ref16)</sup>
- **Sensory signaling in IC/BPS and OAB.** The urothelium is a sensor as well as a barrier. ATP, prostanoids, NO and afferent signaling are implicated in urgency and bladder pain, but these pathways do not establish one mechanism for every patient. Intradetrusor onabotulinumtoxinA may act on sensory as well as motor pathways.<sup>[[4]](#ref4)[[8]](#ref8)[[9]](#ref9)</sup>
