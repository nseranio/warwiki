# The Bladder

The urinary bladder is a distensible muscular reservoir that stores urine and participates in coordinated emptying through the urethra. This page is a source-bounded anatomy and physiology orientation, not a diagnostic, urodynamic, surgical, or treatment protocol.

![Bladder anatomy — male and female](/img/anatomy/openstax-bladder.jpg)

*Urinary bladder anatomy. OpenStax Anatomy & Physiology, CC BY 4.0.*

## Gross anatomy and regional relationships

The bladder lies in the extraperitoneal pelvis behind the pubic bones and can extend into the abdomen as it fills. It is commonly described by an apex, body, fundus (base), trigone, and neck. The **trigone** is the triangular region bounded by the ureteric orifices and internal urethral orifice; it provides a consistent cystoscopic landmark.

The anterior bladder is related to the [Retropubic Space](/docs/foundations/anatomy-physiology/pelvis-support/retropubic-anatomy). Posterior and inferior relationships differ by pelvic anatomy and include genital, colorectal, and pelvic-floor structures. The dome is covered by peritoneum; other surfaces have extraperitoneal relationships. Procedure-specific planes and surgical landmarks require dedicated sources.

## Bladder wall

From lumen outward, the bladder wall includes urothelium, lamina propria, muscularis propria (detrusor), and outer adventitial or serosal covering depending on location. Urothelium is a specialized distensible epithelium whose umbrella cells and tight junctions contribute to a barrier between urine and deeper tissue. The detrusor is smooth muscle arranged as an integrated functional unit that contracts during emptying.

The lower bladder includes the trigone and vesicourethral junction. The distal ureters enter obliquely through the bladder wall, a relationship relevant to the ureterovesical junction. Detailed tumor staging, reflux mechanisms, compliance thresholds, and reconstructive implications belong in focused clinical pages and source packages.

## Neural control of storage and emptying

Bladder function depends on coordinated autonomic, somatic, and central neural control. Parasympathetic fibers from S2–S4 promote detrusor contraction during emptying. Sympathetic pathways support storage by influencing the detrusor and bladder outlet. The pudendal nerve provides voluntary somatic control of the external urethral sphincter.

Sensory information from the bladder contributes to the regulation of filling and emptying. The pontine micturition center coordinates detrusor and outlet activity with input from higher central nervous system pathways. This integrated control means that symptoms and testing results should not be assigned to one structure or pathway without a focused clinical evaluation.
