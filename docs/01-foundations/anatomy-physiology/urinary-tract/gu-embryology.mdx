# GU Embryology

This page is a source-bounded orientation to genitourinary development. It is not a diagnostic algorithm, genetic-counseling resource, or management guide for congenital conditions or differences of sex development.

## Urinary-system development

Much of the urinary and reproductive system arises from intermediate mesoderm. The pronephros regresses early, the mesonephros functions transiently and contributes to parts of the reproductive system, and the metanephros becomes the permanent kidney.

The permanent kidney develops through reciprocal interaction between the **ureteric bud** and **metanephric mesenchyme**. The ureteric bud contributes to the collecting system, including the ureter, renal pelvis, calyces, collecting ducts, and collecting tubules. Metanephric mesenchyme gives rise to nephron components. Disruption of these coordinated developmental processes can contribute to congenital anomalies of the kidney and urinary tract, but an individual anomaly’s cause and clinical significance require focused evaluation.

The cloaca separates into urogenital and anorectal passages during development. The urogenital sinus contributes to lower-urinary-tract structures, while the allantoic remnant becomes the urachus/median umbilical ligament.

## Gonads and internal genital ducts

The bipotential gonad develops in the urogenital ridge. In typical testicular development, SRY-associated signaling supports testis differentiation; Sertoli cells produce anti-Müllerian hormone and Leydig cells produce testosterone. In typical ovarian development, ovarian differentiation follows a distinct active genetic program.

Early embryos have paired mesonephric (Wolffian) and paramesonephric (Müllerian) ducts. Testosterone supports Wolffian-duct derivatives such as the epididymis, vas deferens, seminal vesicles, and ejaculatory ducts. Anti-Müllerian hormone promotes regression of Müllerian ducts in typical testicular development. In typical female internal-genital development, Müllerian ducts contribute to the fallopian tubes, uterus, and upper portion of the vagina, while Wolffian ducts regress.

## External genitalia

External genital development begins from common primordia, including the genital tubercle, urethral folds, labioscrotal swellings, and urogenital sinus. Androgen signaling is important for typical external masculinization; dihydrotestosterone has a particular role in external genital and prostate development. Developmental pathways are biologically complex and do not determine an individual’s identity or predict clinical needs without appropriate multidisciplinary assessment.

## Developmental variation

Congenital urinary-tract, genital-tract, and sex-development variations can arise from changes in organ formation, migration, duct development, hormone signaling, or genetic regulation. Broad categories include renal and ureteric anomalies, Müllerian and Wolffian duct variations, and differences of sex development. This overview intentionally does not provide disease-specific prevalence, genetic-risk estimates, surveillance, testing, or surgical recommendations.
