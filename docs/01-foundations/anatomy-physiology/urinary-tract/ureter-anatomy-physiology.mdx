# The Ureters

The ureters are paired retroperitoneal muscular tubes that convey urine from the renal pelvis to the urinary bladder through coordinated contractions. This page is a source-bounded anatomy and physiology orientation; it does not provide diagnostic, stone, injury-prevention, reconstructive, or treatment guidance.

## Course and regional relations

Each ureter begins at the ureteropelvic junction and descends in the retroperitoneum over the psoas muscle. It enters the bony pelvis near the iliac vessels, follows the pelvic sidewall, and enters the posterolateral bladder through the trigonal region.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[2]](#ref2)</sup>

In the pelvis, the ureter has important sex-specific relations. The uterine artery passes anterior to the distal ureter in typical female anatomy, whereas the vas deferens crosses anteriorly to the distal ureter in typical male anatomy. These are regional anatomy observations; their procedural implications depend on the operation and individual anatomy.<sup>[[2]](#ref2)</sup>

## Wall, vascular supply, and nerves

The ureteral wall has a urothelial mucosa, a smooth-muscle layer, and an outer adventitia. The adventitia contains vessels, lymphatics, and nerves. Smooth muscle in the wall generates the contractions that transport urine.<sup>[[2]](#ref2)</sup>

Blood supply is segmental and varies along the ureter. The proximal ureter receives branches associated with the renal circulation, while the more distal ureter receives branches associated with gonadal, iliac, vesical, uterine, vaginal, or rectal circulations as applicable. Because vascular patterns vary, this is an anatomic overview rather than a dissection plan.<sup>[[2]](#ref2)</sup>

Autonomic and sensory fibers accompany the ureter. Ureteral pain and its assessment require clinical context and are not defined by anatomy alone.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup>

## Peristalsis and the ureterovesical junction

Peristaltic activity begins in the renal-pelvis region and propagates along the ureter to move urine toward the bladder. The ureter enters the bladder obliquely through an intramural segment; this geometry contributes to a passive antireflux mechanism as the bladder fills.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[3]](#ref3)</sup>

The ureteropelvic and ureterovesical junctions are recognized points of narrowing. Their clinical interpretation, including evaluation for obstruction, belongs on focused condition and evaluation pages.<sup>[[2]](#ref2)</sup>
