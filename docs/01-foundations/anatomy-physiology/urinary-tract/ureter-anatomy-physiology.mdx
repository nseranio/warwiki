# The Ureters

The ureters are paired retroperitoneal muscular tubes approximately 25–30 cm long that pump urine from the renal pelvis to the bladder.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[2]](#ref2)</sup> They combine a tenuous blood supply that is easily devascularized, a course that is anatomically predictable but iatrogenically vulnerable, and physiologic behaviors (peristalsis, anti-reflux, response to obstruction) that determine how a reconstruction will function. This article focuses on the surgical anatomy, vascular supply, and functional behavior that matter at the table, not a molecular catalog of pacemaker channels.

---

## Segmental Anatomy

The ureter is conventionally divided into three segments based on the iliac vessels and the bony pelvis. This division is practical: it maps to the course on imaging, the expected relations during dissection, and the typical injury mechanisms for each zone.<sup>[[2]](#ref2)</sup>

| Segment | Landmarks | Key relations |
|---|---|---|
| **Proximal (upper)** | UPJ → crossing of the sacroiliac joint / iliac vessels | On psoas; gonadal vessels cross anterior ~mid-length; aorta/IVC medial; lower pole of kidney / retroperitoneal fat lateral |
| **Middle** | Over the iliac vessels and bony pelvis | Crosses the **bifurcation of the common iliac artery** — the most reliable anatomic landmark on laparoscopy |
| **Distal (pelvic/lower)** | From iliac vessels → UVJ | Deep to superior vesical and uterine/vas deferens structures; enters the bladder obliquely |

### Course and relations

From superior to inferior:

- **Retroperitoneal on psoas.** Lies on the psoas major, medial to the genitofemoral nerve, covered by peritoneum. Mobilization requires recognition that the psoas is *deep* to the ureter.
- **Gonadal vessels.** The gonadal artery and vein cross anterior to the ureter ("water runs over bridge") at approximately the level of the lower pole of the kidney / mid-ureter. In orchiectomy and ureteral dissection for retrocaval or high ureter, the two structures must be distinguished before ligation.
- **Common iliac bifurcation.** The ureter crosses anterior to the bifurcation of the common iliac artery into internal and external iliac arteries. This is the most reliable landmark to find the ureter during pelvic or vascular surgery.
- **Pelvic brim.** After crossing the iliac vessels the ureter descends along the lateral pelvic sidewall against the obturator internus, posterior to the ovary in women, medial to the vas deferens in men.
- **Cardinal ligament and the uterine artery ("water under the bridge").** In the female pelvis the uterine artery crosses anterior (superior) to the ureter near the cervix within the cardinal ligament; the distance varies with anatomy and pathology. This crossing carries an important hysterectomy injury risk from thermal injury, ligation in hemostatic clamps, or inclusion in a cardinal-ligament pedicle.
- **Vas deferens.** In men the vas crosses anterior to the distal ureter as it runs to the seminal vesicle.
- **Ureterovesical junction (UVJ).** The ureter enters the bladder obliquely, travels approximately 1.5–2 cm within the detrusor, and opens at the trigonal corner. The oblique intramural course is the structural basis of the passive anti-reflux mechanism (see below).

**Operative hotspots for ureteral injury.** The lower ureter is often involved in iatrogenic injury, but the exact site depends on the procedure and series:<sup>[[11]](#ref11)</sup>
1. The cardinal ligament / uterine artery crossing during hysterectomy
2. The IP (infundibulopelvic) ligament during oophorectomy: the gonadal vessels run here alongside the ureter
3. The bladder base / trigone during anterior colporrhaphy or anti-incontinence procedures
4. **Over the common iliac bifurcation** during vascular and sigmoid surgery
5. At the UPJ during renal / retroperitoneal dissection
Visual identification and careful dissection near the ureter are central to prevention; routine mobilization of the ureter before every adjacent ligation is not required.<sup>[[11]](#ref11)</sup>

## Blood Supply

The ureter has a segmental blood supply that is longitudinally redundant only because of an adventitial periureteric plexus running along its length. That plexus is why the ureter can be mobilized and yet survive, and why *stripping the adventitia destroys it*.

| Segment | Typical arterial supply | Approach direction |
|---|---|---|
| **Renal pelvis and proximal ureter** | Renal artery branches | Medial |
| **Abdominal (middle) ureter** | Gonadal artery, aorta, common iliac | Medial |
| **Pelvic ureter** | Internal iliac branches — uterine artery (F), superior/inferior vesical, middle rectal, vaginal (F) | Lateral |

Two practical consequences:

- **Adventitial preservation.** Dissect the ureter *with its surrounding adventitial tissue in continuity*. The longitudinal anastomotic plexus running in this adventitia is what perfuses the ureter between segmental feeders. Skeletonizing it, even during an otherwise elegant dissection, produces delayed ischemic stricture.
- **Approach direction matters.** Arterial branches generally reach the abdominal ureter medially and the pelvic ureter laterally. The operative priority is to preserve these segmental feeders and the periureteral adventitial plexus; the choice of dissection plane depends on the exposure and reconstruction, not a universal "opposite-side" rule.<sup>[[12]](#ref12)</sup>

### Venous and lymphatic drainage

Veins parallel the arteries. Lymphatics follow the course of the ureter: renal and proximal ureteral lymph drains to the para-aortic and interaortocaval basins, middle to common iliac, distal to internal iliac and obturator nodes.

## Innervation and Referred Pain

Ureteral innervation is autonomic (sympathetic and parasympathetic) with a dense adventitial and intramural plexus, plus sensory afferents traveling with the sympathetic roots.<sup>[[8]](#ref8)[[9]](#ref9)[[10]](#ref10)</sup>

The operative points:

- **Peristalsis is myogenic, not neurogenic.** Complete autonomic denervation does not abolish peristalsis; the ureter continues to contract. This is why ureter reimplantation, mobilization, and even transposition can work despite disrupting the extrinsic nerve supply.
- **Referred pain follows the sympathetic roots.** The roots are T10–L2. Ureteral pain refers to the flank (upper segment), periumbilical region or groin (middle), and the scrotum, labium majus or anterior thigh (lower or intramural segment), mirroring the migrating stone.
- **Sensory density increases distally** and is sex-dependent, with higher adventitial nerve density in women, particularly in the distal ureter.<sup>[[9]](#ref9)</sup>

## Ureteral Wall, Peristalsis, and What Matters at the Table

The ureter is a three-layer tube: urothelium, smooth muscle arranged in heterogeneously oriented bundles (not discrete circular and longitudinal layers), and adventitia carrying the vessels and nerves.<sup>[[3]](#ref3)[[4]](#ref4)</sup> Peristalsis is generated by pacemaker cells at the pelvis–kidney junction, morphologically distinct atypical smooth muscle cells that fire spontaneous depolarizations and drive a propagating wave distally.<sup>[[6]](#ref6)[[7]](#ref7)</sup>

For operative reasoning the relevant features are:

- **Unidirectional pump, not a passive tube.** Urine is delivered in discrete boluses, 5–7 s per contraction, and the oblique UVJ closes between waves. Any reconstruction that replaces ureter with passive conduit (ileal ureter, Boari flap if capacitance is misjudged) must account for the loss of active propulsion.
- **Acute vs. chronic obstruction can behave differently.** Acute obstruction can increase contractile activity and produce colic. Chronic obstruction can disrupt propagation and coordination of peristalsis, as demonstrated in a porcine model; this does not establish that every dilated human ureter permanently loses contractile function after decompression.<sup>[[13]](#ref13)</sup>
- **Peristalsis depends on L-type calcium channels.** Nifedipine blocks action potential, calcium transient, and contraction; this is relevant clinically only as a footnote for medical expulsive therapy of distal stones.<sup>[[5]](#ref5)</sup>
- **Stents alter, but do not invariably abolish, peristalsis.** A porcine experiment found an initial increase followed by reduced or absent activity with the tested stents; another reported abnormal waves and flow predominantly around the stent after prolonged placement. These animal observations should not be treated as a universal human mechanism or as proof that a stent makes a kink safe.<sup>[[14]](#ref14)[[15]](#ref15)</sup>

## The Ureterovesical Junction and Anti-reflux Mechanism

The UVJ is a passive valve, not a sphincter. Its competence depends on three features, any of which can be disrupted by disease or surgery:<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[2]](#ref2)</sup>

1. **Oblique intramural course.** A submucosal tunnel that is compressed as the bladder fills (flap-valve principle). Tunnel length relative to ureteral diameter contributes to anti-reflux competence. The historical 5 : 1 ratio is a teaching guide, not a universally required measured target; a finite-element study found that applying it at operative bladder fill may overestimate the needed length.<sup>[[16]](#ref16)</sup>
2. **Detrusor backing.** The ureter lies between mucosa and detrusor so filling pressure closes the tunnel against its muscular floor.
3. **Trigonal continuity.** The longitudinal ureteral muscle continues as the superficial trigone and deep trigone, anchoring the orifice and maintaining tunnel geometry.

Surgical implications:

- Anti-reflux reimplantation (for example, Lich-Gregoir, Cohen cross-trigonal or Politano-Leadbetter) aims for a supported, adequately long tunnel; some adult reimplantations with a psoas hitch or Boari flap intentionally use a refluxing anastomosis, so a submucosal tunnel is not mandatory in every reconstruction.<sup>[[18]](#ref18)</sup>
- Loss of trigonal continuity (radical cystectomy with neobladder, extensive trigone resection) removes the native mechanism; refluxing ureteral anastomoses to ileal or colonic reservoirs rely on low reservoir pressure rather than a true valve.

## Clinical Correlations for the Reconstructive Urologist

- **Stone impaction is not confined to three textbook narrowings.** The UPJ, iliac crossing and UVJ are useful landmarks, but in a 94-patient CT series of single stones at emergency presentation, only one stone was at the iliac crossing and most were at the UVJ. This cohort does not define all stone distributions.<sup>[[17]](#ref17)</sup>
- **Iatrogenic injury prevention is anatomy, not force.** Positively identify the ureter and dissect carefully near it while preserving adventitial blood supply. Selective stenting can aid identification in complex cases, but its injury-prevention benefit is uncertain and it does not replace visual identification.<sup>[[11]](#ref11)</sup>
- **Long-segment defects: location, length and vascularity.** Reconstruction depends on the site and extent of the defect, viable tissue, bladder capacity and patient factors. Ureteroureterostomy may suit selected shorter defects; psoas hitch or Boari flap can bridge distal defects; bowel interposition or renal autotransplantation are selected salvage options. Preserve adventitia at cut edges when feasible.<sup>[[11]](#ref11)</sup>
- **Megaureter and post-obstructive dilation.** Chronic obstruction may impair contractile coordination; recovery after relief or reconstruction varies with the underlying lesion and degree of damage. A dilated proximal ureter should not be presumed irreversibly aperistaltic solely from its appearance.<sup>[[13]](#ref13)</sup>
- **Vesicoureteral reflux and the 5 : 1 rule.** Tunnel geometry is one determinant of anti-reflux competence. The historical 5 : 1 ratio is a planning heuristic rather than a proven universal threshold in every operative state or reconstruction.<sup>[[16]](#ref16)</sup>
- **Denervation tolerance.** Because peristalsis is myogenic, extensive ureteral mobilization and transposition can be tolerated functionally if the adventitial blood supply is respected.
- **Sex-specific relations.** In women the uterine-artery crossing is an important pelvic operative hazard; in men the vas deferens crosses the distal ureter. Which relation matters most depends on the procedure and exposure.
