# Steroids — Perioperative Management

The perioperative plan should answer three different questions: **does this patient need adrenal replacement or stress coverage, is an antiemetic steroid appropriate, and how does chronic exposure affect healing?** A single dexamethasone dose and long-term prednisone therapy have different evidence and risks. This workflow uses the 2024 ESE/Endocrine Society guideline for glucocorticoid-induced adrenal insufficiency.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup>

See also: [Corticosteroid pharmacology](/docs/foundations/pharmacology/perioperative-eras/corticosteroids), [Immunosuppression](/docs/foundations/perioperative-care/preoperative-assessment/immunosuppression), [Nausea & Vomiting](/docs/foundations/perioperative-care/intraoperative-care/nausea-vomiting), and [Wound Healing](/docs/foundations/surgical-principles/wound-healing).

## Identify Adrenal-Insufficiency Risk

Record the drug, route, current and previous doses, duration, recent taper, last dose, prior adrenal testing/crisis and interacting medication. Oral exposure expected to pose risk generally exceeds **both 3–4 weeks and a physiologic daily equivalent**: approximately hydrocortisone 15–25 mg or prednisone/prednisolone 4–6 mg. These ranges are guides, not absolute safe/unsafe boundaries.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup> The 2020 UK Association of Anaesthetists/Royal College of Physicians/Society for Endocrinology guideline uses a similar rule of thumb: prednisolone 5 mg/day or more for a month or more by oral, inhaled, intranasal, intra-articular or topical routes may suppress the axis.<sup>[[7]](#ref7)</sup>

- Known adrenal insufficiency and exogenous Cushingoid features require an explicit coverage plan.
- A current prednisone dose below 5 mg does not prove recovery after a higher-dose course or taper.
- A genuinely short course under 3–4 weeks usually does not require tapering or routine HPA testing, but consider repeated exposure and the clinical context.
- Recent discontinuation counts. Mortality is higher in the period just after long-term oral glucocorticoids stop, so a patient who stopped weeks ago without recovery testing is still "at risk" for surgical planning.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup>
- Inhaled, topical, injected and other non-oral steroids can suppress the axis. Higher potency/dose, prolonged use, simultaneous formulations, recent intra-articular injections and strong CYP3A4 inhibitors increase concern. No single inhaled-dose cutoff clears every preparation or patient.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup>

For patients with current/recent exposure in whom glucocorticoid-induced adrenal insufficiency has not been excluded, the guideline recommends stress coverage during relevant stress. The route and dose depend on severity, absorption and the baseline regimen. This is not an instruction to give every person using any steroid a major-surgery infusion.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup>

### Testing for Recovery

When long-term treatment is no longer needed and the patient has reached a physiologic dose, a **morning cortisol** is the guideline's first test for HPA recovery if testing is chosen. Obtain it at 8–9 AM after an appropriate medication hold—usually at least 24 hours; dexamethasone requires separate consideration. Arrange this with the prescribing team rather than withholding necessary treatment around surgery.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup>

| Morning cortisol in this recovery-testing context | Guideline interpretation |
|---|---|
| Above 10 μg/dL (approximately 300 nmol/L) | Recovery is likely; stopping replacement can be considered when the underlying indication permits. |
| 5–10 μg/dL | Continue physiologic replacement and repeat after weeks to months. |
| Below 5 μg/dL (approximately 150 nmol/L) | Continue replacement and repeat after a few months. |

Interpret values as a continuum, using the assay and clinical setting. Estrogen, low binding proteins, critical illness and sleep disruption affect total cortisol. These are **recovery thresholds**, not universal diagnostic cutoffs for every adrenal disorder. Routine dynamic testing is discouraged during taper; when necessary, the standard 250-microgram ACTH test and assay-specific interpretation are preferred over routinely using the 1-microgram test.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup>

## Preserve Required Treatment Without Duplicating Doses

Avoid an unplanned interruption of necessary glucocorticoid therapy. If oral treatment cannot be taken or absorbed, prescribe an appropriate parenteral regimen. Count the glucocorticoid exposure supplied by that regimen: do not automatically add the full oral dose again to an IV dose that already replaces it and provides stress coverage. Document the return to the usual treatment, particularly in transplant recipients or active inflammatory disease.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[2]](#ref2)</sup>

## Stress Coverage

The following is a summary of **ESE/Endocrine Society Table 8 for adults at risk of or with diagnosed glucocorticoid-induced adrenal insufficiency**. These suggested schedules are based substantially on clinical practice; head-to-head evidence is limited and some centers use lower-dose regimens. Agree the plan with anesthesia/endocrinology when risk or recovery is uncertain.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup>

### Minor Stress or a Procedure Under Local Anesthesia

If the patient already takes hydrocortisone at least 40 mg/day, prednisone at least 10 mg/day or dexamethasone at least 1 mg/day, an increase is usually unnecessary for minor stress when stable and absorbing medication.

For a local-anesthetic procedure in someone needing coverage at a lower dose, the table gives **oral hydrocortisone 40 mg total**: 20 mg one hour before, then 10 mg six hours later and another 10 mg after a further six hours. Prednisone 10 mg or dexamethasone 1 mg one hour before are alternative schedules for patients using those drugs at lower doses. Continue increased coverage if still unwell; do not lower a higher baseline dose to these examples.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup>

The actual anesthetic and stress matter. Office cystoscopy, stent exchange, intravesical therapy and scrotal surgery under local anesthesia fit this tier. Cystoscopy or stent work performed under general anesthesia does not automatically follow the office/local schedule.

### General or Regional Anesthesia

Table 8 suggests **hydrocortisone 100 mg IV at induction**, followed immediately by **200 mg over 24 hours**, or 50 mg IV every six hours if infusion is unavailable. Typical urologic examples are TURP, pyeloplasty, ureteral reimplantation, robotic prostatectomy and urethroplasty; the guideline does not classify procedures by name, so match the plan to anesthesia type and expected recovery. For prolonged NPO or long recovery (radical cystectomy with diversion, reconstruction involving bowel, complex posterior urethroplasty, septic emergency surgery, cesarean section), continue the 200 mg/24 h infusion (or 50 mg every six hours) while NPO. Once recovery is uncomplicated and oral absorption is reliable, use increased oral coverage for 48 hours—examples are hydrocortisone 40 mg/day, prednisone 10 mg/day or dexamethasone 1 mg/day—then return to the required preoperative regimen. Preserve a higher baseline dose; infection, reoperation or ongoing illness requires reassessment.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup>

A patient already receiving hydrocortisone at least 200 mg/day, prednisone at least 50 mg/day or dexamethasone approximately 6–8 mg/day usually does not need additional glucocorticoid for moderate/major stress, but the route must remain reliable. These thresholds do not eliminate the need to evaluate deterioration or missed absorption.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup>

Labor and vaginal delivery use the same 100 mg bolus at onset followed by 200 mg/24 h. Cesarean section is treated as the NPO/long-recovery tier. Primary adrenal insufficiency with mineralocorticoid requirements needs an endocrine plan rather than a generic urologic procedure table.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup>

## Suspected Adrenal Crisis

Consider crisis with hypotension, collapse, severe weakness, vomiting/diarrhea, abdominal pain, fever, altered mental status, hyponatremia, hypoglycemia or unexplained deterioration in a patient with known or possible adrenal insufficiency. **Do not wait for fluid-resistant shock or laboratory confirmation.** Treat competing causes such as hemorrhage and sepsis simultaneously.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[2]](#ref2)</sup>

1. Give **hydrocortisone 100 mg IV or IM immediately**.
2. Start prompt isotonic fluid resuscitation, individualized to perfusion, cardiac/renal status and ongoing losses. NICE specifies 1 L of 0.9% saline over 30 minutes in adults with crisis; reassess closely for overload.
3. Continue **hydrocortisone 200 mg/24 hours by infusion**, or 50 mg IV/IM every six hours.
4. Monitor blood pressure, heart rate, electrolytes and glucose; treat hypoglycemia and the precipitating illness.
5. Obtain cortisol/ACTH before treatment if immediately feasible, but never delay hydrocortisone or resuscitation. Arrange endocrine guidance for taper and oral transition.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[2]](#ref2)[[3]](#ref3)</sup>

Hyperkalemia particularly suggests mineralocorticoid-deficient **primary** adrenal disease or another cause; aldosterone is usually preserved in glucocorticoid-induced insufficiency, for which routine fludrocortisone is not indicated.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup>

After stabilization, provide a clear sick-day plan, an emergency steroid card/medical alert and access to an emergency hydrocortisone injection kit with training when indicated. Do not assume a universally available autoinjector.<sup>[[2]](#ref2)[[3]](#ref3)</sup>

## Dexamethasone for PONV

Use the [PONV workflow](/docs/foundations/perioperative-care/intraoperative-care/nausea-vomiting) and [corticosteroid hub](/docs/foundations/pharmacology/perioperative-eras/corticosteroids) for antiemetic selection and doses. Coordinate this with stress coverage; hydrocortisone replacement has not been established as an equivalent antiemetic regimen. A non-steroid antiemetic may avoid unnecessary duplicate glucocorticoid exposure.

**PADDI** randomized 8,880 adults undergoing nonurgent noncardiac surgery to a single 8-mg IV dexamethasone dose or placebo. Among 8,678 in the primary analysis, 30-day SSI occurred in 8.1% versus 9.1%, meeting noninferiority; 24-hour PONV was lower. This supports the studied single dose, not prolonged glucocorticoid exposure or a guarantee of unaffected graft/anastomotic healing. Patients with primary infection and HbA1c above 9% were excluded. Transient hyperglycemia remains relevant, including in patients without diabetes.<sup>[[4]](#ref4)</sup>

## Chronic Exposure and Reconstruction

Glucocorticoids blunt fibroblast proliferation and collagen synthesis, epithelialization, angiogenesis and neutrophil and macrophage function. Duration matters more than a single exposure. A review of animal and human studies found that high-dose systemic corticosteroids for under 10 days had no clinically important effect on wound healing, whereas patients on chronic corticosteroids for at least 30 days before surgery had wound complication rates about 2 to 5 times those of non-users, varying with dose, duration, comorbidity and operation. The animal data (about 30% lower wound tensile strength at 15 to 40 mg/kg/day) are not directly transferable to clinical doses.<sup>[[5]](#ref5)</sup>

For reconstruction, expect the effect on any healing-dependent step: buccal or skin graft take, urethral and ureteral anastomoses, flap survival, wound dehiscence after open pelvic surgery, bowel anastomotic leak after diversion or bowel-based reconstruction, and infection around prosthetic implants. No study cited here gives a prednisone cutoff for buccal-graft failure, urethral restricture or AUS/IPP infection, so counsel on higher baseline risk rather than a threshold.<sup>[[5]](#ref5)</sup>

Before elective reconstruction, coordinate any taper to the lowest dose that safely controls the underlying disease; do not taper solely for surgery when this would destabilize disease or threaten an allograft. Optimize nutrition, glucose, vitamin D and calcium and other modifiable risks, and assess fracture risk under the relevant glucocorticoid-osteoporosis pathway, which matters for prolonged positioning in pelvic surgery. Document consent that healing and infection risk are higher.

Operation-specific guidance should remain scoped. **ECCO's 2026 UC surgical guideline** recommends tapering corticosteroids below 20 mg prednisolone-equivalent before elective proctectomy/restorative surgery, based on low-level evidence. That is not a validated 10- or 20-mg cancellation threshold for every urologic graft, flap or prosthesis.<sup>[[6]](#ref6)</sup>

## Evidence and Controversy

The Table 8 stress-dose regimens rest on physiology and clinical practice, not randomized comparisons. The ESE/Endocrine Society authors state that head-to-head evidence is lacking, that practice varies among centers, and that lower doses are also routinely used in moderate or major stress.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup> The UK guideline likewise calls for a pragmatic approach and says blanket recommendations are not appropriate.<sup>[[7]](#ref7)</sup>

The comparative evidence points the other way, toward giving the usual dose and not adding a large supraphysiologic course:

- **Systematic review.** Marik and Varon found nine studies (two randomized trials, seven cohorts; 315 patients, 389 procedures). In the trials, hemodynamics did not differ between stress dosing and the usual daily dose alone. No patient in the five cohorts that continued the usual dose developed unexplained hypotension or adrenal crisis. The two episodes of unexplained hypotension occurred in cohorts in which the usual dose had been stopped 36 to 48 hours before surgery, and both responded to hydrocortisone and fluid.<sup>[[10]](#ref10)</sup>
- **Randomized trial, secondary adrenal insufficiency.** Glowniak and Loriaux randomized 18 patients taking prednisone at least 7.5 mg daily, with ACTH-confirmed secondary adrenal insufficiency, to perioperative cortisol or saline on top of the usual prednisone dose. Most had major operations. One patient in each group became hypotensive, and both responded to volume; blood pressure and pulse rate were otherwise similar.<sup>[[11]](#ref11)</sup> The trial is small.
- **Randomized noninferiority trial, colorectal surgery.** In 92 steroid-treated patients with IBD having major colorectal surgery, IV hydrocortisone matched to the presurgical oral dose (then tapered) was noninferior to hydrocortisone 100 mg three times daily for postural hypotension on postoperative day 1 (96% versus 95% free of it).<sup>[[12]](#ref12)</sup>
- **Narrative reviews and other guidelines.** Freudzon reports a low incidence of perioperative adrenal insufficiency in chronic glucocorticoid users and small studies showing no survival or hemodynamic difference when stress doses were withheld; the studies are limited in size and applicability, and every patient should take the usual daily dose.<sup>[[9]](#ref9)</sup> The 2021 WSES/SIS-E/WSIS/AAST/GAIS guideline on the acute abdomen in immunocompromised patients found no evidence that a push dose is needed for surgery in patients on current or recent steroids, and advised that they stay on their usual regimen. It gives hydrocortisone 100 mg IV for unexplained, fluid-unresponsive hypotension around surgery. Its grades are moderate strength on intermediate- or low-quality evidence, and it defines chronic therapy as prednisone 20 mg/day or more for at least 3 weeks.<sup>[[13]](#ref13)</sup> A 2026 rheumatology review reaches the same practical position: continuing the home dose is adequate for most patients, and stress dosing is reserved for biochemically confirmed adrenal dysfunction or supraphysiologic doses with moderate- or high-risk surgery.<sup>[[14]](#ref14)</sup>

### Where the Frameworks Disagree

The **ACR/AAHKS 2022 guideline** conditionally recommends continuing the current daily glucocorticoid dose rather than giving supraphysiologic doses on the day of surgery, graded low-quality evidence. Its scope is narrow: adults with rheumatoid arthritis, ankylosing spondylitis, psoriatic arthritis or lupus who take glucocorticoids for that disease and are having elective total hip or knee arthroplasty. It does not apply to primary adrenal insufficiency or primary hypothalamic disease, or to patients with juvenile idiopathic arthritis who received glucocorticoids in childhood. The authors state that they cannot generalize the recommendation to other operations, though the underlying principles may offer a framework. The recommendation is unchanged from the 2017 guideline, and the panel notes that no randomized trials have appeared since then.<sup>[[8]](#ref8)</sup> It is best read as one representative of a wider continue-the-usual-dose approach, not as an orthopedic exception: the trials and reviews above studied general surgical and mixed populations.

The **ESE/Endocrine Society 2024 guideline** recommends stress coverage for patients with current or recent use in whom adrenal insufficiency has not been excluded. It treats a baseline of prednisone 10 mg/day or more as enough for minor stress and 50 mg/day or more as enough for moderate or major stress.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup>

The two disagree for patients on chronic prednisone below 50 mg/day having moderate or major surgery: ESE gives scheduled hydrocortisone stress dosing, whereas ACR/AAHKS, and the trials and reviews above, support the usual dose with rescue for hypotension. Neither position rests on a large randomized trial. The Table 8 schedule above is the default structure of this page; the practical approach below is the lower-intensity alternative.

### Practical Default for Low-Dose Chronic Prednisone

For a patient on chronic prednisone below 10 mg/day having moderate or major surgery under general anesthesia:

1. Give the usual dose on the morning of surgery and never miss it.
2. Consider a single hydrocortisone 100 mg IV bolus at induction. This is a common compromise between the two frameworks, not a recommendation of either.
3. Do not add a prolonged supraphysiologic taper; the randomized data give no signal that one is needed.<sup>[[10]](#ref10)[[12]](#ref12)</sup>
4. Keep hydrocortisone 100 mg IV available as a rescue for unexplained hypotension that does not respond to fluid or vasopressors, and consider adrenal insufficiency alongside hemorrhage and sepsis.<sup>[[13]](#ref13)</sup>

Reserve the full multi-day Table 8 schedule for higher chronic doses, and for known or strongly suspected HPA suppression (Cushingoid features, a recent higher-dose course, or abnormal testing). Primary adrenal insufficiency and hypothalamic-pituitary disease are outside this shortcut and need the endocrine plan. Agree the approach with anesthesia and the prescribing team.

## Urologic Quick Reference

| Scenario | Approach |
|---|---|
| Transplant recipient on prednisone 5 mg/day, elective reconstruction under general anesthesia | Continue the baseline dose. Under the ESE schedule add hydrocortisone 100 mg IV at induction and 200 mg/24 h, then increased oral coverage for 48 hours; the lower-intensity alternative is the usual dose, an optional single 100 mg bolus and a rescue bolus. Agree the plan with the transplant team. |
| IBD patient on prednisone 15 mg/day, cystectomy with diversion | Baseline is at or above 10 mg, but this is major stress with NPO. Use the NPO/long-recovery regimen unless the patient is at or above the 50 mg threshold. |
| Rheumatoid arthritis on prednisone 7.5 mg/day, pyeloplasty | General anesthesia, short recovery: Table 8 gives an induction bolus plus 24-hour infusion, then oral increase for 48 hours. The lower-intensity alternative is the usual dose, an optional single 100 mg bolus and a rescue bolus. |
| Chronic low-dose prednisone, sling revision under local anesthesia | Already at 10 mg/day or more: usually no increase. Below that: raise to prednisone 10 mg one hour before. |
| Inhaled fluticasone for asthma, standard dose | Risk is low, but no single inhaled dose clears every patient; consider recent oral bursts and potent combinations. |
| Unexplained postoperative shock with steroid history | Hydrocortisone 100 mg IV or IM immediately; do not wait for testing. |

The transplant, IBD and rheumatoid rows follow Table 8 for adults at risk of glucocorticoid-induced adrenal insufficiency.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup> The alternative in the first and third rows is described under Where the Frameworks Disagree.

## Perioperative Handoff

Record the baseline indication/dose, adrenal-risk assessment, route while NPO, planned stress regimen, monitoring, oral transition and who will adjust the plan if complications occur. Emergency treatment and chronic immunosuppression changes answer different clinical problems and should not become a single automatic order set.
