# Perioperative Nutrition

Nutrition is the unglamorous axis on which reconstructive outcomes actually turn. An otherwise perfectly constructed ileal-conduit bowel anastomosis leaks if the patient is hypoalbuminemic and catabolic. A urethroplasty buccal graft fails to take in a patient who stopped eating three weeks before the operation. Malnutrition is present in **20–70% of patients undergoing major abdominal surgery** and is an independent predictor of SSI, anastomotic leak, wound dehiscence, and mortality.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[2]](#ref2)</sup> This article covers the evidence-based perioperative nutrition pathway — screening and assessment, preoperative optimization, immunonutrition, the EN/PN hierarchy, parenteral-nutrition formulation and complications, refeeding syndrome, and the reconstructive-urology-specific considerations.

See also: [Wound Healing](/docs/foundations/surgical-principles/wound-healing), [Frailty](/docs/foundations/perioperative-care/preoperative-assessment/frailty), [ERAS](/docs/foundations/perioperative-care/perioperative-protocols/eras), [Bowel Anastomosis](/docs/foundations/surgical-principles/bowel-anastomosis).

## Why Nutrition Matters in GU Reconstruction

Nutrition intersects every outcome the reconstructive urologist cares about:

- **Wound healing** — protein, arginine, vitamin C, zinc, and vitamin A are specific substrates for collagen synthesis, epithelialization, and angiogenesis. Low serum albumin is the single best-characterized predictor of wound-healing failure.
- **Anastomotic integrity** — hypoalbuminemia is independently associated with anastomotic leak after bowel surgery and is a driver of conduit / neobladder leak.
- **Infection resistance** — immunocompetence depends on nutritional status; malnourished patients have higher SSI rates at every contamination class.
- **Graft take** — BMG and bladder-mucosa grafts depend on inosculation-phase angiogenesis, which fails in the catabolic patient.
- **Functional recovery** — sarcopenia and frailty (overlapping but distinct from malnutrition) drive delirium, immobility, and delayed ambulation.

The operational principle: **every patient being considered for major elective reconstruction should be screened, and every screened-at-risk patient should be optimized before the index operation whenever the surgical timeline permits.**

## Nutritional Screening and Assessment

**Screen every surgical patient within 24–48 h of admission** and at regular intervals thereafter.<sup>[[5]](#ref5)</sup>

### Validated Screening Tools

| Tool | Strengths | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| **MUST** (Malnutrition Universal Screening Tool) | Best overall validity in surgical cohorts (~80% sensitivity/specificity)<sup>[[6]](#ref6)</sup> | Uses BMI, weight loss, acute-illness-no-intake |
| **NRS-2002** (Nutritional Risk Screening) | Widely used; identifies ~24% at risk<sup>[[6]](#ref6)</sup> | ESPEN standard; stratifies nutritional status + disease severity |
| **MNA-SF** (Mini Nutritional Assessment — Short Form) | Best discriminatory ability (AUC 0.83); identifies ~58% at risk<sup>[[6]](#ref6)</sup> | Geriatric surgical focus |
| **GLIM** (Global Leadership Initiative on Malnutrition) | Consensus diagnostic framework<sup>[[7]](#ref7)</sup> | Phenotypic + etiologic criteria |

### Indicators of Severe Nutritional Risk<sup>[[8]](#ref8)</sup>

- **BMI &lt;18.5 kg/m²**
- **Unintentional weight loss &gt;10–15% in 6 months**
- **Poor appetite** / intake &lt;50% of baseline for &gt;1 week
- **Serum albumin &lt;3.0 g/dL** (best single lab marker in the surgical patient)
- **Active inflammation / catabolic state**

Albumin is an imperfect marker — it is a negative acute-phase reactant and falls in inflammation independent of nutritional status — but remains the most widely used and most prognostic lab value in the surgical literature.

## Nutrition and Wound Healing — The Reconstructive Evidence

The reconstructive case for aggressive nutritional optimization is not abstract — every dose-response signal in the literature traces back to specific wound-healing endpoints: collagen synthesis, graft take, anastomotic integrity, and flap survival.

### Albumin as the Dominant Preoperative Predictor

A pooled analysis of **200,015 plastic-surgery patients** (NSQIP) found that **albumin ≤ 3.3 g/dL** was associated with a **36.5% complication rate vs 10.4%** above threshold, and reduced albumin was the single strongest independent predictor of any complication (OR 0.460 per g/dL of albumin).<sup>[[32]](#ref32)</sup> Disease-specific data converge:

- **Breast reduction** — hypoalbuminemia drives 30-day morbidity (Knoedler 2025).<sup>[[33]](#ref33)</sup>
- **Autologous breast reconstruction** — hypoalbuminemia + obesity → 16% wound-complication rate vs ~ 9% with either alone (Chiang 2023).<sup>[[34]](#ref34)</sup>
- **Head-and-neck free-flap reconstruction** — malnutrition increases flap-failure risk **3.4×** and 30-day mortality nearly **4×** (Herzog 2024).<sup>[[35]](#ref35)</sup>
- **Free-tissue transfer (NSQIP n = 34,571)** — frailty and hypoalbuminemia have compounding risk-predictive power; the combination is multiplicative, not additive (Panayi 2024).<sup>[[36]](#ref36)</sup>

The operational corollary: **every elective major reconstruction warrants preoperative albumin**, and a value < 3.5 g/dL is an indication for nutritional intervention before the index operation when timing permits.<sup>[[6]](#ref6)[[32]](#ref32)</sup>

### Protein — the Cornerstone Macronutrient

Protein supplies the substrate for collagen, angiogenesis, and humoral immunity.<sup>[[9]](#ref9)</sup> Target intake is **1.2–1.5 g/kg/day perioperatively** for the general reconstructive patient and **up to 60–120 g/day** for post–massive-weight-loss / body-contouring patients.<sup>[[37]](#ref37)[[38]](#ref38)</sup> A controlled abdominoplasty trial of early postoperative protein supplementation (≥ 1.2 g/kg/day initiated within 24 h) showed:<sup>[[39]](#ref39)</sup>

- **Wound dehiscence 6% vs 17%**
- **Seroma 9% vs 18%**
- Healing time shorter by **~ 4 days**

Practical sources: whey isolate, hydrolyzed collagen peptides, oral nutritional supplements (ONS) within 24 h postoperatively.

### Targeted Micronutrients

Evidence by wound type, distilled from the *Plast Reconstr Surg* systematic review and downstream RCTs:<sup>[[40]](#ref40)</sup>

| Nutrient | Wound-healing role | Strongest reconstruction-relevant evidence |
|---|---|---|
| **Vitamin C** | Collagen hydroxylation, neutrophil chemotaxis, antioxidant | Skin incisions, pressure ulcers |
| **Zinc** | DNA / protein synthesis, cell division | Pressure / venous / burn wounds |
| **Vitamin A** | Epithelial differentiation, anti-inflammatory | Burn, diabetic ulcers |
| **Vitamin D** | Immune modulation, tissue repair | Burn, diabetic ulcers |
| **Iron** | Oxygen transport, collagen hydroxylation | Post-bariatric, GLP-1 RA users |
| **Arginine** | Nitric-oxide / collagen substrate | Pressure ulcers, surgical wounds |
| **Vitamin B12** | Cell proliferation, neurologic function | Post-bariatric / ileal-conduit patients |

The Kjaer 2020 inguinal-hernia RCT demonstrated that a multinutrient regimen of **arginine 14 g + glutamine 14 g + vitamin C 1250 mg + zinc 55 mg daily for 14 days pre- and post-surgery significantly increased collagen synthesis** during early wound repair.<sup>[[41]](#ref41)</sup> This is the cleanest mechanistic-endpoint data supporting multinutrient bundling.

### Reconstruction-Specific Take

- **Urethroplasty** — BMG take depends on inosculation-phase angiogenesis; the catabolic patient loses grafts. Pre-op albumin and protein supplementation matter as much as harvest technique.
- **Bowel anastomosis (conduit / neobladder / augmentation)** — albumin < 3.5 g/dL is independently associated with leak; immunonutrition (next section) is the highest-evidence intervention.
- **Free flaps (gracilis, DIEP, ALT, fibula for phalloplasty)** — frailty + hypoalbuminemia compound failure risk; both should be screened preoperatively.<sup>[[35]](#ref35)[[36]](#ref36)</sup>

## Preoperative Optimization

### Fasting and Carbohydrate Loading

Part of the ERAS pathway — see [ERAS](/docs/foundations/perioperative-care/perioperative-protocols/eras):<sup>[[9]](#ref9)[[10]](#ref10)</sup>

- **Clear liquids permitted to 2 h** before induction.
- **Solids to 6–8 h** before induction.
- **Complex-carbohydrate (maltodextrin) drink** the evening before + 2 h before induction in non-diabetic patients — attenuates insulin resistance and reduces LOS by 0.3–1.7 days in major abdominal surgery.

### Oral Nutritional Supplementation for At-Risk Patients

- **Target protein intake: 1.2–1.5 g/kg/day.**<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[10]](#ref10)</sup>
- **Duration: 7–21 days preoperatively** depending on severity of risk.<sup>[[11]](#ref11)</sup>
- Documented reduction in postoperative complications vs no supplementation.

### Prehabilitation

Combined exercise + nutrition + smoking/alcohol cessation for 4–6 weeks before major elective reconstruction. For pelvic oncology (radical cystectomy) there is RCT-level evidence of improved functional recovery; nutritional supplementation alone also improves outcomes independently.<sup>[[10]](#ref10)</sup>

## Immunonutrition

### Definition

Enteral formulas supplemented with **arginine, omega-3 fatty acids (fish oil), glutamine, and/or nucleotides** to modulate the inflammatory response.<sup>[[12]](#ref12)</sup>

### Evidence in GI Cancer Surgery

Meta-analytic outcomes:<sup>[[13]](#ref13)[[14]](#ref14)[[15]](#ref15)</sup>

- **Infectious complications** ↓ 42–48% (OR 0.52–0.58)
- **SSI** ↓ (OR 0.65)
- **Anastomotic leak** ↓ (OR 0.67)
- **LOS** ↓ 1.5–2 days
- Mortality — no significant effect

Benefit is seen **regardless of baseline nutritional status**, and the combination of **perioperative (preop + postop)** immunonutrition outperforms either alone (OR 0.32 for postoperative morbidity).<sup>[[15]](#ref15)[[16]](#ref16)</sup>

### Controversy

Effect size varies by control-group choice (isocaloric standard supplement vs unsupplemented diet) and by industry sponsorship; effects are smaller when compared against an equivalent calorie/protein control.<sup>[[10]](#ref10)</sup>

### Practical Use in Urologic Reconstruction

Immunonutrition for **5–7 days preoperatively** is the highest-evidence nutritional intervention for **radical cystectomy with urinary diversion** — baseline malnutrition rates reach 39% and 30-day complications exceed 50%.<sup>[[42]](#ref42)[[43]](#ref43)</sup> Urology-specific data have matured beyond the GI-cancer extrapolation:

- **Khaleel 2021 (n = 204)** — retrospective, preoperative arginine-based IMN: **infection 25% vs 45%** (OR 0.35), TPN requirement 17% vs 36% (OR 0.38).<sup>[[44]](#ref44)</sup>
- **Hamilton-Reeves 2018 pilot RCT** — perioperative IMN shifted the **Th1/Th2 balance toward Th1**, **peak IL-6 lower by 43%**, and prevented postoperative arginine depletion.<sup>[[45]](#ref45)</sup>
- **Amer 2025 RCT (n = 74)** — structured perioperative nutritional optimization (caloric / protein adjustment + IMN): **time to flatus 40 vs 65 h**, **LOS 7.8 vs 10.6 days**, better preservation of skeletal muscle mass at 30 days.<sup>[[46]](#ref46)</sup>
- **Cochrane (Burden 2019)** — immuno-enhancing nutrition may reduce 90-day complications (RR 0.31); evidence quality low.<sup>[[42]](#ref42)</sup>
- **INCyst trial (Derré 2024, n = 232)** — multicenter RCT of preoperative IMN in cystectomy; results expected after 2026.<sup>[[47]](#ref47)</sup>

Beyond cystectomy, immunonutrition has less clear benefit for non-bowel urologic reconstruction (urethroplasty, prosthetics, fistula repair without bowel handling); preoperative ONS + albumin optimization remain the workhorses there.

### Urogynecologic ERAS Nutrition

The **AUGS-IUGA 2022 Joint Consensus Statement on ERAS After Urogynecologic Surgery** and **ACOG Committee Opinion 750** codify the nutritional pillars across prolapse, incontinence, and pelvic-floor procedures:<sup>[[48]](#ref48)[[49]](#ref49)</sup>

- **Solid-food fasting 6 h, clear fluids permitted to 2 h** before surgery (high-level evidence).
- **Carbohydrate loading** (≥ 45 g clear-carbohydrate drink at 2 h) reduces insulin resistance and shortens LOS by ~ 1.5 days in pooled abdominal-surgery data.<sup>[[50]](#ref50)</sup>
- **Regular diet within 24 h postoperatively**, high-protein ONS while oral intake builds; chewing gum to reduce postoperative ileus.
- **IV fluid discontinuation within 24 h** when oral intake is adequate.

## The EN / PN Hierarchy

Preference order:<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[4]](#ref4)</sup>

1. **Oral nutrition / oral supplements** — always first when feasible.
2. **Enteral nutrition (EN)** — tube feeding via NG, nasojejunal, PEG, PEJ, or surgical jejunostomy.
3. **Parenteral nutrition (PN)** — intravenous when enteral access is impossible or insufficient.

**EN is preferred over PN whenever the gut works.** EN maintains the gut mucosal barrier, supports immune function, reduces infection, and costs less.<sup>[[4]](#ref4)[[18]](#ref18)</sup>

---

## Enteral Nutrition (EN)

### Indications

- High nutritional risk + inadequate oral intake.
- Functioning GI tract without contraindication to luminal nutrients.
- Expected inadequate oral intake for &gt;5–7 days.

### Contraindications

- Complete bowel obstruction
- Bowel discontinuity (unsafe to enter lumen)
- Bowel ischemia
- Ongoing peritonitis

### Timing

- **Initiate within 24 h of surgery when feasible.**<sup>[[17]](#ref17)[[18]](#ref18)</sup>
- Early EN reduces mortality (6.8% → 2.4%) and complications (RR 0.53) vs delayed feeding.

### Formula Selection<sup>[[18]](#ref18)</sup>

| Type | Composition | Use |
|---|---|---|
| **Polymeric (standard)** | Whole protein, intact carb, LCT | Normal GI function |
| **Semi-elemental / elemental** | Peptides or free amino acids, MCT-rich | Malabsorption, pancreatic insufficiency, recent GI surgery |
| **Disease-specific** | Modified for renal, hepatic, pulmonary, glycemic | Per indication |
| **Immunonutrition** | + arginine, omega-3, glutamine, nucleotides | Perioperative cancer surgery |

## Parenteral Nutrition (PN) — TPN vs PPN

### Indications

- EN not feasible or insufficient to meet caloric/protein needs.
- Major upper-GI surgery with anticipated NPO duration ≥7 days.
- High-output enterocutaneous fistula.
- Severe malnutrition preceding major elective reconstruction when enteral route is inadequate.

### Timing

- **ESPEN:** start PN if EN is contraindicated or has been inadequate for &gt;7 days.<sup>[[3]](#ref3)</sup>
- **ASPEN:** start PN within 3–5 days if EN is insufficient in the high-nutritional-risk patient.<sup>[[17]](#ref17)</sup>

### TPN vs PPN — Selection

| Feature | **PPN** (Peripheral) | **TPN** (Central) |
|---|---|---|
| Access | Peripheral IV | Central line (PICC, tunneled, port) |
| Osmolarity limit | **≤900 mOsm/L** | No limit |
| Duration | **7–14 days maximum** | Weeks to indefinite |
| Dextrose concentration | ≤10% | 10–25% |
| Caloric delivery | Often hypocaloric | Full caloric requirements |
| Fluid volume | Larger (2.5–3 L/day) | Concentrated, smaller volumes |
| Typical indication | Bridge therapy, EN supplement, short-term | Long-term, high calorie needs, fluid-restricted |
| Dominant complication | Phlebitis, extravasation | CLABSI, catheter thrombosis |

**PPN patient-selection criteria:**<sup>[[20]](#ref20)</sup>

- No fluid restriction
- Stable electrolytes
- Adequate renal function for fluid load
- Not severely catabolic
- Duration expected ≤10–14 days
- Goal: prevent rather than correct nutritional deficit

## TPN Composition

### Macronutrients<sup>[[18]](#ref18)[[19]](#ref19)[[21]](#ref21)</sup>

**Dextrose (carbohydrate):**

- **3.4 kcal/g**
- Available 2.5–70% concentrations
- Target 125–200 g/day (protein-sparing up to 200 g)
- Infusion rate limit: **4–5 mg/kg/min** in critically ill, **5–7 mg/kg/min** in stable patients
- Supplies 70–85% of non-protein energy

**Amino acids (protein):**

- 3–20% concentration
- **0.10–0.15 g nitrogen/kg/day basal; 0.15–0.20 g nitrogen/kg/day in hypermetabolic states**
- ≈ **1.0–1.5 g protein/kg/day** in most surgical patients

**Lipids:**

- 10%, 20%, 30% emulsions (1.1, 2.0, 2.9–3.0 kcal/mL)
- Provide 30–50% of non-protein energy
- Essential fatty acid requirement: ~200 g lipid emulsion/week
- **Soybean-oil–based** (Intralipid) — high omega-6, linked to hepatobiliary complications (PNALD)
- **Newer mixed-oil formulations (SMOFlipid — soybean + MCT + olive + fish oil; Omegaven — fish oil alone)** — reduced inflammation, less hepatotoxicity<sup>[[18]](#ref18)[[22]](#ref22)</sup>

### Micronutrients<sup>[[18]](#ref18)[[23]](#ref23)</sup>

| Component | Daily requirement | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| Sodium | 1–2 mEq/kg | Adjust for losses |
| Potassium | 1–2 mEq/kg | Monitor closely |
| Calcium | 10–15 mEq | |
| Magnesium | 8–20 mEq | |
| **Phosphorus** | 20–40 mmol | Critical for refeeding-syndrome prevention |
| Multivitamins | Daily | All fat- and water-soluble |
| Trace elements | Daily | Cr, Cu, Mn, Se, Zn |
| Vitamin K | As needed | Omit if on warfarin |

### Formulation Types<sup>[[18]](#ref18)</sup>

- **Three-in-one (TNA — total nutrient admixture):** dextrose + amino acids + lipid in one bag.
- **Two-in-one:** dextrose + amino acids together; lipid separately.

## TPN Complications

### Catheter-Related<sup>[[18]](#ref18)[[19]](#ref19)[[24]](#ref24)</sup>

- **CLABSI** — most common serious complication.
- Catheter-related thrombosis.
- Exit-site infection.
- Occlusion, mechanical damage, dislodgement.

### Metabolic<sup>[[18]](#ref18)[[19]](#ref19)[[25]](#ref25)</sup>

| Complication | Driver | Management |
|---|---|---|
| Hyperglycemia | Excess dextrose, insulin resistance | Limit dextrose infusion rate, insulin |
| Hypoglycemia | Abrupt PN discontinuation | **Taper PN gradually — do not stop abruptly** |
| Hypertriglyceridemia | Excess lipid | Limit to 1 g/kg/day, monitor trigs |
| Electrolyte imbalance | Inadequate provision, refeeding | Daily labs, adjust formulation |
| Refeeding syndrome | Rapid feeding in malnourished patient | See dedicated section |
| Hepatic dysfunction (PNALD) | Overfeeding, soybean lipid, absent enteral feeding | Cyclic PN, mixed-oil / fish-oil lipid, maintain some EN |
| Metabolic bone disease | Long-term PN, aluminum contamination | Vitamin D, calcium, phosphorus monitoring |
| Azotemia | Excess protein, dehydration | Adjust amino acids for renal function |

### Parenteral Nutrition–Associated Liver Disease (PNALD)

Conjugated bilirubin ≥2 mg/dL in a patient on prolonged PN, other causes excluded.<sup>[[26]](#ref26)</sup>

- Incidence: up to 50% of infants on long-term PN; up to 80% in low-birth-weight infants with intestinal failure; histologic changes as early as 14 days.<sup>[[26]](#ref26)[[27]](#ref27)</sup>
- **Progression:** steatosis → cholestasis → chronic hepatitis → cirrhosis → liver failure.
- **Mechanism:** soybean-oil lipid (high stigmasterol, pro-inflammatory omega-6, low vitamin E), absent enteral feeding, dysbiosis, sepsis, caloric overload.<sup>[[26]](#ref26)[[28]](#ref28)</sup>
- **Prevention and treatment:**<sup>[[22]](#ref22)[[27]](#ref27)[[28]](#ref28)[[29]](#ref29)</sup>
  - Maintain some enteral feeding whenever feasible.
  - Cyclic PN rather than continuous.
  - Avoid overfeeding.
  - **Fish-oil lipid (Omegaven) or mixed-oil (SMOFlipid)** — can reverse cholestasis.
  - Limit soybean-oil dose.
  - Prevent/treat line sepsis aggressively.
  - Preserve intestinal length.

## Refeeding Syndrome

### Pathophysiology

In the chronically malnourished patient, refeeding with carbohydrate triggers insulin release, which drives intracellular shift of phosphate, potassium, and magnesium. Thiamine requirement spikes.<sup>[[19]](#ref19)[[30]](#ref30)</sup>

### Clinical Manifestations

- **Cardiac:** arrhythmia, heart failure.
- **Respiratory:** weakness, respiratory failure (phosphate-dependent diaphragm function).
- **Neurologic:** confusion, ataxia, ophthalmoplegia — **Wernicke encephalopathy** from thiamine depletion.
- Fluid overload, edema.

### Diagnostic Criteria<sup>[[5]](#ref5)</sup>

- **Imminent:** &gt;30% phosphate drop from baseline, or phosphate ≤0.6 mmol/L within 72 h of starting nutrition.
- **Manifest:** electrolyte shifts + clinical symptoms.

### Risk Factors (NICE Criteria)<sup>[[5]](#ref5)</sup>

- BMI &lt;16 kg/m²
- Unintentional weight loss &gt;15% in 3–6 months
- Insufficient nutritional intake for &gt;10 days
- Low baseline K, phosphate, or Mg
- History of alcohol or drug use
- Recent medications — insulin, chemotherapy, antacids, diuretics

### Prevention and Treatment<sup>[[19]](#ref19)[[5]](#ref5)[[31]](#ref31)</sup>

**Prophylactic supplementation — before and during first 10 days:**

- **Thiamine 200–300 mg/day IV**
- **Potassium 2–4 mmol/kg/day**
- **Phosphate 0.3–0.6 mmol/kg/day**
- **Magnesium 0.2–0.4 mmol/kg/day**
- Full multivitamin

**Feeding approach:**

- **Start at 10–20 kcal/kg/day** (not goal-feed).
- Advance slowly to target over **5–10 days.**
- Limit sodium.
- Daily electrolyte monitoring.

## Postoperative Feeding in GU Reconstruction

### Early Oral / Enteral Feeding

ERAS principle: **drinks and food on day of surgery when tolerated; regular diet once liquids are tolerated, without waiting for flatus.**<sup>[[3]](#ref3)[[17]](#ref17)</sup>

### Supplemental Parenteral Nutrition (SPN)

For patients with poor EN tolerance after major abdominal surgery:<sup>[[2]](#ref2)</sup>

- **Early SPN (day 3)** with concurrent EN reduces nosocomial infection vs late SPN (day 8).
- Indicated when EN delivers &lt;60% of caloric target by day 3.

### Protein Focus After Discharge

Protein delivery matters more than total calorie delivery for healing.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup>

- **Target 1.2–1.5 g/kg/day.**
- Inadequate protein intake is common in the post-discharge setting — counsel explicitly.

### Reconstruction-Specific Considerations

- **Radical cystectomy + urinary diversion:** the benchmark complex GU operation for nutrition pathways — typical 30-day readmission is often nutrition-driven (failure-to-thrive, dehydration, metabolic acidosis). ERAS + alvimopan + no routine MBP is the standard.
- **Augmentation cystoplasty with bowel:** monitor for chronic metabolic acidosis from urinary reabsorption through the bowel segment; supplement bicarbonate as needed.
- **Ileal-conduit bowel-segment harvest:** preserve at least 20 cm terminal ileum for B12 and bile-salt absorption; monitor long-term B12.
- **Pouches and neobladders:** supplementation with bicarbonate and B12 is part of routine follow-up.
- **Urethroplasty / substitution grafts:** minor nutritional footprint except in malnourished patients; preoperative albumin &lt;3.0 g/dL is a reason to delay and optimize.

## GLP-1 Receptor Agonists in Perioperative Care

GLP-1 RAs (semaglutide, tirzepatide, liraglutide) have moved from a diabetes-and-obesity therapy to a population-scale exposure that the reconstructive surgeon now meets weekly. They reshape both **candidacy** (weight loss enables previously contraindicated procedures) and **risk** (appetite suppression, gastric emptying delay, micronutrient depletion).

### Expanding Surgical Candidacy

GLP-1 RA–mediated weight loss can move a patient from a class III obesity bucket — where free-flap and complex reconstructive outcomes are clearly worse — into class I/II. A study of **5,618 DIEP breast reconstruction patients** found class I/II obese patients on GLP-1 RAs had **lower revision rates** than class I/II obese non-users (p = 0.037) and class III obese patients (p = 0.046), consistent with the hypothesis that GLP-1-facilitated weight reduction improves reconstructive candidacy.<sup>[[51]](#ref51)</sup>

For reconstructive urology / urogynecology, the analogous candidacy questions are emerging: complex fistula repair, urethroplasty, sacrocolpopexy, AUS / IPP, abdominoperineal phalloplasty, and post–massive-weight-loss body contouring after concurrent panniculectomy.

### Wound Healing — Mixed Signal

The wound-healing evidence is conflicting and population-dependent:<sup>[[52]](#ref52)[[53]](#ref53)[[54]](#ref54)</sup>

| Study | Population | Wound signal |
|---|---|---|
| Lee 2025 (NSQIP-matched, non-diabetic) | Abdominoplasty / panniculectomy / breast reduction | **Higher** wound complications on GLP-1 RAs (abdominoplasty 9.8% vs 3.6%, p = 0.001) |
| Koenig 2026 (10-yr panniculectomy) | Non-bariatric panniculectomy | **More delayed healing** (18.5% vs 7.5%), **fewer seromas** (4.9% vs 14%) |
| Aschen 2025 (Ann Surg) | Broad surgical population including diabetics | **Lower** wound dehiscence (RR 0.71) and hematoma (RR 0.44) |

The most plausible reconciliation: in diabetics, improved glycemic control dominates; in non-diabetics on GLP-1 RAs for weight loss, **appetite suppression → protein deficit → impaired collagen synthesis** dominates. The mechanistic argument is supported by lab data — breast-surgery patients on GLP-1 RAs had significantly lower **serum albumin (4.06 vs 4.22 g/dL, p = 0.003)** and **prealbumin (23.52 vs 25.55 mg/dL, p = 0.02)**.<sup>[[55]](#ref55)</sup> Preclinical mouse / rat models paradoxically show GLP-1 RAs **promote** healing (VEGF up, TNF-α down, M2 macrophage polarization) — relevant to chronic wound biology but apparently overwhelmed by the protein deficit in acute surgical wounds.<sup>[[56]](#ref56)</sup>

### Perioperative Anesthesia — Aspiration Risk

The FDA has added pulmonary-aspiration warnings to all GLP-1 RA labels. The **2026 ADA Standards of Care** recommend a **personalized approach** rather than blanket holds — considering indication, dose, GI symptoms, surgery type, and urgency.<sup>[[57]](#ref57)</sup> Key strategies:

- **24-h clear-liquid diet** preoperatively
- **Preoperative gastric ultrasound** when feasible to assess residual gastric volume
- **Full-stomach precautions** if any GI-symptom suggestion of delayed emptying
- If the drug is held, implement an **alternative glycemic-management plan**

Counterintuitively, the **OCULUS RCT (JAMA Intern Med 2026)** in upper endoscopy found that patients who **held** their GLP-1/GIP agonists had **higher** clinically significant residual gastric volumes (25%) than those who **continued** (3%, p = 0.003) — challenging the assumption that holding the drug guarantees an empty stomach.<sup>[[58]](#ref58)</sup>

### Nutritional Optimization on GLP-1 RAs

The same biology that makes GLP-1 RAs effective for weight loss — appetite suppression and delayed gastric emptying — produces a **protein-deficit reconstructive patient** unless preempted. The Mehta 2025 *PRS* guidance for plastic surgeons recommends:<sup>[[37]](#ref37)</sup>

- **Individualized protein timing plans** (small, frequent, protein-anchored meals)
- **Spacing protein intake** throughout the day to maximize absorption against delayed gastric emptying
- **Aggressive preoperative correction of micronutrient deficiencies** (iron, B12, vitamin D, calcium, folate, thiamine)
- **Weight-stability confirmation** at ~ 8–12 months on therapy before body contouring; awareness of significant weight recurrence after drug discontinuation
- For post-bariatric patients specifically: minimum **2 adult multivitamins with minerals, 1200–1500 mg elemental calcium (citrated, divided doses), ≥ 3000 IU vitamin D, B12 supplementation** per AACE/TOS/ASMBS guidelines.<sup>[[59]](#ref59)</sup>

### Practical Reconstructive-Surgeon Checklist

1. **Ask about GLP-1 RA use at every preoperative visit** — patients often don't volunteer "weight-loss injections."
2. **Check albumin and prealbumin preoperatively**; correct if low.
3. **Coordinate with anesthesia** about hold-vs-continue per local protocol and the 2026 ADA personalized framework.
4. **Counsel about delayed wound healing**, particularly for abdominal-wall / breast / body-contouring procedures.
5. **Confirm weight stability** before elective body contouring; document baseline weight and trajectory.

## Monitoring

**Baseline:** CMP, CBC, triglycerides, magnesium, phosphorus, albumin, prealbumin.

**Patients on PN:**<sup>[[18]](#ref18)</sup>

- Daily labs until stable, then ≥ weekly.
- Glucose — check as needed for glycemic control (target 140–180 mg/dL).
- Triglycerides with lipid infusion.

**Long-term PN:** iron, ferritin, zinc, copper, folate, B12, vitamin D, vitamin K.

**Clinical:** weight, hydration status, signs of fluid overload or dehydration, catheter site, feeding tolerance.

## Key Recommendations

1. **Screen every surgical patient** for malnutrition within 24–48 h of admission.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[3]](#ref3)</sup>
2. **Optimize preoperatively** with oral supplementation (1.2–1.5 g/kg/day protein) for 7–21 days in malnourished patients.<sup>[[10]](#ref10)[[11]](#ref11)</sup>
3. **Carbohydrate loading** in non-diabetic patients as part of ERAS.<sup>[[9]](#ref9)</sup>
4. **Consider immunonutrition** for 5–7 days before major GI/oncologic surgery — particularly radical cystectomy.<sup>[[13]](#ref13)[[14]](#ref14)[[15]](#ref15)</sup>
5. **Use EN preferentially over PN** when the gut works.<sup>[[3]](#ref3)[[4]](#ref4)</sup>
6. **Initiate EN within 24 h of surgery** when feasible.<sup>[[17]](#ref17)</sup>
7. **Delay PN** to 5–7 days postoperatively unless high nutritional risk.<sup>[[3]](#ref3)[[4]](#ref4)</sup>
8. **Use mixed-oil or fish-oil lipid emulsions** to reduce PNALD risk.<sup>[[22]](#ref22)[[27]](#ref27)</sup>
9. **Prevent refeeding syndrome** — 10–20 kcal/kg/day start, thiamine 200–300 mg, phosphate/K/Mg repletion before and during first 10 days.<sup>[[5]](#ref5)[[19]](#ref19)</sup>
10. **Maintain some enteral feeding** whenever possible to preserve gut barrier and prevent PNALD.
