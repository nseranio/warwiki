# Perioperative Nutrition

Nutritional care in GU reconstruction centers on **identifying malnutrition, meeting actual intake deficits, choosing a safe feeding route and preventing refeeding complications**. Low albumin and loss of muscle identify risk, but neither establishes that a graft or anastomosis will fail. Decisions about postponing surgery depend on the nutritional assessment, urgency and expected benefit of optimization.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[2]](#ref2)</sup>

This is the perioperative workflow. [Nutritional Assessment](/docs/evaluation/laboratory-studies/nutritional-assessment) covers screening, examination, laboratory interpretation and body composition. See also [Frailty](/docs/foundations/perioperative-care/preoperative-assessment/frailty), [ERAS](/docs/foundations/perioperative-care/perioperative-protocols/eras), [Wound Healing](/docs/foundations/surgical-principles/wound-healing) and [Bowel Anastomosis](/docs/foundations/surgical-principles/bowel-anastomosis).

## Why Nutrition Matters in GU Reconstruction

Nutrition intersects the outcomes a reconstructive surgeon cares about.

- **Wound healing:** energy, amino acids, vitamins and trace minerals are the substrate for fibroblast proliferation, collagen synthesis and epithelialization; deficiencies impair each step.<sup>[[24]](#ref24)</sup>
- **Anastomoses and infection:** malnutrition and underfeeding are risk factors for postoperative complications, and ESPEN treats nutritional therapy as mandatory for surgical patients at nutritional risk.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup>
- **Graft and flap take:** buccal mucosa grafts and flaps depend on imbibition, inosculation and new vessel growth. It is reasonable to expect a catabolic patient to heal worse, but no urethroplasty-specific trial has shown that correcting a nutrition measure prevents graft failure.
- **Recovery:** sarcopenia and frailty, which overlap with but are distinct from malnutrition, drive delirium, immobility and delayed ambulation.

The working principle: screen every patient being considered for major elective reconstruction, and treat an identified deficit before the index operation when the surgical timeline allows.

## Assessment Before Major Reconstruction

Screen early enough to intervene before elective surgery, then reassess when intake or clinical condition changes. Ask about weight trajectory, recent intake, swallowing, vomiting/diarrhea, restrictive diets, prior bowel/bariatric surgery, alcohol use and ability to obtain or prepare food. Examine for muscle and fat loss, edema and functional impairment.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[2]](#ref2)</sup>

| Assessment | Appropriate use |
|---|---|
| **MUST** | Best-supported screen in a 1,649-patient multicenter major-abdominal-surgery cohort: sensitivity and specificity near 80% against SGA, with good prognostic validity<sup>[[23]](#ref23)</sup> |
| **NRS-2002** | ESPEN-associated screen combining nutritional status and disease severity; flagged only 24% of that cohort as at risk<sup>[[23]](#ref23)</sup> |
| **MNA-SF** | Suited to older adults; best discrimination on its ordinal scale (AUC 0.83) but flagged 58.6% as at risk, so follow an abnormal screen with assessment<sup>[[23]](#ref23)</sup> |
| **GLIM** | Diagnostic framework after screening: at least one phenotypic and one etiologic criterion; not itself a substitute for the initial screen |
| **Weight, intake, muscle and function** | Establish the deficit and monitor recovery; obesity does not exclude malnutrition |
| **Albumin / prealbumin** | Prognostic context influenced by inflammation, fluid balance, hepatic synthesis and renal/GI losses; not measures of body protein or adequate feeding |

Do not prescribe albumin infusion, supplements or postponement solely to normalize a laboratory value. Hypoalbuminemia should prompt evaluation of its cause and overall surgical risk. Its association with complications in plastic-surgery and free-flap cohorts does not prove that increasing albumin prevents GU graft failure.<sup>[[2]](#ref2)</sup>

ESPEN's high-metabolic-risk definition needs any one of: weight loss over 10–15% within six months, BMI below 18.5 kg/m², or NRS-2002 of at least 5 or SGA grade C (the 2017 version also listed albumin below 30 g/L after excluding hepatic or renal insufficiency). ESPEN 2025 accepts hypoalbuminemia as a prognostic factor but attributes it mainly to disease severity and catabolism, not nutritional status.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup>

## Nutrition and Wound Healing: The Reconstructive Evidence

Most of the data below are observational and come from plastic and reconstructive cohorts. They identify who is at risk. They do not show that correcting a laboratory value prevents graft, flap or anastomotic failure.

### Albumin as a Risk Marker

| Cohort | Finding |
|---|---|
| ACS-NSQIP plastic surgery, 200,015 patients | Albumin ≤3.3 g/dL: any complication 36.5% vs 10.4%; reduced albumin was the strongest independent predictor (OR 0.460)<sup>[[25]](#ref25)</sup> |
| NSQIP autologous breast reconstruction, 10,865 procedures | Wound complications 16% with obesity plus albumin below 3.5 g/dL, vs 9.2% for obesity alone or hypoalbuminemia alone<sup>[[26]](#ref26)</sup> |
| NSQIP head-and-neck free flap, 3,038 patients | Albumin below 3.5 g/dL: flap failure 6.3% vs 2.1% (OR 3.37, 95% CI 1.38–8.21); death OR 3.98 (1.70–9.63)<sup>[[27]](#ref27)</sup> |
| NSQIP free tissue transfer, 34,571 patients (albumin available in 16,250) | Frailty (mFI-5) and hypoalbuminemia each independently predicted complications; the two together predicted outcomes better than either alone<sup>[[28]](#ref28)</sup> |

Because albumin also falls with inflammation, read these figures as risk stratification and a prompt to look for the cause, not as a target to raise.<sup>[[2]](#ref2)[[1]](#ref1)</sup>

### Protein

Protein supplies the substrate for collagen, angiogenesis and immune function. For malnourished patients planning elective GI surgery, the ASCRS/SAGES guideline notes that oral supplements targeting **1.2–1.5 g/kg/day for 1–2 weeks** have been associated with fewer complications (weak recommendation, moderate-quality evidence).<sup>[[3]](#ref3)</sup> Body-contouring guidance after massive weight loss cites 60–120 g/day.<sup>[[38]](#ref38)</sup>

A retrospective cohort of 200 non-bariatric full abdominoplasty patients compared protein supplementation (at least 1.2 g/kg/day as whey isolate or hydrolyzed collagen, started within 24 hours and continued at least 14 days) with none. Dehiscence was **6% vs 17%** (P = .018), seroma **9% vs 18%** (P = .048) and time to complete healing **16.8 vs 20.5 days**. Surgical site infection (4% vs 10%) did not differ significantly. The design is observational and single-procedure.<sup>[[29]](#ref29)</sup>

### Micronutrients

Evidence for vitamins and minerals is wound-type specific. A systematic review of 36 studies (2,339 patients) found benefit reported for vitamin C and zinc in pressure ulcers, zinc in venous ulcers, vitamins A, D and E in diabetic ulcers and burns, and vitamin E in hypertrophic scars; it found no official recommendation for routine supplementation.<sup>[[30]](#ref30)</sup>

| Nutrient | Wound-healing role | Where trials report benefit<sup>[[30]](#ref30)</sup> |
|---|---|---|
| **Vitamin C** | Collagen hydroxylation, neutrophil function, antioxidant | Pressure ulcers |
| **Zinc** | DNA and protein synthesis, cell division | Pressure ulcers, venous ulcers, burns |
| **Vitamin A** | Epithelial differentiation, inflammatory modulation | Burns, diabetic ulcers |
| **Vitamin D** | Immune modulation, tissue repair | Burns, diabetic ulcers |

An exploratory randomized trial in 21 men undergoing Lichtenstein hernia repair gave **arginine 14 g, glutamine 14 g, vitamin C 1,250 mg and zinc 55 mg daily** from 14 days before to 14 days after surgery, on top of about 1.5 g/kg/day protein in both arms. Wound-fluid procollagen (CICP) rose from day 1 to day 2 only in the supplemented group and was 49% higher on day 2 (P = .10), and the serum collagen marker PRO-C5 fell postoperatively only in controls. These are mechanistic collagen-synthesis biomarkers, not clinical wound outcomes.<sup>[[31]](#ref31)</sup>

### Applying This in GU Reconstruction

- **Urethroplasty and grafts:** treat measured malnutrition, weight loss or poor intake before elective grafting. There is no urethroplasty-specific trial of albumin or protein optimization.
- **Bowel-containing reconstruction:** malnourished patients carry higher morbidity, so apply the ESPEN pathway below. See [Bowel Anastomosis](/docs/foundations/surgical-principles/bowel-anastomosis).<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup>
- **Free flaps** (gracilis, DIEP, ALT, fibula for phalloplasty): screen for frailty and malnutrition together, since the combined signal was stronger than either alone.<sup>[[27]](#ref27)[[28]](#ref28)</sup>

## Preoperative Optimization

- **Address a measured intake deficit:** food fortification, dietitian support and a balanced oral nutritional supplement (ONS) when ordinary food is insufficient. ESPEN recommends an ONS that matches the deficit in calories, protein and micronutrients before major abdominal surgery in malnourished or high-metabolic-risk patients.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup> A commonly used protein target in malnourished elective GI surgical patients is **1.2–1.5 g/kg/day for 1–2 weeks**; choose the dosing weight and modify the target for renal disease, obesity, catabolism and other individual factors.<sup>[[3]](#ref3)</sup>
- **Severe malnutrition / high metabolic risk:** ESPEN 2025 supports preoperative nutrition even when elective surgery must be postponed; consider **10–14 days**. Historical ESPEN high-risk criteria include marked weight loss, BMI below 18.5, NRS at least 5 or SGA C, or albumin below 30 g/L after excluding hepatic/renal insufficiency. The albumin criterion denotes metabolic/surgical risk, not a stand-alone malnutrition diagnosis.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[2]](#ref2)</sup>
- **Use oral/enteral delivery when feasible.** Preoperative PN is reserved for severe malnutrition/high metabolic risk when these routes cannot meet needs; ESPEN suggests 10–14 days, with a minimum of seven, in that selected group.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup>
- **Correct identified deficiencies:** avoid routine high-dose zinc, vitamin A or other micronutrient combinations without an indication. A small mechanistic wound study is not sufficient evidence for a universal graft-healing supplement regimen.
- **Combine nutrition with risk-adjusted prehabilitation:** exercise and management of smoking, alcohol use, symptoms and comorbidities should fit the available surgical timeline.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup>

### Fasting and Carbohydrate Drinks

For elective patients without increased aspiration risk, standard guidance allows clear liquids until two hours and a light meal until six hours before anesthesia; fatty/heavy meals require longer fasting. Follow the anesthetic plan for obstruction, delayed gastric emptying, emergency surgery or other increased-risk situations.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[3]](#ref3)</sup>

A protocolized carbohydrate drink (maltodextrin-type complex carbohydrate, given the evening before and up to two hours before anesthesia) may improve comfort and the metabolic response before major abdominal surgery. ESPEN grades this B/0; ASCRS/SAGES gives a weak recommendation for patients without diabetes. A Cochrane review of 27 trials found about 0.3 day shorter stay and no difference in overall complications, and a 2025 meta-analysis of 44 trials in major elective non-cardiac surgery found 0.56 day shorter stay (95% CI 0.02–1.10).<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[3]](#ref3)[[40]](#ref40)</sup> Do not prescribe carbohydrate drinks indiscriminately in suspected gastroparesis or poorly controlled diabetes. GLP-1/GIP therapy requires the additional assessment below.

## Immunonutrition: What Changes Practice

Immunonutrition adds selected substrates such as arginine, omega-3 fatty acids and nucleotides to standard nutrition. GI-cancer trials and meta-analyses underpin the **ESPEN 2025 grade B recommendation** to offer it preoperatively or perioperatively around major tumor surgery, for five to seven days before surgery in GI cancer. That recommendation predates the phase 3 SWOG cystectomy report.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup>

### GI-Cancer Evidence

| Analysis | Population | Result |
|---|---|---|
| Khan 2023 meta-analysis | 37 RCTs, 3,793 patients | Infectious complications OR 0.58 (0.47–0.72); SSI OR 0.65 (0.52–0.81); anastomotic leak OR 0.67 (0.47–0.93); length of stay 1.94 days shorter. Benefit was seen with preoperative or perioperative dosing and independent of nutritional status<sup>[[32]](#ref32)</sup> |
| Adiamah 2019 meta-analysis | 16 RCTs, 1,387 patients, preoperative Impact | Infectious complications OR 0.52 (0.38–0.71); stay 1.57 days shorter; no effect on noninfectious complications or mortality<sup>[[33]](#ref33)</sup> |
| Ricci 2023 component network meta-analysis | 87 studies, 8,375 patients, major abdominal surgery | Best morbidity result: perioperative enteral/oral arginine, glutamine and PUFA, OR 0.32 (0.10–0.98), moderate certainty with a wide interval; effects on mortality, infection and stay were uncertain<sup>[[34]](#ref34)</sup> |

Effect size varies with the control arm (isocaloric standard supplement vs unsupplemented diet) and with industry sponsorship, and is smaller against an equivalent calorie and protein control.<sup>[[3]](#ref3)</sup> These GI-cancer effects should not be assumed to transfer to cystectomy or non-bowel reconstruction.

### Radical Cystectomy — SWOG S1600, 2026

**Specialized immunonutrition did not significantly reduce the primary complication outcome compared with standard ONS.** This double-blind phase 3 trial randomized 203 patients at 13 US centers; 178 contributed to the primary analysis. Both groups received matched calorie/protein supplements for five days before and five days after cystectomy.<sup>[[4]](#ref4)</sup>

| Primary 30-day result | Specialized immunonutrition | Standard ONS |
|---|---:|---:|
| Any complication | **56/90 (62.2%)** | **51/88 (58.0%)** |

The odds ratio was **1.18 (95% CI 0.64–2.18)**. High-grade (Clavien-Dindo III–V) 30-day complications were 11.1% vs 12.5% (OR 0.86, 0.34–2.17). Adverse events, mostly nausea, were more frequent with immunonutrition (34.1% vs 20.5%). Severe malnutrition was excluded. This result does not establish equivalence, test supplements against no supplementation, or establish benefit in severely malnourished patients. Two-year survival numerically favored immunonutrition (overall survival 87.4% vs 78.2%) but the difference was not statistically significant and does not justify prescribing it for cancer control.<sup>[[4]](#ref4)</sup>

For cystectomy counseling, prioritize adequate nutrition; specialized formulations should not be presented as proven superior to standard ONS.

### Interpreting Earlier Evidence

- **Cochrane 2019:** eight cystectomy trials (500 patients) of mostly small, dated design. The immunonutrition comparison rested on 29 patients; the 90-day complication estimate was **RR 0.31, 95% CI 0.08–1.23**, with low certainty. The interval includes no benefit. In one 157-patient trial, parenteral nutrition increased 30-day complications compared with oral nutrition (RR 1.40, 1.07–1.82; low certainty).<sup>[[5]](#ref5)</sup>
- **Khaleel 2021, 204 patients (retrospective, single institution):** preoperative oral arginine-based immunonutrition was associated with less postoperative infection (25% vs 45%; adjusted OR 0.35) and less TPN use (17.3% vs 35.6%; OR 0.38), with no significant difference in high-grade complications, readmission, ileus or length of stay.<sup>[[35]](#ref35)</sup>
- **Hamilton-Reeves 2018 pilot RCT, 29 men:** five days of specialized immunonutrition before and after cystectomy shifted the Th1/Th2 balance toward Th1, lowered day-2 interleukin-6 by 42.8% and prevented the postoperative fall in plasma arginine. This is an immunologic endpoint, not a clinical outcome.<sup>[[36]](#ref36)</sup>
- **Amer 2026, 74 patients:** a combined calorie/protein and immunonutrition pathway improved recovery measures (time to flatus 39.6 vs 64.8 hours; length of stay 7.8 vs 10.6 days; wound healing 14.2 vs 15.5 days; better skeletal muscle preservation at 30 days), but the primary overall complication outcome was not significantly different (**54% vs 68%; P=.236**). The bundle cannot isolate the effect of immunonutrition.<sup>[[6]](#ref6)</sup>
- **INCyst:** the cited publication describes a planned 232-patient trial. A protocol is not an efficacy result; do not assign benefit or a firm publication date from it.<sup>[[7]](#ref7)</sup>

Evidence from cystectomy or GI cancer should not be assumed to demonstrate benefit in routine urethroplasty, prosthetic surgery or prolapse repair.

## Choosing and Starting Nutrition Support

**Oral food/ONS → enteral tube feeding when oral intake is inadequate → PN when oral/enteral delivery is contraindicated or insufficient.** Route and timing depend on physiology and the intake deficit, not simply the postoperative day.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup>

| Clinical situation | Practical approach |
|---|---|
| Awake, hemodynamically stable and tolerating intake after surgery | Resume oral intake early; routine waiting for flatus is unnecessary |
| Unable to meet needs orally, especially with malnutrition or expected prolonged inadequate intake | Consider EN within 24 hours when safe; select access according to anatomy and aspiration risk |
| Expected inability to eat for at least five days, or already below 50% of energy needs for over seven days | Begin nutrition therapy promptly rather than waiting for further deterioration |
| Oral/EN delivery remains below 50% of requirements within three to four days | ESPEN 2025 recommends supplemental PN to address the deficit |
| Oral/EN contraindicated, particularly with severe malnutrition | Start PN as soon as feasible with appropriate metabolic precautions |

Severe shock, suspected bowel ischemia, obstruction, uncontrolled GI bleeding or an unsuitable intestinal fistula may preclude EN. A functioning bowel alone does not establish that feeding is safe. Most postoperative patients who require tube feeding can use a balanced **standard polymeric formula**; recent bowel surgery alone does not mandate an elemental formula.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup>

ESPEN recommends starting EN within 24 hours when calories cannot be met orally, particularly when oral energy and protein intake is expected to stay below 50% for the next seven days. In malnourished or high-metabolic-risk patients, start EN at 10–30 mL/h and advance only while electrolytes stay stable; reaching the caloric target can take up to five days. Weigh intraoperative nasojejunal or jejunostomy access for esophagectomy, total gastrectomy and pancreatoduodenectomy; it is rarely relevant to GU reconstruction. Malnourished patients needing tube feeding beyond four weeks warrant PEG or jejunal access.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup>

### Why Early Supplemental PN Is Selective

In **Gao 2022**, 230 adults after elective major abdominal surgery were randomized to supplemental PN on day 3 or day 8. Eligibility required **NRS-2002 at least 3, EN at or below 30% of the energy target on day 2, and an expected stay longer than seven days**. Infection occurred in **10/115 (8.7%) vs 21/114 (18.4%)**, respectively; absolute difference 9.7 percentage points (95% CI 0.9–18.5). Length of stay and noninfectious complications were not significantly different.<sup>[[8]](#ref8)</sup>

This supports filling a substantial deficit in selected at-risk patients. It does not support automatic PN on postoperative day 3 after an uncomplicated reconstruction.

## Parenteral Nutrition: Prescription and Safety

**Peripheral/central describes venous access; supplemental/total describes how much nutrition PN provides.** These are separate decisions. Peripheral PN can bridge a short deficit when osmolarity, volume and venous tolerance permit: ESPEN suggests it for supplementary PN expected to last under seven days, formulations above about **900 mOsm/L** are poorly tolerated peripherally, and dextrose should not exceed 10% to limit thrombophlebitis.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[22]](#ref22)</sup> Central access permits more concentrated admixtures but adds insertion, infection and thrombosis risks; ESPEN asks that a central line placed solely for PN be justified by the expected duration. Central access does not remove metabolic or compatibility limits.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[11]](#ref11)</sup>

### What the Prescription Must Specify

| Component | Check before prescribing |
|---|---|
| Energy and amino acids | An average adult needs about 25–30 kcal/kg and 1–2 g/kg protein; use a predictive equation and a dietitian, and adjust for current oral/EN intake, organ function and refeeding risk. Amino acids come as 3–20% solutions, and excess protein raises renal load. Count energy from dextrose fluids and propofol<sup>[[22]](#ref22)</sup> |
| Dextrose | 3.4 kcal/g in 2.5–70% solutions, usually 70–85% of non-protein energy. Keep infusion at or below 5–7 mg/kg/min in stable patients and 4 mg/kg/min or less in the critically ill to limit hyperglycemia; do not give an identical glucose load to every patient<sup>[[22]](#ref22)</sup> |
| IV lipid | 10%, 20% and 30% emulsions provide 1.1, 2.0 and 2.9–3.0 kcal/mL. Lipid is needed to prevent essential fatty acid deficiency; keep it at or below 30% of energy. Check product, dose, infusion rate, allergies, triglycerides and other lipid sources<sup>[[22]](#ref22)</sup> |
| Electrolytes / fluid | Renal function, urine and GI losses, acid–base state, existing replacement and admixture compatibility |
| Vitamins / trace elements | Daily provision as appropriate, deficiency treatment and adjustment for losses or organ dysfunction |
| Administration | Verified access, pharmacy-compatible formulation, pump, filter and line-care protocol |

Typical daily additives for a two-in-one adult formulation (individualize for losses, renal and hepatic function):<sup>[[22]](#ref22)</sup>

| Additive | Usual daily amount |
|---|---|
| Sodium, potassium | 1–2 mEq/kg each |
| Calcium | 10–15 mEq |
| Magnesium | 8–20 mEq |
| Phosphorus | 20–40 mmol (also relevant to refeeding prevention) |
| Adult multivitamin | 10 mL daily, with or without vitamin K |
| Trace elements | Chromium, copper (0.3–0.5 mg), manganese, selenium (60–100 mcg), zinc (3–5 mg); give copper and manganese conservatively in hepatic dysfunction and consider extra zinc with high small-bowel losses; iron is not routinely included |

A three-in-one admixture contains dextrose, amino acids and lipid; a two-in-one contains dextrose and amino acids with lipid administered separately. ESPEN prefers all-in-one bags to multiple-bottle systems. Nutrition-support and pharmacy review should reconcile the complete prescription and monitoring plan.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[22]](#ref22)</sup>

### Lipids, Liver Tests and Warfarin

- **SMOFlipid** is a mixed soybean/MCT/olive/fish-oil emulsion used as a source of calories and essential fatty acids in PN. The adult label recommends 1–2 g/kg/day with a 2.5 g/kg/day maximum, with baseline and ongoing triglyceride monitoring and a lower starting dose when triglycerides are elevated; the 1 g/kg/day figure quoted for soybean-oil emulsions is not a ceiling for every IV lipid product.<sup>[[16]](#ref16)[[22]](#ref22)</sup>
- **Omegaven** is a fish-oil emulsion whose US indication is **pediatric PN-associated cholestasis**, not prevention of that condition or routine adult postoperative PN. It is not interchangeable with a standard adult lipid prescription.<sup>[[11]](#ref11)</sup>
- Abnormal liver tests on PN require assessment for sepsis, biliary disease, drugs, overfeeding and intestinal failure. Avoid overfeeding, treat infection and retain oral/EN intake when safe. Long-term PN may require cycling and lipid adjustment with the nutrition team; infant cholestasis statistics do not predict adult outcomes.
- **Do not automatically omit vitamin K because a patient takes warfarin.** Vitamin K-containing multivitamins and lipid products can affect anticoagulation; keep intake consistent where possible and monitor INR to guide warfarin adjustment.<sup>[[11]](#ref11)[[12]](#ref12)</sup>

### Monitoring

Track the amount actually delivered, tolerance, fluid balance, weight, glucose, renal function, sodium/potassium, magnesium and phosphate. Check triglycerides with IV lipids and liver tests during PN; frequency depends on acuity, initiation, recent changes and stability. Albumin/prealbumin should not be used as targets proving adequate nutrition.<sup>[[2]](#ref2)[[9]](#ref9)[[16]](#ref16)</sup>

If feeds are interrupted while insulin remains active, prevent and monitor for hypoglycemia with the diabetes/nutrition team. PN discontinuation needs an individualized glucose/insulin plan; a blanket instruction that PN must never be stopped abruptly is inappropriate. Investigate catheter infection, thrombosis, fluid overload and metabolic disturbance promptly.<sup>[[13]](#ref13)</sup>

## Refeeding Syndrome

Refeeding can cause dangerous phosphate, potassium and magnesium shifts and increased thiamine demand after prolonged undernutrition: carbohydrate triggers insulin release, which drives these ions into cells. Assess risk **before oral, enteral or parenteral feeding**, including calories in IV dextrose. Normal initial electrolytes do not exclude depleted stores.<sup>[[9]](#ref9)</sup>

Manifestations include arrhythmia and heart failure, respiratory weakness or failure (phosphate-dependent diaphragmatic function), fluid overload and edema, and neurologic change from thiamine depletion, including Wernicke encephalopathy. It is more common with PN but also occurs with EN.<sup>[[9]](#ref9)[[22]](#ref22)</sup>

### Recognize Risk and Deterioration

NICE high risk requires **one** major criterion (BMI below 16, weight loss over 15% in 3–6 months, negligible intake over 10 days, or low potassium/phosphate/magnesium), **or two** lesser criteria (BMI below 18.5, weight loss over 10% in 3–6 months, negligible intake over five days, or the specified alcohol/medication history). Alcohol use or a diuretic alone is not the whole definition.<sup>[[10]](#ref10)</sup>

ASPEN's consensus diagnostic framework uses a fall in one or more of phosphate, potassium or magnesium within five days of reintroducing or substantially increasing calories: **10–20% mild, 20–30% moderate, and over 30% and/or associated organ dysfunction due to these shifts or thiamine deficiency severe**. Interpret changes clinically and consider other causes; a single phosphate threshold is not a universal diagnostic definition.<sup>[[9]](#ref9)</sup>

### Use One Coherent Protocol

| Element | ASPEN 2020 adult consensus approach |
|---|---|
| Initial feeding | **10–20 kcal/kg in the first 24 hours** or 100–150 g dextrose; count all carbohydrate sources and advance by about one-third of goal every 1–2 days as tolerated |
| Severe electrolyte deficits | Delay initiation or escalation until severely low phosphate, potassium or magnesium is corrected; reassess less severe deficits before increasing calories |
| Thiamine prophylaxis | **100 mg before feeding/dextrose**, then 100 mg/day for 5–7 days or longer in severe starvation, chronic alcohol use or other high-risk circumstances; provide multivitamins |
| Electrolyte monitoring | Baseline potassium, magnesium and phosphate; **every 12 hours for the first three days in high-risk patients**, more frequently if clinically necessary |
| Falling levels or clinical deterioration | Replace by established protocols; reduce calorie/dextrose delivery when levels fall precipitously or resist correction; consider stopping nutrition in life-threatening deterioration |
| Clinical monitoring | Vitals, fluid balance, daily weight and cardiorespiratory monitoring when unstable |

These are prevention/management recommendations, not a Wernicke encephalopathy treatment regimen. Give thiamine promptly, but **do not delay emergency glucose treatment of hypoglycemia**; see [Thiamine](/docs/evaluation/laboratory-studies/nutritional-assessment/thiamine).<sup>[[9]](#ref9)</sup>

NICE uses a different high-risk regimen (recommendation 1.4.8): at most **10 kcal/kg/day** initially (5 in extreme risk, such as BMI below 14 or negligible intake for over 15 days), increasing to full needs over four to seven days, with **oral thiamine 200–300 mg/day** for the first ten days and a balanced multivitamin and trace-element supplement. Its likely requirements are **potassium 2–4 mmol/kg/day, phosphate 0.3–0.6 mmol/kg/day and magnesium 0.2 mmol/kg/day IV (0.4 oral)**. These are estimates, not automatic orders in renal failure or when pre-feeding levels are high. Follow a complete institutional protocol rather than mixing these doses with ASPEN's schedule.<sup>[[10]](#ref10)</sup>

## GLP-1 / GIP Medicines and Surgery

GLP-1 receptor agonists (semaglutide, liraglutide, dulaglutide) and the dual GIP/GLP-1 agonist tirzepatide are now a common preoperative exposure. They affect candidacy (weight loss can move a patient into a lower obesity class) and risk (appetite suppression, delayed gastric emptying, and possible protein and micronutrient deficits).

### Aspiration Planning

Ask about GLP-1/GIP use at every preoperative visit; patients often do not volunteer "weight-loss injections." The FDA has added a pulmonary-aspiration warning to the labels of these drugs.

The **multisociety guidance of October 2024** (ASA, AGA, ASMBS, ISPCOP and SAGES) replaced the 2023 ASA consensus advice to hold weekly agents for a week before elective surgery. It states that most patients may continue their GLP-1 RA, and directs the team to assess risk features for delayed gastric emptying: the dose-escalation phase, higher doses, weekly rather than daily dosing, GI symptoms and comorbidities that slow gastric emptying. Patients at elevated risk should follow a 24-hour liquid diet before the procedure and the anesthetic plan should be adjusted; point-of-care gastric ultrasound can help assess residual contents.<sup>[[20]](#ref20)</sup> The 2025 SPAQI multidisciplinary consensus differs from the multisociety statements in its medication and fasting recommendations, so follow your anesthesia department's protocol.<sup>[[21]](#ref21)</sup>

The **ADA 2026** framework individualizes management by indication, glycemia, drug/dose, GI symptoms, procedure, urgency and anesthesia. Options include a 24-hour liquid nutrition protocol, gastric ultrasound where appropriate and full-stomach precautions. If holding treatment is expected to worsen glycemia, establish an alternative plan. Medication withholding does not guarantee an empty stomach.<sup>[[13]](#ref13)</sup>

**OCULUS 2026:** 68 patients were randomized and 60 included in the prespecified interim analysis; clinically significant residual gastric contents occurred in **3.1% after holding one dose vs 25.0% after continuing**. The absolute difference was 21.9 percentage points (90% CI 7.0–36.7); the trial stopped early. This was elective upper endoscopy under moderate sedation/monitored anesthesia care in stable users, not a trial of all major operations.<sup>[[14]](#ref14)</sup>

In the 35 patients having upper endoscopy alone, the event rate was 46.7% when continuing vs 5.0% when holding one dose. All events prevented adequate endoscopic examination; no aspiration events occurred, but the trial was too small to establish aspiration safety. None of the 25 patients undergoing concurrent colonoscopy after clear liquids/bowel preparation had the primary event. That subgroup does not isolate or prove the effect of a liquid diet. Patients with retained contents were asymptomatic, so symptom screening alone cannot exclude risk.<sup>[[14]](#ref14)</sup>

### Nutrition and Wound Healing

Ask about appetite, actual protein/energy intake, vomiting, weight-loss rate and muscle/function. GLP-1 treatment does not automatically cause protein malnutrition. Investigate a demonstrated deficit and coordinate nutrition and prescribing care; albumin differences alone do not prove the mechanism of wound complications.<sup>[[2]](#ref2)</sup>

Current wound studies are observational and differ in population, and the signal is mixed.

| Study | Population | Wound signal |
|---|---|---|
| Aschen 2025 (Ann Surg) | **Patients with diabetes**, 35,020 matched procedures | Less dehiscence (RR 0.71, 0.58–0.88) and hematoma (RR 0.44, 0.22–0.89); no difference in infection or bleeding<sup>[[17]](#ref17)</sup> |
| Lee 2026 (TriNetX) | **Non-diabetic** panniculectomy, abdominoplasty and breast reduction | More wound complications with GLP-1 RA: abdominoplasty 9.8% vs 3.6% (P = .001), panniculectomy 4.7% vs 2.7% (P = .05), breast reduction 2.6% vs 1.3%<sup>[[18]](#ref18)</sup> |
| Koenig 2026 | 373 non-bariatric panniculectomies (81 users) | More delayed healing (18.5% vs 7.5%), fewer seromas (4.9% vs 14.0%); no difference in infection or hematoma<sup>[[19]](#ref19)</sup> |

One proposed reconciliation is that glycemic control dominates in patients with diabetes, whereas in non-diabetic patients taking the drug for weight loss, appetite suppression may create a protein deficit that impairs collagen synthesis. This is a hypothesis. In a 30-versus-30 pilot of breast implant explantation with mastopexy, users had lower albumin (4.06 vs 4.22 g/dL) and prealbumin (23.5 vs 25.6 mg/dL), but complication differences were not significant.<sup>[[39]](#ref39)</sup> In the Koenig cohort, users' prealbumin was higher.<sup>[[19]](#ref19)</sup>

These findings do not establish that continuing or stopping a GLP-1 medicine improves healing after GU reconstruction.

### Candidacy and Nutritional Planning

In a TriNetX cohort of 5,618 DIEP flap patients, class I/II obese patients on a GLP-1 RA had lower revision rates than class I/II obese non-users (P = .037) and class III patients (P = .046), with no difference in dehiscence, consistent with weight loss improving candidacy for complex reconstruction.<sup>[[37]](#ref37)</sup> The analogous question for GU work (fistula repair, urethroplasty, sacrocolpopexy, AUS/IPP, phalloplasty) is open.

Because these drugs suppress appetite and slow gastric emptying, plan protein intake deliberately. Body-contouring guidance recommends individualized protein timing plans, spacing protein through the day in small frequent meals, and correcting micronutrient deficiencies (iron, B12, vitamin D, calcium, folate, thiamine) before surgery, and notes that many patients fall short of the 60–120 g/day protein targets.<sup>[[38]](#ref38)</sup>

Practical checklist: ask about GLP-1/GIP use; screen intake, weight-loss rate and muscle function; treat an identified protein or micronutrient deficit; agree a hold-or-continue and fasting plan with anesthesia; and document baseline weight and trajectory before elective body-contouring or abdominal-wall procedures.

## GU Reconstruction and Follow-up

- **Cystectomy/diversion:** assess intake, hydration, renal function, stoma or catheter function and GI recovery. Recurrent vomiting or failure to thrive needs evaluation for surgical and medical causes, not supplements alone.
- **Bowel-containing urinary reconstruction:** monitor metabolic complications and B12 according to segment, resection, renal function and follow-up findings. Preserving a fixed terminal-ileum length does not guarantee normal absorption; bicarbonate and B12 are individualized rather than automatic for every pouch. See [Urinary Acidifiers & Alkalinizers](/docs/foundations/pharmacology/urinary-diversion-specific/urinary-acidifiers) and [Vitamin B12 Supplementation](/docs/foundations/pharmacology/urinary-diversion-specific/vitamin-b12-supplementation).
- **Urogynecologic procedures:** the AUGS-IUGA ERAS consensus rates fasting for 6 hours (solids) with clear fluids up to 2 hours before surgery, and chewing gum to reduce ileus, as high-level-evidence components; early tolerated oral intake belongs in the same pathway. Major abdominal-cancer effect sizes should not be applied directly to an uncomplicated vaginal prolapse or sling operation.<sup>[[15]](#ref15)</sup>
- **Urethroplasty, fistula and flap surgery:** treat identified malnutrition and deficiency, but do not use albumin alone to predict failure, choose a technique or mandate delay.

Before discharge, document intake targets, the supplement/feeding plan, hydration and symptom advice, laboratory monitoring where indicated, and who will reassess recovery.
