# Electrolyte Abnormalities

Perioperative electrolyte disturbances reflect renal function, medications, fluid balance, gastrointestinal losses, nutrition and redistribution. Bowel exposed to urine adds a persistent metabolic load. Assess the patient, trend and cause alongside the laboratory result. IV treatment requires the appropriate monitored setting, product concentration and institutional protocol.

See also: [Nutrition](/docs/foundations/perioperative-care/postoperative-management/nutrition), [ERAS](/docs/foundations/perioperative-care/perioperative-protocols/eras), and [Cardiovascular Risk](/docs/foundations/perioperative-care/preoperative-assessment/cardiovascular-risk).

## Initial Assessment

- Establish symptoms, onset, renal function, urine output, volume status and recent treatments. Review diuretics, renin–angiotensin system drugs, supplements, bowel preparation, insulin, chemotherapy and nutritional intake.
- Repeat an unexpected or potentially unreliable result; do not delay emergency treatment for an unstable patient. Check associated disturbances, particularly magnesium with hypokalemia and calcium during phosphate replacement.
- Select fluid according to volume and acid–base status. Balanced crystalloids are commonly appropriate; saline can aggravate hyperchloremic acidosis.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup>
- Abnormal preoperative potassium is associated with adverse outcomes.<sup>[[2]](#ref2)[[3]](#ref3)</sup>

## Sodium

### Hyponatremia

Sodium below 135 mmol/L requires interpretation with glucose, measured osmolality and clinical context. For hypotonic hyponatremia, assess urine osmolality, urine sodium, medications and volume status; consider adrenal insufficiency. Postoperative pain, nausea and antidiuresis can make hypotonic fluid hazardous.<sup>[[4]](#ref4)</sup>

Seizures, coma or other severe neurologic manifestations require urgent monitored 3% saline, using the institution's bolus protocol.<sup>[[4]](#ref4)[[5]](#ref5)</sup> The initial objective is a small rise sufficient to reverse cerebral edema, approximately 4–6 mmol/L, followed by controlled correction rather than immediate normalization.<sup>[[4]](#ref4)[[5]](#ref5)</sup>

For chronic or uncertain-duration hyponatremia, distinguish the correction goal from its upper limit:<sup>[[5]](#ref5)</sup>

- European guidance limits the rise to 10 mmol/L in the first day and 8 mmol/L per day thereafter.<sup>[[5]](#ref5)</sup>
- With high osmotic demyelination risk (especially sodium ≤105 mmol/L, hypokalemia, malnutrition, alcohol use disorder or advanced liver disease), the US/Irish panel recommends no more than 8 mmol/L in any 24 hours, aiming for 4–6.<sup>[[5]](#ref5)</sup>
- Monitor sodium frequently and track urine output. Recovery of water excretion can cause rapid overcorrection even after saline stops. Potassium replacement also contributes to correction.
- If limits are being exceeded, obtain urgent specialist help for measures such as desmopressin and replacement of water losses or sodium relowering.
- The evidence behind the limits is observational: a 2026 meta-analysis of 43 studies linked overcorrection to neurologic complications (OR 4.23) but rated the certainty very low, and the association was not significant in low-risk-of-bias studies. Correction limits remain the safe default, but severe hyponatremia still requires treatment.<sup>[[24]](#ref24)</sup>

Subsequent treatment follows the cause: volume replacement for hypovolemia, withdrawal of provoking drugs and usually fluid restriction for SIADH, or management of heart or liver failure for hypervolemic disease. Vaptans and other second-line treatments require specific indications and monitoring.<sup>[[4]](#ref4)</sup>

### Hypernatremia

Evaluate water access, losses, osmotic diuresis and diabetes insipidus. Restore circulation first if shock is present, then replace water and ongoing losses with an individualized plan. Duration, neurologic findings and serial sodium measurements guide correction. In adults, a fall of about 12 mEq/L over 24 hours is a conventional correction guide, but adult studies have not established that faster lowering causes cerebral edema, and the optimal rate remains uncertain. Account for acuity and ongoing losses, and reassess sodium serially rather than relying on a calculated water deficit.<sup>[[6]](#ref6)[[25]](#ref25)</sup>

## Potassium

### Hypokalemia

Potassium below 3.5 mmol/L is low; ≤2.5 mmol/L, arrhythmia, marked weakness or paralysis or important ECG abnormalities warrant urgent assessment. Investigate gastrointestinal or renal losses, intracellular shifts and magnesium deficiency.<sup>[[7]](#ref7)</sup>

- Prefer oral replacement when the gut functions and urgent IV correction is unnecessary. KCl is particularly useful with chloride depletion; citrate or bicarbonate may suit selected patients with acidosis. Equal milliequivalents of other potassium salts are not intrinsically ineffective.<sup>[[7]](#ref7)</sup>
- **Never administer IV potassium as an undiluted injection or IV push.** Use a controlled infusion, verify renal function and urine output, and reassess potassium. The cited US KCl label uses a usual ceiling of 10 mEq/hour when potassium is above 2.5 mEq/L; faster rescue regimens require a specific protocol and close monitoring. Product concentration and vascular access also matter.<sup>[[8]](#ref8)</sup>
- Correct magnesium deficiency alongside potassium; do not postpone urgent potassium treatment until magnesium has fully normalized. The rise after a replacement dose varies with continuing losses, redistribution and renal clearance.<sup>[[7]](#ref7)[[15]](#ref15)</sup>

**TIGHT K (2024).** The trial randomized 1,690 patients undergoing isolated CABG (1,667 in the efficacy analysis). A supplementation trigger of 3.6 rather than 4.5 mEq/L was noninferior for postoperative atrial fibrillation: 27.8% versus 26.2%, risk difference 1.7 percentage points (95% CI −2.6 to 5.9; prespecified noninferiority margin 10 points). This supports reducing unnecessary supplementation in that selected cardiac-surgery population.<sup>[[9]](#ref9)</sup>

### Hyperkalemia

**UKKA, updated July 2026:** mild 5.5–5.9, moderate 6.0–6.4, severe ≥6.5 mmol/L. Severe hyperkalemia requires emergency treatment. For hospitalized patients with potassium ≥6.0 mmol/L, obtain an urgent 12-lead ECG (UKKA 1B); use at least continuous 3-lead monitoring for potassium ≥6.5 mmol/L, hyperkalemic ECG changes, or potassium 6.0–6.4 mmol/L when clinically unwell or a rapid rise is expected (UKKA 1C). A normal tracing does not exclude danger.<sup>[[10]](#ref10)</sup>

| Purpose | Treatment |
|---|---|
| Protect the heart with hyperkalemic ECG changes | For a non-arrested patient, UKKA suggests **30 mL of 10% calcium gluconate over 10 minutes** or **10 mL of 10% calcium chloride over 5 minutes** (2C). Chloride is preferred in arrest/peri-arrest; gluconate otherwise (1C). In cardiac arrest, follow the resuscitation bolus protocol. Reassess the ECG and consider repeating calcium if toxic changes persist after 5–10 minutes.<sup>[[10]](#ref10)[[11]](#ref11)</sup> |
| Shift potassium | **10 units soluble insulin with 25 g glucose by IV infusion** (recommended for severe disease, suggested for moderate). If pretreatment glucose is **below 7 mmol/L (126 mg/dL)**, follow with **10% glucose at 50 mL/hour for 5 hours**. Nebulized albuterol/salbutamol **10–20 mg** is an adjunct.<sup>[[10]](#ref10)</sup> |
| Remove potassium | Obtain urgent renal/critical-care input for severe or refractory disease. For patients receiving maintenance hemodialysis with K ≥6.5 mmol/L, UKKA recommends urgent dialysis (1A) and IV calcium for toxic ECG changes even if dialysis is immediately available (1C).<sup>[[10]](#ref10)</sup> Binders cannot replace immediate stabilization.<sup>[[10]](#ref10)[[12]](#ref12)</sup> |

**Calcium does not lower potassium.** These salts are not interchangeable milliliter-for-milliliter: 10% gluconate contains about one-third the calcium of 10% chloride. Confirm preparation and IV patency.<sup>[[11]](#ref11)</sup>

**After insulin, UKKA monitoring:** glucose at baseline and 30, 60, 90, 120, 180, 240, 300 and 360 minutes; potassium at 1, 2, 4, 6 and 24 hours. Continue monitoring with repeat treatment. IV bicarbonate is not routine.<sup>[[10]](#ref10)</sup> For patients treated for hyperkalemia during admission, UKKA recommends arranging community blood monitoring at discharge and considering recurrence risk before reinstating medication that may have contributed (both 1B).<sup>[[10]](#ref10)</sup>

UKKA includes sodium zirconium cyclosilicate in the emergency pathway for severe hyperkalemia (K ≥6.5 mmol/L), whereas US labeling states it should not be used as emergency treatment for life-threatening hyperkalemia because of delayed onset.<sup>[[10]](#ref10)[[12]](#ref12)</sup> In either setting it must not delay calcium, insulin–glucose or dialysis.<sup>[[10]](#ref10)[[12]](#ref12)</sup>

**ECG patterns.** Hypokalemia flattens the T wave and produces U waves, ST depression and QT prolongation; severe depletion brings ventricular arrhythmias including torsades. Hyperkalemia can produce peaked T waves, PR prolongation, P-wave loss, QRS widening, a sine-wave pattern and asystole without a fixed sequence.<sup>[[10]](#ref10)</sup> The tracing is insensitive, so a normal ECG does not exclude danger.<sup>[[7]](#ref7)[[10]](#ref10)</sup>

## Calcium

### Confirm the Abnormality

Total, albumin-adjusted and ionized calcium are not interchangeable. A 2025 study of 22,658 adults found substantial misclassification with commonly used albumin-adjustment formulas, particularly in hypoalbuminemia. When clinical findings, albumin or acid–base disturbance make total calcium difficult to interpret, measure ionized calcium rather than assuming a correction formula establishes the diagnosis.<sup>[[13]](#ref13)</sup>

### Hypocalcemia

Assess magnesium, phosphate, renal function, PTH, vitamin D and relevant drugs. Symptoms include paresthesias, tetany, laryngospasm, seizures and QT prolongation. In surgical practice it is most often seen after thyroid or parathyroid surgery, but it also accompanies severe vitamin D deficiency, massive transfusion and magnesium depletion.<sup>[[14]](#ref14)</sup>

The Society for Endocrinology uses albumin-adjusted total calcium below 1.9 mmol/L (approximately 7.6 mg/dL), or symptomatic hypocalcemia at any low level, as an emergency threshold, not an ionized-calcium cutoff. Its regimen is 10–20 mL of 10% calcium gluconate in 50–100 mL of 5% dextrose over 10 minutes with ECG monitoring, repeated if needed, followed when indicated by an infusion of 100 mL of 10% calcium gluconate in 1 L of 0.9% saline or 5% dextrose at 50–100 mL/hour. Avoid large-volume calcium infusions in end-stage renal failure or dialysis. This differs from the hyperkalemic membrane-stabilization regimen above.<sup>[[14]](#ref14)</sup>

Stable patients may receive oral calcium and treatment of the cause. Calcitriol is not automatically indicated for every low calcium result: it is used in settings such as hypoparathyroidism, with titration and monitoring. Correct coexisting magnesium deficiency. Thiazides are not routine acute calcium rescue.<sup>[[14]](#ref14)[[15]](#ref15)</sup>

### Hypercalcemia

Confirm calcium status, assess hydration and measure PTH to guide the differential. Consider malignancy, primary hyperparathyroidism and medications; do not assume that prostate cancer with bone metastases explains the result.<sup>[[16]](#ref16)</sup>

- Rehydrate with isotonic saline according to volume status and cardiac or renal reserve, reassessing frequently. The Society for Endocrinology algorithm allows up to 4–6 L over 24 hours in patients who tolerate it, with closer monitoring in renal impairment and older patients. Loop diuretics are used only for fluid overload; they do not lower calcium effectively.<sup>[[16]](#ref16)</sup>
- Urgency follows the adjusted calcium: below 3.0 mmol/L is often asymptomatic, 3.0–3.5 mmol/L usually warrants treatment, and above 3.5 mmol/L is urgent because of arrhythmia and coma risk. The ECG shows a shortened QT.<sup>[[16]](#ref16)</sup>
- Bisphosphonate options in that guidance are zoledronic acid 4 mg over 15 minutes or pamidronate 30–90 mg at 20 mg/hour, with the calcium nadir at 2–4 days.<sup>[[16]](#ref16)</sup>
- For hypercalcemia of malignancy, the Endocrine Society recommends an IV bisphosphonate or denosumab; its conditional preference for denosumab rests on very low-certainty evidence. The guideline does not restrict denosumab to bisphosphonate-refractory disease.<sup>[[17]](#ref17)</sup>
- For adults with hypercalcemia of malignancy and total or albumin-adjusted calcium above 14 mg/dL (3.5 mmol/L), the Endocrine Society suggests adding calcitonin to an IV bisphosphonate or denosumab initially; limit calcitonin to 48–72 hours because of tachyphylaxis. Renal function, vitamin D and hypocalcemia risk affect antiresorptive selection and monitoring. Treat the underlying malignancy.<sup>[[17]](#ref17)</sup>

## Magnesium

Hypomagnesemia may accompany hypokalemia and hypocalcemia. Look for gastrointestinal losses, poor intake, renal wasting and drugs such as PPIs, diuretics, calcineurin inhibitors and platinum chemotherapy. Metabolic alkalosis is not universal. Urinary output from an ileal conduit is not equivalent to loss from a gastrointestinal ileostomy.<sup>[[15]](#ref15)</sup>

Use oral magnesium for uncomplicated mild deficiency, adjusting to tolerability; diarrhea can limit replacement. Severe or symptomatic deficiency requires monitored IV magnesium sulfate and repeat measurements; dilute a 50% preparation before IV administration and follow local peripheral or central concentration limits.<sup>[[18]](#ref18)</sup> NHS SPS guidance uses 5 g (20 mmol) initially for severe or symptomatic deficiency, ordinarily infused at 1–2 g/hour; institutional regimens vary. This is a replacement regimen, not an arrest or torsades protocol. Individualize dosing in renal impairment and obtain specialist advice for AKI, advanced CKD, dialysis or transplant recipients. Monitor magnesium, potassium, calcium, renal function and blood pressure; use ECG monitoring with severe symptoms.<sup>[[18]](#ref18)</sup>

Treat the underlying cause of persistent deficiency. SGLT2 inhibitors and prebiotics have emerging magnesium-related evidence but are not standard acute replacement. An ileal conduit alone does not establish a need for chronic parenteral magnesium.<sup>[[15]](#ref15)</sup>

Excess magnesium is most concerning when renal clearance is impaired. Stop magnesium sources and urgently assess hypotension, bradycardia, respiratory depression or loss of the patellar reflex, an early sign; the US label caps magnesium sulfate at 20 g per 48 hours in severe renal insufficiency; severe toxicity requires critical-care or renal input, with ventilatory support and IV calcium when indicated.<sup>[[23]](#ref23)</sup>

## Phosphate

Hypophosphatemia is usually defined as below 2.5 mg/dL (0.8 mmol/L); below 1 mg/dL (0.32 mmol/L) is severe. Investigate refeeding, redistribution, gastrointestinal or renal loss, and medications. Remember phosphate wasting after kidney transplantation and repeated ferric carboxymaltose infusions.<sup>[[19]](#ref19)</sup>

- Severe or symptomatic deficiency (such as respiratory weakness, arrhythmia or rhabdomyolysis) generally warrants IV phosphate in a monitored setting.
- Use a product-specific protocol; choose sodium versus potassium phosphate according to potassium and renal function. Monitor phosphate, calcium and potassium. Avoid rapid infusion and automatic full dosing in renal impairment.
- Oral replacement usually suits stable patients who require supplementation and can absorb it. Divided doses may cause diarrhea. Treat the cause; active vitamin D is not a universal replacement for ordinary vitamin D deficiency.

The 2025 umbrella systematic review found 33 reviews, guidelines and consensus statements and substantial variation in dosing and indications. It excluded ICU settings.<sup>[[19]](#ref19)</sup>

## Refeeding Syndrome

Use the [Nutrition pathway](/docs/foundations/perioperative-care/postoperative-management/nutrition#refeeding-syndrome) for risk assessment, thiamine, calorie initiation and monitoring. It distinguishes ASPEN and NICE recommendations. Check potassium, magnesium and phosphate before feeding. Following the linked ASPEN pathway, delay starting or advancing calories until severely low levels are corrected; replace electrolytes and monitor during nutrition advancement.<sup>[[26]](#ref26)</sup> Do not delay emergency glucose for hypoglycemia while arranging thiamine.

## GU-Specific Considerations

### Bowel Exposed to Urine

| Segment | Characteristic metabolic concern |
|---|---|
| Ileum or colon, including ileocolonic reservoirs | **Hyperchloremic metabolic acidosis**; potassium depletion can coexist. Risk depends on surface area, contact time and renal reserve. |
| Jejunum, now rarely used | Hyponatremia, hypochloremia, hyperkalemia, dehydration and metabolic acidosis. |
| Stomach, historical/selected reconstruction | Hypochloremic, hypokalemic **metabolic alkalosis**. |

These patterns guide investigation. Reservoirs generally expose more bowel to urine for longer than short conduits. Correction of acidosis can worsen potassium depletion, so monitor both.<sup>[[20]](#ref20)[[21]](#ref21)</sup>

Follow renal function, electrolytes and bicarbonate, drainage and upper-tract anatomy long term. Investigate obstruction, infection, dehydration and stones when results deteriorate. The EAU bladder cancer guideline states that B12 should be measured annually after cystectomy with bowel diversion, citing low B12 in 17% of patients (guideline text, not a graded recommendation); neither a fixed ileal length nor a fixed number of postoperative years reliably excludes deficiency.<sup>[[22]](#ref22)</sup>

Individualize alkali treatment to persistent acidosis, potassium, renal function and sodium and volume burden. See [Urinary Acidifiers & Alkalinizers](/docs/foundations/pharmacology/urinary-diversion-specific/urinary-acidifiers) and [Vitamin B12 Supplementation](/docs/foundations/pharmacology/urinary-diversion-specific/vitamin-b12-supplementation). Catheterizable reservoirs need their prescribed emptying schedule; changing an external appliance does not empty an obstructed urinary conduit.

### Perioperative Application

After major reconstruction, assess ongoing losses, renal function, medications and nutrition before repeated replacement. There is no validated universal instruction to push every cystectomy patient's potassium above 4.0 mmol/L or normalize every mild laboratory deviation before surgery; a 317-patient retrospective study associated higher-normal preoperative potassium with lower 30-day morbidity but did not test supplementation.<sup>[[27]](#ref27)</sup>

Classic dilutional TURP syndrome follows absorption of electrolyte-free irrigation during monopolar resection. Bipolar TURP and HoLEP use saline, reducing that mechanism; substantial saline absorption can still cause volume overload and acid–base disturbance.<sup>[[28]](#ref28)[[29]](#ref29)</sup>
