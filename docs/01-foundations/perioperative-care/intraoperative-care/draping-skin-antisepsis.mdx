# Surgical Draping & Skin Antisepsis

Plan the prepared field around the actual incisions, mucosal access and positioning. An abdominal skin preparation, a vaginal preparation and an adhesive incise drape answer different questions; evidence for one does not establish the safety or effectiveness of another.

## Select the Preparation by Site and Product

| Site or situation | Practical approach |
| --- | --- |
| **Intact abdominal skin** | Use an alcohol-based antiseptic unless contraindicated (CDC 2017). WHO favors chlorhexidine–alcohol; an iodophor–alcohol preparation is a trial-supported alternative. Check the exact product, allergy history and procedure.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[2]](#ref2)[[3]](#ref3)[[11]](#ref11)</sup> |
| **External genital or perineal skin** | Account for adjacent urethral and vaginal mucosa, folds and pooling. Do not transfer an abdominal-skin protocol to every genital site. Product labeling differs even between chlorhexidine preparations: the 26-mL ChloraPrep applicator is not intended for areas smaller than 8.4 × 8.4 in, and the 4% aqueous CHG label excludes the genital area and warns against contact with eyes, ears and mouth.<sup>[[3]](#ref3)[[7]](#ref7)[[8]](#ref8)</sup> |
| **Vagina** | Use a preparation suitable for vaginal antisepsis under the institutional protocol. ACOG describes povidone-iodine and selected low-alcohol chlorhexidine formulations; vaginal chlorhexidine use is off-label. **Do not use the usual 70% isopropyl-alcohol skin preparations intravaginally.**<sup>[[3]](#ref3)</sup> |
| **Known antiseptic allergy or another contraindication** | Choose an alternative formulation appropriate for the site. Check the actual ingredient and reaction rather than assuming all products with the same antiseptic name are interchangeable.<sup>[[2]](#ref2)[[7]](#ref7)[[8]](#ref8)</sup> |

**The chlorhexidine percentage is not the alcohol percentage.** ACOG's description of a commonly used 4% chlorhexidine soap containing 4% alcohol does not establish that every 4% chlorhexidine product has that composition or is suitable for genital use. For example, the cited 4% Micro-Guard CHG label specifically excludes the genital area.<sup>[[3]](#ref3)[[8]](#ref8)</sup>

## What the Higher-Level Evidence Establishes

**Alcohol-containing skin antiseptics:** earlier comparisons favoring chlorhexidine–alcohol over aqueous povidone-iodine do not isolate the effect of chlorhexidine from that of alcohol. The following large trials clarify comparisons between alcohol-containing preparations:<sup>[[3]](#ref3)</sup>

| Study | Main result | Limit of application |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **PICASSO, JAMA 2024** | In a cluster-randomized crossover trial of elective cardiac or abdominal surgery, 3,321 patients were analyzed (pregnant patients and emergency operations were excluded). SSI occurred in 80/1,570 (5.1%) with povidone-iodine in alcohol and 97/1,751 (5.5%) with chlorhexidine in alcohol. Povidone-iodine met the prespecified **2.5-percentage-point noninferiority margin**.<sup>[[4]](#ref4)</sup> | Supports that tested alternative in those settings; the abdominal subgroup (6.8% vs 9.9%, RR 0.69, 95% CI 0.46–1.02) was underpowered. Does not establish universal equivalence or suitability for mucosa. |
| **PREPARE, NEJM 2024** | In closed-fracture surgery, SSI was 2.4% with iodine povacrylex–alcohol versus 3.3% with chlorhexidine–alcohol (OR 0.74, 95% CI 0.55–1.00). The open-fracture comparison did not show a clear difference.<sup>[[5]](#ref5)</sup> | **Iodine povacrylex is not povidone-iodine.** These are orthopedic skin-preparation findings, not a GU or vaginal-preparation trial. |

A 2025 meta-analysis of 10 randomized trials in abdominal surgery likewise found no significant difference between chlorhexidine–alcohol and iodine–alcohol (RR 1.20, 95% CI 0.94–1.54).<sup>[[12]](#ref12)</sup>

**Vaginal preparation:** a 2025 hysterectomy systematic review included 10 studies and 9,618 patients, but only four small randomized trials (306 participants); the rest were nonrandomized comparisons. Chlorhexidine was associated with fewer positive vaginal cultures, while the pooled SSI estimate did not show a clear reduction versus povidone-iodine (OR 1.22, 95% CI 0.91–1.63). Adverse-event reporting was limited. Reduced culture positivity should not be presented as proven clinical infection prevention or as reassurance for every concentration and formulation.<sup>[[6]](#ref6)</sup>

## Drying, Hair and Fire Prevention

Follow the specific applicator's instructions for application, contact time, treated area and drying. Prevent pooling in skin folds, beneath the patient or near tourniquets; remove soaked materials before draping or using an ignition source.<sup>[[2]](#ref2)[[7]](#ref7)</sup>

The cited 26-mL ChloraPrep label requires complete drying before draping, with at least three minutes on hairless skin and potentially up to one hour in hair. These are product-specific instructions, not a universal three-minute rule for all antiseptics. Do not blot an alcohol preparation dry to shorten the prescribed process.<sup>[[7]](#ref7)</sup>

Avoid routine hair removal. When hair interferes with surgery, WHO recommends clipping and discourages shaving. The older scrotal-razor consensus should not be generalized into permission to shave every male genital operative field; use a procedure-specific protocol that avoids skin injury.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[3]](#ref3)</sup>

## Sterile Drapes and Adhesive Incise Drapes

**Sterile surgical drapes remain part of the operative barrier.** WHO permits either suitable sterile reusable woven or disposable non-woven systems; available SSI evidence has not established a clear winner. Material integrity, fluid resistance, validated processing, waste and cost remain relevant.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup> The 2026 AUGS sustainability white paper likewise found no definitive evidence that disposable drapes and gowns lower surgical site infection, and lists reusable drapes and gowns among suggested measures where reprocessing is readily available (most life cycle analyses it reviewed favored reusable items).<sup>[[13]](#ref13)</sup>

Adhesive incise drapes are a separate issue:

- Cochrane found higher SSI risk with plain adhesive drapes than with no adhesive incise drape (five trials, 3,082 participants; RR 1.23, 95% CI 1.02–1.48). Two trials of iodophor-impregnated drapes showed no clear benefit (1,113 participants; RR 1.03, 95% CI 0.66–1.60). These were older, varied procedures and products.<sup>[[9]](#ref9)</sup>
- WHO conditionally advises against using adhesive incise drapes, with or without antimicrobial impregnation, for SSI prevention. NICE advises avoiding routine plain incise drapes and using an iodophor-impregnated product if an incise drape is required, unless contraindicated by allergy. This is a difference in guidance, not evidence that all sterile draping should be abandoned.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[2]](#ref2)</sup>

## GU Setup and the Wider Infection-Prevention Plan

Coordinate abdominal, perineal, vaginal and oral donor-site access when those fields are actually needed. Preserve field separation and manage instrument and glove changes when moving between contaminated and clean phases. Positioning and safeguards against sliding should follow the planned operation and [positioning guidance](/docs/foundations/perioperative-care/intraoperative-care/positioning-nerve-injury); there is no universal lithotomy position, Trendelenburg angle or chest-strap prescription for GU reconstruction.

A urologic before-and-after study reported superficial SSI falling from 3.57% to 1.37% after introducing a multicomponent OR bundle. The design cannot determine which component caused the change or prove that every bundle outperforms every individual intervention.<sup>[[10]](#ref10)</sup> Use the companion pages for [antimicrobial prophylaxis](/docs/foundations/pharmacology/infection-prophylaxis/perioperative-antibiotic-prophylaxis), [prosthetic infection prevention](/docs/foundations/pharmacology/infection-prophylaxis/prosthetic-infection-biofilm), [hand antisepsis](/docs/foundations/perioperative-care/intraoperative-care/surgical-hand-antisepsis) and [gloving](/docs/foundations/perioperative-care/intraoperative-care/surgical-gloving).
