# Perioperative Analgesia

A useful pain plan enables breathing, sleep and mobilization while limiting adverse effects. Combine appropriate nonopioid drugs, local/regional techniques and nonpharmacological measures, with opioids when needed. The combination should fit the patient and operation; adding every available drug is not the goal of multimodal care.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup>

This is the **clinical workflow**. Agent dosing, renal/hepatic adjustments and label restrictions are maintained in [NSAIDs & Analgesics](/docs/foundations/pharmacology/neuropathic-pelvic-pain/nsaids-analgesics), [Local Anesthetics](/docs/foundations/pharmacology/neuropathic-pelvic-pain/local-anesthetics), [Gabapentinoids](/docs/foundations/pharmacology/neuropathic-pelvic-pain/gabapentinoids) and [Liposomal Bupivacaine](/docs/foundations/pharmacology/intraoperative-adjuncts/liposomal-bupivacaine). See [Nerve Blocks](/docs/foundations/perioperative-care/intraoperative-care/nerve-blocks) for regional technique selection.

## Preoperative Interventions

### Risk Screening and Planning

Document baseline pain and function, previous difficult recoveries, current analgesics and actual daily opioid exposure. Assess kidney/liver function, sleep apnea, respiratory disease, frailty and concurrent sedatives. Discuss expected pain, functional goals, rescue treatment and the plan after discharge. Assess distress and substance-use history without assuming that either makes reported pain unreliable.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup>

### Patient Education

Preoperative education improves the pain plan and is a guideline recommendation.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup> Cover the expected severity and time course of pain, the scheduled drugs and when to use rescue medication, non-drug coping (positioning, relaxation, early walking), side effects that need a call, and the plan for stopping opioids. Tell patients that the aim is tolerable pain that allows walking, coughing and sleep, not zero pain.

For a patient taking long-term opioids, distinguish **baseline treatment** from the additional requirement for surgical pain. An automatic “opioids only as rescue” pathway can leave baseline dependence untreated. Coordinate changes with the usual prescriber and acute-pain team.

**Buprenorphine for opioid-use disorder should not routinely be stopped or tapered perioperatively.** The 2021 multisociety expert panel emphasizes recurrence risk, multimodal analgesia and coordinated follow-up. Additional analgesia can be provided while buprenorphine continues; complex dose changes require an individualized plan.<sup>[[2]](#ref2)</sup>

### Nonopioid Foundation

Acetaminophen and/or an NSAID can form a scheduled foundation when appropriate. Prefer oral treatment when it is feasible; IV administration is not inherently more effective. Reassess suitability as bleeding, perfusion, oral intake and kidney function change.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup>

| Decision | Practical check |
|---|---|
| **Acetaminophen** | Count all combination products; use weight- and liver-appropriate limits rather than a universal adult maximum |
| **NSAID/COX-2 inhibitor** | Review kidney injury, hypovolemia, GI bleeding/ulcer risk, cardiovascular risk and concurrent antithrombotics; avoid duplicate NSAIDs |
| **Bowel anastomosis** | Discuss procedure- and agent-specific concerns with the surgeon; see [Bowel Anastomosis](/docs/foundations/surgical-principles/bowel-anastomosis) |
| **Ketorolac** | Verify the age-, weight- and renal-function-specific dosing limits and the combined oral/parenteral duration limit in the drug hub |
| **Dexamethasone** | Select for the overall antiemetic/analgesic plan; it is not a mandatory 8–10 mg dose for every surgical patient. See [PONV](/docs/foundations/perioperative-care/intraoperative-care/nausea-vomiting) and [Steroids](/docs/foundations/perioperative-care/preoperative-assessment/steroids) |

### Scheduled Dosing Reference

Scheduled (around-the-clock) nonopioid dosing for the first days generally works better than waiting for pain to return, provided the ceilings below are respected. Agent-level contraindications and comparative data are in the [NSAIDs & Analgesics](/docs/foundations/pharmacology/neuropathic-pelvic-pain/nsaids-analgesics) hub.

| Agent | Labeled adult dosing boundary | Points that change the plan |
|---|---|---|
| **Acetaminophen (IV)** | ≥50 kg: 1,000 mg every 6 h or 650 mg every 4 h, maximum 4 g/24 h from all routes; &lt;50 kg: 15 mg/kg every 6 h or 12.5 mg/kg every 4 h, maximum 75 mg/kg/day | Count combination opioid tablets; severe hepatic disease is a contraindication; lower ceilings for malnutrition, alcohol use or hepatic risk<sup>[[12]](#ref12)</sup> |
| **Ketorolac (IV/IM)** | Single dose 30 mg if &lt;65 years; 15 mg if ≥65, renally impaired or &lt;50 kg. Daily maximum 120 mg, or 60 mg in those lower-dose groups. Total IV/IM plus oral course no longer than 5 days | Contraindicated with active or prior GI bleeding, advanced renal impairment, hemorrhagic diathesis or incomplete hemostasis, and with aspirin or other NSAIDs; give after hemostasis is secure<sup>[[13]](#ref13)</sup> |
| **Oral ibuprofen, celecoxib, meloxicam** | Use the product label for the patient's age, weight and renal function | Same kidney, GI, cardiovascular and antithrombotic screen; do not stack NSAIDs |

### Gabapentinoids

Routine new perioperative gabapentin/pregabalin is not supported by the 2020 synthesis of **281 trials and 24,682 participants**, which found no clinically important overall analgesic benefit and more adverse effects. FDA highlights respiratory risk with opioids or other CNS depressants, respiratory disease and older age.<sup>[[3]](#ref3)[[4]](#ref4)</sup>

Existing therapy requires medication reconciliation, renal adjustment and assessment of sedation, indication and withdrawal risk. Neither automatic full-dose continuation during organ dysfunction nor abrupt cessation of established treatment is a sound universal rule.

## Intraoperative Interventions

### Ketamine

Subanesthetic ketamine can be considered for painful surgery, including selected opioid-tolerant patients. **Bolus treatment and infusions both have evidence**; it is incorrect to restrict all benefit to an infusion or all use to opioid-tolerant patients. The ASRA/AAPM/ASA acute-pain guidance finds an opioid-sparing role, while emphasizing variable regimens and limited evidence in important comorbid populations.<sup>[[5]](#ref5)</sup>

Use an anesthesia/acute-pain protocol with trained staff and respiratory/hemodynamic monitoring. Review cardiovascular instability, active psychosis, pregnancy and hepatic disease. “Subanesthetic” dosing does not guarantee preserved airway safety or freedom from psychomimetic effects.<sup>[[5]](#ref5)</sup>

### Intravenous Lidocaine

IV lidocaine for analgesia is an **off-label, high-risk intervention**, selected under a monitored protocol. Its risk–benefit balance varies by procedure; it is not simply a fallback whenever NSAIDs cannot be used. The 2021 consensus uses ideal-body-weight dosing and generally limits an infusion to 24 hours, with specialist reassessment for any extension.<sup>[[6]](#ref6)</sup>

:::warning[Coordinate lidocaine with every local-anesthetic intervention]
The consensus advises avoiding concurrent IV lidocaine and other local-anesthetic techniques during their period of action. It specifies **at least four hours between a nerve/fascial-plane block or port-site infiltration and starting IV lidocaine**, and **four hours after stopping IV lidocaine before a new block or wound/epidural bolus**. These intervals do not override longer formulation-specific restrictions, including EXPAREL. Consult the complete protocol for catheter-infusion and small-dose spinal exceptions.<sup>[[6]](#ref6)[[8]](#ref8)</sup>
:::

### Surgical Site Infiltration

Agree on one cumulative local-anesthetic plan before injections begin. Count both sides of a block, wound infiltration, topical use and planned rescue injections. A concentration and volume are not a complete prescription without the resulting milligram dose, formulation and patient factors.

EXPAREL is not interchangeable milligram-for-milligram with plain bupivacaine. Its label supports local infiltration and specified peripheral blocks; **pudendal, penile, spermatic-cord and TAP blocks are not among its established regional indications**. Do not promise 72-hour analgesia at every GU site or copy a bilateral pudendal dose from an unrelated block. The label advises avoiding additional local anesthetic within **96 hours**, subject to its specific administration/compatibility instructions.<sup>[[8]](#ref8)</sup>

## GU-Specific Regional Analgesia

The **2026 ASA guideline** supports fascial plane blocks for adult open abdominal, retroperitoneal and pelvic surgery, and for minimally invasive abdominal surgery, to reduce pain and/or opioid requirements during the first 24 hours. Choice remains procedure- and patient-specific; it does not establish an ESP/QL/TAP hierarchy. See [Nerve Blocks](/docs/foundations/perioperative-care/intraoperative-care/nerve-blocks).<sup>[[11]](#ref11)</sup>

Select a block by the actual incision and pain generators, not the operation's name alone. Regional anesthesia can reduce somatic pain without treating all visceral discomfort or catheter symptoms. Technique details and supporting evidence belong in [Nerve Blocks](/docs/foundations/perioperative-care/intraoperative-care/nerve-blocks).

| Technique | Potential role and limitation |
|---|---|
| **Pudendal nerve block** | Selected perineal/vaginal procedures; coverage does not extend to all pelvic visceral pain |
| **Dorsal penile/ring block** | Penile surgery; additional surgical sites may require separate analgesia |
| **TAP/rectus sheath** | Incisional abdominal-wall pain; coverage depends on approach and incision |
| **Ilioinguinal/iliohypogastric** | Selected inguinal or lower abdominal incisions; not complete testicular/visceral coverage |
| **QL/ESP** | Selected abdominal/flank operations; spread and clinical benefit vary, without a universal superiority ranking |

### Intrathecal (Spinal) Morphine

Intrathecal morphine can provide prolonged analgesia but can also cause delayed respiratory depression, pruritus, nausea and urinary retention. Choose the dose and postoperative location with anesthesia before administration.

For **single-injection neuraxial hydrophilic opioids such as conventional morphine**, ASA/ASRA recommends monitoring for **at least 24 hours**: at least hourly for the first 12 hours, then at least every two hours for the next 12. Assess **ventilation, oxygenation and consciousness**; pulse oximetry alone is not the full monitoring plan. Higher-risk patients or concurrent sedatives/systemic opioids may require more intensive monitoring. Different formulations and catheter regimens have different schedules.<sup>[[7]](#ref7)</sup>

## Postoperative Assessment and Rescue

Reassess pain during activity as well as at rest, along with sedation, respiration, nausea, bowel function and urinary drainage. New or disproportionate pain may indicate a complication: examine the patient before simply escalating medication.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup>

Use an individualized immediate-release opioid when additional acute-pain relief is needed, generally through the oral route once feasible. PCA may be appropriate when pain is severe or oral dosing is impractical. Account for baseline tolerance, organ function and all sedatives. A fixed oxycodone dose is not appropriate for every older, opioid-naive or renally impaired patient.

Non-drug measures add comfort at low risk. Early mobilization limits sensitization and deconditioning, relaxation and breathing techniques reduce distress, and TENS or other cognitive modalities can be offered as adjuncts.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup> Position changes and procedure-appropriate cooling or elevation of genital and perineal swelling may help. Protect skin and grafts from direct ice, pressure and unapproved compression. Rescue regional treatment requires a fresh review of antithrombotics, infection, prior local-anesthetic exposure and the cause of pain.

At discharge, specify which agents continue, their stop/taper plan and the clinician responsible for follow-up. Reconcile acetaminophen in combination tablets, provide a bowel plan when needed, and explain symptoms that require prompt review.

## Pathway Summary

| Phase | Core elements | Adjust or omit when |
|---|---|---|
| **Preoperative** | Risk screen, education, baseline opioid and buprenorphine plan, acetaminophen and/or NSAID, regional plan | Renal, GI, cardiovascular or bleeding contraindication to NSAIDs; severe hepatic disease |
| **Intraoperative** | Incision-matched fascial plane, pudendal or penile block and/or wound infiltration; opioid-sparing rather than opioid-free anesthesia; selected ketamine or IV lidocaine under protocol | Anticoagulation or infection at the block site; unresolved cumulative local-anesthetic dose |
| **Postoperative** | Scheduled nonopioids, non-drug measures, activity-based reassessment, individualized immediate-release opioid for breakthrough pain | New or disproportionate pain, which needs examination before escalation |
| **Discharge** | Named agents, stop or taper dates, bowel plan, acetaminophen total, who follows up | Long-term opioid or buprenorphine patients need the prescriber named |

## Newer Evidence and Agents

### Suzetrigine (Journavx)

Suzetrigine, a selective peripheral Nav1.8 sodium-channel blocker, is FDA-approved (January 2025) for **moderate-to-severe acute pain, including postoperative pain, in adults**. Two pivotal randomized trials enrolled **1,118 abdominoplasty patients (447 on suzetrigine) and 1,073 bunionectomy patients (426 on suzetrigine)** and demonstrated improvement versus placebo over 48 hours. In the abdominoplasty trial the difference from hydrocodone/acetaminophen was not statistically significant, so superiority to an opioid was not shown. These operations do not establish effectiveness for major GU reconstruction.<sup>[[9]](#ref9)</sup>

Before prescribing, note that **concomitant strong CYP3A inhibitors are contraindicated**, review hepatic impairment, and advise additional nonhormonal contraception during treatment and for 28 days afterward. The label states that acute-pain use **beyond 14 days has not been studied**. A peripheral nonopioid mechanism is not evidence of zero adverse-event risk; see the [analgesic drug hub](/docs/foundations/pharmacology/neuropathic-pelvic-pain/nsaids-analgesics).<sup>[[9]](#ref9)</sup>

### Comparing Multimodal Combinations

Graham et al. (2025) analyzed **23,238 procedures** in six Veterans Health Administration hospitals using an instrumental-variable observational design. Any multimodal analgesia was associated with 6.8 fewer oral morphine equivalents in inpatients (95% CI −10.2 to −3.4) and a 1.0-point lower pain score in outpatients. NSAID plus dexamethasone and NSAID plus regional anesthesia gave the largest reductions in opioid use (about 29.5 and 28.4 oral morphine equivalents), and acetaminophen contributed less than the NSAID and dexamethasone components. This was not randomized allocation to each combination and does not justify withholding otherwise suitable acetaminophen or prescribing NSAIDs despite contraindications.<sup>[[10]](#ref10)</sup>
