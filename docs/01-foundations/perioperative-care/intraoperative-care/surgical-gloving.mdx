# Surgical Gloving

Surgical gloves protect the operative field and the team's hands. Use sterile, powder-free gloves intended for surgical use, maintain hand antisepsis, and replace a damaged or contaminated barrier promptly. Gloves do not replace [surgical hand antisepsis](/docs/foundations/perioperative-care/intraoperative-care/surgical-hand-antisepsis).<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[2]](#ref2)</sup>

## Selecting Gloves

Choose the surgical product, fit and allergy profile together. Examination-glove material comparisons do not establish the best glove for a prolonged reconstructive operation: the frequently cited nitrile-versus-latex failure study tested medical examination gloves under simulated use.<sup>[[3]](#ref3)</sup>

| Selection issue | Practical approach |
|---|---|
| Intended use | Check sterile surgical labeling, package integrity and manufacturer instructions; an examination glove is not a substitute merely because it is nitrile.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup> |
| Vinyl and copolymer | In the examination-glove study, vinyl and copolymer gloves failed water testing far more often (8.2% each) than nitrile (1.3%) or latex (2.2%). Use a surgical-grade latex, polyisoprene, polychloroprene or nitrile glove for operative work, not vinyl.<sup>[[3]](#ref3)[[5]](#ref5)</sup> |
| Fit | Fit every team member before their first scrub. Refit when the manufacturer or product changes, or when comfort or performance suffers, and stock several sizes and styles. Trial the actual glove combination for movement and instrument control. There is no established rule that everyone should wear an inner glove half a size larger (that draft statement failed consensus).<sup>[[4]](#ref4)</sup> |
| Natural rubber latex allergy | Use a suitable synthetic surgical glove and a latex-safe perioperative pathway.<sup>[[5]](#ref5)[[6]](#ref6)</sup> |
| Delayed contact dermatitis | Consider rubber-accelerator allergy and occupational dermatology assessment. Latex-free does not necessarily mean accelerator-free; select the specific formulation accordingly.<sup>[[7]](#ref7)</sup> |

**Powdered surgical and examination gloves are banned in the United States**; the FDA rule took effect January 18, 2017. Select powder-free products and never wash, disinfect or reuse disposable medical gloves.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[8]](#ref8)</sup>

The ban rests on the harms of cornstarch powder: peritoneal adhesions and granulomatous peritonitis, impaired wound healing and wound infection, and carriage of latex allergen. In rats, starch or glove powder on the peritoneum increased adhesions and intra-abdominal tumor-cell growth. In a separate rat model, powdered and latex gloves each raised adhesion scores and cytokine levels, and powder-free synthetic gloves produced none.<sup>[[16]](#ref16)[[17]](#ref17)[[18]](#ref18)</sup>

## Donning and Replacing Gloves

For initial closed gloving, keep the hands within the gown sleeves until the gloves cover the cuffs. Assisted gloving requires a sterile team member to handle the new sterile glove. A circulator can assist with removal of a contaminated glove using appropriate protection, without pulling the gown cuff over the hand; a sterile team member then assists with regloving. If sterility cannot be maintained, change the gown and gloves using the institution's aseptic technique.<sup>[[2]](#ref2)</sup>

The evidence comparing particular donning methods mostly measures simulated contamination. A small randomized simulation found less fluorescent cuff contamination with closed-assisted than open-assisted donning; a separate gloves-first study also used a contamination surrogate. Neither establishes elimination of clinical contamination or a reduction in SSI.<sup>[[9]](#ref9)[[10]](#ref10)</sup>

At a recognized breach, stop the relevant contact and restore an intact sterile barrier. Evaluate both layers when double gloved, and follow the occupational-exposure protocol if a sharp injury or blood exposure occurred. An indicator system does not guarantee that the inner glove or skin is intact.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[2]](#ref2)[[11]](#ref11)</sup>

## Double Gloving and Indicator Systems

**Double gloving reduces inner-barrier perforations and blood contamination of the hands.** The 2014 Cochrane review included 34 randomized trials and 6,890 person-operations. Compared with single gloves, double gloves reduced perforations of the protective inner barrier (rate ratio 0.29, 95% CI 0.23–0.37) and blood stains on skin (RR 0.35, 95% CI 0.17–0.70). These are occupational-exposure outcomes; evidence for preventing actual needlestick injuries was imprecise.<sup>[[11]](#ref11)</sup>

Three further Cochrane and later findings shape use. One small trial found triple gloving lowered perforations further (RR 0.03, 95% CI 0.00–0.52), but the evidence is low quality.<sup>[[11]](#ref11)</sup> A 2025 meta-analysis of 18 trials also found fewer inner-glove perforations with double gloving (OR 0.2, 95% CI 0.14–0.31) and less hand contamination (OR 0.28, 95% CI 0.14–0.54).<sup>[[22]](#ref22)</sup> Longer, deeper operations and the primary surgeon carry the highest perforation risk, which is where double gloving matters most.<sup>[[19]](#ref19)[[22]](#ref22)</sup>

The 2026 Enz consensus says double gloving should be considered for surgery, with recognized exceptions where single gloves are appropriate. When double gloving, it advises that all scrubbed team members wear an indicator inner glove. This is a manufacturer-funded consensus, not a trial establishing a universal standard for every incision.<sup>[[4]](#ref4)</sup> Direct evidence that double gloving alone reduces patient SSI remains insufficient. The Cochrane review found two trials with no infections in either arm, and WSES grades the evidence 2B.<sup>[[12]](#ref12)[[13]](#ref13)</sup>

Fit and tactile performance still matter. Outer-glove perforation rates were similar for double and single gloves in the Cochrane review (RR 1.10, 95% CI 0.93–1.31), which the authors read as no loss of dexterity. That is a surrogate; test an appropriate combination rather than dismissing a reported loss of sensation or control.<sup>[[11]](#ref11)[[4]](#ref4)</sup>

**Indicator gloves detect breaches; they do not prevent them.** In the Cochrane trials, indicator systems did not reduce the total number of perforations in an operation. A 2025 systematic review found a two- to six-fold higher detection rate than two same-colored gloves (that review had manufacturer funding and author employment). Change gloves as soon as a spot appears and still inspect the inner glove.<sup>[[11]](#ref11)[[23]](#ref23)</sup>

## Perforation Rates

Perforations are common and often unnoticed. The median control-group rate in the Cochrane trials was 18.5 perforations per 100 person-operations.<sup>[[11]](#ref11)</sup> In a prospective series of 70 open abdominal operations (280 glove pairs), 10.7% of gloves and 54.3% of operations had a perforation, and 78% of the perforations went unnoticed. Most were in the non-dominant hand. Risk was 5.8 times higher for surgeons than for other team members, and 12.8 times higher once operating time reached 61 minutes.<sup>[[19]](#ref19)</sup> A Tunisian water-leak study of 284 gloves found 16.5% perforated and none noticed; 61.7% of the perforated gloves came from urology cases, but the study did not report perforation rates by department, so this is not a comparative risk.<sup>[[20]](#ref20)</sup> A gastrointestinal series of 5,267 gloves found no difference between single (10.2%) and double (10.0%) gloving overall, but the inner glove failed less often than the outer (5.7% versus 11.6%).<sup>[[21]](#ref21)</sup>

## When to Change Gloves

| Trigger | Action and evidence |
|---|---|
| Known or suspected perforation, contamination or loss of integrity | Replace promptly with sterile technique; do not wait for a scheduled interval.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[2]](#ref2)</sup> |
| Prolonged wear | Incorporate planned changes into the local protocol. Enz 2026 suggests considering a **60–120-minute interval for single or outer gloves**, alongside procedural steps. This is not a demonstrated universal SSI-prevention threshold. Observational glove-testing data point the same way: outer-glove perforations rose after 60 minutes of wear (12.6% versus 7.1%) and accumulated fastest up to 90 minutes.<sup>[[4]](#ref4)[[21]](#ref21)</sup> |
| Inner glove during a long case | Change for a breach or contamination; an intact inner glove is not guaranteed safe for 240 minutes. One cohort found inner-glove perforations higher beyond 240 minutes (7.2% versus 4.4%), but the optimal routine inner-glove interval is unresolved.<sup>[[4]](#ref4)[[21]](#ref21)</sup> |
| Prosthesis handling | Plan a clean handling step with new sterile gloves under the device and institutional protocol. Arthroplasty reviews mainly measure glove contamination/perforation; they do not prove a particular timing regimen prevents AUS, penile-prosthesis or mesh infection. The arthroplasty reviews and the Enz consensus favor changing after draping, before handling the implant, before closure and at least hourly.<sup>[[4]](#ref4)[[14]](#ref14)</sup> |
| Abdominal wound closure after a contaminated intra-abdominal phase | Change the team's gloves and use separate sterile closure instruments as a combined intervention; see ChEETAh below. Enz recommends this change as part of a bundle in general surgery. WHO 2018 made no recommendation on it before ChEETAh, citing lack of evidence.<sup>[[4]](#ref4)[[15]](#ref15)</sup> |

The WSES position paper states that interval changes may protect staff (Grade 2C). Its discussion cites a 90-minute observational recommendation; its formal statement does not mandate that interval for every procedure.<sup>[[13]](#ref13)</sup>

### ChEETAh: a major trial relevant to abdominal reconstruction

ChEETAh randomized 81 hospitals in seven low- and middle-income countries, enrolling 13,301 patients undergoing clean-contaminated, contaminated or dirty abdominal surgery. Before handling wound edges for closure, the scrub team changed gloves (outer gloves if double gloved) and used a separate sterile needle holder, forceps and scissors.<sup>[[15]](#ref15)</sup>

Among patients assessed for the primary outcome, 30-day SSI occurred in 931/5,789 (16.1%) intervention patients versus 1,280/6,768 (18.9%) controls; adjusted RR 0.87 (95% CI 0.79–0.95). The intervention combined glove and instrument changes, so the trial cannot isolate the benefit of either component.<sup>[[15]](#ref15)</sup>

The cohort included 679 urologic operations, but the trial was not specific to urinary diversion or prostheses. Almost all operations were open; clean surgery and cesarean delivery were excluded. Apply the finding most directly to comparable abdominal reconstruction, without assigning the same effect size to clean implant surgery or purely endoscopic procedures.<sup>[[15]](#ref15)</sup>

## Latex Allergy Planning

Confirm the allergy history during preoperative planning and communicate it to the operating room, anesthesia and recovery teams. For confirmed natural rubber latex allergy, use synthetic surgical gloves and check the equipment and devices that will contact the patient.<sup>[[5]](#ref5)[[6]](#ref6)</sup>

ASCIA's operating-suite guidance recommends first-case scheduling and additional room preparation where powdered latex exposure could leave airborne allergen. It explicitly states that those extra measures are unnecessary in an operating suite that does not use powdered latex gloves. Do not impose first-case scheduling on every latex-allergic patient regardless of the local environment. Latex-safe equipment and communication remain necessary.<sup>[[6]](#ref6)</sup>
