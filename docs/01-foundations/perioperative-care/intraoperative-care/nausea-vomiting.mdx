# Postoperative Nausea & Vomiting (PONV)

Plan prevention, prompt rescue and discharge treatment together. PONV can delay drinking, mobilization and discharge; persistent vomiting after abdominal reconstruction also warrants assessment for a surgical or metabolic cause. This workflow follows the **Fifth Consensus Guidelines**, first published online in 2025, with current product-label safety precautions.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup>

See also: [Anesthesia](/docs/foundations/perioperative-care/intraoperative-care/anesthesia), [Analgesia](/docs/foundations/perioperative-care/intraoperative-care/analgesia), [ERAS](/docs/foundations/perioperative-care/perioperative-protocols/eras), [Constipation & Ileus](/docs/foundations/perioperative-care/postoperative-management/constipation), and [Corticosteroid pharmacology](/docs/foundations/pharmacology/perioperative-eras/corticosteroids).

## Assess Risk and Reduce Emetic Exposure

The adult Apfel score assigns one point each for female sex, nonsmoking, prior PONV/motion sickness and anticipated postoperative opioids. Historical approximate risks with 0–4 factors are 10%, 20%, 40%, 60% and 80%; these are population estimates rather than an individual's prediction after prophylaxis. The Fifth Consensus groups patients as low risk (0 factors), medium risk (1–2) and high risk (3 or more). Apfel does not capture the operation itself: urologic, gynecologic, laparoscopic and bariatric procedures each carry added risk, so an ordinary-looking score can understate exposure after pelvic reconstruction. Many centers now give multimodal prophylaxis to every general-anesthesia patient, which does not make risk assessment obsolete.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup>

| Adult risk | Prevention approach |
|---|---|
| No Apfel factors | Low risk; individualize prevention and account for consequences of vomiting. An ERAS pathway may still use general multimodal prophylaxis. |
| One or two factors | Use **two preventive interventions**, usually antiemetics from different classes plus appropriate baseline-risk reduction. |
| Three or four factors, or severe prior PONV | Multimodal prophylaxis with additional agents as appropriate and stronger baseline-risk mitigation. No single fixed triple regimen suits every patient. |

Use opioid-sparing analgesia, adequate hydration, propofol-based TIVA or regional/local techniques when suitable, and minimize volatile anesthetic/nitrous-oxide exposure. Propofol TIVA lowers PONV risk to roughly the level of volatile anesthesia plus a 5-HT₃ antagonist or droperidol, and it also adds to other antiemetics. Nitrous oxide as maintenance increases PONV. IV acetaminophen given before pain begins reduces nausea as part of multimodal analgesia. Avoid turning one retrospective model's blood-loss, positioning or laboratory associations into universal treatment thresholds. Adequate hydration does not mean indiscriminate fluid loading in cardiac or renal disease.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup>

## Select a Practical Adult Prophylactic Regimen

Dexamethasone plus a 5-HT₃ antagonist is a well-studied combination. Add or substitute another class according to previous response, QT risk, retention/delirium risk and formulary availability. Combination therapy is generally more effective, but not every combination is superior to every single agent. The large Cochrane network review included 585 trials; evidence for vomiting prevention was stronger than evidence for uncommon serious harms. Its drug rankings do not establish a universally safest regimen.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[2]](#ref2)</sup>

| Option | Adult workflow example | Important qualification |
|---|---|---|
| Dexamethasone | 4–8 mg IV at induction | Monitor glucose; coordinate with existing glucocorticoid coverage. |
| Ondansetron | 4 mg IV near the end of surgery | Timing differs from dexamethasone. Oral prophylaxis is not dose-equivalent to 4 mg IV. |
| Palonosetron | 0.075 mg IV | Longer acting; avoid routine repeat dosing in PACU. |
| Aprepitant | 40 mg orally before surgery | An NK₁ alternative/addition; review interactions and hormonal-contraception counseling. |
| IV aprepitant, APONVIE | 32 mg over 30 seconds before induction | FDA-labeled adult prophylaxis formulation; do not substitute chemotherapy formulations/doses automatically. |
| Amisulpride, BARHEMSYS | 5 mg IV over 1–2 minutes at induction | Prevention dose differs from the 10-mg treatment dose; QT precautions apply. |
| Haloperidol | 0.5–2 mg IV (1 mg is roughly equivalent to droperidol 0.625 mg) | Off label. Comparable efficacy to 5-HT₃ antagonists in trials, with QT and sedation effects. Often used where droperidol is restricted. |
| Metoclopramide | 10 mg IV | Weak antiemetic at this dose; consider only when other dopamine antagonists are unavailable. |
| Fosaprepitant | 150 mg IV (off label) | Ranked highest for vomiting prevention in the Cochrane network analysis, with moderate-certainty evidence; the analysis had no adverse-event data for it. |
| Promethazine | 6.25 mg IV for treatment (larger doses add sedation) | Injectable-administration and tissue-injury precautions below; sedation and anticholinergic effects. |
| Droperidol | Consensus prophylactic dose 0.625 mg IV near the end of surgery | US boxed warning, patient selection and ECG monitoring requirements remain relevant. |
| Transdermal scopolamine | One patch the prior evening; US PONV label removes it 24 hours after surgery | Delayed onset; retention, cognitive and heat-related risks may favor another agent. |

These are selected adult examples, not interchangeable order sets. Some consensus-supported uses are off label; use the specific product's administration instructions and patient-specific limits.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[3]](#ref3)[[4]](#ref4)[[5]](#ref5)[[6]](#ref6)</sup>

### Safety Checks That Change Selection

- **QT and interactions:** assess QT-prolonging combinations, potassium/magnesium and cardiac history. BARHEMSYS labeling advises avoiding congenital long-QT syndrome and concurrent droperidol, with ECG monitoring in susceptible patients. Droperidol's US label reserves use for inadequate response/intolerance to other treatments, contraindicates known/suspected QT prolongation, and requires a pretreatment ECG plus monitoring for 2–3 hours afterward.<sup>[[4]](#ref4)[[5]](#ref5)</sup>
- **Ondansetron:** avoid congenital long-QT syndrome; concomitant apomorphine is contraindicated. Severe hepatic impairment limits the total daily dose to 8 mg. The labeled oral adult PONV prophylaxis dose is 16 mg one hour before induction.<sup>[[7]](#ref7)</sup>
- **Dexamethasone:** a studied single perioperative dose has reassuring SSI evidence, but can cause transient hyperglycemia. Required hydrocortisone coverage is not a proven equivalent antiemetic regimen. Coordinate total steroid exposure and consider a non-steroid antiemetic; see [Steroids](/docs/foundations/perioperative-care/preoperative-assessment/steroids) for PADDI and adrenal coverage.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup>
- **Scopolamine:** can worsen urinary retention and confusion; avoid angle-closure glaucoma. After urogynecologic surgery the patch is associated with failed voiding trials: 45.9% versus 29.4% in one retrospective chart review of 449 women (adjusted OR 1.75, 95% CI 1.08–2.85), while a pooled analysis of four retrospective cohorts (752 patients) found a nonsignificant RR of 2.35 (95% CI 0.61–9.07). The Fifth Consensus suggests choosing another antiemetic in this group. Its 2025 label adds hyperthermia/decreased-sweating precautions. Avoid excessive heat, and remove the patch and seek assessment if hyperthermia or concerning neuropsychiatric effects occur. Follow its removal instructions, including before MRI.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[6]](#ref6)[[10]](#ref10)[[11]](#ref11)</sup>
- **Aprepitant:** review CYP interactions, warfarin monitoring and backup nonhormonal contraception for 28 days after exposure.<sup>[[3]](#ref3)</sup>

:::warning[Injectable promethazine requires a specific administration plan]
Severe tissue injury, including gangrene, can occur. FDA prefers **deep IM administration**. If IV administration is necessary, follow the current diluted-infusion instructions using a large vein, preferably a central catheter; do not use a hand/wrist vein or administer an undiluted IV push. The FDA instructions specify normal-saline dilution, a concentration no greater than 1 mg/mL and infusion over 20–40 minutes. Stop and evaluate infusion-site pain. Promethazine is contraindicated below age two because of respiratory-depression risk.<sup>[[8]](#ref8)[[9]](#ref9)</sup>
:::

### Class Side Effects at a Glance

| Class | Main adverse effects |
|---|---|
| 5-HT₃ antagonists | Headache and constipation; QT prolongation (ondansetron label) |
| Dopamine antagonists (droperidol, haloperidol, amisulpride, metoclopramide) | Sedation, extrapyramidal effects, QT prolongation (dose- and agent-dependent) |
| Dexamethasone | Transient hyperglycemia |
| Antihistamines and promethazine | Sedation, dry mouth, dizziness; anticholinergic urinary retention |
| Scopolamine | Dry mouth, blurred vision, dizziness, urinary retention, confusion |
| NK₁ antagonists | Constipation, fatigue, headache (IV aprepitant label) |

Antihistamine and anticholinergic antiemetics matter most in older men with outlet obstruction and after outlet or prolapse surgery, where they can tip a borderline bladder into retention.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[3]](#ref3)[[5]](#ref5)[[6]](#ref6)[[7]](#ref7)</sup>

## Treat Breakthrough PONV

Check which agents were given, at what time and at what dose. Assess pain/opioids, hydration, hypotension and other causes; after major abdominal surgery, consider ileus, obstruction, leak or infection when symptoms or examination warrant it. A nasogastric tube is a treatment for selected distension/obstruction, not routine PONV rescue.

1. **Choose a different pharmacologic class** after failed prophylaxis. If no prophylaxis was given, ondansetron 4 mg IV is one option.
2. Amisulpride **10 mg IV over 1–2 minutes** is labeled for adult treatment after no prophylaxis or prophylaxis with another class; apply its QT precautions.
3. Immediate repeat dosing of the same class is usually ineffective. After **more than six hours**, a repeat short-acting 5-HT₃ antagonist or butyrophenone can be considered when alternatives are unavailable, within dose and safety limits.
4. Do not routinely repeat long-acting palonosetron, aprepitant/fosaprepitant or dexamethasone in PACU. Scopolamine's delayed onset makes it unsuitable as the sole immediate rescue for active vomiting.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[4]](#ref4)</sup>

Prevention efficacy does not automatically establish rescue efficacy; IV aprepitant's FDA indication is prophylaxis. Escalate persistent symptoms and reassess the diagnosis rather than repeatedly stacking sedating or QT-prolonging medicines.

## Children and Discharge Planning

Use pediatric risk assessment and weight-based dosing, not adult Apfel probabilities. The Fifth Consensus recommends one preventive agent for children without clear risk factors, two for those with 1–2 factors, and at least two plus baseline-risk mitigation for higher risk. Evidence does not establish a routine third drug for every high-risk child. Its common-dose examples are ondansetron **0.05–0.1 mg/kg (up to 4 mg)** and dexamethasone **0.15 mg/kg (up to 4 mg)**; the VPOP and POVOC scores replace Apfel, and post-pubertal female sex, anticholinesterase reversal and volatile anesthesia add risk; pediatric anesthesia should determine age-appropriate indications and administration.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup>

Before ambulatory discharge, address continuing nausea, hydration and access to rescue treatment. Consider longer-acting prophylaxis for patients at high risk of postdischarge nausea/vomiting. Give return instructions for persistent inability to drink, severe/distending abdominal pain, fever or other concerning symptoms.

In GU practice, urologic procedures are themselves associated with higher PONV risk, and postdischarge nausea affects roughly 37% of outpatients in the first 48 hours (predictors include female sex, age under 50, prior PONV and opioid use). Ambulatory GU cases therefore justify a discharge plan.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup> Retention risk particularly matters after outlet and urogynecologic surgery. A freely draining ileal conduit does not develop bladder retention simply because an antihistamine is given; sedation, bowel effects and the patient's actual reconstructed anatomy remain relevant. Alvimopan treats selected postoperative ileus risk and does not replace antiemetic prophylaxis.
