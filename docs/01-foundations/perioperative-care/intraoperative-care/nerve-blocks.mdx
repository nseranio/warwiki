# Nerve Blocks

Regional blocks can reduce postoperative pain and opioid requirements when their coverage matches the operation. Two groups matter in reconstructive, functional and urogynecologic practice: **fascial plane blocks** (TAP, quadratus lumborum, erector spinae, rectus sheath) for abdominal-wall analgesia, and **targeted peripheral blocks** (pudendal, dorsal penile, penile ring, ilioinguinal/iliohypogastric, spermatic cord, periurethral, uterosacral or cervical) for perineal, penile, scrotal and office-based female procedures. Select the technique with the anesthesia team using the incision, anticipated visceral pain, patient anatomy, anticoagulation, existing neurologic deficits and available monitoring. The 2026 ASA guideline supports fascial plane blocks within multimodal analgesia for adult open abdominal, retroperitoneal and pelvic surgery, and for minimally invasive abdominal surgery. It does not rank ESP, QL and TAP against one another.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup>

This is the block-selection and safety workflow. Use [Local Anesthetics](/docs/foundations/pharmacology/neuropathic-pelvic-pain/local-anesthetics) for formulation-specific doses and [Liposomal Bupivacaine](/docs/foundations/pharmacology/intraoperative-adjuncts/liposomal-bupivacaine) for its label and comparative evidence. [Analgesia](/docs/foundations/perioperative-care/intraoperative-care/analgesia) covers systemic treatment and postoperative monitoring; [Anesthesia](/docs/foundations/perioperative-care/intraoperative-care/anesthesia) covers the anesthetic plan.

See also: [Nausea & Vomiting](/docs/foundations/perioperative-care/intraoperative-care/nausea-vomiting), [ERAS](/docs/foundations/perioperative-care/perioperative-protocols/eras), [Pelvic Neuroanatomy](/docs/foundations/anatomy-physiology/pelvis-support/pelvic-neuroanatomy).

## Local Anesthetic Pharmacology

### Plan the total exposure

Before injection, agree on the agent, concentration, total milligrams, sites and timing with everyone administering local anesthetic. Include bilateral blocks, wound infiltration, urethral or mucosal preparations, catheters and any IV lidocaine. Toxicity is additive; changing agents or dividing injections between surgeon and anesthesiologist does not reset the dose allowance.<sup>[[2]](#ref2)[[3]](#ref3)</sup>

A generic mg/kg ceiling is not a guarantee of safety. The product, route, vascularity of the site, age, body size, hepatic or cardiac function and repeat dosing matter. Choose the lowest effective exposure and follow the actual product label and institutional protocol. Adult block volumes must not be transferred to children.<sup>[[2]](#ref2)[[3]](#ref3)</sup>

### Amide agents used for blocks

| Agent | Usual concentration | Onset | Typical nerve-block duration | Labeled maximum (adult) |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Lidocaine** | 0.5–2% | Fast | 1–2 h | 4.5 mg/kg, not above 300 mg, without epinephrine; 7 mg/kg, not above 500 mg, with epinephrine<sup>[[4]](#ref4)</sup> |
| **Bupivacaine** | 0.25–0.5% | Slower | 4–8 h | Not more than 400 mg in 24 h; repeat no more often than every 3 h<sup>[[3]](#ref3)</sup> |
| **Ropivacaine** | 0.2–0.75% | Slower | 4–8 h | Label lists about 250 mg as the nerve-block dose and 770 mg per 24 h as the maximum recommended dose<sup>[[5]](#ref5)</sup> |

Onset and duration are typical ranges that vary with site, volume and adjuvants. Weight-based ceilings for bupivacaine (about 2–2.5 mg/kg), ropivacaine (about 3 mg/kg) and levobupivacaine (2 mg/kg in the vulvar series cited below) are teaching conventions, not label limits. Amides are metabolized hepatically and true allergy is rare. Esters (chloroprocaine, tetracaine, procaine) are hydrolyzed by plasma cholinesterase, and allergy is more common.

Four principles govern block behavior:<sup>[[6]](#ref6)</sup>

- **Lipid solubility** determines intrinsic potency.
- **Protein binding** determines duration.
- **pKa** determines onset; a lower pKa gives faster onset.
- **Mass of drug**, not volume or concentration, primarily drives onset, success and duration. Large-volume ropivacaine fascial plane blocks can reach high plasma concentrations, so reducing the mass injected is a safety lever.

### Mixing and adjuvants

Mixing lidocaine with bupivacaine or ropivacaine is common. In a randomized comparison of femoral and sciatic blocks, the mixture shortened onset (about 16 versus 23–28 minutes for the long-acting agent alone) but shortened block duration, and the investigators could not show a safety benefit because the lower long-acting plasma level was offset by lidocaine exposure.<sup>[[7]](#ref7)</sup> The current bupivacaine label does not recommend mixing or intercurrent use of other local anesthetics because clinical data are insufficient. Any planned combination needs a compatibility and cumulative-dose assessment. Liposomal bupivacaine has additional restrictions below.<sup>[[3]](#ref3)</sup>

Adjuvants are used to extend block duration:

- **Epinephrine 1:200,000** causes local vasoconstriction, slows systemic absorption and can act as an early marker of intravascular injection. Avoid it in end-artery fields such as the penis (see the penile ring block).
- **Dexamethasone** is the adjuvant with the most data. In a meta-analysis of 11 trials (914 patients), perineural dexamethasone prolonged analgesia by about 3 hours over systemic dexamethasone. The effect was clear with bupivacaine (4.0 h) but not significant with ropivacaine (2.0 h), and perineural use is off-label.<sup>[[8]](#ref8)</sup>
- **Clonidine and dexmedetomidine** are α2 agonists that extend sensory block, with sedation, bradycardia and hypotension as costs. Verify agent, preservative content and route in a pharmacy or anesthesia protocol; a drug given intravenously and the same drug injected around a nerve are different interventions, and efficacy cannot be assumed to transfer.
- **Bicarbonate** buffers lidocaine for infiltration pain (see below); it is not a duration adjuvant.

Single-shot blocks with standard agents plus adjuvants typically cover the first 12–24 hours. Beyond that, a continuous catheter is the established way to extend analgesia.

For IV lidocaine, follow the separation intervals and monitoring in [Analgesia](/docs/foundations/perioperative-care/intraoperative-care/analgesia#intravenous-lidocaine): a block or infiltration must be coordinated with the infusion, including longer restrictions after liposomal bupivacaine.

## Injection Best Practices

- Confirm consent, procedure, side, baseline sensory and motor findings, anticoagulants and the rescue plan. Deep blocks require assessment under the applicable [regional-anesthesia antithrombotic rules](/docs/foundations/perioperative-care/perioperative-protocols/antithrombotic-therapy); the current reference is the ASRA fifth edition (2025), which now describes anticoagulant doses as "low" and "high" rather than prophylactic and therapeutic.<sup>[[9]](#ref9)</sup>
- Use aseptic technique and appropriate anatomical or ultrasound localization. Ultrasound lowers LAST risk but does not eliminate intravascular injection or nerve injury.
- Use the lowest effective dose. Aspirate before incremental injections; negative aspiration is not proof of correct placement. Inject slowly, observe spread when using ultrasound and stop if placement is uncertain, injection is unexpectedly painful or resistance is abnormal. Bolus delivery drives both intravascular toxicity and infiltration pain.
- Use appropriate cardiovascular, respiratory and consciousness monitoring during and after injection. LAST can be delayed; the ASRA advisory recommends at least 30 minutes of close monitoring after potentially toxic doses, with 30–45 minutes specifically discussed for truncal blocks.
- Keep oxygen, resuscitation equipment, 20% lipid emulsion and the ASRA checklist immediately available at every block location. An office block needs the same capacity to recognize and initially manage toxicity as its drug exposure warrants.<sup>[[2]](#ref2)[[3]](#ref3)</sup>

### Reducing injection pain in awake and office cases

These maneuvers apply to office urethral procedures, perineal infiltration and awake penile or scrotal work:

- **Buffer lidocaine with sodium bicarbonate.** Lidocaine with epinephrine is far more acidic than tissue (pH about 4, versus about 6 for plain lidocaine). Titration data support roughly 1 mL of 8.4% bicarbonate per 10 mL of 1% lidocaine with epinephrine; plain lidocaine needs less. Use a validated pharmacy preparation; bupivacaine and ropivacaine can precipitate at higher pH, so buffer them only under a specific protocol.<sup>[[10]](#ref10)</sup>
- **Warm the solution** toward body temperature.
- Use small-gauge needles (27–30 G) and a slow infiltration rate.
- After the first puncture, inject through already-anesthetized tissue; the initial needle insertion is the most painful.
- Use vibration or cold skin cooling at the injection site, and a circumferential field block around a lesion rather than injecting into it.
- Explain the sequence and allow anesthetic onset before proceeding.

Warming and buffering follow a preparation protocol; neither replaces accurate placement or dose control.

### Local Anesthetic Systemic Toxicity (LAST)

Neurologic symptoms may include circumoral numbness, metallic taste, tinnitus, agitation, altered consciousness or seizures. Cardiovascular toxicity (arrhythmia, hypotension, arrest) can occur without a preceding neurologic warning. Stop local anesthetic, summon help and use the [ASRA LAST checklist](https://asra.com/docs/default-source/guidelines-articles/local-anesthetic-systemic-toxicity-rgb.pdf).<sup>[[2]](#ref2)[[11]](#ref11)</sup>

The posted 2020 checklist specifies:<sup>[[11]](#ref11)</sup>

- Support the airway; prefer benzodiazepines for seizures. Consider lipid emulsion early.
- **20% lipid, under 70 kg:** bolus approximately 1.5 mL/kg over 2–3 minutes, then 0.25 mL/kg/min. Over 70 kg: approximately 100 mL over 2–3 minutes, then approximately 250 mL over 15–20 minutes.
- If unstable, repeat the bolus and double the infusion. Continue infusion for more than 15 minutes after hemodynamic stability; maximum lipid dose 12 mL/kg.
- Resuscitation differs from standard ACLS: use smaller epinephrine doses, starting below 1 microgram/kg; avoid vasopressin, beta-blockers, calcium-channel blockers and local-anesthetic antiarrhythmics. Consider extracorporeal support for refractory collapse; prolonged resuscitation may be needed.
- Observe at least 2 hours after seizure or 4–6 hours after cardiovascular instability, with further care determined after cardiac arrest.

## Fascial Plane Blocks for Abdominal and Pelvic Reconstruction

### Transversus Abdominis Plane (TAP) Block

The target is the plane between internal oblique and transversus abdominis, which carries the T10–L1 anterior branches. Lateral and subcostal approaches cover different portions of the abdominal wall; select the approach to the actual incision and extraction site. TAP primarily addresses somatic abdominal-wall pain (skin, muscle, parietal peritoneum of the incision) and does not provide reliable visceral analgesia.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[12]](#ref12)</sup>

Reconstructive uses: open ileal conduit or continent reservoir, ureteral reconstruction (ureteroureterostomy, Boari flap, ileal ureter), enterocystoplasty and other lower-abdominal incisions, Pfannenstiel incisions, and combined abdominoperineal urethroplasty. Its effect may fade sooner than surgeon-administered wound infiltration in some studies, and large-volume ropivacaine TAP blocks can produce high plasma concentrations, so count the mass injected.<sup>[[6]](#ref6)</sup>

### Quadratus Lumborum (QL) Block

Specify the approach: anterior (transmuscular) QL targets the QL–psoas interface; posterior QL lies behind the muscle; lateral QL is at its lateral fascial border. These are distinct injections. Spread and effect vary, and QL must not be presented as a guaranteed visceral block or categorically superior to TAP.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[12]](#ref12)</sup> It is chosen when coverage cephalad to a TAP block is wanted: robotic ureteral reimplantation, pyeloplasty, augmentation, and flank or subcostal incisions.

### Erector Spinae Plane (ESP) Block

The target is deep to the erector spinae muscles at the transverse process. Select the vertebral level for the surgical territory (T7–T9 in the urologic series below). Paravertebral spread and visceral analgesia are variable; a technically successful ESP block does not replace a rescue plan.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[12]](#ref12)</sup>

### Rectus Sheath Block

The target is between rectus abdominis and its posterior sheath where that sheath is present. It is an option for midline somatic incisional pain (augmentation, Mitrofanoff, ileal ureter) and umbilical or periumbilical port sites. Coverage depends on injection level and spread; it does not cover all lateral ports or visceral pain.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[12]](#ref12)</sup>

### What the comparative evidence supports

The ASA 2026 guideline's recommendations concern pain and/or opioid reduction in the first 24 hours, with moderate evidence for adult abdominal surgery. Its minimally invasive nephrectomy/urology subgroup found modest pain reductions and lower opioid use; the evidence does not establish a single best block for every reconstruction.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup>

Micali et al. (2024) studied 190 patients prospectively: 97 received bilateral ESP (20 mL of 0.375% ropivacaine plus dexmedetomidine 0.5 mcg/kg per side at T7–T9) and 93 received bilateral TAP (20 mL of 0.375% or 0.25% ropivacaine). Allocation reflected clinician choice, and the regimens differed: ESP included dexmedetomidine, whereas TAP included perioperative morphine. Lower pain and rescue use in the ESP group are observational associations, not randomized proof of ESP superiority. There was no QL group.<sup>[[13]](#ref13)</sup>

A randomized trial of 110 patients after laparoscopic nephrectomy found lower 24-hour opioid consumption with ESP than QL (median 13 versus 25 µg sufentanil equivalents; median difference −8 µg, 95% CI −15 to 0, P = 0.041) and about one point lower pain scores. The absolute opioid difference was small and the trial was in nephrectomy, so it does not establish a hierarchy for reconstruction.<sup>[[14]](#ref14)</sup>

## Peripheral Blocks Specific to Urology

### Pudendal Nerve Block

The pudendal nerve (S2–S4) supplies the perineum, penis or clitoris, urethral sphincter and much of the perineal skin. A pudendal block may be useful for the perineal component of urethroplasty, perineal urethrostomy, AUS placement, rectourethral or other fistula repair and vaginal surgery, with additional anesthesia for deeper pelvic work, abdominal incisions or flap donor sites. It is not a reliable block of all bladder, trigonal or upper-vaginal visceral sensation.

**Ischial-spine approaches and Alcock-canal approaches are distinct.** Options include a transvaginal or transperineal injection at the ischial spine (through the sacrospinous ligament), nerve-stimulator guidance, ultrasound guidance at the Alcock canal, and fluoroscopic transgluteal injection for chronic pudendal neuralgia. Specify the target and guidance method rather than applying one needle direction or volume to all. Bendtsen's report of the Alcock-canal technique involved only three patients and two cadavers and used a small volume; it established feasibility, not comparative superiority or freedom from sacral plexus spread.<sup>[[15]](#ref15)</sup>

Catheter-related bladder discomfort is one supported use. In 175 men having TURP or TURBT under general anesthesia, bilateral nerve-stimulator-guided pudendal block lowered CRBD incidence through 12 hours (52% versus 69% at 12 h), with less moderate-to-severe CRBD (6% versus 16% at 12 h). This trial was in transurethral surgery, not reconstruction.<sup>[[16]](#ref16)</sup>

#### Urogynecology: trials disagree

Khalil et al. randomized 57 women aged 20–53 with stage I–II prolapse undergoing anterior or posterior colporrhaphy to nerve-stimulator-guided pudendal block (0.7 mL/kg of the anesthetic mixture at four sites) or general anesthesia. Pain and analgesic consumption were lower with block; return to normal daily activity took 3.6 versus 12.2 days, and satisfaction was 71.4% versus 27.8%. Because the comparator was general anesthesia alone rather than a general anesthetic with a sham block, this supports pudendal block as an option for selected repairs, not as a universal primary anesthetic.<sup>[[17]](#ref17)</sup>

Two later trials add a block to a general anesthetic and are negative or marginal. In a sham-controlled trial of 71 women (mostly prolapse repair; 9 mL of 0.25% bupivacaine plus triamcinolone), pain scores were equivalent in the PACU and on day 4, day-1 pain was lower but below the prespecified 20-mm clinical threshold, and opioid use did not differ significantly.<sup>[[18]](#ref18)</sup> In a 44-patient trial of transvaginal multicompartment repair within an ERAS pathway (20 mL of 0.5% bupivacaine bilaterally versus usual care), 24-hour opioid use (median 8 versus 6.7 MME) and return to activity did not differ.<sup>[[19]](#ref19)</sup> Where ERAS pathways already produce low opioid use, a pudendal block should not be expected to add much.

For major vulvar and groin excision, surgeon-administered ilioinguinal plus pudendal blocks (levobupivacaine up to 2 mg/kg divided over four sites) were followed by pain scores of 0–3 in 17 of 18 women, an uncontrolled observational series.<sup>[[20]](#ref20)</sup>

### Dorsal Penile Nerve Block

The paired dorsal penile nerves (pudendal branches) run deep to Buck fascia on either side of the dorsal vein and supply dorsal and ventral penile skin. Approaches include the traditional two-puncture subpubic technique, ultrasound guidance at the compartment just deep to Buck fascia, and an ultrasound-guided perineal approach. Avoid the adjacent vessels and corpora, and assess the ventral or frenular territory, which may need supplementary infiltration. Indications include hypospadias repair and revision, circumcision, paraphimosis reduction, priapism treatment and penile shaft procedures such as grafting or scar revision.<sup>[[21]](#ref21)</sup>

In 104 children having circumcision, an ultrasound-guided perineal dorsal penile block with 3–5 mL of ropivacaine 0.25% plus lidocaine 0.8% delayed first analgesic request compared with a caudal block and caused less leg numbness.<sup>[[22]](#ref22)</sup> A 33-child hypospadias RCT reported first analgesic requirement at approximately 32 versus 21 hours for ultrasound-guided dorsal penile versus nerve-stimulator-guided pudendal block. These are study-specific time-to-rescue results, not expected durations for every patient or proof that ultrasound is mandatory for all penile surgery.<sup>[[23]](#ref23)</sup>

### Penile Ring Block

Circumferential subcutaneous infiltration at the base of the shaft blocks the small cutaneous branches from both the dorsal nerves and the perineal branches of the pudendal. It can supplement or, for simple skin procedures such as circumcision, replace a dorsal block, and is quicker to perform in the awake office setting. Use a small needle, inject slowly with frequent aspiration and avoid a large-volume, high-pressure injection in tissue with uncertain perfusion.

Plain local anesthetic is standard. Do not add epinephrine or another vasoconstrictor without a specific reason: current bupivacaine/epinephrine labeling calls for carefully restricted quantities in the penis and warns of ischemic injury or necrosis. Assess the exact product and tissue perfusion before use.<sup>[[3]](#ref3)</sup>

### Spermatic Cord Block

Cord and surrounding-tissue blockade is widely used for office and operating-room scrotal-content procedures: vasectomy and vasovasostomy, hydrocelectomy, spermatocelectomy, microsurgical varicocelectomy and microTESE (as an adjunct to sedation or general anesthesia), scrotal biopsy and microsurgical cord denervation. The genital branch of the genitofemoral nerve travels with the cord; the ilioinguinal nerve runs nearby in the inguinal canal, outside the cord's internal contents. Skin innervation is broader, so a cord block may need separate cutaneous or ilioinguinal coverage.<sup>[[24]](#ref24)</sup>

Technique: identify the cord at the external ring or over the pubic tubercle, stabilize it between thumb and index finger, identify the vas and vessels, and place anesthetic around the intended neural tissue rather than into those structures. Total volumes of about 10–20 mL are a practice convention; count the total milligrams with any skin infiltration. Allow several minutes for onset and supplement with skin infiltration at the incision.

For **isolated unilateral chronic scrotal content pain**, AUA 2025 permits diagnostic cord and/or ilioinguinal block as expert opinion. Relief can inform counseling about denervation, but does not guarantee surgical success or exclude other pain generators.<sup>[[25]](#ref25)</sup>

### Ilioinguinal / Iliohypogastric Block

The ilioinguinal (L1) and iliohypogastric (T12–L1) nerves supply parts of the lower abdominal wall and inguinal and genital skin. Ultrasound-guided targeting is usually in the internal oblique–transversus abdominis plane, not between external and internal oblique. It can complement orchiopexy, hydrocelectomy, inguinal or Pfannenstiel incisional analgesia and, with pudendal block, major vulvar surgery; it does not anesthetize the entire testis, scrotum or vulva.<sup>[[12]](#ref12)[[20]](#ref20)[[24]](#ref24)</sup>

### Periurethral Block (Female)

This is local infiltration, used with topical anesthesia in selected office urethral procedures, particularly bulking, and sometimes for cystoscopy in women who do not tolerate topical anesthesia alone, urethral dilation or calibration, transurethral botulinum toxin injection and small periurethral procedures. A common office approach is topical lidocaine jelly at the meatus, then small-gauge infiltration of about 5–10 mL of 1% lidocaine around the meatus and periurethral tissue, aspirating before each injection and allowing several minutes for onset. This is office convention rather than a validated protocol; choose preparation, placement and monitoring from the actual procedure and device instructions, and count topical and injected anesthetic together.

**The bulking material is not a local anesthetic.** Bulkamid is polyacrylamide hydrogel and water; analgesia is a separate intervention. The FDA-hosted physician instructions describe separate anesthetic preparation and prohibit mixing the hydrogel with other substances. Routine cystoscopy, intradetrusor Botox and urethral procedures have different pain sources and do not all require a periurethral block.<sup>[[26]](#ref26)</sup>

### Uterosacral / Cervical and Paracervical Blocks

Local injection around the cervix or uterosacral region is a separate technique from pudendal blockade. In Barba's 40-woman nonrandomized pilot of vaginal hysterectomy and pelvic floor repair through uterosacral suspension, patients chose whether to receive a pre-emptive block of ropivacaine plus clonidine at 2, 4, 8 and 10 o'clock of the cervix after induction of general or spinal anesthesia. Pain was lower at 1 hour and at 24 hours with cough, but rescue-opioid use and hospital stay did not differ significantly. This does not establish a universal additive regimen or a replacement for the primary anesthetic.<sup>[[27]](#ref27)</sup>

Product restrictions matter: EXPAREL is contraindicated for obstetrical paracervical anesthesia. A gynecologic infiltration report does not override that contraindication.<sup>[[28]](#ref28)</sup>

## Liposomal Bupivacaine (Exparel) — the Evidence

The current label distinguishes local infiltration (adults up to 266 mg) from its established adult regional indications: interscalene, popliteal sciatic and adductor canal blocks (133 mg for the first two). Pudendal, penile, spermatic-cord and TAP regional use are not established labeled indications. Do not transfer an approved limb-block dose to a bilateral GU block or promise 72 hours of analgesia.<sup>[[28]](#ref28)</sup>

Avoid additional local anesthetic within 96 hours after EXPAREL, while following the label's specific permitted concurrent bupivacaine regimens (bupivacaine HCl not above a 1:2 milligram ratio to EXPAREL when admixed). It must not be admixed with lidocaine or other non-bupivacaine local anesthetics; administration after local lidocaine requires at least 20 minutes. Detailed dosing remains on the [pharmacology hub](/docs/foundations/pharmacology/intraoperative-adjuncts/liposomal-bupivacaine).<sup>[[28]](#ref28)</sup>

Comparative evidence is consistently modest:

- Ilfeld 2021 reviewed all 76 randomized trials available. Of 36 trials of infiltrated liposomal versus unencapsulated bupivacaine or ropivacaine, 4 (11%) reported a clinically relevant, statistically significant benefit. Of 12 trials, 11 (92%) suggested a peripheral nerve block with plain bupivacaine outperformed infiltrated liposomal bupivacaine. Trials at high risk of bias favored liposomal bupivacaine in 84% (16 of 19), versus 14% (4 of 28) at low risk.<sup>[[29]](#ref29)</sup>
- **Perineural meta-analysis (Hussain 2021):** 9 trials, 619 patients. Pooled 24–72 h pain area under the curve improved by 1.0 cm·h (below the 2.0 cm·h minimal important difference), and the difference became nonsignificant (0.7 cm·h, P = 0.100) after excluding one industry-sponsored trial. No difference in opioid use, time to first analgesic request, satisfaction, hospital stay or functional recovery.<sup>[[30]](#ref30)</sup>
- **Fascial plane blocks (Hussain 2024):** 16 trials, 1,287 patients. No difference in pain area under the curve (SMD −0.21, 95% CI −0.43 to 0.01, P = 0.058) or in day-2 and day-3 outcomes, whether or not plain bupivacaine was mixed in.<sup>[[31]](#ref31)</sup>
- **Peripheral nerve and field blocks (Nguyen 2023):** 27 trials, 2,122 patients. Rest pain was 0.9 points lower at 24 h and 0.7 points lower at 48–72 h; there was no difference in morphine use at any time. The authors graded the evidence as moderate.<sup>[[32]](#ref32)</sup>
- **A positive signal:** in an 80-patient supraclavicular block trial, adding liposomal bupivacaine to plain bupivacaine lowered 48-hour rest pain area under the curve (0.6 versus 1.4), but daily pain differences after day 1 were not significant.<sup>[[33]](#ref33)</sup>

Practical stance for GU reconstruction: standard bupivacaine or ropivacaine, with or without dexamethasone, is the default for peripheral and fascial plane blocks. A pain-score gain of about 1 point with no opioid-use difference rarely justifies the substantial cost premium, and for analgesia beyond 24 hours a continuous catheter with standard agent is the established alternative.

## Practical Block Selection by Reconstructive / Functional Operation

These are options to discuss, not mandatory protocols or dosing orders.

| Surgical territory | Options | Coverage to confirm |
|---|---|---|
| Abdominal reconstruction: augmentation, channel creation, ureteral repair/diversion | Incision-matched TAP, rectus sheath, QL or ESP; selected neuraxial techniques | Midline versus lateral/extraction-site pain, visceral pain, cumulative local anesthetic |
| Pfannenstiel incision (autologous sling, simple prostatectomy, retropubic work) | TAP or ilioinguinal/iliohypogastric | Lateral extent of the incision; visceral pain not covered |
| Perineal urethroplasty, AUS or fistula repair | Pudendal plus local infiltration as appropriate | Deep dissection, abdominal component and gracilis/other donor site |
| Penile surgery, including prosthesis and plaque surgery | Dorsal penile and/or ring block | Ventral/frenular, proximal and deeper operative territories; perfusion |
| Scrotal-content or inguinal surgery | Cord, skin infiltration and/or ilioinguinal/iliohypogastric block | Cutaneous versus cord/testicular pain and expected traction |
| Vaginal or vulvar repair | Procedure-specific pudendal, local or other regional technique; pre-emptive uterosacral/cervical block | Upper vaginal/visceral and anterior vulvar territories; trial evidence for added benefit is mixed |
| Office urethral bulking | Topical anesthesia with procedure-specific local infiltration | Patient comfort and separate device/anesthetic preparation |

Confirm block effect before relying on it, retain rescue analgesia, and give instructions about residual numbness, weakness, delayed toxicity and whom to contact after discharge.

## Key Takeaways

1. **Mass of drug**, not volume or concentration, primarily drives block onset, success and duration; count every source of local anesthetic toward one total.
2. **Aspirate, inject slowly, monitor, and keep lipid emulsion available** at every block location. LAST lipid dosing is 1.5 mL/kg over 2–3 minutes then 0.25 mL/kg/min (under 70 kg).
3. **Buffer lidocaine** (about 1 mL of 8.4% bicarbonate per 10 mL of 1% lidocaine with epinephrine) for awake injections; it is the simplest injection-pain lever.
4. **Perineural dexamethasone** extends bupivacaine blocks by roughly 4 hours in pooled data; the ropivacaine effect is not established and the route is off-label.
5. **No fascial plane block has been shown to be best** for reconstruction. ESP has shown lower opioid use than QL in one nephrectomy RCT and than TAP in an observational urologic series.
6. **Pudendal block** suits perineal urethroplasty, AUS and fistula work as an adjunct; in vaginal prolapse surgery the evidence is mixed, favorable against general anesthesia alone and neutral when added to an ERAS general anesthetic.
7. **Dorsal penile block** (ultrasound-guided where available) and ring block cover penile skin surgery; avoid epinephrine in penile blocks unless there is a specific reason.
8. **Spermatic cord block** covers scrotal-content surgery; a diagnostic cord block is permitted by AUA 2025 for isolated chronic scrotal content pain but does not guarantee denervation success.
9. **Periurethral infiltration** is the office companion to female bulking and other urethral procedures; the bulking agent is not an anesthetic.
10. **Liposomal bupivacaine** offers about 1 point of pain-score improvement with no opioid-use difference across meta-analyses; it is not labeled for pudendal, penile, cord or TAP use.
