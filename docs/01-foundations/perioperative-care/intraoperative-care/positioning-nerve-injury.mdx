# Positioning & Nerve Injury

Positioning is a shared surgical, anesthesia and nursing responsibility, and it is one of the few decisions in urologic surgery where a few minutes of planning changes the risk of a serious, lasting complication. A brachial plexus stretch from a lateral position, a well-leg compartment syndrome after a long lithotomy case, and postoperative visual loss after a long head-down case are uncommon but disabling. Neuropathy, pressure injury, compartment syndrome and ocular injury can still occur despite precautions; neither a particular stirrup nor a checklist guarantees prevention. Choose the least extreme position that provides adequate access, and reassess when the operation or the patient's physiology changes.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[2]](#ref2)</sup>

The positions used in reconstructive and functional urologic surgery (supine, lithotomy, steep Trendelenburg, lateral, prone, jackknife, split-leg and supine PCNL positions) each carry nerve, compartment and pressure risks, and specific steps keep the position from becoming a complication.

See also: [Pelvic Neuroanatomy](/docs/foundations/anatomy-physiology/pelvis-support/pelvic-neuroanatomy), [Anesthesia](/docs/foundations/perioperative-care/intraoperative-care/anesthesia), [Nerve Blocks](/docs/foundations/perioperative-care/intraoperative-care/nerve-blocks) and [ERAS](/docs/foundations/perioperative-care/perioperative-protocols/eras).

## General Principles

1. **Stay within the patient's awake range of motion.** Before induction, identify restricted joint movement, existing weakness or sensory loss, vascular disease and vulnerable skin. Positions that stretch a joint or muscle group beyond what the patient tolerates awake (for example the hamstrings in lithotomy) risk stretch injury.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup>
2. **Pad and offload bony prominences** (heels, sacrum, greater trochanter, elbow, lateral malleolus, occiput, fibular head). Pressure injury is the most common positioning injury. Avoid padding or restraints that are too tight, because they can themselves cause injury.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[2]](#ref2)</sup>
3. **Keep arm abduction to 90° or less in a supine patient** and avoid stretching the elbow or shoulder beyond a comfortable range.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup>
4. **Recheck after the drapes go on, after table changes or docking, and after any suspected movement.** The ASA advisory recommends periodic perioperative checks to confirm the position is maintained; it does not prescribe a fixed interval, so set one appropriate to the duration and risk of the case.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup>
5. **Document the position, supports, prolonged elevation or tilt, and any change made.** A written check is both a safety and a medicolegal record.

Confirm limb alignment, accessible IV lines and airway, and protection from retractors and equipment after positioning. Transfers should preserve spinal alignment and avoid traction on the airway, limbs, catheters and drains. Test a planned steep tilt before draping and use pressure-distributing supports and dry linens.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[2]](#ref2)</sup>

## Supine

**Setup.** Arms are tucked at the sides (standard for pelvic and minimally invasive work) or on padded armboards abducted to 90° or less. Keep the neck neutral, pad and offload the heels, and protect the fingers from table attachments. For tucked arms, keep the forearms neutral with the palms toward the thighs and protect the elbows. On armboards, a supinated or neutral forearm reduces pressure on the ulnar (postcondylar) groove.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup>

**Urologic applications.** Robotic pelvic surgery (with Trendelenburg), transperitoneal partial nephrectomy, cystectomy and diversion, and urethroplasty with separate leg access.

**Main risks.**

- **Ulnar neuropathy.** In a review of 1,129,692 anesthetics at one center, persistent ulnar neuropathy (more than 3 months) occurred in 1 per 2,729 patients. It was more common in men, in very thin and obese patients, and after hospitalization longer than 14 days. Anesthetic technique and intraoperative position were not associated with it, and many cases first appeared more than 24 hours after surgery. Padding is still sensible, but positioning does not explain every ulnar injury.<sup>[[6]](#ref6)</sup>
- **Heel pressure injury.** Pad both heels and offload them with a wedge or heel-suspension device.
- **Brachial plexus stretch.** Avoid abduction beyond 90° or shoulder extension.
- **Occipital pressure injury.** Uncommon; reposition or pad the head in prolonged cases.

Assess both compression and stretch: generous padding does not correct excessive shoulder traction or a tightly tucked arm.

## Lithotomy — Low, Standard, and Exaggerated

Lithotomy is the position most often linked to lower-extremity positioning injury in urologic surgery. The three variants are usually described by hip and knee angle. The ranges below are conventional teaching descriptions rather than validated prescriptions: use only the elevation, flexion and abduction the operation requires.

| Variant | Hip flexion (typical) | Typical use |
|---|---|---|
| **Low lithotomy** | about 40–60° | Cystoscopy, TURP, robotic pelvic surgery with leg access |
| **Standard lithotomy** | about 80–100° | Vaginal surgery, slings, perineal urethroplasty |
| **Exaggerated (high) lithotomy** | about 110–140° | Posterior urethroplasty, combined abdominoperineal cases |

Move both legs slowly and together, and use symmetric, properly fitted supports. Keep the ankle and knee in line with the contralateral shoulder, protect the fibular head, and avoid forced hip rotation, excessive hip flexion with an extended knee, and concentrated calf or popliteal pressure.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[2]](#ref2)</sup>

### Nerve injury

In a prospective study of 991 adults in lithotomy, 15 (1.5%; 95% CI 0.8–2.5%) developed a lower-extremity neuropathy. The nerves involved were the obturator (5 patients), lateral femoral cutaneous (4), sciatic (3) and common peroneal (3). Nearly all had paresthesia rather than weakness, symptoms were noted within 4 hours of the end of anesthesia, and 14 of 15 resolved within 6 months. Longer time in lithotomy, especially beyond 2 hours, was the main risk factor.<sup>[[7]](#ref7)</sup> Much of that series was gynecologic and general surgery, so extrapolate cautiously to long reconstructive cases.

| Nerve | Usual mechanism | Presentation |
|---|---|---|
| **Common peroneal** | Compression against the fibular head by a stirrup post or strap | Foot drop, dorsal foot numbness |
| **Femoral** | Excess hip flexion or abduction; retractor blades on the psoas | Quadriceps weakness, saphenous sensory loss |
| **Sciatic** | Hip flexion with knee extension (hamstring stretch) | Posterior leg and foot deficits |
| **Obturator** | Extreme hip abduction; obturator fossa retraction | Medial thigh sensory loss, adductor weakness |
| **Lateral femoral cutaneous** | Compression near the ASIS or groin flexion | Lateral thigh paresthesia (meralgia paresthetica) |
| **Saphenous** | Pressure at the medial tibial plateau from leg supports | Medial leg and foot paresthesia |

Nerve injury may arise from positioning or surgical retraction. New foot drop, quadriceps weakness or sensory loss needs examination and localization. Duration, severity and persistence vary; a single pooled "lithotomy neuropathy rate" cannot predict an individual case.

### Well-Leg Compartment Syndrome (WLCS)

Elevating the legs, external compression and impaired perfusion can produce compartment syndrome in a previously uninjured leg. Each 2.5 cm of height above the heart lowers calf mean arterial pressure by about 2 mmHg, and lithotomy lowers lower-extremity pressure slightly more than the height gradient alone predicts.<sup>[[3]](#ref3)[[8]](#ref8)</sup>

**Incidence.** Estimates vary widely with the operation and the method of ascertainment. A postal survey of 261 UK consultant urologists reported 65 cases, 51 after cystectomy with diversion, an estimated 1 in 500 after cystectomy, and rarity in procedures shorter than 4 hours. Risk factors were blood loss, peripheral vascular disease and obesity.<sup>[[9]](#ref9)</sup> The 2019 multidisciplinary guideline quotes roughly 1 in 3,500 for abdominopelvic surgery generally and lists younger age, higher BMI and vascular disease as risk factors.<sup>[[3]](#ref3)</sup> These come from surveys and case series, so treat them as approximations.

**Time limit.** The guideline recommends minimizing elevation, not exceeding 4 hours of uninterrupted elevation above the heart, followed by at least 15 minutes at or below heart level. Plan earlier changes when feasible; four hours is a ceiling, not a guaranteed safe window. If a step of the operation prevents lowering, document the reason and lower as soon as it is safe.<sup>[[3]](#ref3)</sup>

**Mitigation.**

- Use lithotomy only for the part of the operation that needs it. Do the abdominal or supine portions first, or reposition, rather than holding the legs up throughout.<sup>[[8]](#ref8)</sup>
- Keep hip and knee flexion and leg elevation to what is needed, and avoid combining maximal elevation with steep tilt when access allows.
- No leg-support design has been proved to eliminate WLCS. In the guideline's review, ankle slings were acceptable for short procedures (under about 30 minutes), while Allen-type stirrups and knee supports raised compartment pressures when the legs were elevated. Avoid tight calf straps and excessive ankle dorsiflexion.<sup>[[3]](#ref3)</sup>
- Assess correctly fitted mechanical VTE prophylaxis separately.

:::danger[Postoperative leg pain can be time-critical]
Severe or escalating pain, tense swelling, weakness or sensory change after pelvic surgery warrants urgent assessment for compartment syndrome. Symptoms usually appear on waking, and more than 99% within 24 hours. Normal pulses do not exclude it. Obtain immediate orthopedic or vascular help; do not delay treatment for DVT imaging or CK results. A CK above 2,000 U/L is uncommon after surgery and raises concern. If the initial clinical assessment is equivocal, repeat it within 30 minutes. Compartment-pressure measurement may assist when examination is unreliable or uncertainty persists; the guideline treats diastolic pressure minus compartment pressure &lt;30 mmHg in any compartment as an indication for immediate bilateral four-compartment fasciotomy. Clinically likely WLCS also requires emergency open four-compartment fasciotomy, with treatment under way within one hour of diagnosis.<sup>[[3]](#ref3)</sup>
:::

### Exaggerated Lithotomy and Rhabdomyolysis

Prolonged exaggerated lithotomy during urethral reconstruction carries a documented risk of lower-extremity injury.<sup>[[10]](#ref10)[[11]](#ref11)</sup> In 10 urethroplasty patients, serum CK rose above 1,000 U/L after several hours in exaggerated lithotomy despite minimal muscle dissection, and one had myoglobinuria.<sup>[[10]](#ref10)</sup> In 185 open urethroplasties, 18 (10%) had a position-related lower-extremity complication (compartment syndrome, rhabdomyolysis or neurapraxia), 4 of them severe.<sup>[[11]](#ref11)</sup> Risk rose with stricture length and with the duration of surgery and of lithotomy; procedures under 5 hours carried minimal risk, and stirrup type, height, weight and BMI did not change it. For long penile skin flap repairs, the authors harvested the flap supine and then moved to high lithotomy for the perineal transfer, which virtually eliminated severe complications.<sup>[[11]](#ref11)</sup>

Limit exaggerated lithotomy to the portion of the operation requiring that exposure. A prone approach is not interchangeable with a planned perineal reconstruction. The [fistula-repair atlas](/docs/surgical-techniques/04h-fistula-repair) describes approach-specific access; York–Mason is commonly performed prone jackknife, rather than being a standard example of exaggerated lithotomy.

Prolonged exaggerated lithotomy has been associated with muscle injury.<sup>[[10]](#ref10)</sup> In the 185-patient urethroplasty series, stricture length and time in surgery or high lithotomy were associated with position-related lower-extremity complications; BMI and stirrup type were not.<sup>[[11]](#ref11)</sup> In a separate national inpatient analysis of major urologic operations, younger age, male sex, diabetes, chronic kidney disease, obesity and bleeding were associated with rhabdomyolysis; these associations were not validated specifically for urethroplasty.<sup>[[25]](#ref25)</sup> Postoperative muscle pain or swelling, oliguria, dark urine or rising creatinine may signal rhabdomyolysis, although symptoms can be absent. In a suspected case, check CK, creatinine and potassium and follow serial CK and potassium; hyperkalemia may require urgent treatment.<sup>[[23]](#ref23)</sup> Aggressive intravenous hydration is the treatment mainstay; a 2025 systematic review and meta-analysis of urine alkalinization across five studies found no significant reduction in acute kidney injury, acute renal failure or dialysis need, so routine bicarbonate administration is not established practice.<sup>[[20]](#ref20)</sup>

## Trendelenburg for Robotic Pelvic Surgery

Robotic prostatectomy, cystectomy and deep pelvic reconstruction are performed head-down, usually with the legs in low lithotomy. Use the smallest tilt and shortest exposure compatible with the operation.

**Physiologic effects.** Head-down tilt with pneumoperitoneum raises intracranial and intraocular pressure, reduces functional residual capacity and raises airway pressure, and can cause facial, conjunctival and laryngeal edema. Coordinate the need for continued tilt with anesthesia in prolonged cases. Extubation depends on airway assessment and recovery, not on an automatic cuff-leak test triggered by a fixed number of hours. Maintain an emergency undocking and access plan; see [Anesthesia](/docs/foundations/perioperative-care/intraoperative-care/anesthesia).

**Preventing slide.** Patient slide is the main mechanical risk. Use a pressure-distributing anti-slide surface or bean-bag that fits the patient and table, and test the position before draping. The ASA advisory recommends avoiding shoulder braces in a steep head-down position; braces concentrate force on the shoulder and can injure the brachial plexus.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup>

**Nerve injury in robotic surgery.**

| Study | Design | Finding |
|---|---|---|
| Göksu 2025 | 868 RARP patients, retrospective with patient recall | 5.6% positional peripheral nerve injury (49 patients); 55% upper, 51% lower extremity; higher BMI, operative time and Trendelenburg time in affected patients (exploratory ROC cutoffs of BMI 29, operative time 212 min and Trendelenburg time 157 min; AUC 0.58–0.67, without external validation); mean recovery about 6.5 months<sup>[[12]](#ref12)</sup> |
| Koç 2012 | 377 RALP patients on a split-leg table | 1.3% (5 patients); longer anesthesia-to-emergence time the only risk factor (about 496 vs 366 min); three had features of femoral mononeuropathy<sup>[[13]](#ref13)</sup> |
| Grossmann 2025 | RCT, 98 RARP patients, ankle-brace vs shoulder-support fixation | Postoperative-day-1 neuropathy in 15% (shoulder support) vs 6.5% (ankle brace), not significant; all but one sensory; none persisted at a median 12 months<sup>[[14]](#ref14)</sup> |
| Bjøro 2020 | Systematic review, 11 studies of robotic surgery in lithotomy and steep Trendelenburg | Incidence 0.16–10.0%; prolonged operative time, high ASA class, comorbidity and high BMI were risk factors<sup>[[15]](#ref15)</sup> |

Grossmann et al. found mainly sensory deficits and no persistent neuropathy at a median 12 months.<sup>[[14]](#ref14)</sup> In Göksu et al.'s retrospective series, 14 patients with lower-limb injury had weakness and 3 of 49 injured patients still had numbness at 12 months; longer operative and Trendelenburg times and higher BMI were associated with injury.<sup>[[12]](#ref12)</sup> Femoral-pattern symptoms occurred in 3 of 5 injured patients in Koç et al.'s split-leg series, which had no positioning comparator.<sup>[[13]](#ref13)</sup> The small RCT was underpowered to compare fixation methods.

### Eyes and Postoperative Visual Loss

Protect closed eyelids, tape or shield the eyes, and keep all devices clear of the globe. Verify that the head has not shifted and that the eyes remain free of pressure. Visual loss has several possible mechanisms; direct globe compression is particularly relevant to retinal arterial occlusion and should not be conflated with every case of ischemic optic neuropathy. Adequate blood pressure and avoidance of anemia are sensible in any long, steeply tilted case.<sup>[[4]](#ref4)</sup>

The ASA 2019 visual-loss advisory concerns prone spine surgery, where prolonged means more than 4 hours and substantial blood loss more than 800 mL. It recommends keeping the head level with or above the heart when possible, in a neutral position, monitoring blood pressure and hemoglobin, checking the eyes periodically, and ophthalmologic assessment when a high-risk patient wakes. It is not a guideline mandating timed returns to level during robotic GU surgery, and GU-specific benefit and an optimal check interval are not established. New visual impairment after any operation requires urgent ophthalmological assessment.<sup>[[4]](#ref4)</sup>

Corneal abrasion is the most common ophthalmic complication of general anesthesia for nonocular surgery. It is usually treatable when recognized, but microbial keratitis or permanent scarring can follow; eye protection does not eliminate the risk.<sup>[[21]](#ref21)[[24]](#ref24)</sup>

## Lateral Decubitus — Flank Position

**Setup.** Position the operative side up. Roll the torso and pelvis together. Elevate the kidney rest and flex the table at the level of the iliac crest to open the space between the crest and the 12th rib only as far as exposure requires. Flex the down leg, extend the up leg, and place a pillow or padding between the legs. Support the dependent thorax with a roll caudal to the axilla, not in it, and support both arms without traction.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[2]](#ref2)</sup>

**Urologic applications.** Open and laparoscopic nephrectomy, adrenalectomy, retroperitoneal lymph node dissection, open pyeloplasty and other flank-approach upper-tract reconstruction.

**Main risks.**

- **Brachial plexus, up side:** traction from an unsupported arm or tape pulling on the shoulder.
- **Brachial plexus, down side:** compression from a roll placed in the axilla instead of below it.
- **Common peroneal nerve, down leg:** compression between the fibular head and the table; pad the fibular head.
- **Rhabdomyolysis and pressure injury:** prolonged focal pressure on the flank, trochanter, knee and lateral malleolus, particularly in obese patients. Pronounced flank pain or swelling after a long case warrants evaluation rather than being dismissed as an expected incision symptom.
- **Eye and ear:** pressure from a bean-bag or head support; check both after turning.
- **Intercostal neurovascular stretch** from aggressive flexion.

## Prone, Prone Jackknife and Split-Leg Positions

**Setup.** Support the chest and pelvis on a frame or bolsters so the abdomen hangs free. Free abdomen preserves ventilation and limits IVC compression. Tuck the arms or place them on boards with the shoulders abducted 90° or less and the elbows flexed about 90°. Arrange the head support so that airway security and the absence of globe pressure can be checked.<sup>[[2]](#ref2)[[4]](#ref4)</sup>

**Urologic applications.** Classic prone PCNL, prone jackknife for posterior or transanal approaches (York–Mason for rectourethral fistula), and split-leg prone setups for combined transanal and perineal access.

**Main risks.**

- **Ocular injury:** direct pressure on the globe is the feared scenario. Check eye position after every repositioning.
- **Brachial plexus:** shoulder abduction beyond 90°.
- **Ulnar neuropathy:** elbow compression.
- **Breast and genital pressure:** check after positioning and reposition if compressed.
- **Elevated intra-abdominal and airway pressure:** from inadequate abdominal free space.

**Jackknife** adds table flexion at the hip to elevate the buttocks for perineal or transanal access. It adds back and hip ligament strain and lower-extremity venous congestion. **Split-leg** setups add abduction for combined access and bring lithotomy-type peroneal and femoral risks into the prone position. Both require reassessment of joint range, pressure points and emergency access, and neither removes the risks of a prolonged operation. Reassess after turning or flexing the table.

## Supine Variants for PCNL and Combined Access

The **supine Valdivia position** (described in 1998 in a series of 557 patients) and its **Galdakao modification** allow simultaneous percutaneous and retrograde access in a single setup, which is useful for complex stones and combined antegrade and retrograde endoscopy (ECIRS).<sup>[[16]](#ref16)[[17]](#ref17)</sup>

### Setup (as commonly described)

- The patient lies supine on a standard table with the ipsilateral flank and shoulder raised about 30° on a wedge or fluid bag.
- The ipsilateral arm is placed across the chest; the contralateral arm rests on a board.
- The leg on the puncture side is kept extended and out of the way of the flank, while the contralateral leg is abducted and flexed so the perineum is accessible for flexible ureteroscopy. Exact leg arrangements vary with the table, body habitus, target calyx and planned endoscopy, so confirm leg and arm supports with the operating team before induction.

### Advantages and limits

- One position for combined antegrade and retrograde work, and easier airway access than prone.
- A smaller working space for puncture. Retrorenal colon can occur in either position; one CT series found it more often prone than supine.<sup>[[22]](#ref22)</sup> Review preoperative CT for retrorenal or interposed colon, pleura and other organs; do not infer a safe tract from the position's name.
- Complex upper-pole access is sometimes more ergonomic prone.

### Evidence

In the CROES global registry of 5,803 PCNLs, 4,637 (80.3% of cases with recorded position) were prone.<sup>[[18]](#ref18)</sup> Prone PCNL had a higher stone-free rate (77.0% versus 70.2%) and shorter operative time (82.7 versus 90.1 min) but more transfusion (6.1% versus 4.3%) and fever (11.1% versus 7.6%); the authors concluded the choice should be tailored to the patient and surgeon.<sup>[[18]](#ref18)</sup> In a randomized trial of 112 adults with complex stones, the primary postoperative-day-1 success rates were 62.5% supine versus 57.1% prone; secondary 90-day stone-free rates were 55.4% versus 50.0%.<sup>[[19]](#ref19)</sup> Clavien grade 3 or higher complications occurred in 3.6% versus 14.3%, respectively, in this small trial.<sup>[[19]](#ref19)</sup> The EAU 2026 evidence summary states that prone and supine positions are equally safe with equivalent stone-free rates (LE 1a); it cites meta-analyses in which supine PCNL had a shorter operative time and, in the larger analysis, fewer major complications. Supine positioning allows simultaneous retrograde access with a flexible ureteroscope. Neither position is safer for every obese or cardiopulmonary-limited patient, and prone positioning remains an established option.<sup>[[5]](#ref5)</sup>

## Common Positioning Complications — Summary

| Complication | Highest-risk position(s) | Prevention |
|---|---|---|
| Ulnar neuropathy | Supine, tucked arms; any long case | Neutral or supinated forearm; pad the elbow; recognize that not all cases are positional |
| Brachial plexus injury | Lateral (both sides), prone, steep Trendelenburg with braces | Roll caudal to the axilla; avoid shoulder braces in steep head-down; abduction 90° or less |
| Lower-extremity neuropathy (obturator, LFCN, sciatic, peroneal, femoral) | Lithotomy, split-leg | Least elevation and flexion needed; pad the fibular head; limit time; avoid retractor pressure on the psoas |
| Well-leg compartment syndrome | Prolonged lithotomy, especially with blood loss or obesity | 4-hour ceiling, then at least 15 minutes level; avoid tight supports; low threshold to assess |
| Rhabdomyolysis | Long exaggerated lithotomy, long flank cases in obese patients | Limit time; supine harvest before lithotomy where possible; check CK when suspicious |
| Postoperative visual loss / corneal injury | Prone, prolonged head-down | Eyes taped and free of pressure; maintain blood pressure; periodic eye checks |
| Pressure injury | Any long case | Pad and offload bony prominences; reposition when possible |

## Preoperative Positioning Plan — Practical Checklist

1. Decide the position before induction, using the patient's awake range of motion as the limit.
2. Estimate case duration; if lithotomy may exceed 4 hours, flag WLCS in the team brief and time-out, agree when to lower or reposition the legs, and document leg-elevation and recovery intervals.<sup>[[3]](#ref3)</sup>
3. Confirm the operative side at the time-out.
4. Pad every bony prominence, and confirm nothing presses on the fibular head.
5. Place the roll caudal to the axilla in lateral decubitus.
6. Tuck arms with neutral or supinated forearms; keep abduction to 90° or less.
7. Protect the eyes; verify no globe pressure in prone and head-down cases.
8. Use an anti-slide surface for steep Trendelenburg and avoid shoulder braces.
9. Document the position and recheck after draping, after table changes and at intervals suited to the case.
10. Before extubation and on the ward, examine for new motor or sensory deficits, tense swelling or visual change.

---

## Postoperative Check and Handover

Record the position, supports, prolonged elevation or tilt, changes made and any intraoperative concern. Assess new motor or sensory deficits and skin injury when the patient can participate. Severe pain, rapidly progressive weakness, tense swelling or visual change needs urgent evaluation. Compartment syndrome, neuraxial complications and direct surgical injury must not be mislabeled as routine positional neuropraxia.
