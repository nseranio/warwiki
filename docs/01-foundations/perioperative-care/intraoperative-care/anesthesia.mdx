# Anesthesia — An Overview for the Reconstructive Urologist

The surgeon and anesthesia team should agree on the expected duration, position, blood loss, airway access after docking, analgesia and postoperative destination. This overview covers those decisions and recognition of important complications. Drug dosing and block technique belong in the linked pharmacology and procedural articles.

See also: [Analgesia](/docs/foundations/perioperative-care/intraoperative-care/analgesia), [Nerve Blocks](/docs/foundations/perioperative-care/intraoperative-care/nerve-blocks), [Nausea & Vomiting](/docs/foundations/perioperative-care/intraoperative-care/nausea-vomiting), [Positioning & Nerve Injury](/docs/foundations/perioperative-care/intraoperative-care/positioning-nerve-injury) and [ERAS](/docs/foundations/perioperative-care/perioperative-protocols/eras).

## Anesthetic Techniques and Services

| Term | What it describes | Reconstructive application |
|---|---|---|
| **General anesthesia** | Drug-induced unconsciousness; airway, ventilation, analgesia and immobility require a coordinated plan | Most prolonged abdominal and robotic reconstruction |
| **Regional anesthesia** | Neuraxial or peripheral blockade, with or without sedation or general anesthesia | Selected endoscopic/perineal operations or postoperative analgesia |
| **Local anesthesia** | Infiltration of the operative field | Selected minor procedures; total local-anesthetic exposure must include all injections and blocks |
| **Monitored anesthesia care (MAC)** | An anesthesia service, **not a particular depth of sedation** | Selected procedures requiring anesthesia assessment, monitoring, titration and capacity to rescue or convert to general anesthesia |

ASA explicitly distinguishes MAC from moderate sedation. Purposeful response to verbal or light tactile stimulation describes moderate sedation; a patient receiving MAC may move through different depths. Neither the label “MAC” nor initially preserved spontaneous breathing removes the need for airway rescue capability.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup> Typical urologic uses are flexible cystoscopy, urodynamics, stent exchange and some prostate biopsies, usually with propofol, an opioid, dexmedetomidine or midazolam in varying combinations.

## General Anesthesia — Induction, Maintenance, Emergence

**Induction** includes preoxygenation, a plan for loss of airway reflexes and appropriate monitoring. Hypnosis, analgesia and muscle relaxation are separate effects: a hypnotic or neuromuscular blocker does not substitute for analgesia. **Maintenance** must accommodate pneumoperitoneum, position, blood loss and changing surgical stimulation. **Emergence** includes recovery of ventilation and muscle strength, analgesic transition and an explicit extubation plan.

Volatile anesthesia and propofol-based total intravenous anesthesia are both reasonable for noncardiac surgery. The 2024 AHA/ACC guideline finds no apparent difference in cardiovascular events between these approaches; this does not establish that they have identical effects on every outcome. The same guideline regards neuraxial or general anesthesia as reasonable when neuraxial anesthesia is feasible.<sup>[[2]](#ref2)</sup>

On environmental grounds, the 2026 AUGS sustainability white paper suggests that urogynecologists encourage anesthesia colleagues to use regional anesthesia and intravenous propofol when appropriate, because unmetabolized inhaled agents (desflurane above all) carry a large greenhouse-gas effect; it notes that the supporting evidence is inconsistent, and the anesthetic choice remains with the anesthesia team.<sup>[[21]](#ref21)</sup>

Propofol-based anesthesia can reduce baseline PONV risk as part of a combined prevention plan; see [PONV](/docs/foundations/perioperative-care/intraoperative-care/nausea-vomiting). A patient susceptible to malignant hyperthermia requires a nontriggering anesthetic and appropriate workstation preparation, not simply a change in the maintenance infusion.<sup>[[7]](#ref7)</sup>

## Regional Anesthesia

| Technique | Delivery | Best suited to |
|---|---|---|
| **Spinal** | Single subarachnoid injection; dense block of finite duration | Short endoscopic, perineal and lower pelvic operations (TURP, TURBT, some transvaginal repairs) |
| **Epidural** | Catheter; titratable, continuous infusion | Selected major open abdominal operations; continued postoperative analgesia |
| **Combined spinal-epidural** | Rapid spinal block plus a catheter for prolongation | Longer operations that also need postoperative epidural analgesia |

Either neuraxial route may be unsuitable because of the operation, patient physiology, infection, coagulation status or inability to manage complications. Epidural analgesia is an option for selected major open abdominal operations, rather than a requirement for every major GU reconstruction.<sup>[[2]](#ref2)</sup>

**What neuraxial blockade offers.** In a Cochrane overview of nine reviews, neuraxial blockade compared with general anesthesia alone reduced 0–30-day mortality (RR 0.71, 95% CI 0.53–0.94; 20 studies, 3,006 participants) and pneumonia (RR 0.45, 95% CI 0.26–0.79; 5 studies, 400 participants), with no difference in myocardial infarction (RR 1.17, 95% CI 0.57–2.37; 6 studies, 849 participants). Adding a neuraxial block to general anesthesia did not change mortality (RR 1.07, 95% CI 0.76–1.51; 18 studies, 3,228 participants) but reduced pneumonia (RR 0.69, 95% CI 0.49–0.98; 9 studies, 2,433 participants). The included trials are small and mostly older, so these figures support choosing neuraxial anesthesia where it suits the operation, not as a mortality strategy.<sup>[[11]](#ref11)</sup>

**Risks to plan for.** Hypotension, motor weakness, urinary retention, postdural puncture headache (less frequent with atraumatic needles), and, rarely, spinal epidural hematoma. Treat hypotension according to its cause; a large fluid bolus is not an automatic response to every sympathectomy. New neurological deficits after neuraxial anesthesia require prompt assessment rather than attribution to positioning or a slowly resolving block.

Antithrombotic timing applies to both insertion and catheter removal, varies with drug, dose and renal function, and must be coordinated with postoperative prophylaxis. The fifth-edition ASRA guideline (2025) reorganizes recommendations by low-dose and high-dose regimens, keeps deliberately conservative interruption intervals because hemorrhagic complications are too rare to test in trials, and adds drug-specific assays where they can guide timing. Use the current edition for the exact interval rather than remembered tables. See [Antithrombotic Therapy](/docs/foundations/perioperative-care/perioperative-protocols/antithrombotic-therapy).<sup>[[12]](#ref12)</sup>

### Peripheral Blocks and Local Anesthetic Safety

Peripheral blocks complement the anesthetic but have procedure- and approach-dependent coverage; no fascial-plane block reliably replaces the full visceral, somatic and systemic analgesic plan. Options most used in GU work:

- **TAP or quadratus lumborum block:** abdominal-wall analgesia for open pelvic surgery, cystectomy and nephrectomy; quadratus lumborum reaches further toward the flank and retroperitoneum.
- **Rectus sheath block:** midline laparotomy or umbilical port sites.
- **Pudendal block:** perineal urethroplasty and vaginal surgery, as an adjunct.
- **Ilioinguinal/iliohypogastric block:** inguinal and scrotal surgery.

See [Nerve Blocks](/docs/foundations/perioperative-care/intraoperative-care/nerve-blocks) for technique. Count every injection (surgeon infiltration, blocks, tumescent or urethral instillation) toward one total local-anesthetic dose, and tell the anesthesia team what has been given.

**Local anesthetic systemic toxicity (LAST)** presents as perioral numbness, tinnitus, seizure or sudden arrhythmia and hypotension, sometimes after the injection has finished. The ASRA checklist (2020 version) is: call for help and obtain the LAST rescue kit; secure the airway; treat seizures with a benzodiazepine (low-dose propofol only if none is available); and consider 20% lipid emulsion early, as a bolus of about 1.5 mL/kg over 2–3 minutes then about 0.25 mL/kg/min if under 70 kg, or about 100 mL bolus then about 250 mL over 15–20 minutes if over 70 kg. Repeat the bolus and double the infusion if the patient remains unstable, up to a maximum of 12 mL/kg. LAST resuscitation differs from standard ACLS: use smaller epinephrine doses (start below 1 mcg/kg) and avoid vasopressin, calcium-channel blockers, beta-blockers and further local anesthetic. Observe for at least 2 hours after a seizure and 4–6 hours after cardiovascular instability.<sup>[[16]](#ref16)</sup>

## Airway Management

### Anticipated Difficult Airway

The ASA 2022 guideline supports an awake approach when difficult intubation is suspected and there is concern about difficult mask or supraglottic ventilation, aspiration, inability to tolerate brief apnea, or difficult emergency invasive airway access. Patient cooperation, physiology, expertise and available equipment affect the chosen pathway.<sup>[[3]](#ref3)</sup>

Before robotic docking or restrictive positioning, confirm how the anesthesia team can reach the airway and how the robot and drapes will be released in an emergency. A previously easy intubation does not eliminate risk from subsequent edema, tube movement or impaired access.

### Unanticipated Difficult Intubation — DAS

The DAS 2025 update keeps the linear algorithm: Plan A tracheal intubation, Plan B supraglottic airway ventilation, Plan C facemask ventilation, Plan D emergency front-of-neck access. It prioritizes continuous oxygen delivery, first-attempt success (video laryngoscopy first where available), waveform capnography to confirm ventilation, and moving on when a plan fails.<sup>[[4]](#ref4)</sup> The earlier 2015 guideline capped laryngoscopy at three attempts plus one by a more experienced operator and supraglottic insertion at three attempts. Treat this as a ceiling, not a requirement to persist through hypoxemia or trauma.<sup>[[17]](#ref17)</sup>

After failed intubation, a second-generation supraglottic airway is the rescue route. If oxygenation is restored, pause and decide whether waking the patient is safest. Failed supraglottic rescue leads to a final optimized mask-ventilation attempt; cannot-intubate/cannot-oxygenate requires emergency front-of-neck access (scalpel-bougie-tube technique, practiced beforehand rather than improvised). Prepare for escalation early.<sup>[[4]](#ref4)[[17]](#ref17)</sup>

### Extubation Is Part of the Airway Plan

Assess readiness, airway swelling, ventilation, neuromuscular recovery and likely difficulty of reintubation. Choose the location, timing and skilled assistance accordingly. Awake versus deep extubation is an individualized anesthesia decision; selected difficult airways may need a staged approach or continued ventilation. Document difficult airway events and inform the patient.<sup>[[3]](#ref3)</sup>

## Neuromuscular Blockade and Reversal

Paralysis provides neither unconsciousness nor analgesia. Depth should follow operative need and a feasible recovery plan, rather than a routine request for maximal blockade. The ASA 2023 recommendations emphasize quantitative monitoring at the adductor pollicis and a train-of-four ratio of at least 0.9 before extubation. Clinical strength tests or visual or tactile twitch assessment alone cannot reliably establish recovery.<sup>[[5]](#ref5)</sup>

For rocuronium or vecuronium, sugammadex is preferred over neostigmine at deep, moderate or shallow block. Neostigmine is a reasonable alternative at minimal block, quantitatively defined as a ratio of 0.4 to below 0.9. A patient who has spontaneously recovered to at least 0.9 does not need pharmacological antagonism. These recommendations do not make sugammadex a reversal agent for every neuromuscular blocker.<sup>[[5]](#ref5)</sup>

Persistent weakness, upper-airway obstruction or hypoventilation in recovery warrants immediate assessment and airway support; consider residual blockade alongside opioids, sedation and other causes.

| Agent | Type | Duration and notes |
|---|---|---|
| **Succinylcholine** | Depolarizing | Ultra-short; rapid-sequence induction. Contraindicated with known or suspected malignant hyperthermia susceptibility, skeletal muscle myopathies, and after the acute phase of major burns, multiple trauma, extensive denervation or upper motor neuron injury (severe hyperkalemia)<sup>[[20]](#ref20)</sup> |
| **Rocuronium, vecuronium** | Non-depolarizing, intermediate | Typically 30–60 minutes; sugammadex-reversible |
| **Cisatracurium** | Non-depolarizing, benzylisoquinolinium | Hofmann elimination; useful in renal or hepatic failure; not reversed by sugammadex |

**Deep blockade for laparoscopic and robotic surgery.** Surgeons often ask for deep block to widen the working space. A 2024 Cochrane review of 42 trials (3,898 participants, mostly intraperitoneal oncologic resections) found deep versus moderate block likely made little or no difference to health-related quality of life or operating time, and the evidence for serious adverse events (38/1,150 versus 38/1,076 intraoperatively) and pain scores was very uncertain. Request deep block when exposure genuinely needs it, and plan reversal with sugammadex.<sup>[[13]](#ref13)</sup>

## Depth of Anesthesia and Awareness

Processed EEG can supplement clinical assessment and anesthetic-delivery monitoring. BIS studies commonly targeted 40–60, but the index is not a stand-alone guarantee of unconsciousness and is affected by drugs and artifacts. The 2019 Cochrane review (52 studies, 41,331 participants) found low-certainty evidence of less awareness compared with clinical-sign guidance (about 3 versus 9 per 1,000; only five of 27 studies reported events), but no evidence of a difference compared with end-tidal anesthetic-gas guidance (about 1 per 1,000 in both groups). It does not justify a routine instruction to lighten anesthesia to BIS 55–70 before surgery ends.<sup>[[6]](#ref6)</sup>

Delirium prevention is also not assured by EEG guidance. In ENGAGES, 1,232 older adults were randomized; among 1,213 assessed, delirium occurred in 157/604 (26.0%) with EEG guidance versus 140/609 (23.0%) with usual care, without a significant reduction. This trial does not negate other uses of EEG monitoring.<sup>[[10]](#ref10)</sup>

## Malignant Hyperthermia

MH is a pharmacogenetic hypermetabolic crisis of skeletal muscle. Reactions occur in about 1 in 10,000 to 1 in 250,000 anesthetics, while the genetic abnormality may be present in as many as 1 in 400 people. Mortality has fallen from about 80% to under 5% with dantrolene and protocolized care.<sup>[[14]](#ref14)</sup> Suspect MH with an unexplained hypermetabolic picture during or after exposure to triggering volatile anesthetics or succinylcholine; do not wait for marked fever. A rising end-tidal CO<sub>2</sub> despite increased minute ventilation is an early clue, followed by tachycardia, rigidity, hyperkalemia, acidosis and rhabdomyolysis; hyperthermia is often late. Succinylcholine-associated masseter rigidity warrants evaluation but is not by itself diagnostic of MH.<sup>[[7]](#ref7)[[8]](#ref8)</sup>

:::danger[Do not treat 10 mg/kg as a dantrolene ceiling]
Stop triggering agents, call for help and the MH cart, and give high-flow 100% oxygen. MHAUS recommends IV dantrolene 2.5 mg/kg rapidly, repeated according to the response. More than 10 mg/kg may be necessary for persistent rigidity or hypermetabolism; lack of response despite large doses should also prompt reconsideration of the diagnosis. The US Ryanodex label gives a minimum of 1 mg/kg by IV push with further boluses to a cumulative 10 mg/kg, repeating from 1 mg/kg if signs return, so follow the MH cart's product instructions.<sup>[[19]](#ref19)</sup>

Cool when core temperature exceeds 39°C or is rising rapidly, stopping active cooling below 38°C. Treat hyperkalemia, acidosis and arrhythmias; avoid calcium-channel blockers with dantrolene. Monitor temperature, ventilation, potassium, CK, renal function, urine and coagulation. After stabilization, observation in an appropriate PACU or ICU setting should continue for at least 24 hours.<sup>[[7]](#ref7)</sup>
:::

Use the institution's MH cognitive aid for the full crisis and post-crisis protocol. The surgical team's immediate roles include stopping nonessential surgery, obtaining supplies and helping free airway access.

## Perioperative Anaphylaxis

Unexpected severe hypotension or bronchospasm may represent anaphylaxis without visible skin findings. Stop suspected triggers and nonessential surgery, call for help, support oxygenation and ventilation, and give epinephrine with rapid crystalloid resuscitation. In the monitored perioperative setting, trained anesthesia clinicians give an initial IV epinephrine bolus (50 micrograms in adults) with a 500–1,000 mL crystalloid bolus, then an infusion if the response is poor; this differs from the usual community IM pathway. Follow the dedicated perioperative algorithm.<sup>[[9]](#ref9)</sup>

Antihistamines and corticosteroids must not delay epinephrine or resuscitation. Suspected triggers include antibiotics, neuromuscular blockers, dyes, colloids, latex, and hidden exposures such as chlorhexidine-coated lines or catheters and lubricants; sugammadex can also cause anaphylaxis.<sup>[[9]](#ref9)[[18]](#ref18)</sup> After stabilization, obtain timed tryptase samples (once stable, 1–4 hours after onset, and at least 24 hours later), document all exposures and arrange specialist allergy investigation rather than permanently labeling every administered drug as the culprit.<sup>[[9]](#ref9)</sup>

## Regional Versus General — What the Evidence Shows

| Question | Finding |
|---|---|
| Volatile versus propofol TIVA, cardiovascular events | No apparent difference; either is reasonable (2024 AHA/ACC, Class 2a)<sup>[[2]](#ref2)</sup> |
| Neuraxial versus general, cardiovascular events | No apparent difference when neuraxial is feasible (2024 AHA/ACC, Class 2a)<sup>[[2]](#ref2)</sup> |
| Postoperative delirium, regional versus general, hip fracture (RAGA, 950 patients) | delirium 6.2% (29 patients) with regional versus 5.1% (24) with general anesthesia among 941 evaluable; no significant difference<sup>[[15]](#ref15)</sup> |
| Mortality and pneumonia, neuraxial versus general | Lower with neuraxial in small, mostly older trials; see above<sup>[[11]](#ref11)</sup> |

Choose regional or general anesthesia for procedure-specific advantages (analgesic continuation, avoiding airway instrumentation, anticoagulation timing, positioning) rather than expecting a major cardiovascular-outcome difference.

## The Surgeon's Intraoperative Communication Checklist

- **Before incision:** expected duration, position, docking and emergency access, blood loss, bowel work, local anesthetic or block plan and postoperative destination.
- **During surgery:** communicate changing stimulation, bleeding and operative difficulty; request changes in blockade or hemodynamic support with their clinical purpose.
- **Before closure:** agree on extubation readiness, quantitative neuromuscular recovery, temperature, PONV prevention and analgesic transition.
- **At handover:** report airway difficulty, reactions, blood loss, blocks and all local-anesthetic doses. Account for baseline opioid treatment and tolerance rather than imposing “rescue opioids only” on every patient.

## Key Points

| Topic | Point |
|---|---|
| Airway | Video laryngoscopy first where available; declare failure early; know the front-of-neck pathway |
| Neuromuscular block | Quantitative monitoring; train-of-four ratio at least 0.9 before extubation; sugammadex for rocuronium or vecuronium at deep to shallow block |
| Malignant hyperthermia | Stop triggers; dantrolene 2.5 mg/kg (MHAUS), more than 10 mg/kg may be needed |
| Local anesthetic | One running total across all injections; LAST needs early lipid emulsion and reduced-dose epinephrine |
| Neuraxial | Insertion and catheter-removal timing follow current ASRA guidance |
| Anaphylaxis | Epinephrine and fluid first; timed tryptase |
