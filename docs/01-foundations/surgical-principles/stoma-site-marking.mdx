# Stoma Site Marking

Preoperative marking and education are recommended whenever a stoma may be needed. The joint WOCN Society/AUA/ASCRS position statement includes urinary diversions and emphasizes individualized assessment by a trained clinician. An appropriate skin site must also be compatible with bowel perfusion, mesenteric reach and the planned reconstruction.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup>

See also: [Ileal Conduit](/docs/surgical-techniques/04c-urinary-diversion/ileal-conduit), [Cutaneous Ureterostomy](/docs/surgical-techniques/04c-urinary-diversion/cutaneous-ureterostomy), [Principles of Urinary Diversion](/docs/surgical-techniques/04c-urinary-diversion/urinary-diversion-principles), [Continent Catheterizable Channels](/docs/surgical-techniques/04b-bladder-reconstruction/catheterizable-channels), and [Incisions & Closure](/docs/foundations/surgical-principles/incisions-closure).

## What the Evidence Supports

Marking is associated with fewer complications and better self-care. A 2021 systematic review included 20 studies, with 19 contributing to quantitative synthesis: pooled odds ratios were 0.47 (95% CI 0.36–0.62) for complications and 0.34 (0.18–0.64) for self-care deficits. The complication analysis had substantial heterogeneity (I² 70.6%). The pooled data combine ostomy types.<sup>[[2]](#ref2)</sup> A separate meta-analysis reported an inconclusive urostomy subgroup estimate for stoma and peristomal complications (OR 0.53, 95% CI 0.23–1.21; p = 0.132); it neither establishes a urinary-specific reduction nor rules one out.<sup>[[6]](#ref6)</sup>

The ASCRS 2022 ostomy guideline recommends that potential stoma sites be marked preoperatively by a trained provider when possible (statement 2; strong recommendation, moderate-quality evidence, 1B, upgraded from 1C in 2015). It states that urostomies are beyond its scope, so it is fecal-stoma guidance. The joint statement is the multidisciplinary guidance that includes urinary stomas.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[3]](#ref3)</sup>

The ASCRS guideline also cites a systematic review of 10 studies (2109 patients) in which marking was associated with fewer stoma and peristomal complications (OR 0.52, 95% CI 0.42–0.64) and fewer hernias (OR 0.25, 95% CI 0.09–0.71).<sup>[[3]](#ref3)</sup>

A historical 137-patient ileal-conduit cohort found stoma complications in 20 patients (14.6%), predominantly parastomal hernia. Higher BMI was associated with complications. This is a single-cohort estimate from 2001–2005 and does not show that marking prevents every event.<sup>[[4]](#ref4)</sup>

## Who and When

Arrange assessment by a certified ostomy nurse and the operating team during preoperative planning. Other appropriately trained clinicians can mark a site when specialist nursing is unavailable. In the study ASCRS cites, sites chosen by surgeons were a median of 2 cm from those chosen by ostomy nurses, most badly sited stomas were too low, and surgeon seniority made no difference; colorectal surgeons were more concordant with the nurses than general surgeons were.<sup>[[3]](#ref3)</sup> There is no requirement to wait until the day before surgery. The mark is preserved with indelible ink or a waterproof cover, and backup sites are documented when the intraoperative choice is uncertain.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup>

Education and marking should occur together when feasible. ASCRS recommends preoperative and postoperative education by a specialized provider such as a wound, ostomy and continence nurse (statement 1; strong recommendation, moderate-quality evidence, 1B) and follow-up for teaching, care and support (statement 3; strong recommendation, low-quality evidence, 1C). The guideline cites a 42-patient randomized trial of fecal-stoma education with a shorter hospital stay (8 versus 10 days, p = 0.02) and faster stoma-care proficiency (5.5 versus 9 days, p &lt; 0.001).<sup>[[3]](#ref3)</sup> A 2025 meta-analysis of colorectal-stoma education found no significant pooled reduction in length of stay; its certainty was very low and it did not evaluate urinary diversion.<sup>[[7]](#ref7)</sup>

## Assessment Checklist

- Examine standing, sitting and supine, adapted to the patient's mobility. Identify folds that appear only on sitting or bending.
- Assess visibility and reach, hand function, vision, clothing and beltline, occupation, braces and habitual activities.
- Identify the rectus muscle and a flat pouching surface; avoid creases, scars, bony prominences and proximity to the umbilicus. A flat area of about 5 cm of smooth peristomal skin is the usual teaching for secure pouch adhesion. Test the intended appliance against the actual abdominal contour.
- Confirm the planned incontinent pouching system versus continent catheterizable channel. Their access and construction requirements differ.
- Mark the preferred site and, when needed, clearly ranked alternatives. Discuss these with the operating surgeon.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup>

A right-sided site is common for an ileal conduit, but a fixed ASIS-to-umbilicus fraction should not replace this assessment. Rectus placement is a recommended siting principle; it is not a guarantee against parastomal hernia.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[3]](#ref3)</sup>

## Adaptation to the Patient

| Situation | Practical adjustment |
|---|---|
| **Protuberant abdomen or substantial pannus** | An upper abdominal site may improve visibility and shorten the passage through a thick abdominal wall; verify conduit reach. |
| **Wheelchair use, contractures or limited reach** | Assess in the patient's usual seated position and equipment; demonstrate access with the patient or their chosen caregiver. |
| **Prior surgery or existing stomas** | Consider scars, hernias, pouch footprints and the proposed incision together; a contralateral or higher site may be preferable. |
| **Two separate stomas** | Plan enough room for both appliances and consider different horizontal levels if belts will be used. |

The joint statement presents these as individualized planning considerations, not fixed geometric rules.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup>

## Connect Marking With Construction and Follow-up

For an incontinent ileal conduit, adequate protrusion, perfusion and a tension-free passage help appliance fitting. A flush or retracted conduit warrants assessment for leakage and skin injury; it does not automatically mean the diversion has failed. A continent catheterizable stoma may intentionally sit at skin level, with reliable catheter access as a central objective.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[5]](#ref5)</sup> For fecal stomas, ASCRS recommends protrusion above the skin when possible (statement 6; strong recommendation, low-quality evidence, 1C), usually at least 2 cm for an ileostomy and 1 cm for a colostomy. It lists selective mesenteric vessel ligation, end-loop stomas and an upper abdominal site in obese patients as ways to gain length, and it excludes urostomies.<sup>[[3]](#ref3)</sup>

Taneja and Godoy described preparing the ileal-conduit stoma before abdominal-wall transposition in 45 consecutive cases. This uncontrolled series describes a technical option and has no comparison with conventional maturation.<sup>[[5]](#ref5)</sup>

Before discharge, confirm appliance or catheter skills, access to supplies and a contact for problems. Continue ostomy nursing follow-up for leakage, skin changes, changing body contour and difficulties with independent care.<sup>[[3]](#ref3)</sup>
