# Flaps in Genitourinary Reconstruction

A flap is a unit of tissue transferred with its **native blood supply preserved**, distinguishing it from a graft which is transferred without one. Flaps are selected when the recipient site is inadequately vascularized for graft take, when bulk or structural support is required, or when the defect is too large or complex for simpler reconstruction.

This page is the **index to the flap library** — overview of classification, the geometric plasty techniques, the flap-by-flap navigation table, and the by-indication selection principles. **Each flap has its own dedicated page** with full anatomy, harvest technique, outcomes, and limitations; click the flap names in the table to go there.

---

## Flap Classification Overview

Every classification axis below descends directly from the layered, tiered-perfusion anatomy of the skin — see the [**Skin** anatomy page](/docs/foundations/anatomy-physiology/skin) for the layers, vascular plexuses, and biomechanics that the terms here refer to. Flaps are classified along several intersecting axes:

| Axis | Categories |
|---|---|
| **Blood supply** | Random (subdermal plexus, no named vessel) · Axial (named artery along flap axis) · Perforator (named perforating vessel through muscle/fascia) |
| **Tissue composition** | Cutaneous · Fasciocutaneous · Myocutaneous · Adipofascial · Omental |
| **Movement** | Advancement · Rotation · Transposition · Interpolation |
| **Transfer method** | Pedicled (continuous vascular connection) · Free (microvascular anastomosis at recipient site) |

By vascular pattern, **Nakajima's classification** splits cutaneous flaps into five types — **cutaneous, fasciocutaneous, adipofascial, septocutaneous, and musculocutaneous** — with the fasciocutaneous group further divided into six subtypes by their input to the fascial plexus.<sup>[[11]](#ref11)</sup> In GU reconstruction, **fasciocutaneous** and **myocutaneous** flaps predominate. Most pedicled flaps used in perineal and pelvic reconstruction are **axial**, based on named vessels with reliable anatomy.

## Vascular Basis of Flap Design — Angiosome and Perforasome

Two anatomic models predict how far a flap can reach beyond its named vessel before it outruns its blood supply.

**The angiosome concept** (Taylor & Palmer, 1987) describes three-dimensional vascular territories supplied by source arteries and their accompanying veins.<sup>[[12]](#ref12)</sup> In experimental and selected clinical observations, a perforator's clinical territory could extend into that of an adjacent perforator.<sup>[[12]](#ref12)[[13]](#ref13)</sup> This is a planning model, **not a universal guarantee** that one adjacent territory is safe or that a second is impossible. Actual perfusion, venous drainage, prior surgery and the chosen flap still need assessment.

**The perforasome theory** (Saint-Cyr, 2009) refined this to the territory of an *individual perforator* and added the rules that govern flap axis:<sup>[[14]](#ref14)</sup>

- Each perforasome links to its neighbors through **direct linking vessels** (true parallel anastomoses) and **indirect linking vessels** (connections through the subdermal plexus).
- The **vascular axis of a flap follows the axiality of the linking vessels** — orient the flap along them.
- Perforators **near a joint direct flow away from the joint**; mid-trunk perforators have **multidirectional** flow.
- Both the **subdermal plexus and the deep fascia** contribute to flap perfusion.

**Clinical translation:** main source arteries may be predictable, but individual perforators vary in size and position. Handheld Doppler or selected vascular imaging can assist planning, with intraoperative reassessment and an alternative design if the expected perforator is unsuitable.<sup>[[15]](#ref15)</sup> These principles underwrite the propeller and perforator flaps used in perineal and genital reconstruction ([PMTP](/docs/foundations/surgical-principles/flaps/pmtp-propeller), [SCIP](/docs/foundations/surgical-principles/flaps/scip), [IGAP / IPAP](/docs/foundations/surgical-principles/flaps/igap-gluteal-fold), [EPAP](/docs/foundations/surgical-principles/flaps/epap)).

## Random Pattern Flap Design Principles

Some **local genital and perineal flaps** in this library — including certain rhomboid, bilobed, rotation and advancement designs — are *random-pattern* flaps supplied through a broad tissue base and the subdermal network. Do not place every penile/preputial or scrotal flap in this category: their vascularized fascial pedicles or named perforators may make them axial or perforator-based designs. Perfusion can decline toward a random flap's tip, but neither a linear fall nor a single dominant necrosis mechanism applies to every design.<sup>[[16]](#ref16)[[17]](#ref17)</sup> The following historical and experimental rules are planning aids, not site-independent safety thresholds.

### Length-to-width ratio

Traditional length-to-width teaching varies by location and flap design. Stell's **pig random-flap experiments** suggested limiting length to about base width and roughly 12.5 cm; these are not universal human or genital-flap limits.<sup>[[17]](#ref17)</sup> Human laser-speckle observations found a relation between ratio and perfusion, while another pig experiment found absolute length and thickness more predictive than the ratio alone. Assess the actual vascular base and tissue rather than applying a fixed 1:1, 2:1 or 3:1 prescription.<sup>[[18]](#ref18)[[31]](#ref31)</sup>

### Geometry — the arbor principle

A large area can survive on a **narrow base with a short pedicle**, but not on a **long narrow pedicle**: blood flows radially from the base in a tree-foliage ("arbor") pattern. Small random flaps should therefore be **broadened immediately adjacent to a short, narrow base** rather than drawn as long rectangles.<sup>[[19]](#ref19)</sup>

### Orientation to relaxed skin tension lines

Place closure lines **parallel to RSTLs** wherever possible — parallel wounds need less force to close, retract less, and benefit more from undermining.<sup>[[20]](#ref20)</sup> Flap advancement **perpendicular** to RSTLs minimizes stress within the flap; advancement parallel to RSTLs raises stress at the distal flap. Incisions along cosmetic-unit junctions give the best scar quality. (See the [Skin biomechanics](/docs/foundations/anatomy-physiology/skin#biomechanics) section.)

### Undermining — technique and extent

Undermining lowers closure tension up to a point of diminishing returns:

- **Undermining out to ~3× the defect diameter** captures most of the available tension reduction; beyond that adds little.<sup>[[21]](#ref21)</sup>
- On the scalp, **5 cm of subgaleal undermining** achieves an ~83% reduction in closing tension; extending to 15 cm reaches ~92% — statistically significant but a clinically modest further gain.<sup>[[22]](#ref22)</sup>
- Undermine in the **subcutaneous plane** (above the deep fascia for most sites) to keep the subdermal plexus with the flap.

### Tension and blood flow

There is an **inverse relationship between closing tension and flap blood flow**. The frequently quoted **250 g** necrosis signal came from 27 *piglet* random flaps with impaired blood supply; it is not a clinical cutoff for all human flaps.<sup>[[23]](#ref23)</sup> Design generously and offload tension as anatomy permits. Published A-to-T advancement geometry and undermining experiments are design examples, not mandatory dimensions for genital/perineal closure.<sup>[[21]](#ref21)</sup>

### Preserve the subdermal plexus

The subdermal plexus is the lifeline of a random flap:

- Avoid unplanned thinning that divides the flap's vascular network. A **5×10 cm experimental random-flap** study reported lower survival after thinning; its percentages do not set a rule for all perforator or deliberately thinned flaps.<sup>[[24]](#ref24)</sup>
- When **de-epithelializing** a buried flap, preserve the vascular dermis where the design requires it. The cited perfusion experiment concerns **DIEP flaps**, not a universal 25.9% perfusion loss for every random genital flap.<sup>[[25]](#ref25)</sup>
- Keep the flap **full-thickness** and avoid cautery on its undersurface.

### The delay phenomenon

In selected designs, a **surgical delay** — staged partial incision or elevation before transfer — can improve perfusion and survival.<sup>[[26]](#ref26)[[27]](#ref27)</sup> A **38-rat random-flap model** found increased distal VEGF at day 3 and perfusion after day 5, with less average necrosis after a five-day interval; it did not establish a universal human or GU-flap interval.<sup>[[27]](#ref27)[[28]](#ref28)</sup> Delay cannot rescue every unfavorable pedicle or substitute for viability assessment. Microneedling preconditioning remains experimental.<sup>[[29]](#ref29)</sup>

### Pharmacologic adjuncts

In a review of **rat dorsal random-flap experiments**, several agents, including pentoxifylline, sildenafil and adrenergic antagonists, were studied for distal necrosis.<sup>[[30]](#ref30)</sup> These results do not establish a human GU-flap drug protocol. Flap design, vascular assessment and defect-specific planning remain central.

### Summary

| Principle | Best practice | Rationale |
|---|---|---|
| Length-to-width | Consider site-specific dimensions; no universal ratio | Human and animal studies differ; neither sets a fixed genital limit<sup>[[17]](#ref17)[[18]](#ref18)[[31]](#ref31)</sup> |
| Shape | Broaden adjacent to a short, narrow base (arbor) | Radial flow from base; long narrow pedicles fail<sup>[[19]](#ref19)</sup> |
| Orientation | Align closure with RSTLs / cosmetic-unit junctions | Less tension and retraction; better scars<sup>[[20]](#ref20)</sup> |
| Undermining | Enough to reduce closure tension while preserving the vascular plane | Experimental/scalp dimensions are not universal<sup>[[21]](#ref21)[[22]](#ref22)</sup> |
| Tension | Minimize and assess perfusion | The &gt;250 g finding was in piglets with impaired flow<sup>[[23]](#ref23)</sup> |
| Thickness | Preserve the vascular plane; thinning is design-dependent | Unplanned thinning harmed experimental random flaps<sup>[[24]](#ref24)</sup> |
| De-epithelialization | Preserve vascular dermis where required | Cited perfusion estimate comes from DIEP, not all random flaps<sup>[[25]](#ref25)</sup> |
| Delay | Consider selectively; interval depends on flap and patient | Five-day benefit was demonstrated in a rat model<sup>[[26]](#ref26)[[27]](#ref27)</sup> |

## Geometric Plasty Techniques

Several reconstructive maneuvers rearrange local tissue through defined geometric patterns rather than moving tissue from a distant donor. These are **part of the flap toolkit** and appear repeatedly in GU reconstructive practice:

| Technique | Principle | Primary GU role |
|---|---|---|
| **[Y-V plasty](/docs/foundations/surgical-principles/flaps/y-v-plasty)** | Y-shaped incision → V-shaped closure; advances triangular flap into contracted segment to **lengthen** perimeter | Refractory bladder neck contracture, VUAS after radical prostatectomy |
| **[V-Y advancement](/docs/foundations/surgical-principles/flaps/v-y-advancement)** | V-shaped incision → Y-shaped closure; advances triangular flap to **cover** a defect | Buried penis / penile lengthening (suprapubic V-Y); vulvar reconstruction (pubolabial, medial thigh, gluteal V-Y); scrotal and perineal coverage; perineoplasty for lichen-sclerosus introital stenosis; episiotomy-dehiscence repair |
| **[Z-plasty](/docs/foundations/surgical-principles/flaps/z-plasty)** | Two transposed triangular flaps; **reorients scar by up to 90°**, lengthens by 50–70%, and breaks linear contractures | Pediatric genital reconstruction (hypospadias / chordee / penoscrotal webbing / bifid scrotum); foreskin Z-plasty for phimosis; frenuloplasty; transverse vaginal septum (Garcia / Grünberger); iatrogenic vaginal constriction; posterior-fourchette dyspareunia (Plymouth Procedure) |
| **[Rhomboid (Limberg) flap](/docs/foundations/surgical-principles/flaps/rhomboid-limberg)** | Rhombic transposition flap (60° / 120°); 1:1 length-to-width ratio; random vascularization; flap chosen along the line of maximal extensibility | Small posterior / commissure vulvar defects (bilateral Limberg); epispadias-exstrophy penile elongation; scrotal SCC / EMPD reconstruction |
| **[Bilobed flap](/docs/foundations/surgical-principles/flaps/bilobed)** | Double-transposition flap with two sequential lobes around a shared pivot (Zitelli 90–100° arc); distributes tension across two donors | Vulvoperineal defects spanning urogenital + anal triangles (bilobed PAP flap); deep pelvic / perineal defects after APR / exenteration / Fournier's (bilobed [gracilis](/docs/foundations/surgical-principles/flaps/gracilis)); perineal-body reconstruction after fourth-degree obstetric injury |
| **[Propeller flap](/docs/foundations/surgical-principles/flaps/propeller-flap)** | Island flap rotated 90–180° around a single perforator (Tokyo Consensus); freestyle design | Aesthetic penoscrotal resurfacing (IPAP gluteal-fold propeller); vulvar reconstruction (IPAP, gull-wing, lotus petal, perineal-perforator switch); extensive perineal defects after Fournier's / ELAPE / exenteration ([PMTP](/docs/foundations/surgical-principles/flaps/pmtp-propeller), vPMT, DEPAP, SCIP, EPAP); perineal urethrostomy revision |
| **[Lotus petal flap](/docs/foundations/surgical-principles/flaps/lotus-petal)** | Gluteal-fold/internal-pudendal-territory fasciocutaneous flap; donor scar may be concealed | Selected vulvar, pelvic-floor and posterior-perineal reconstruction; options for neovaginal or scrotal coverage depend on defect and pedicle |

**Key insight:** Y-V plasty is a local tissue rearrangement option for selected refractory bladder-neck or anastomotic stenosis. It should not be presented as the operation of choice for every such patient.

## Flap Selection Principles by Indication

### Urethral strictures (anterior urethra)

- **Vascularization matters** — assess donor skin, dartos pedicle and recipient bed for the selected penile-flap design.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup>
- **Onlay versus tube:** the McAninch–Morey observational cohort reported **7/54 onlay (13%) versus 7/12 tubularized (58%) recurrences**. Different defect severity and selection prevent declaring every onlay superior.<sup>[[2]](#ref2)</sup>
- **Failed urethroplasty or obliteration:** consider vascularized tissue when the bed cannot support a graft; prior surgery alone does not make all grafts unsuitable.
- **Sexual function:** assess each patient's baseline and follow-up rather than exporting a series mean IIEF-5 score to all flaps.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup>

### Bladder neck contracture / VUAS

- **Recurrent or complex stenosis:** consider reconstructive options, including [Y-V plasty](/docs/foundations/surgical-principles/flaps/y-v-plasty), according to anatomy, continence and previous treatment; there is no universal two-failure threshold.
- **Robotic Y-V:** small selected series report feasibility and success, not proven equivalence with open repair.<sup>[[3]](#ref3)</sup>
- **ICG fluorescence:** may assist selected intraoperative localization; not required for every scarred field.
- **Transurethral mucosal realignment:** Abramowitz described this as an endoscopic technique, **not a Y-V flap**; its reported first-pass rate is series-specific.<sup>[[4]](#ref4)</sup>

### Vesicovaginal and urethrovaginal fistula

- **Selected simple VVF:** direct repair may suffice without mandatory interposition.
- **Compromised or recurrent VVF:** consider [Martius](/docs/foundations/surgical-principles/flaps/martius) or [omental](/docs/foundations/surgical-principles/flaps/omental) interposition according to route, tissue quality and defect; neither is mandatory for every case.
- **VVF with vaginal wall deficit:** [labia majora fasciocutaneous flap](/docs/foundations/surgical-principles/flaps/labia-majora-fasciocutaneous) — the flap provides both fistula interposition *and* vaginal wall tissue<sup>[[5]](#ref5)</sup>
- **Recurrent VVF after prior interposition:** reassess defect, tissue viability, route and need for another vascularized option; failure of Martius does not mandate a fixed flap sequence.
- **Single-layer annular vaginal flap:** Tang's selected series described this transvaginal technique and reported **82.3% first-time success**; comparative resource or catheter benefits are not established for every patient.<sup>[[6]](#ref6)</sup>

### Perineal / genital coverage (Fournier's, extensive defects)

- **Active Fournier's gangrene first needs debridement and source control.** Protect exposed structures during wound care, then select closure, graft or flap once the wound and patient permit definitive reconstruction.
- **Flap outcomes:** Alammar's 2026 review included **619 selected patients and 625 flaps**; it reported **1.6% total flap loss** in the published flap cases, not a comparative failure rate versus grafts or evidence for immediate coverage during active infection.<sup>[[7]](#ref7)</sup>
- **Regional choices** include medial thigh, pudendal thigh, ALT and gracilis in that review. Choice depends on defect size/depth, exposure, local tissue and surgical expertise rather than a universal rank.<sup>[[7]](#ref7)</sup>
- **Perforator propeller options** ([PMTP](/docs/foundations/surgical-principles/flaps/pmtp-propeller), DEPAP, SCIP, EPAP) can cover selected defects without microvascular anastomosis; their reach and venous risks vary.
- **Recorded reasons for flap use** in that selected review included exposed structures (52%), function (39%) and cosmesis (4%); these are reported indications, not a treatment algorithm.<sup>[[7]](#ref7)</sup>

### Penile shaft coverage

- **Modified bilateral scrotal flap** ("butterfly" design) — restores penile skin length from bilateral scrotal donors<sup>[[8]](#ref8)</sup>
- **External Pudendal Artery Perforator (EPAP) hemi-scrotal flap** — sensate, thin, pigmented coverage of circumferential penile shaft defects with wider rotation arc than conventional scrotal flaps<sup>[[9]](#ref9)</sup>
- **[SCIP flap](/docs/foundations/surgical-principles/flaps/scip)** — thin free flap alternative with inguinal donor concealment

### Vulvovaginal reconstruction (oncologic)

- **Small defects:** rhomboid (Limberg) flap or V-Y advancement
- **Moderate defects:** lotus petal or superficial external pudendal artery flaps based on distinct vascular territories
- **Large defects / full vaginal reconstruction:** bilateral [Singapore / pudendal thigh flaps](/docs/foundations/surgical-principles/flaps/singapore-pudendal-thigh) or [VRAM](/docs/foundations/surgical-principles/flaps/vram) or [gracilis myocutaneous](/docs/foundations/surgical-principles/flaps/gracilis)
- **Compound perineal + vulvar defects:** bilobed pudendal artery perforator flap preserves distinct tissue character of urogenital and anal triangles<sup>[[10]](#ref10)</sup>
