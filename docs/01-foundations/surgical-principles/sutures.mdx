# Sutures

**Sutures** are the materials used to approximate and support tissue in urologic reconstruction. Suture selection is rarely taught systematically and is mostly learned by imitation. A substitution urethroplasty, vesicourethral anastomosis, ureteral reimplant or augmentation cystoplasty involves 6–10 separate suture-and-needle decisions, and defaults vary between training lineages. This article summarizes the properties of common materials, the evidence for specific choices and typical selections by procedure.

See also: [Barbed Sutures](/docs/foundations/surgical-principles/barbed-sutures), [Incisions & Closure](/docs/foundations/surgical-principles/incisions-closure), [Bowel Anastomosis](/docs/foundations/surgical-principles/bowel-anastomosis), [Needles](/docs/foundations/surgical-principles/needles), [Wound Healing](/docs/foundations/surgical-principles/wound-healing).

---

## The Five Properties of the Ideal Suture

Surgical sutures are rated on five axes:<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[2]](#ref2)</sup>

1. **Predictable tensile strength** over the wound-healing window.
2. **Good handling characteristics.** Pliability, memory, feel at the needle.
3. **Secure knot** with a predictable number of throws.
4. **Minimal tissue reactivity.**
5. **Resistance to infection and bacterial colonization.**

No suture achieves all five. The selection decision trades them off against the biology of the tissue being repaired and the time the repair has to heal.

---

## Classification

Sutures are classified on three axes:

- **Absorbability:** absorbable (loses strength and is degraded) versus non-absorbable (not designed to be absorbed; long-term strength still varies by material).
- **Structure:** monofilament (single strand) versus multifilament / braided (many strands).
- **Origin:** natural (silk, catgut) versus synthetic (polymers).

The main practical divide is monofilament versus multifilament, which affects tissue drag, knot behavior and bacterial adherence.

## Absorbable Sutures

### Fast Absorbable (Loses Strength Within 30 Days)

| Suture | Structure | Trade name | Strength retention | Urologic use |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Polyglactin 910** | Braided (monofilament in sizes 9-0 and 10-0) | Vicryl | 25% at 4 wk for sizes 6-0 and larger; absorbed in 56–70 days<sup>[[41]](#ref41)</sup> | Bladder mucosa, ureteral reimplant, vaginal cuff |
| **Polyglycolic acid (PGA)** | Braided multifilament in the classic Dexon product | Dexon | Product-dependent; broadly short-absorbable | Largely displaced by newer absorbables |
| **Poliglecaprone 25** | Monofilament | Monocryl | Undyed: 50–60% at 1 wk, 20–30% at 2 wk; dyed: 60–70% at 1 wk, 30–40% at 2 wk (manufacturer data)<sup>[[42]](#ref42)</sup> | Subcuticular skin, vesicourethral anastomosis, bladder neck reconstruction |
| **Chromic catgut** | Natural monofilament | Chromic | Enzymatic degradation, variable | Largely historical; some pediatric skin |

### Slow Absorbable (Strength Retained &gt;30 Days)

| Suture | Structure | Trade name | Strength retention | Urologic use |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Polydioxanone** | Monofilament | PDS II | Long strength retention; absorbed approximately 182–238 days (product-specific) | Urethroplasty, fascial closure (STITCH), selected long-healing anastomoses |
| **Polyglyconate** | Monofilament | Maxon | Long-absorbable; check product-specific profile | Alternative slow-absorbable material |

PDS II is a commonly selected slow-absorbable monofilament for fascial closure and repairs needing support over weeks; the cited in-vitro comparison is specific to its tested materials and conditions.<sup>[[4]](#ref4)[[35]](#ref35)</sup> Vicryl Plus is not a slow-absorbable formulation: it is triclosan-coated polyglactin 910 with the manufacturer's listed absorption of 56–70 days, like the corresponding standard coated Vicryl product.<sup>[[36]](#ref36)</sup>

### Degradation Mechanism

- **Natural sutures** (catgut, silk) degrade by enzymatic proteolysis; degradation is highly variable and tissue-reactive.
- **Synthetic absorbables** (Vicryl, Monocryl, PDS) degrade by hydrolysis, which is predictable and weakly reactive. Natural and synthetic absorbables remain suture options with different degradation mechanisms.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[2]](#ref2)</sup>

## Non-Absorbable Sutures

| Material | Structure | Trade name | Strength | Urologic use |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Polypropylene** | Monofilament | Prolene | Non-absorbable; long-lasting | Sling suspensory arms, selected mesh fixation and permanent support outside urinary lumen |
| **Nylon** | Mono- or multifilament | Ethilon, Nurolon | Long-term | Skin closure, tendon |
| **Silk** | Multifilament, natural | Silk | Loses strength over months in vivo | Historical; highest tissue reactivity and microbial adherence<sup>[[5]](#ref5)[[6]](#ref6)</sup> |
| **Polyester** | Multifilament, coated | Ethibond, Ti-Cron | Durable non-absorbable support | Selected permanent fascia/sling fixation outside the urinary lumen; not a routine AUS cuff suture |
| **PTFE / Gore-Tex** | Monofilament | CV-series | Most stable tensile strength in one 14-day artificial-saliva comparison of eight 3-0 materials<sup>[[4]](#ref4)</sup> | Vascular / IVC reconstruction |
| **Stainless steel** | Monofilament | — | Maximum strength | Sternal closure (not urologic) |

## Monofilament vs Multifilament — the Decision That Matters Most

| Property | Monofilament | Multifilament |
|---|---|---|
| Tissue drag | Low | High |
| Tensile strength for a given size | Varies by polymer and test condition<sup>[[4]](#ref4)</sup> | Varies by polymer and test condition |
| Knot security | Lower (more throws required) | Higher |
| Handling / pliability | Stiffer; more memory | Better; more pliable |
| Bacterial adherence | Often lower in comparative models | Often higher in comparative models (filament interstices can retain bacteria) |
| Infection risk | Depends on material, site and procedure | Depends on material, site and procedure |
| Tissue reactivity | Low | Higher |
| Suture sinus risk | Low | Higher |

For the urologist: monofilament is often preferred where bacterial wicking, permanent exposure or prolonged tissue support is a concern, but the choice is not an absolute rule for every contaminated field or implant case. Device instructions, tissue, exposure and local infection-control practice govern the particular repair. Multifilament Vicryl remains a useful option for short-healing mucosal approximation where handling matters (for example, bladder mucosa or vaginal cuff).

## Size — USP Gauge

USP size designations include 11-0 and 10-0 for microsurgery, then 6-0, 4-0, 0, 1, 2 and 5. More zeros indicate a smaller diameter: 6-0 is finer than 4-0. Available sizes depend on the material and product.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[53]](#ref53)</sup>

**Principle: use the smallest size that accomplishes the purpose.**<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup>

In a bench skin-suturing model, larger diameter (3-0 versus 5-0 nylon) produced higher tear-through force, which is relevant in friable tissue or tissue under tension.<sup>[[10]](#ref10)</sup> In one oral-surgery bench test, suture size did not change the knot-slippage endpoint; laparoscopic tests found larger monofilaments less predictable with some knot sequences.<sup>[[7]](#ref7)[[8]](#ref8)</sup>

### Typical Sizes by Urologic Task

| Task | Suture | Size |
|---|---|---|
| Urethroplasty — spongiosum / ventral onlay | Vicryl for spongiosum; Vicryl or Monocryl for ventral onlay | 4-0 (spongiosum); 5-0 or 6-0 (ventral onlay) |
| Urethroplasty — graft fixation | PDS or Monocryl | 6-0 |
| Vesicourethral anastomosis (RARP) | Monocryl or V-Loc | 3-0 |
| Ureteral reimplant mucosa-to-mucosa | Vicryl or Monocryl | 4-0 or 5-0 |
| Ureteroureterostomy | Monocryl or PDS | 4-0 or 5-0 |
| Bladder mucosa | Vicryl | 3-0 |
| Bladder detrusor / seromuscular | Vicryl | 2-0 or 3-0 |
| Augmentation cystoplasty — bowel to bladder | Vicryl or PDS | 3-0 |
| Pyeloplasty (open or robotic) | Monocryl or Vicryl | 4-0 or 5-0 |
| AUS cuff capsular closure / IPP corporotomy | PDS or Vicryl | 2-0 |
| Sling arm fixation / mesh anchor | Prolene | 0 or 1 |
| Fascia — midline laparotomy | PDS (monofilament slow-absorb) | 2-0 (STITCH small-bites) |
| Subcuticular skin | Monocryl | 4-0 |

## Knot Security — Material and Throws

Number of throws required for a secure knot depends on the specific material, knot sequence, loading and test conditions.<sup>[[7]](#ref7)[[8]](#ref8)[[14]](#ref14)</sup> A 2016 oral-surgery bench study found that square and surgeon's knots generally needed four throws and sliding knots five in its tested materials; an additional throw after five did not improve that study's outcome.<sup>[[7]](#ref7)</sup> In contrast, a 2023 laparoscopic bench study found that tested monofilament half-knot/half-hitch sequences needed six throws with blocking hitches, whereas tested polyfilament sequences were secure with four or five.<sup>[[8]](#ref8)</sup> The number of initial twists and subsequent locking sequence also matter.<sup>[[15]](#ref15)</sup>

Monofilament can need more throws or different blocking hitches because of lower friction; a neat-looking knot is not necessarily secure. Verify the sequence appropriate to the suture and procedure rather than applying a single throw-count recipe.

For the practical maneuver that improves run-down and reduces glove perforation with monofilament and UHMWPE braids, see [Glove Wetting for Knot Tying](/docs/foundations/surgical-skills/glove-wetting). For why running sutures through bacitracin ointment or petroleum jelly is a tradition with measurable mechanical and safety costs, see [Suture Lubrication](/docs/foundations/surgical-skills/suture-lubrication).

## Triclosan-Coated (Antibacterial) Sutures

**Vicryl Plus** (polyglactin 910) and **PDS Plus** are triclosan-coated sutures intended to inhibit bacterial growth on the suture. Their effect on surgical site infection varies by operative setting and by which trials are included in the analysis.<sup>[[18]](#ref18)[[19]](#ref19)</sup>

**Evidence is conflicting:**

- A 2023 meta-analysis of 29 RCTs reported a 24% reduction in SSI (RR 0.76, 95% CI 0.67–0.87), with a significant effect only in the abdominal-surgery subgroup.<sup>[[16]](#ref16)</sup>
- A 2025 *JAMA Network Open* meta-analysis of 31 RCTs (17,968 participants) found fewer SSIs (RR 0.75, 95% CI 0.65–0.86; moderate-certainty evidence).<sup>[[18]](#ref18)</sup>
- WHO 2016 made a conditional recommendation for triclosan-coated sutures, and NICE recommends considering them, especially for paediatric surgery.<sup>[[17]](#ref17)[[46]](#ref46)</sup>
- A high-quality 2022 Lancet Infectious Diseases meta-analysis restricted to the best-conducted RCTs in clean-contaminated, contaminated, or dirty surgery (5 trials, 8,619 analyzed patients; clean procedures excluded) found no significant SSI difference (16.8% vs 18.4%) and concluded that guideline recommendations for routine use should be reconsidered.<sup>[[19]](#ref19)</sup>

**Practical stance.** A 2025 Surgical Infection Society guideline recommends triclosan-coated suture for abdominal incision closure to reduce SSI (Grade 1A), while the 2022 restricted high-quality-trial analysis and 2025 broader analysis reach different pooled conclusions.<sup>[[37]](#ref37)[[19]](#ref19)[[18]](#ref18)</sup> The comparative evidence does not by itself establish benefit for urinary anastomoses, mesh or device pockets or actively infected fistula tissue. Choose the product and layer according to its instructions, site-specific risk and cost rather than assuming a contaminated-field benefit across all urologic uses.

## Barbed Sutures — Knotless Running Closure

**Dedicated deep-dive article.** The full product biomechanics (V-Loc 90/180, Quill SRS, Stratafix, MONOFIX), per-indication evidence, the upper-tract pyeloplasty controversy (Liatsikos 5/6 stricture recurrences with Quill in early cases<sup>[[39]](#ref39)</sup>, Radford: 4/10 children remained asymptomatic despite worsening hydronephrosis and MAG-3 drainage after stent removal following V-Loc closure of the renal pelvis<sup>[[40]](#ref40)</sup>, Anand 2022 meta<sup>[[28]](#ref28)</sup>), the overtightening-to-avoid technique, SBO risk from exposed tails (Clapp 2020), and the where-to-use-and-where-to-avoid clinical decision framework live at [Barbed sutures](/docs/foundations/surgical-principles/barbed-sutures). The overview below is the summary; the dedicated article is the reference.

**Barbed sutures** are self-anchoring monofilament sutures with helically arranged barbs cut into the suture surface. The barbs grip tissue as the suture is passed, eliminating the need for knot tying and distributing tension along the closure instead of concentrating it at knots.<sup>[[11]](#ref11)[[20]](#ref20)[[21]](#ref21)</sup> The reconstructive value is practical: in deep pelvic, laparoscopic, and robotic fields, a running closure can be tensioned without an assistant constantly following the suture.

The main reconstructive uses are:

- **Robot-assisted vesicourethral anastomosis.** The classic GU application
- **Selected robotic pyeloplasty.** May shorten suturing, but upper-tract failure signals require material- and technique-specific caution
- **Renorrhaphy.** Holds compression during sliding-clip parenchymal closure
- **Cystotomy or bladder closure.** Especially in robotic cystectomy, partial cystectomy, or fistula repair
- **Sacrocolpopexy mesh attachment** and **vaginal cuff closure.** Knotless running pelvic closure. For mesh attachment, check the selected product and regional instructions: the cited V-Loc 90 and 180 technical sheets exclude synthetic-graft fixation.<sup>[[43]](#ref43)[[44]](#ref44)</sup>

### Types

| Type | Mechanism | Examples | Practical issue |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Unidirectional** | Barbs face one direction; terminal loop anchors first pass | V-Loc, Stratafix Spiral | Easy robotic workflow; extrusion/dehiscence signal is most prominent in body-wall and subcutaneous use |
| **Bidirectional** | Barbs face away from the midpoint; closure proceeds from center outward | Quill | More even tension distribution; no terminal loop |

### Mechanism and handling

| Principle | Effect |
|---|---|
| **Self-anchoring** | Barbs grip tissue on passage; no knot required |
| **No assistant needed to hold tension** | Useful in robotic VUA, pyeloplasty, renorrhaphy, and deep pelvic closure |
| **Slippage prevention** | Running closure maintains approximation without repeated retensioning<sup>[[22]](#ref22)</sup> |
| **Distributed tension** | May reduce focal knot bulk, but avoiding clinical ischemia has not been established for every closure<sup>[[21]](#ref21)</sup> |
| **Not easily backed out** | Tension must be set deliberately; over-tightening is harder to correct |

Biomechanical studies show that barbed closures may have lower ultimate tensile strength than layered conventional closure in skin models, but still exceed clinical wound tension requirements; tissue reaction is generally mild and comparable to conventional monofilament sutures.<sup>[[23]](#ref23)[[24]](#ref24)</sup> In urinary-tract reconstruction, the main question is less raw tensile strength and more whether a continuous mucosa-to-mucosa closure remains watertight without focal strangulation.

### Urologic and reconstructive evidence

| Application | Evidence signal | Reconstructive interpretation |
|---|---|---|
| **Vesicourethral anastomosis** | In Zorn's 70-patient RCT, anastomosis time was 13.1 vs 20.8 min, with less tension readjustment; no statistically significant pad-free continence difference was detected through 6 months (mean follow-up 6.2 months).<sup>[[22]](#ref22)</sup> A meta-analysis of 3 randomized and 6 observational studies (786 cases) found about 4 min shorter anastomosis time without a detected difference in leak or continence.<sup>[[25]](#ref25)</sup> | A well-studied GU application for shortening suturing time. |
| **Pyeloplasty** | Ex vivo porcine testing found shorter suture time, and nonrandomized clinical comparisons reported shorter overall operative time.<sup>[[26]](#ref26)[[27]](#ref27)</sup> In an early adult series using Quill for the ureteropelvic anastomosis, 5/6 developed recurrent stricture.<sup>[[39]](#ref39)</sup> In ten children whose renal pelvis was closed with V-Loc, 4/10 had worsening hydronephrosis and MAG-3 drainage after stent removal while asymptomatic; three underwent redo pyeloplasty.<sup>[[40]](#ref40)</sup> A five-study 2022 meta-analysis estimated higher redo risk with barbed suture (RR 6.00, 95% CI 0.78–46.14; not statistically significant).<sup>[[28]](#ref28)</sup> | Not proven equivalent for redo risk; match product and tissue, and review the dedicated upper-tract caution before using. |
| **Partial nephrectomy renorrhaphy** | In a single-surgeon consecutive series (15 conventional, then 15 V-Loc repairs), mean warm ischemia time was 24.7 versus 18.5 minutes; no V-Loc slippage or tearing was reported.<sup>[[29]](#ref29)</sup> | Useful for compression and clip-sliding workflow; not a reconstructive anastomosis but a common urologic use. |
| **Gynecologic / urogynecologic closure** | Systematic reviews report shorter cuff-closure time and operative time, with no consistent increase in cuff dehiscence.<sup>[[12]](#ref12)[[30]](#ref30)</sup> | Barbed suture can be considered for vaginal-cuff closure. For sacrocolpopexy mesh attachment, check the chosen product's regional instructions: the cited V-Loc 90 and 180 technical sheets exclude synthetic-graft fixation.<sup>[[43]](#ref43)[[44]](#ref44)</sup> |
| **Fascial closure** | A meta-analysis of 7 studies (12,278 patients, mostly non-randomized) found barbed sutures were associated with lower SSI (OR 0.41, 95% CI 0.31–0.53) but did not detect a statistically significant difference in fascial complications.<sup>[[31]](#ref31)</sup> | Consider only a product whose instructions support the intended fascial use: Medtronic's US information says fascial-closure safety and effectiveness for V-Loc 90 and 180 have not been established.<sup>[[47]](#ref47)</sup> PDS small-bites remains the elective-midline evidence anchor. |

### Complications and precautions

The safety signal is context-dependent. A meta-analysis of 17 randomized trials found shorter suture and operative times with barbed sutures and more postoperative complications overall (OR 1.43, 95% CI 1.05–1.96), with results varying by subgroup; the authors judged the safety evidence insufficient.<sup>[[11]](#ref11)</sup> In plastic or body-contouring literature, unidirectional barbed sutures show higher wound-dehiscence and suture-extrusion odds, especially in subcutaneous or superficial closure.<sup>[[13]](#ref13)[[32]](#ref32)</sup>

Practical precautions:

- **Avoid skin and superficial subcutaneous closure** when extrusion would be clinically obvious or difficult to manage.
- **Trim tails flush** and bury free ends where possible.
- **Avoid free intraperitoneal barbed loops**; early case reports linked exposed tails to small-bowel obstruction, although large cohort data do not support a frequent causal relationship.<sup>[[33]](#ref33)[[34]](#ref34)</sup>
- **Do not over-tighten mucosa-to-mucosa anastomoses**; barbs hold tension, so ischemic narrowing is easy to create if each bite is cinched too aggressively.
- **Single-layer running closure** is often preferable to stacked barbed layers in soft tissue; two-layer barbed closure has been associated with higher wound separation in some body-contouring data.<sup>[[32]](#ref32)</sup>

### Product examples

| Product | Direction | Material pattern | Common reconstructive use |
|---|---|---|---|
| **V-Loc 90 / 180** | Unidirectional | Absorbable; listed tensile-strength interval 14 days for 90 versus 21 days for 180, with absorption in 90–110 versus 180 days<sup>[[43]](#ref43)[[44]](#ref44)</sup> | Robotic VUA<sup>[[45]](#ref45)</sup>, cystotomy closure, selected pyeloplasty<sup>[[45]](#ref45)</sup>; match the variant to the repair |
| **Stratafix Spiral** | Unidirectional | PDO / Monocryl-family variants | Running closure in robotic or laparoscopic fields |
| **Quill** | Uni- or bidirectional, by variant<sup>[[51]](#ref51)</sup> | Absorbable or non-absorbable variants | Center-outward closure with the bidirectional version; body-wall and pelvic closure depend on the chosen product |

**Summary.** A barbed suture is a material choice within an operation. It is most useful when knot tying and tension maintenance are the bottleneck; they are least attractive in superficial closure, friable ischemic tissue, or any field where exposed barbs could catch bowel or extrude.

## Practical Selection by GU Reconstructive Procedure

| Operation | Mucosa / inner layer | Muscular / outer layer | Fascia | Skin |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Urethroplasty (bulbar BMG)** | 5-0 Vicryl or Monocryl (graft/mucosa) | 4-0 Vicryl (spongiosum) | 2-0 Vicryl (Colles') | 4-0 Monocryl subcuticular |
| **Radical prostatectomy (open)** | 3-0 Monocryl (VUA) | 3-0 Vicryl (bladder neck) | 0 PDS loop | staples or 4-0 Monocryl |
| **RARP (robotic)** | 3-0 V-Loc 180 (VUA) | — | 0 PDS loop | 4-0 Monocryl |
| **Ureteral reimplant** | 4-0 Monocryl (mucosa) | 3-0 Vicryl (detrusor) | 0 PDS | 4-0 Monocryl |
| **Pyeloplasty** | 4-0 or 5-0 Monocryl (pelvis to ureter) | — | 0 PDS | 4-0 Monocryl |
| **Augmentation cystoplasty** | 3-0 Vicryl or PDS (bowel-to-bladder) | 3-0 Vicryl (serosa reinforcement) | 0 PDS loop | 4-0 Monocryl |
| **VVF repair** | 3-0 Vicryl (bladder) | 3-0 Vicryl (interposition) | 0 PDS | 4-0 Monocryl |
| **AUS implant** | No routine urinary mucosal suture | Tunical closure only in a transcorporal variant; technique-specific material<sup>[[38]](#ref38)</sup> | Closure per incision and device approach | Closure per incision and device approach |
| **Pubovaginal sling** | 0 or 1 Prolene (fascia arms) | 2-0 Vicryl (anterior vaginal wall) | 0 PDS if abdominal | 4-0 Monocryl |
| **Cystectomy + ileal conduit** | 3-0 Vicryl (bowel) + 3-0 Monocryl (ureter-to-conduit) | 3-0 Vicryl | 1 PDS loop or 2-0 small-bites | staples |

These are typical defaults, not a rigid protocol; fellowship tree, surgeon preference, and case-specific anatomy drive local variation. In a 559-patient randomized trial of elective midline laparotomy, 2-0 PDS small bites (5 mm from the edge and 5 mm apart) reduced estimated 13-year cumulative incisional hernia versus looped PDS large bites (10 mm by 10 mm): 34% versus 49%, with median follow-up of 8 years.<sup>[[48]](#ref48)</sup>

## Alternatives to Sutures

| Option | Urologic use | Trade-off |
|---|---|---|
| **Skin staples** | Midline laparotomy, cystectomy skin, lateral nephrectomy skin | Fast, low reaction; removal discomfort |
| **Tissue adhesive** (2-octylcyanoacrylate / Dermabond) | Pediatric skin, low-tension incisions | No removal; not for high-tension or infected wounds |
| **Surgical tape** (Steri-Strips) | Adjunct after sutures/staples removed | Limited tension tolerance |

For selected surgical skin incisions, a Cochrane review found no clear infection difference between tissue adhesive and sutures, but more wound dehiscence with adhesive (RR 3.35, 95% CI 1.53–7.33; low-quality evidence). This does not support adhesive for high-tension, infected or deeper reconstructive closures.<sup>[[9]](#ref9)</sup>

## Complications — Suture-Attributable

- **Suture sinus or extrusion.** In laparotomy closure, sinus or fistula-tract formation was less frequent with absorbable than non-absorbable sutures (RR 0.49, 95% CI 0.26–0.94; low-quality evidence).<sup>[[3]](#ref3)</sup> Multifilament sutures are also associated with more sinus formation than monofilament.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup>
- **Stitch abscess.** Multifilament higher risk than monofilament.
- **Tissue reactivity.** In a rabbit reproductive-tract microsuture study, tissue reaction varied by suture material, caliber, and time after placement.<sup>[[52]](#ref52)</sup>
- **Encrustation or calculi.** Any suture exposed to urine can become a calculus nidus, especially with prolonged exposure; calculi have also been reported after Vicryl pyeloplasty. Avoid non-absorbable full-thickness passes through urothelium and minimize exposed suture and knots.<sup>[[49]](#ref49)[[50]](#ref50)</sup>
- **Erosion.** Polypropylene mesh, and permanent suture placed through the wall of the urinary tract or vagina, can erode into the lumen over time.
