# Intraoperative Bowel Handling & Injury Management

Bowel injury may occur during access, adhesiolysis, bowel harvest or pelvic dissection. Management depends on injury depth and viability, anatomic location, contamination and the patient's physiology. Recognize and control an injury promptly, assess the adjacent bowel and mesentery, and involve a surgeon experienced in intestinal repair when needed. Much of the management evidence comes from trauma and general surgery; applying it to elective urinary reconstruction requires clinical judgment.<sup>[[5]](#ref5)</sup>

## Bowel Handling — Prevent Injury and Adhesion

Gentle handling, moisture, hemostasis, preservation of blood supply and limiting foreign material are the core principles. The ASRM 2019 committee opinion supports meticulous technique but cautions that improvements in adhesion scores do not establish prevention of pain, infertility or bowel obstruction.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup>

| Practice | Application and limits |
|---|---|
| **Plan access and exposure** | Review previous operations and imaging; enter away from expected dense adhesions when feasible. Minimally invasive access does not eliminate adhesions or enterotomy risk. In a Scottish retrospective cohort of 72,270 abdominal or pelvic operations, readmission directly related to adhesions within 5 years was 1.7% after laparoscopic and 4.3% after open surgery.<sup>[[18]](#ref18)</sup> |
| **Limit traction and desiccation** | Use moist protection and atraumatic instruments such as a [Babcock](/docs/foundations/tools/instruments/clamps/babcock) or [Cadiere](/docs/foundations/tools/instruments/robotic/cadiere). Avoid crushing or sustained traction on the bowel wall. |
| **Dissect adhesions under direct vision** | Choose sharp or gentle blunt dissection according to the tissue plane. Dense bowel adhesions usually need controlled sharp dissection; no technique makes a poorly defined plane safe. A prospective abdominal-surgery cohort reported bowel injury in 50/475 adhesiolysis procedures (10.5%); this is not a universal risk estimate for reoperative diversion. No bowel defects occurred in operations without adhesiolysis, and mortality after adhesiolysis complicated by a bowel defect was 4/50 (8%) versus 7/425 (1.6%) after uncomplicated adhesiolysis.<sup>[[2]](#ref2)</sup> |
| **Use energy selectively** | Monopolar, bipolar and ultrasonic devices can all injure bowel. Keep activation and residual hot surfaces away from bowel, use the device's instructions, and inspect suspected contact sites. Experimental energy or cooling-time measurements do not define a universal human safety threshold.<sup>[[8]](#ref8)[[10]](#ref10)</sup> |
| **Reduce adhesion-promoting injury** | Preserve perfusion, prevent desiccation, remove devitalized material when indicated, control bleeding and minimize lint or other foreign material.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup> |
| **Protect bowel during closure** | Keep bowel clear of fascial bites and confirm removal of packs and protective devices. Temporary open-abdomen dressings require a protective visceral layer; foam or synthetic mesh must not directly contact exposed bowel.<sup>[[3]](#ref3)</sup> |

### Adhesion Barriers

Seprafilm is an adjunct to surgical technique. Its labeling distinguishes reduction of adhesions and reoperation for adhesive small-bowel obstruction from prevention of all postoperative obstruction. Follow the indication and placement instructions; the evidence does not establish routine benefit in every reconstructive procedure.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[16]](#ref16)</sup>

:::warning[Fresh intestinal anastomosis]
**Do not wrap Seprafilm directly around a fresh intestinal suture or staple line.** This is a contraindication associated with increased leak-related events, and it is more than a reason for extra caution. Consult the current product instructions for other contraindications and use in infection or complex inflammatory fields.<sup>[[16]](#ref16)</sup>
:::

## Iatrogenic Bowel Injury — Management Algorithm

1. **Recognize and expose the injury.** Control spillage, assess depth, perfusion and mesenteric damage, and look for additional injuries.
2. **Assess physiology and contamination.** Resuscitation, antimicrobial treatment when indicated and timely source control take priority over completing the planned reconstruction.
3. **Choose repair, resection with anastomosis or diversion.** A viable, limited injury in a stable patient differs from devascularized bowel in shock. Primary repair is often appropriate; diversion is not mandatory for every enterotomy or episode of peritonitis.<sup>[[5]](#ref5)</sup>

### Serosal vs Full-Thickness Injury

A superficial serosal abrasion is different from a muscular defect or enterotomy. Inspect the entire injured area; a limited serosal defect with intact viable deeper layers may require no closure, while a deeper partial-thickness defect may warrant seromuscular repair. Avoid imbrication that narrows the lumen or compromises perfusion. Full-thickness injury needs definitive treatment, and thermal damage may extend beyond the visible surface.<sup>[[4]](#ref4)[[5]](#ref5)[[9]](#ref9)</sup>

### Small Bowel

For a limited injury with healthy edges, primary closure can preserve bowel. Orient closure to maintain the lumen. Resection is appropriate for devascularization, destructive injury or multiple adjacent defects that cannot be repaired reliably. Anastomosis requires adequate perfusion and acceptable physiology; shock, ongoing transfusion or vasopressor needs, edema, contamination and the ability to close the abdomen influence whether it should be deferred or a stoma created.<sup>[[5]](#ref5)</sup>

Reported trauma leak rates should not be presented as a fixed risk for ileal conduit, augmentation or repeat bowel harvest. Neither hand-sewn nor stapled construction compensates for ischemia or tension; see [Bowel Anastomosis](/docs/foundations/surgical-principles/bowel-anastomosis).

### Colon

Primary repair is appropriate for many nondestructive injuries; resection with anastomosis can be appropriate for destructive injuries in selected patients. A 2025 EAST retrospective multicenter study of 2,022 AAST grade I and II colon injuries (1,314 primary repairs, 708 resections with anastomosis) associated resection with anastomosis with more infectious complications and a longer stay than primary repair. It supports avoiding unnecessary resection, but treatment selection and injury severity may confound the comparison.<sup>[[6]](#ref6)</sup>

For destructive injuries, a single-center retrospective series of 101 resections managed with liberal primary anastomosis reported an 8.0% leak rate and no colon-related mortality; leaving the colon in discontinuity was an independent risk factor for mortality.<sup>[[19]](#ref19)</sup> A meta-analysis of 14 studies (2,071 patients) found an overall leak rate of 4.63% after primary repair or anastomosis, and a higher leak rate in damage-control patients than in others (16.7% versus 3.2%).<sup>[[20]](#ref20)</sup>

Diversion becomes more attractive when perfusion, physiology, tissue quality or the consequences of a leak make an anastomosis unsafe. The type of stoma depends on the injury and remaining bowel; a loop ostomy is not interchangeable with an end stoma in every circumstance. In a single-center retrospective comparison of 115 diversions, patients without a planned ventral hernia had shorter reversal operative time (95 versus 245 minutes), a shorter reversal stay (6 versus 10 days) and fewer reversal complications (0% versus 36%) with a loop ostomy than with an end colostomy.<sup>[[5]](#ref5)[[21]](#ref21)</sup>

### Rectum

#### Anatomy First — Intraperitoneal vs Extraperitoneal

Classify the injured surface relative to the peritoneal reflection. A simple upper-third or middle-third rule does not capture it. The upper rectum has anterior and lateral peritoneal coverage, the middle rectum anterior coverage, and the lower rectum none; the reflection varies by surface and patient. Mid-rectal injury may require operative assessment to establish whether an intraperitoneal component exists.<sup>[[17]](#ref17)</sup>

In a retrospective AAST study of 785 patients at 22 centers, intraperitoneal injuries managed with proximal diversion had more abdominal complications than those managed without it (22% versus 10%), and diversion was not associated with improved outcomes. An international survey of 280 surgeons found that 81% avoided routine colostomy for hemodynamically stable intraperitoneal colorectal injuries.<sup>[[13]](#ref13)[[22]](#ref22)</sup>

| Injury pattern | Management considerations |
|---|---|
| **Confirmed partial-thickness rectal injury** | Does not require the same operative pathway as a full-thickness injury. Ensure that full-thickness or evolving thermal injury has been excluded.<sup>[[17]](#ref17)</sup> |
| **Intraperitoneal full-thickness injury** | Generally follow colon-injury principles: primary repair or resection/anastomosis according to destruction and perfusion. Consider diversion selectively for adverse physiology, tissue quality or associated injury.<sup>[[5]](#ref5)[[17]](#ref17)</sup> |
| **Nondestructive penetrating extraperitoneal injury** | EAST 2016 conditionally recommends proximal diversion, based on low-quality evidence. The later WTA algorithm allows primary transanal repair without diversion for selected accessible nondestructive injuries; inaccessible injuries generally undergo diversion. These are trauma frameworks, not a universal rule for every iatrogenic pelvic injury.<sup>[[14]](#ref14)[[17]](#ref17)</sup> |
| **Destructive or inaccessible extraperitoneal injury** | Proximal diversion is generally used. Extensive pelvic dissection solely to expose and close an otherwise inaccessible defect can add injury; specialist assessment determines whether additional repair/debridement is needed.<sup>[[17]](#ref17)</sup> |

#### Repair Technique

Debride nonviable edges, maintain the lumen and obtain a secure closure without tension. A two-layer closure is commonly described, but no evidence supports one suture or layer recipe for every rectal injury. Test the repair as appropriate and assess adjacent urinary or vaginal reconstruction. Transanal access may permit repair of selected low defects; accessibility and expertise matter.<sup>[[17]](#ref17)</sup>

After rectal injury during prostatectomy or other GU surgery, vascularized tissue may be interposed between adjacent repairs when feasible. Yee and Ornstein's report describes robotic repair with omental interposition in two cases; it does not establish a universal standard for all RALP, cystectomy or AUS injuries. Choose the flap for the actual defect and anatomy. A Martius flap is not a routine substitute for omentum in male pelvic surgery.<sup>[[15]](#ref15)</sup>

Prior radiation, contamination or poor tissue quality can lower the threshold for diversion and vascularized coverage. They do not make every primary repair impossible. Decide with colorectal and reconstructive input. Radiation history alone is not an automatic indication.

#### Presacral Drainage and Distal Rectal Washout

Do not add these routinely for penetrating extraperitoneal rectal injury. EAST recommendations are conditional; WTA similarly discourages routine use. In the AAST cohort, distal rectal washout (OR 3.4, 95% CI 1.4–8.5) and presacral drainage (OR 2.6, 95% CI 1.1–6.1) were independently associated with abdominal complications. Observational associations with more complications do not prove that drainage caused those complications. Avoiding routine prophylactic presacral drainage does not mean leaving an established abscess or uncontrolled contamination undrained.<sup>[[13]](#ref13)[[14]](#ref14)[[17]](#ref17)</sup>

## Thermal / Electrosurgical Injuries

Thermal injury may be occult and progress to delayed necrosis. A normal-looking surface immediately after contact does not exclude deeper damage. In a literature survey of 266 laparoscopic bowel perforations, 69% were not recognized at surgery and 50% were caused by electrocautery.<sup>[[7]](#ref7)</sup> In ex vivo porcine jejunum, all samples receiving 3.8 J or more had visible burns, and as little as 10 J caused full-thickness burns; shielded instruments with active electrode monitoring leaked too little energy to burn.<sup>[[8]](#ref8)</sup> In a porcine stomach model, the laparoscopic hook remained above 50 °C for at least 15 seconds after activation, and the authors advise no tissue contact for that interval; endoknives cooled in 4 seconds.<sup>[[10]](#ref10)</sup> These experimental values explain the hazard, but neither a fixed joule limit nor a fixed cooling interval reliably defines safe contact across devices.

If thermal injury is suspected, inspect the affected segment and obtain experienced surgical assessment. Nonviable or uncertain full-thickness damage often requires excision or resection back to healthy tissue; simply oversewing a burn may leave progressive injury behind. The extent of treatment depends on depth, viability and mechanism, and it does not call for automatic segmental resection for every superficial contact.<sup>[[4]](#ref4)[[9]](#ref9)</sup>

## Recognizing Missed Injuries

Escalate unexplained or worsening abdominal pain, distention, failure to recover bowel function, tachycardia, fever, abnormal leukocyte count, metabolic deterioration or sepsis. Either leukocytosis or leukopenia can occur; absence of fever or a normal initial test does not exclude injury. Bishoff's unrecognized laparoscopic perforations presented with severe single trocar-site pain, distention, diarrhea and leukopenia, followed by cardiopulmonary collapse from sepsis within 96 hours.<sup>[[7]](#ref7)</sup> In a series of 32 patients reoperated within 30 days for a missed injury, the mean interval to reoperation was 5.3 days (range 1–13); adhesions, previous operations and a laparoscopic approach were the commonly documented factors. These series describe presentation over several days, not a safe period to wait.<sup>[[11]](#ref11)</sup>

An unstable patient or one with generalized peritonitis needs urgent surgical evaluation and source control. A stable patient may undergo contrast-enhanced CT, serial examination and additional investigation according to the suspected site; persistent clinical concern warrants reassessment even after nondiagnostic imaging. Do not delay necessary exploration to obtain repeated tests.<sup>[[5]](#ref5)</sup>

## Damage Control and the Open Abdomen

Damage-control surgery is for severe physiologic compromise or a specific need for staged source control or viability assessment. It is not the default response to a recognized enterotomy during an otherwise stable elective case. Control bleeding and contamination, leave bowel in discontinuity when immediate anastomosis is unsafe, and protect exposed viscera.<sup>[[3]](#ref3)[[5]](#ref5)</sup>

WSES recommends re-exploration within 24–48 hours, sooner with instability or deterioration. At relook, reassess perfusion, resuscitation, contamination and abdominal closure; elapsed time alone does not make an anastomosis safe. Close the abdomen as soon as source control and physiology permit. The WSES bowel-injury guideline describes a delayed primary anastomosis as usually performed at the relook laparotomy within 48–72 hours, and it can be performed at the third or fourth relook if required.<sup>[[3]](#ref3)[[5]](#ref5)</sup>

To reduce entero-atmospheric fistula risk, protect bowel from direct foam or mesh contact, avoid exposed anastomoses against the temporary closure, and pursue early closure when feasible. With an established fistula, isolate effluent, control sepsis and provide nutritional support. NPWT can assist wound/effluent management but does not itself establish fistula cure. Definitive reconstruction is delayed until the patient and abdomen are suitable; a consensus conference suggested correction of an established fistula after 6 to 12 months (grade C), and timing is individualized.<sup>[[3]](#ref3)[[12]](#ref12)</sup>

## Reconstructive-Urology / Urogyn Implications

- **Reoperative diversion or augmentation:** obtain prior bowel and mesenteric details, preserve usable intestine and plan adequate time for adhesiolysis. There is no validated universal 20% enterotomy risk.
- **Sacrocolpopexy and pelvic dissection:** protect adjacent sigmoid and small bowel, assess suspected energy contact, and reconsider implant or reconstruction plans if contamination occurs.
- **Radiated pelvic reconstruction:** assess the actual bowel segment, mesentery and tissue quality; plan colorectal support and alternatives when repair is uncertain.
- **Recognized rectal injury:** document location, depth, tissue quality, repair, testing and any interposition/diversion; communicate the postoperative monitoring plan explicitly.
