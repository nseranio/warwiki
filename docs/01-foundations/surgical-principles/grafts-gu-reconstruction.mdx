# Grafts in Genitourinary Reconstruction

A **free tissue graft** is transferred without a vascular pedicle and must acquire support from its recipient bed. A **flap** arrives with its own blood supply. This distinction matters when choosing between a free mucosal patch, a pedicled skin flap and a vascularized bowel segment.

This page compares donor options and core principles. The linked donor and named-operation pages contain the detailed harvest and reconstruction evidence.

## Graft Take: Imbibition and Inosculation

### Imbibition (0–48 hours)

Initially, fluid and nutrients diffuse from the recipient bed into the graft, which is edematous and pale during this phase. Fluid collecting between graft and bed interrupts this exchange. The first one to two days are a useful approximate teaching interval, not a fixed viability clock for every tissue or patient.<sup>[[12]](#ref12)</sup>

### Inosculation (48 hours – 1 week)

**Inosculation** describes connections between graft and recipient vessels; **neovascularization** includes ingrowth of new host vessels. These processes overlap. The classic Converse experiment demonstrated vascular entry by 48 hours in rat skin grafts; it does not prescribe an exact clinical timetable for every GU mucosal graft. A viable skin graft usually turns pink as perfusion returns over roughly the first week.<sup>[[2]](#ref2)</sup>

### Factors Affecting Graft Take

| Factor | Practical implication |
|---|---|
| Recipient-bed vascularity | Assess actual viable tissue; marked scarring, ischemia or radiation injury may require vascularized reconstruction |
| Contact and drainage | Prevent hematoma, seroma and other collections that separate graft from bed; use appropriate fixation, including [quilting](/docs/foundations/surgical-skills/quilting-stitch) where indicated |
| Shear | Plan support and immobilization for the operation and anatomical site; there is no universal 48–72-hour regimen |
| Infection and tissue viability | Debride nonviable tissue and control infection; colonization alone is not synonymous with invasive infection |
| Thickness and preparation | Remove unwanted fat carefully while preserving the required graft layers; neither extreme thinning nor full thickness guarantees take |
| Patient and wound factors | Consider perfusion, smoking, diabetes, nutrition, prior treatment and postoperative wound mechanics |

For skin-graft biology and fixation evidence, see [STSG](/docs/foundations/surgical-principles/grafts/stsg) and [FTSG](/docs/foundations/surgical-principles/grafts/ftsg). For a poorly vascularized urethral bed, consider the role of [vascularized flaps](/docs/foundations/surgical-principles/flaps-gu-reconstruction).<sup>[[10]](#ref10)</sup>

**BMG ureteroplasty:** a graft needs an appropriate vascular environment. Omentum is a commonly used adjunct, but Zhao's initial four-patient series was not a comparison proving that reconstruction without omentum fails. The support and wrapping plan depends on the repair and available tissues; see the [operative page](/docs/surgical-techniques/04d-upper-tract-reconstruction/bmg-onlay-ureter).<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup>

## Graft Detail

### Buccal Mucosa Graft (BMG)

Buccal mucosa provides hairless, non-keratinizing stratified squamous lining. Oral mucosa is the usual first donor considered when a urethral graft is required and suitable tissue is available. It does not become urothelium after transfer.<sup>[[8]](#ref8)[[10]](#ref10)</sup>

Assess oral health, previous harvest, mouth opening and the required dimensions. Identify and protect the parotid duct and surrounding structures. Donor closure, bilateral harvest and the choice of buccal versus another oral site require individual planning; the detailed [BMG page](/docs/foundations/surgical-principles/grafts/buccal-mucosa) describes the relevant comparisons.

**Uses.** Buccal mucosa is placed as a dorsal, ventral or lateral onlay in anterior urethroplasty, as an augmentation in augmented anastomotic repair, and as a graft in selected posterior reconstruction combined with anastomotic repair. It is also used to augment the female urethra and, in a four-patient robotic series, to patch proximal or multifocal ureteral strictures not amenable to primary anastomosis (100% success at median 15.5 months).<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[8]](#ref8)</sup>

**Harvest.** The cheek is retracted with a self-retaining retractor. The parotid (Stensen) duct opening, near the upper second molar, and the oral commissure are identified and avoided. The graft is elevated with scissors, and the donor site may be closed or left open; both are accepted (see the BMG page for the supporting trials).

**Limitations.** Cheek harvest can cause swelling, numbness and reduced mouth opening. Prior harvest, oral fibrosis, radiation or restricted mouth opening can limit the available graft.<sup>[[8]](#ref8)</sup>

### Lingual Mucosa Graft

Lingual mucosa is an alternative or supplemental oral donor, not simply an inferior fallback. Discuss tongue movement, speech, taste and eating symptoms alongside the different morbidity of cheek harvest. Safe harvest follows the actual ventrolateral anatomy; a choice of surface does not automatically eliminate nerve injury.<sup>[[10]](#ref10)</sup> Lingual mucosa is thinner than buccal mucosa and may suit the distal urethra and meatus. The AUA amendment cites a meta-analysis of 12 studies with no difference in success between buccal and lingual grafts; buccal harvest carried more donor-site swelling, numbness and reduced mouth opening, and lingual harvest more difficulty with speech and tongue protrusion.<sup>[[8]](#ref8)</sup> Lingual mucosa is used when cheek tissue is unavailable, or with buccal mucosa to add length in long strictures. See [Lingual Mucosa](/docs/foundations/surgical-principles/grafts/lingual-mucosa).

### Labial Mucosa Graft

Here labial means oral lip. Inner-lip mucosa is distinct from the vermilion and from female genital labia. Its use is not inherently female-specific. Restricted donor territory and possible persistent lip discomfort, numbness, salivary or cosmetic changes affect selection.<sup>[[10]](#ref10)</sup> See [Oral-Lip Mucosa](/docs/foundations/surgical-principles/grafts/labial-mucosa); identify the exact donor whenever reading a female-urethroplasty report that uses the word “labial.”

### Rectal Mucosa Graft

Rectal mucosa is a selected salvage option when adequate oral tissue is unavailable or unsuitable. It has columnar epithelium and requires assessment of both recipient reconstruction and rectal donor morbidity. Transanal endoscopic, robotic and single-port harvests have been described; none is established as universally superior.<sup>[[3]](#ref3)[[4]](#ref4)[[6]](#ref6)[[7]](#ref7)[[9]](#ref9)</sup>

**Indications.** Rectal mucosa is used when oral mucosa is unavailable, insufficient or unsuitable, mainly in long-segment and panurethral strictures. In the multi-institutional TURNS series of 13 patients, 9 (69%) had a failed prior buccal graft urethroplasty, 6 (46%) had lichen sclerosus and 3 (23%) had strictures after gender-affirming surgery. Median stricture length was 13 cm and mean graft length was 10.6 cm (range 3 to 16). Repairs were dorsal or ventral onlays or the first stage of a staged repair.<sup>[[3]](#ref3)</sup> The AUA amendment recommends oral mucosa as the first choice (Expert Opinion) and does not specifically address rectal mucosa.<sup>[[8]](#ref8)</sup> Colonic mucosal grafts for the urethra were first described by Thyrmos in 1902, but early techniques required sigmoid resection.<sup>[[5]](#ref5)</sup>

**Harvest approaches.** All are transanal, under general anesthesia in lithotomy.

| Approach | Report | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| Transanal endoscopic microsurgery (TEM) | Palmer 2016 (four patients) | Introduced in 2013 as a minimally invasive alternative to sigmoid resection<sup>[[4]](#ref4)</sup> |
| Robotic transanal minimally invasive surgery (R-TAMIS) | Howard 2019 (six urethral strictures and one vaginal stricture) | GelPOINT Path platform with submucosal hydrodissection; mean graft 11.4 × 3.0 cm; flexible sigmoidoscopy confirmed hemostasis<sup>[[7]](#ref7)</sup> |
| Single-port endorobotic | Emrich Accioly 2022 and Ozgur 2023 (three patients in total) | da Vinci SP with GelPOINT Path, pneumorectum 12 mmHg; dissection begins about 2 cm proximal to the sphincter complex; one 21 × 3 cm graft<sup>[[6]](#ref6)[[9]](#ref9)</sup> |

The mucosa is lifted with a submucosal injection, harvested as mucosa alone and thinned before placement. Reported advantages are graft length and avoidance of oral morbidity. Six patients in Howard's series regained bowel function on postoperative day one, and those with a prior cheek harvest subjectively reported less pain.<sup>[[6]](#ref6)[[7]](#ref7)</sup> Limitations are the absence of head-to-head comparisons with oral mucosa, small short-term series, the need for a colorectal surgeon experienced in transanal platforms, and unknown long-term behavior of a columnar epithelium in the urethra.<sup>[[3]](#ref3)[[4]](#ref4)</sup>

| Report | Actual population and finding | Main limitation |
|---|---|---|
| Granieri 2019 | 13 reconstructions; two recurrences at median follow-up 13.5 months; no bowel-related complication reported | Small heterogeneous salvage cohort; other complications included glans dehiscence, urethrocutaneous fistula and compartment syndrome |
| Palmer 2016 | Four patients; one recurrence; **median follow-up 18 months** | Ten months was the time of the recurrence, not median follow-up |
| Howard 2019 | **Six urethral and one vaginal stricture**; favorable graft take and no reported complications at median 17 months | Not seven urethroplasties; subjective pain comparison with prior cheek harvest is not randomized evidence |
| Emrich Accioly 2022 | One patient; a 21 × 3 cm graft and discharge on day two | Technical feasibility, not an expected graft size or discharge rule for all patients |
| Ozgur 2023 | Two cases describing single-port harvest | Small technical report; does not establish long-term donor safety |

Do not sum these potentially overlapping institutional reports to estimate worldwide experience. Absence of a reported bowel complication in small series is not proof of zero risk. Staged graft take has also been illustrated, but early incorporation does not establish durable stricture-free survival.<sup>[[5]](#ref5)</sup>

**Product safety:** older harvest descriptions used ORISE gel. It was removed from the market in a Class 2 recall initiated in October 2022 because of foreign-body reactions that presented as masses from retained gel. It should not be recommended as a current lifting agent.<sup>[[11]](#ref11)</sup> Plan contemporary instrumentation and lifting agents with the colorectal team using current product labeling. See [Enterourethroplasty](/docs/surgical-techniques/04a-urethral-reconstruction/enterourethroplasty) for the distinction between a free mucosal graft and vascularized bowel substitution.

### Intestinal Segments

Most bowel segments used for augmentation, diversion or intestinal vaginoplasty retain their mesenteric supply. Sigmoid vaginoplasty is not McIndoe skin-graft vaginoplasty. Mucus and absorption are consequences to manage, not an intrinsic advantage for every urinary reconstruction.

| Segment | Properties | Common uses |
|---|---|---|
| Ileum | Compliant, mucus-producing, long mesentery | Augmentation, orthotopic neobladder, ileal conduit |
| Sigmoid colon | Thicker wall, larger caliber | Augmentation, colon conduit, sigmoid vaginoplasty |
| Ileocecal segment | Ileocecal valve, cecal reservoir | Ileocecal augmentation, continent cutaneous diversion |

Reservoir configuration, bowel length and location and urine contact determine risks such as hyperchloremic metabolic acidosis, mucus obstruction, infection, stones and B12 deficiency after terminal ileal use. An augmented bladder also requires long-term surveillance. The ileocecal valve may contribute to a continence mechanism; it is not automatically a ureteral antireflux mechanism. Use the detailed [intestinal-segment page](/docs/foundations/surgical-principles/grafts/intestinal-segments), [augmentation principles](/docs/surgical-techniques/04b-bladder-reconstruction/augmentation-principles) and [diversion principles](/docs/surgical-techniques/04c-urinary-diversion/urinary-diversion-principles) for selection and lifelong follow-up.

### Penile / Preputial Skin Graft

A free preputial graft, a pedicled preputial island flap and tubularization of the native urethral plate are different operations. Duckett is a flap technique; standard TIP does not require a free graft. Circumcision limits preputial availability, but it does not remove all penile skin.

Assess healthy donor tissue and actual hair distribution. Avoid genital skin in LS-related urethral strictures. Single-stage free-graft tubes should not be presented as routine urethral substitutes.<sup>[[10]](#ref10)</sup> See [Penile / Preputial Skin](/docs/foundations/surgical-principles/grafts/penile-preputial-skin).

### Saphenous Vein Graft

Vein used as a vascular conduit differs from an opened patch used for another reconstructive purpose. The AUA 2018 ED guideline states that penile arterial reconstruction may be considered in young men with focal arterial occlusion and no generalized vascular disease or veno-occlusive dysfunction (Conditional Recommendation), and that penile venous surgery is not recommended (Moderate Recommendation).<sup>[[14]](#ref14)</sup> See [Saphenous Vein](/docs/foundations/surgical-principles/grafts/saphenous-vein) for the specific applications and limitations.

### Posterior Auricular Skin Graft

Postauricular skin is a selected alternative donor. Available area, thickness, hair, pigmentation and closure vary; a concealed location does not guarantee no visible morbidity. Its use is not confined to short pediatric strictures.<sup>[[10]](#ref10)</sup> See [Posterior Auricular Skin](/docs/foundations/surgical-principles/grafts/posterior-auricular).

### Skin Grafts (STSG and FTSG)

| Property | STSG | FTSG |
|---|---|---|
| Composition | Epidermis and partial dermis | Epidermis and full dermis |
| Immediate recoil after harvest | Generally less | Generally greater |
| Later contraction during healing | Generally greater | Generally less |
| Donor wound | Re-epithelializes from retained structures | Usually closed primarily |
| Hair | Depends on donor and harvest | Depends on donor and preparation |
| Penile coverage | Established option for selected defects | Established option for selected defects |

Meshed split-thickness grafts (commonly expanded 1:1.5 or 1:2) conform to irregular perineal or scrotal contours, for example after debridement for Fournier gangrene. Neither graft has a guaranteed take rate, minimal contraction in every site or proven universal superiority for penile coverage. Donor, recipient bed, dimensions and postoperative support determine suitability. See the full [STSG](/docs/foundations/surgical-principles/grafts/stsg) and [FTSG](/docs/foundations/surgical-principles/grafts/ftsg) discussions.

For vaginal agenesis, supported dilation is first-line for most patients ready for treatment; McIndoe or other surgery follows informed discussion and still requires postoperative dilation.<sup>[[13]](#ref13)</sup> In a McIndoe neovagina, split-thickness skin is grafted over a vaginal mold. Dressing choice at split-thickness donor sites is addressed in a Cochrane review of hydrogel dressings, which included only two randomized trials.<sup>[[15]](#ref15)</sup>

### Bladder Mucosa Graft

Bladder mucosa is a urothelial donor with historical and selected contemporary applications. It was used in pediatric hypospadias and complex urethral reconstruction and as a free graft in vesicovaginal fistula repair. Harvest morbidity, tissue handling and outcomes require comparison with other available donors; native urothelium is not automatically the ideal substitute. See [Bladder Mucosa](/docs/foundations/surgical-principles/grafts/bladder-mucosa).

### Tissue-Engineered Grafts & Bioscaffolds

Cell-free matrices and cell-seeded constructs differ in preparation, regulation, recipient-bed requirements and evidence. They should not be treated as interchangeable alternatives to autologous oral mucosa. See [Tissue-Engineered Grafts & Bioscaffolds](/docs/foundations/surgical-principles/grafts/tissue-engineered) and the relevant guideline restrictions.<sup>[[10]](#ref10)</sup>

## Graft Selection Summary

Start with the defect and viable recipient tissue, then compare suitable donors and the morbidity of each. Separate graft versus flap, patch versus tube, skin versus mucosa, and early graft incorporation versus durable function. | Scenario | Commonly used tissue | Comment |
|---|---|---|
| Anterior urethral stricture | Oral mucosa, usually buccal | AUA 2023 Expert Opinion: oral mucosa is the first choice for grafts<sup>[[8]](#ref8)</sup> |
| Long stricture with limited cheek tissue | Bilateral buccal, or buccal with lingual mucosa | Buccal and lingual mucosa are equivalent alternatives (AUA Strong Recommendation)<sup>[[8]](#ref8)</sup> |
| Oral mucosa unavailable or exhausted, very long stricture | Rectal mucosa | Salvage option in small series<sup>[[3]](#ref3)</sup> |
| Female urethral stricture | Oral mucosa graft or vaginal flap | AUA 2023 favors urethroplasty over endoscopic treatment<sup>[[8]](#ref8)</sup> |
| Long ureteral stricture | Buccal mucosa onlay with vascularized support | Small series<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup> |
| Scrotal or perineal resurfacing | Meshed STSG | Conforms to irregular contours |
| Penile shaft coverage | STSG or FTSG | FTSG contracts less; donor closure and take differ |
| Bladder augmentation | Ileum | Vascularized segment, not a free graft |
| Pediatric hypospadias | Preputial skin, or oral mucosa for a tubularized neourethra | Depends on tissue available |

Start with the defect and viable recipient tissue, then use the linked donor pages and named-operation guidance for patient-specific selection. The table lists usual choices, not rules.
