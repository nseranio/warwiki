# Surgical Ergonomics

Work-related musculoskeletal symptoms are common among surgeons, although prevalence varies by specialty, procedure, and how symptoms are measured. Urology combines open, laparoscopic, robotic, microscopic, and endoscopic work, each with different ergonomic demands.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[18]](#ref18)</sup> This article frames surgical ergonomics as a core reconstructive-surgery principle: operating-room setup and work practices are important modifiable contributors to career-length operative capacity.

See also: [Incisions & Closure](/docs/foundations/surgical-principles/incisions-closure), [Robotic Platforms](/docs/foundations/tools/technology/robotics/platforms).

## Scope of the Problem

### Prevalence among surgeons

In a systematic review and meta-analysis, reported career prevalence of specific diagnoses was approximately 17% for cervical spine degeneration, approximately 18% for rotator-cuff pathology, approximately 19% for lumbar spine degeneration, and approximately 9% for carpal tunnel syndrome; these are pooled prevalence estimates, not an individual surgeon's predicted risk.<sup>[[6]](#ref6)</sup> Neck, back, and shoulders are commonly affected sites across specialties.<sup>[[4]](#ref4)[[5]](#ref5)</sup>

In that review, 12% of respondents with a work-related musculoskeletal disorder reported work leave, restriction, modification, or retirement; denominators and definitions differed among contributing studies.<sup>[[6]](#ref6)</sup> In one included survey of 103 injured surgeons, 19% reported their injury to the institution.<sup>[[6]](#ref6)</sup> Limited formal ergonomics training has also been reported.<sup>[[4]](#ref4)</sup> These findings identify reporting and education gaps without establishing a uniform risk for every surgeon.

### Prevalence among urologists

In a survey of 285 responding urologists, 245 (86%) reported a musculoskeletal complaint in the preceding 12 months; 62% considered their complaints work-related. Endourology (OR 3.06; 95% CI 1.37–6.80) and laparoscopy (OR 1.70; 95% CI 1.27–2.28) were associated with chronic complaints in this survey, not proven causal risks for all urologists.<sup>[[7]](#ref7)</sup> In a more recent cohort, 55% reported physical pain in the OR and 42% during an endoscopic case within the last month.<sup>[[16]](#ref16)</sup> One-third of surveyed urologists described their ergonomics knowledge as minimal and 8% as nonexistent.<sup>[[7]](#ref7)</sup>

### Risk factors and disparities

The core risk factors are static non-neutral posture, repetitive motion, prolonged procedural duration, suboptimal table and monitor height, and poor instrument design.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup> Female surgeons face disproportionately elevated risk because most surgical instruments, handles, and console geometries were engineered for the male 50th-percentile hand and stature.<sup>[[2]](#ref2)</sup> Trainees report higher pain levels than attendings, linked to workload and the absence of ergonomic teaching.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup>

## Core Ergonomic Principles

The central concept is **neutral positioning**: keeping the spine, shoulders, elbows, and wrists within a biomechanical window that minimizes static muscle loading and joint stress.<sup>[[2]](#ref2)[[3]](#ref3)[[8]](#ref8)</sup>

| Region | Target neutral position |
|---|---|
| Neck | Minimal flexion/extension; avoid rotation and lateral bending |
| Shoulders | Relaxed, minimal abduction (ideally &lt;30°) |
| Elbows | 90–120° of flexion |
| Wrists | Neutral (avoid sustained ulnar/radial deviation) |
| Back | Upright, lumbar lordosis preserved |
| Feet | Flat, weight distributed; step stool as needed |

**Operating-room setup checklist**

- **Table height.** For open surgery, at or slightly below the surgeon's elbow; for laparoscopy, low enough that elbows can sit at 90–120° with relaxed shoulders
- **Monitor placement.** Eye level or slightly below; 50–70 cm viewing distance for laparoscopy; directly in line with the operative field to avoid neck rotation
- **Foot pedals.** Directly in front of the surgeon to avoid hip rotation and asymmetric loading
- **Instruments and trays.** Within easy arm reach (&lt;60 cm) to avoid repeated trunk flexion and shoulder reaching
- **Loupes and headlights.** Fitted with enough declination angle that the neck does not need to flex more than ~15° to see the field
- **Anti-fatigue mats and step stools.** Accommodate height differences across the surgical team and reduce lower-extremity strain
- **Lighting.** Sufficient overhead illumination so that the surgeon does not unconsciously lean in

## Modality-Specific Considerations

### Open surgery

Open cases combine prolonged standing, static posture, manual retraction, and forceful movements. The highest-load positions are sustained neck flexion (loupes, deep pelvis), shoulder abduction (retraction), and static lumbar extension (long standing cases).<sup>[[9]](#ref9)</sup> For the reconstructive urologist, deep-pelvic work (posterior urethroplasty, VVF and RUF repair, complex fistula reconstruction, radical perineal reconstruction) is the dominant ergonomic stressor.

### Laparoscopic surgery

Laparoscopy imposes monitor-driven neck posture, sustained arm elevation, and static grip force on some instrument handles. Surveys have reported musculoskeletal symptoms in roughly 70–90% of regularly operating laparoscopic surgeons, but design and response biases limit generalization.<sup>[[10]](#ref10)[[11]](#ref11)</sup> Prolonged reconstructive cases such as laparoscopic pyeloplasty, ureteral reimplantation, and sacrocolpopexy warrant particular attention to fixed posture.

### Robotic-assisted surgery

Robotic consoles offer seated operating with forearm and head support. In a paired cross-sectional study of 12 experienced surgeons performing hysterectomy, median neck-muscle activity was 7.4% versus 5.3% of maximal EMG activity during laparoscopic versus robotic surgery (p&lt;0.05); lower-back activity was higher with robotic surgery.<sup>[[10]](#ref10)</sup> A separate multicenter randomized comparison measured perceived workload rather than EMG and found lower physical-workload scores with robotic surgery.<sup>[[12]](#ref12)</sup> In a survey, neuromusculoskeletal disorders were reported by 1 of 14 predominantly robotic surgeons (approximately 7%) versus approximately 60–67% in other modality groups; this small, nonrandomized subgroup cannot show that robotic surgery prevents injury.<sup>[[14]](#ref14)</sup>

Robotic surgery is not ergonomically neutral. Static neck posture at the console, prolonged low-back isometric loading, and eye strain are under-recognized.<sup>[[11]](#ref11)[[13]](#ref13)</sup> The bedside assistant inherits many of the laparoscopic ergonomic liabilities (static standing, awkward reach between arms, suboptimal monitor placement) and is often forgotten in console-centric ergonomic discussions.

### Endoscopic work

Cystoscopy, ureteroscopy, and percutaneous endoscopic work (as performed in reconstructive practice for fistula inspection, graft or flap surveillance, neo-urethral evaluation) generate distinctive ergonomic stress: sustained asymmetric stance, wrist deviation on rigid and flexible scopes, and, during fluoroscopic cases, the cumulative musculoskeletal load of lead aprons. Endourology had the largest modality-specific association with chronic musculoskeletal complaints in one urologist survey.<sup>[[7]](#ref7)</sup>

### Microsurgery

Microsurgical work (vasoepididymostomy, microsurgical testicular sperm extraction, microsurgical subinguinal varicocelectomy, microvascular free-flap work for phalloplasty or complex perineal reconstruction) magnifies every open-surgery ergonomic risk: the field is small, the magnification is high, and any postural drift is rapidly felt. Microscope ergonomics (ocular height, tilt, binocular angulation) must be set before scrubbing, not after.

## Urology-Specific Recommendations

### Endoscopic cases

- Adjust the cystoscopy tower and monitor to eye level before starting
- Use an adjustable-height procedure chair where the case allows
- Position foot pedals directly in front of the surgeon, not to the side
- Take microbreaks during long ureteroscopic or percutaneous work
- Consider lighter flexible scopes and ergonomic handle grips
- For lead-apron fluoroscopic cases, use two-piece aprons and offload onto support stands between runs

### Laparoscopy

- Plan trocar placement to minimize extreme instrument angulation
- Adjust table height so elbows sit at 90–120° with relaxed shoulders
- Place monitors at eye level, 50–70 cm from the surgeon
- Use articulating instruments for deep pelvic work to reduce wrist deviation
- Schedule microbreaks during long reconstructive cases

### Robotic console

- Reset all console parameters (seat height, armrest, interpupillary distance, viewer angle) at the start of every case; do not inherit the prior surgeon's settings
- Keep feet flat on pedals without ankle dorsiflexion
- Armrests should support the forearms without shoulder elevation
- Use clutching frequently to return the wrists to neutral
- Plan safe opportunities to change posture or pause during long cases; the 20–40-minute stretch interval was studied mainly outside robotic console work
- For the bedside assistant: anti-fatigue mat, dedicated assistant monitor at eye level, and pre-planned trocar choreography

### Open reconstructive surgery

- Set table height for the specific field; deep pelvic work is lower than the surgeon's elbow
- Verify loupe declination before scrubbing
- Use self-retaining retractors (Bookwalter, Omni, Turner-Warwick, Balfour variants) where appropriate to reduce sustained manual retraction
- Step stools for both short surgeons and tall assistants
- Consider anti-fatigue mats and, where appropriate, compression stockings for prolonged standing; no universal three-hour threshold is established here

## Evidence-Based Mitigation Strategies

### Intraoperative interventions

#### Targeted stretching micro breaks (TSMBs)

Targeted stretching microbreaks are a studied intraoperative ergonomic intervention. The multicenter cohort by Park et al. (2017, Ann Surg) included 66 participating surgeons and operating-room staff across four centers and 341 procedures. It used 1.5–2-minute guided stretching breaks every 20–40 minutes targeting hands / wrists, forearms, shoulders, neck, and lower back, performed at the sterile field without regowning.<sup>[[19]](#ref19)</sup>

- Significantly improved post-procedure pain scores in the neck, lower back, shoulders, upper back, wrists and hands, knees, and ankles.
- No statistically significant operative-duration difference was detected between microbreak and comparison procedures; this does not establish equivalence.
- No statistically significant difference in reported benefit was detected between laparoscopic and open procedures; this also does not establish equivalence.
- 57% of surgeons perceived improved physical performance; 38% improved mental focus.
- 87% planned to continue TSMBs beyond the study.

The 2023 ASGE ergonomics guideline conditionally recommends microbreaks for endoscopy-related injury prevention on very-low-certainty evidence. It cites a small, 12-surgical-day web-app-prompted stretch pilot in which participants self-reported less pain or discomfort (100%), improved physical performance (91.7%), and better mental focus (83.3%); these are pilot perceptions, not independently validated clinical outcomes.<sup>[[25]](#ref25)</sup>

The 2025 Kjærgaard et al. systematic review and meta-analysis found reduced pain with active microbreaks in pooled standardized estimates (effect sizes −0.24 to −0.62 across body regions). Passive microbreak findings were described narratively rather than pooled; overall evidence certainty was very low to low by GRADE.<sup>[[21]](#ref21)</sup>

**Practical implementation:**

- Stretches performed at the sterile field with hands above waist level, with no regowning required.
- Circulating nurse or timer app (e.g., phone, OR display) to prompt breaks.
- Protocol is adaptable to open, laparoscopic, and robotic cases.
- Mayo Clinic printable guides are available as the reference protocol.

#### Other intraoperative interventions

- **Position alternation.** When feasible, shift between sitting and standing during staged reconstructive cases.
- **Streamlined instrument passing** to reduce repetitive reach.
- **Adequate tie length.** Short ties drive awkward wrist and shoulder posture.
- **Scheduled handoff points** for very long cases. Genuine two-surgeon teams outperform solo marathons ergonomically.

### Equipment and technology

- Ergonomic chairs with adjustable height, lumbar support, and armrests for seated cases
- Height-adjustable tables that reach both low (open pelvic) and high (bariatric robotic) extremes
- Lighter-weight instruments with neutral-grip handles
- Anti-fatigue mats
- Graduated compression stockings

### Exercise-based prevention

The Giagio et al. 2019 multicenter RCT (Ann Surg) randomized 141 surgeons by division to a preventive program combining OR ergonomic principles with physical exercises supervised by a physical therapist or to usual practice. At six months, the study reported:<sup>[[20]](#ref20)</sup>

- **Lower-back symptoms** in 31/58 program participants (53.4%) versus 49/68 controls (72.0%) with available six-month data (p = 0.04); the article's abstract gives a different percentage pair, so the tabulated denominators are used here.
- **Analgesic consumption** in 15.5% of the program group versus 30.9% of controls at six months (p = 0.03); these are between-group values, not a before-and-after change.
- Improved general-health quality of life at both 3 and 6 months (p = 0.04).
- **Physical inactivity** was associated with WMSDs in the study's observational risk-factor analysis (OR 2.44), not established as a treatment effect by randomization.

**Resistance training** is a plausible component of prevention. A surgeon-focused commentary proposes moderate-intensity sessions of about 20 minutes twice weekly, drawing on evidence from other populations; that dose and its effect on surgeons' pain, concentration, fatigue, or mortality are not established by a surgeon-specific trial.<sup>[[22]](#ref22)</sup> Rathee et al. (2025) studied self-administered strengthening exercises combined with intraoperative targeted stretch microbreaks during thyroidectomy and mastectomy and reported less EMG-measured muscle fatigue and postural strain, which is early evidence for a multimodal stretch-and-strengthen approach, not proof of long-term injury prevention.<sup>[[23]](#ref23)</sup>

The combined message of the evidence is to address both acute load (TSMBs) and chronic deconditioning (resistance training) rather than rely on either one alone.

### Education

The Comprehensive Operating Room Ergonomics (CORE) pilot used occupational-therapy-based intervention to significantly reduce RULA ergonomic risk scores addressing biomechanical, psychophysical, and psychosocial risks.<sup>[[15]](#ref15)</sup> A urology-specific pilot combined didactic training with real-time physical-therapist feedback during endoscopic cases and improved ergonomic knowledge among participating urologists.<sup>[[16]](#ref16)</sup> Evidence from adjacent specialties (MIGS, orthopedics) supports the same conclusion: short structured didactics plus intraoperative feedback works.<sup>[[17]](#ref17)</sup>

Gleave et al. (2025) proposed a comprehensive residency curriculum integrating education, microbreaks, ergonomic adjustment, and physical fitness. Their narrative review cites a prior combined-program experience in which 85% of residents reported reduced discomfort and 93% expected better OR performance; these were self-reports, not measured long-term performance. Introducing ergonomic training during residency may help establish useful habits, but the optimal curriculum is not yet established.<sup>[[24]](#ref24)</sup>

### Behavioral

- Regular off-the-clock exercise and stretching, particularly posterior-chain and rotator-cuff strengthening
- Ambidexterity training
- Conscious posture self-monitoring: a verbal check-in every 20 minutes during a long case
- Institutional wellness and load-management programs

---

## System-Level Change

Surgeon WMSDs are not a personal-failure problem; they are an operating-room design problem.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup> Durable improvement requires institutional buy-in:

- Ergonomics integrated into surgical training curricula (residency and fellowship)
- Ergonomic assessment part of the evaluation process for new instruments, platforms, and workflows
- Anonymous WMSD needs assessments to target wellness programs
- Protected time and institutional policies supporting microbreaks
- Funding for ergonomic innovation and for urology-specific research
- Formal collaboration with occupational therapists and ergonomists

For the reconstructive urologist and urogynecologist whose operative career depends on sustained manual precision over decades, the ergonomic environment is a primary determinant of how long, and how well, they will operate, not a wellness adjunct.
