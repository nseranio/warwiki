# Needles

The needle is half of the suture decision and is at least as important. Point geometry should suit the tissue being approximated; comparative skin-model data should not be read as a measured buccal-graft dehiscence benefit. Blunt rather than sharp suture needles reduce occupational exposure during suitable closures.<sup>[[4]](#ref4)[[10]](#ref10)</sup>

See also: [Sutures](/docs/foundations/surgical-principles/sutures), [Incisions & Closure](/docs/foundations/surgical-principles/incisions-closure), [Bowel Anastomosis](/docs/foundations/surgical-principles/bowel-anastomosis).

---

## Needle Anatomy

Every surgical needle has three components:<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup>

1. **Point (tip).** Determines tissue penetration characteristics.
2. **Body.** Determines strength and curvature.
3. **Swage.** Where the suture is attached. A swaged needle need not have a 1:1 needle-to-suture diameter ratio; a closer match is a product-specific design that can reduce the needle hole around the suture.<sup>[[3]](#ref3)[[18]](#ref18)</sup>

Materials and manufacturing matter: needle alloy, geometry, coating and finishing can affect resistance to bending, sharpness and tissue drag.<sup>[[2]](#ref2)[[3]](#ref3)</sup> Check the selected manufacturer's specifications rather than assuming that one alloy or finishing process is the sharpest across all products.

## Needle Point Types

The point determines how the needle interacts with tissue on entry:<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[9]](#ref9)</sup>

| Point type | Cross-section | Action | Urologic use |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Conventional cutting** | Triangular, cutting edge on **inner** curve | Cuts tissue | Tough skin; largely displaced by reverse cutting |
| **Reverse cutting** | Triangular, cutting edge on **outer** curve | Cuts tissue; reduces cutout risk | **Skin closure** (standard); penile skin; fascia superficial layer |
| **Taper (round body)** | Round, tapers to a sharp point | Dilates tissue without cutting | **Bowel, urinary tract, vessels, fascia** — the urologic default |
| **Blunt taper (blunt point)** | Round, blunt tip | Passes suitable soft tissue with greater force | **Fascial closure when feasible** — reduces staff needlestick exposure in the studied operations |
| **Taper-cut** | Round body + cutting tip | Punctures, then dilates | Vascular anastomosis; tough scar tissue |

**Tissue-tearing evidence.** In a 2025 *artificial-skin* model, mean force required to tear through the material was higher with a taper-point BB needle (4.07 N) than with a conventional cutting PC-3 needle (3.26 N); a reverse-cutting PS-3 needle measured 3.75 N. Larger tested suture diameters also resisted tear-through more. These bench results are not a clinical estimate of graft-edge dehiscence or proof that every taper/needle combination is preferable.<sup>[[10]](#ref10)</sup>

## Needle Curvature

| Curvature | Use |
|---|---|
| **3/8 circle** | Skin closure in relatively large, superficial wounds<sup>[[21]](#ref21)</sup> |
| **1/2 circle** | Confined spaces; a 5/8 circle may be more useful deep in the pelvis<sup>[[22]](#ref22)</sup> |
| **5/8 circle** | Deep, confined pelvic or urogenital spaces<sup>[[23]](#ref23)</sup> |
| **Straight** | Accessible superficial tissue; rarely used in urology |
| **Compound curve** | Specialized (transseptal cardiac, some microsurgical) |

For pelvic urology (vesicourethral anastomosis, reimplant, fistula repair), a 1/2-circle needle can be used in confined spaces; a 5/8-circle needle may be more useful when the operative site is deep in the pelvis.<sup>[[24]](#ref24)</sup> For perineal urethroplasty, the choice between 1/2 and 3/8 circle depends on exposure: a 1/2 circle is designed for confined space, whereas a 3/8 circle is easier to manipulate with wider access.<sup>[[26]](#ref26)</sup>

## Needle Codes — the Ethicon Naming Convention

These are manufacturer-specific example codes; a suffix does not consistently encode length. Confirm point, curvature, size and attached suture on the actual product label or chart.<sup>[[17]](#ref17)</sup>

| Code | Point | Typical curvature | Urologic association |
|---|---|---|---|
| **CT** (Circle Taper) | Taper | 1/2 circle | Bowel (CT-1, CT-2, CT-3); augment, pyeloplasty |
| **SH** (Small Half-circle) | Taper | 1/2 circle | Urethra, ureter, small-diameter pelvic work |
| **RB** (Round Body) | Taper | 1/2 circle | Vessels, fine vascular |
| **UR** (Urology) | Taper | 5/8 circle | Deep pelvis — dorsal vein complex, bladder neck reconstruction |
| **MH** (Medium Half-circle) | Taper | 1/2 circle | Fascia, peritoneum |
| **CTX** | Taper point | 1/2 circle | Large taper-point closure needle |
| **CTXB / CTB / SHB** | Blunt point | 1/2 circle | Fascial or other suitable soft-tissue closure when a blunt point can pass safely |
| **PS** (Plastic Surgery) | Reverse cutting | 3/8 circle | Skin; small precise cutting |
| **FS** (For Skin) | Reverse cutting | 3/8 circle | Skin closure |
| **CP** (Cutting Point) | Reverse cutting | 1/2 circle | Tougher skin/fascia |
| **TP** (Taper Point) | Taper | 1/2 circle | Bowel, fascia variants |

A UR-6 on 3-0 Monocryl (a common vesicourethral anastomosis stitch in open RP) is a very different tool from a CT-1 on 2-0 Vicryl (bowel anastomosis). The 5/8-circle UR needle is a taper-point option for deep, confined pelvic or urogenital work.<sup>[[17]](#ref17)[[25]](#ref25)</sup>

## Selection by Urologic Tissue

| Tissue | Point | Example needle |
|---|---|---|
| **Skin** | Reverse cutting | PS-2, FS, X-1 |
| **Urethra (mucosa-to-spongiosum)** | Taper | SH, RB-1 |
| **Urethra (graft fixation, bulbar BMG)** | Taper | RB-1 on 5-0 or 6-0 PDS |
| **Ureter** | Taper | SH, TF |
| **Bladder mucosa / detrusor** | Taper | CT-1, CT-2, SH |
| **Bowel (for urinary diversion)** | Taper | CT-1, CT-2 |
| **Fascia (mass closure)** | Blunt point when feasible; taper point otherwise | CTXB / CTB / SHB; CTX is **not** blunt |
| **Vascular (renal vein, IVC, iliac)** | Taper or taper-cut | RB-1, CV needles |
| **Deep pelvic apex / dorsal vein complex** | Taper | UR-6 |
| **Tendon / sling suspensory arms** | Taper | CT-1 on Prolene |

## Size — Gauge vs USP

Two parallel systems:

- **Hollow or biopsy needles** use Stubs Iron Wire Gauge, in which a higher number means a smaller diameter. 18 G &gt; 22 G &gt; 25 G.<sup>[[5]](#ref5)[[6]](#ref6)</sup>
- **Surgical suture needles** are sized by length (in mm) and codified under the suture manufacturer's naming convention rather than "gauge."

Gauge is relevant to urologists primarily in biopsy, access, and percutaneous drainage, and not in suture selection.

## Blunt vs Sharp Suture Needles — the Safety Evidence

Percutaneous needlestick injury is a defining occupational hazard of surgery. A Cochrane review of 10 randomized trials found that blunt-tip suture needles reduced staff exposure in the studied operations:<sup>[[4]](#ref4)</sup>

- **Glove perforation reduced by 54%** (RR 0.46, 95% CI 0.38–0.54).<sup>[[4]](#ref4)</sup>
- **Needlestick injuries reduced by 69%** (RR 0.31, 95% CI 0.14–0.68).<sup>[[4]](#ref4)</sup>
- **Prevents one glove perforation in every 6 operations.**
- The ten included trials addressed abdominal closure, cesarean section, vaginal repair and hip replacement; they do not establish use for subcuticular skin closure.

**Surgeon acceptance.** Blunt needles are rated "more difficult but still acceptable"; they require higher penetration force, move more slowly, and produce a tactile "pop" through fascia.<sup>[[4]](#ref4)</sup>

**Implementation gap.** Despite the evidence existing for decades and American College of Surgeons / OSHA / NIOSH recommendations to use blunt needles for fascial closure, adoption is poor. In a 2006 survey of 104 obstetrician-gynecologists in one small US region, 12% reported currently using blunt needles; in a French survey, blunt-tipped needles were available in 49% of surveyed operating theaters.<sup>[[4]](#ref4)[[11]](#ref11)</sup>

**Practical recommendation for urologic fascial closure** (for example, an open abdominal incision): consider a blunt-point needle where the tissue and technique permit, while checking the actual product and using a sharp needle where a blunt one cannot pass appropriately. The evidence concerns staff exposure; patient wound outcomes were not assessed in these trials.<sup>[[4]](#ref4)</sup>

## Safe Needle Handling

### Grip

- **Palmar grip** (all fingers around the holder, rings not used) produced more accurate needle exits than the tested ring grip in Seki's 16-surgeon simulation when surgeons sutured without wavering (mean deviation 1.4 versus 2.0 mm); accuracy was equivalent when surgeons waver-corrected. This is a training observation, not a demonstrated clinical-outcome advantage.<sup>[[12]](#ref12)</sup>
- **Load the needle** at approximately 90° to the holder axis; grasp at the two-thirds mark from the tip.
- **Follow the curve** during passage; do not pry or torque the body of the needle (it will bend or break).

### Needle Transfer

- **Protected transfer technique:** secure the needle tip against the shaft of the needle holder before handing off.<sup>[[14]](#ref14)[[15]](#ref15)</sup> In a retrospective survey of 107 scrub nurses, 40 (37%) recalled a prior injury with unprotected transfer and 9 (8%) with protected transfer; five recalled injuries with both.<sup>[[14]](#ref14)</sup> These are respondent histories, not per-transfer injury rates or a randomized estimate of risk reduction.<sup>[[14]](#ref14)</sup>
- Before returning to the tray or handing off, pivot the needle 90° toward the instrument joint.
- Never touch the needle point to the needle driver's jaws (dulls the tip).

### Microskills That Matter

Assessed in the 2022 JAMA Network Open curriculum study:<sup>[[13]](#ref13)</sup>

- **Loading** the needle at the correct angle.
- **Following** the curve through tissue.
- **Atraumatic release** from tissue.
- **Pitch-and-catch.** Forceps-assisted transfer of the needle back to the needle driver.
- **Push-push-pull.** Reloading the needle while still in tissue, avoiding forceps so the non-dominant hand stays on retraction.

## Specialty Needles

### Spinal and Epidural

Spinal needle design matters when neuraxial analgesia is selected. A prostatectomy-specific PROSPECT review recommends transversus abdominis plane block as the first-choice regional technique for laparoscopic or robotic prostatectomy.<sup>[[20]](#ref20)</sup>

| Needle | Tip | PDPH risk |
|---|---|---|
| **Quincke** | Cutting, beveled | Higher |
| **Greene** | Cutting | Higher |
| **Whitacre** | Pencil-point (atraumatic) | **Lower** |
| **Sprotte** | Pencil-point (atraumatic) | **Lower** |
| **Atraucan** | Atraumatic | Lower |

Network meta-analysis: the 26-gauge atraumatic needle has the lowest combined probability of post-dural-puncture headache and procedural failure.<sup>[[9]](#ref9)</sup>

### Biopsy Needles (Percutaneous Urologic)

- **Fine needle aspiration:** 21–25 G; primarily for cytology, but adequate cell blocks or other validated cytology preparations may also support molecular testing.<sup>[[19]](#ref19)</sup>
- **Core needle biopsy:** 18–20 G automatic firing with variable throw length; histologic architecture, sufficient for molecular work.<sup>[[7]](#ref7)[[8]](#ref8)</sup>
- Choose gauge, trajectory and sampling device for the target and required specimen; complication risk depends on the organ, route and patient. The skin-suturing bench study in reference 10 does not provide a percutaneous-biopsy complication rate.

## Needle–Tissue Interaction Physics

Three phases during penetration:<sup>[[16]](#ref16)</sup>

1. **Deformation.** Tissue stretches before puncture.
2. **Insertion.** Needle advances through tissue.
3. **Extraction.** Needle withdrawn, tissue relaxes.

In Jiang's bench experiments, these variables had a large influence on insertion force:

- **Needle diameter** (0.7–3.2 mm); larger diameters require more force.
- **Tip shape** (blunt, diamond, conical, beveled); blunter tips require more force.
- **Bevel angle** (10–85°); smaller angles require less force.
- **Insertion velocity** (0.5–10 mm/s), drive mode (robot-assisted or hand-held) and interrupted versus continuous insertion.
- **Tissue type.** Skin, muscle, fat, liver capsule and vessel produce different force curves.

These bench experiments show how needle geometry and tissue affect insertion force; they do not measure urethral trauma from curved suture needles.<sup>[[16]](#ref16)</sup> In operations where a blunt fascial needle can pass the tissue, randomized trials support its use to reduce staff glove perforation and needlestick exposure.<sup>[[4]](#ref4)</sup>
