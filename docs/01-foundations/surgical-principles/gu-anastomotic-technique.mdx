# GU Anastomotic Technique — Evidence Review

Urologic reconstruction lives and dies by its anastomoses. This article is a **cross-cutting evidence review** of how we sew at each reconstructive site — running vs interrupted for the vesicourethral anastomosis, barbed vs conventional, Rocco posterior reconstruction, Bricker vs Wallace ureteroileal, non-transecting vs transecting EPA, and the principles that carry across ureteral reimplantation. It complements the adjacent reference articles: [Sutures](/docs/foundations/surgical-principles/sutures) (materials and selection), [Needles](/docs/foundations/surgical-principles/needles) (needle anatomy), [Bowel anastomosis](/docs/foundations/surgical-principles/bowel-anastomosis) (bowel-specific technique), and the named techniques in [Surgical skills](/docs/foundations/surgical-skills) (per-stitch detail). The focus here is **what the RCTs and meta-analyses show about choosing technique at each anastomotic site** — not the step-by-step of any one stitch.

---

## 1. The Vesicourethral Anastomosis (VUA)

The most technically demanding step of radical prostatectomy and the best-studied anastomosis in urology. The relevant technique debates are three:

- Running vs interrupted suturing
- Barbed vs conventional suture
- Posterior (Rocco) reconstruction

### Running vs interrupted — conflicting evidence, approach-dependent

| Study | Design | n | Comparison | Finding |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Can 2025** | RCT (open RRP) | 70 | Running vs interrupted | Running: **faster (21 vs 33 min; p = 0.001)**, less leakage (9% vs 23%). Interrupted: **better early Qmax at 1 mo** (23.3 vs 17.2 mL/s; p = 0.003). **Continence similar.**<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup> |
| **Matsuyama 2015** | RCT (open RRP) | 60 | Running vs interrupted | Running: earlier catheter removal (HR 5.23; p = 0.003), **higher 1-mo pad-free rate** (20.7% vs 3.3%; p = 0.046), **0% vs 10% stricture rate**<sup>[[2]](#ref2)</sup> |
| **Köhler 2020** | Retrospective (open RRP) | 211 | Running vs interrupted | Interrupted: **dramatically better early continence** (7.7% vs 42.2% incontinence; p &lt; 0.001); shorter catheterization (4 vs 5 d); similar extravasation<sup>[[3]](#ref3)</sup> |
| **Poulakis 2006** | Prospective (endoscopic extraperitoneal RP) | 250 | Running vs interrupted | Similar continence (91.5% vs 93% at 6 mo); similar extravasation and stricture; **running faster (16 vs 24 min; p &lt; 0.001)** and easier (difficulty 1 vs 3)<sup>[[4]](#ref4)</sup> |

**Interpretation.** Running is **faster and technically simpler**. The "purse-string" concern — that continuous tightening over a spherical anastomosis narrows the ring — motivates the interrupted camp, and may explain Köhler's strong early-continence signal in open RRP. **By 3–6 months continence equalizes across series.** The approach usually decides the technique: **running dominates in robotic and laparoscopic surgery** because intracorporeal knot-tying is the rate-limiting step of interrupted work.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[2]](#ref2)[[3]](#ref3)[[4]](#ref4)</sup>

### Barbed (V-Loc) vs conventional — robotic standard

**Barbed suture (V-Loc) has become the default for VUA during robotic prostatectomy.** Two meta-analyses and a pivotal RCT support the signal:

- **Li 2015 meta** (9 studies, n = 786) — ↓ anastomosis time (WMD −3.98 min; p = 0.0001), ↓ total operative time (WMD −10.06 min; p = 0.0003), ↓ posterior reconstruction time (WMD −0.93 min; p = 0.002); **no difference** in leakage, continence at any time point, or catheterization duration.<sup>[[5]](#ref5)</sup>
- **Bai 2015 meta** (10 studies, n = 747) — shorter operative time (MD −10.54 min; p &lt; 0.001); confirms the signal.<sup>[[6]](#ref6)</sup>
- **Zorn 2012 RCT** (n = 70) — reconstruction time 13.1 vs 20.8 min; p &lt; 0.001.<sup>[[7]](#ref7)</sup>

### The barbed-suture learning-curve pitfall

:::warning[Barbed suture — the overtightening trap]
The self-anchoring property that eliminates knot-tying also eliminates the **self-correcting slack** of a conventional running line. **Williams 2010 RCT** (n = 81) found barbed suture associated with **more frequent cystogram extravasation (20% vs 2.8%; p = 0.019)** and longer catheterization (11.1 vs 8.3 d). After the authors modified technique to deliberately avoid overtightening, the leak rate dropped to 6.3%.<sup>[[8]](#ref8)</sup> The barbs prevent the suture from loosening on its own — any overtightening stays in the anastomotic ring until the patient's tissue remodels around it.
:::

**Practical rule:** run the line loose. If it looks snug, it is too tight.

### Posterior (Rocco) reconstruction

Posterior musculofascial plate reconstruction is one of the most studied technical modifications in prostatectomy. The evidence shows a **modest benefit for early continence only** — no long-term effect and no complications.

| Source | Finding |
|---|---|
| **Rosenberg 2021 Cochrane** (8 RCTs, n = 963) | **1-week continence:** may improve (RR 1.25; 95% CI 0.90–1.73; low certainty) — ~84 more men per 1,000 continent, but CI includes no effect. **3-month continence:** little to no effect (RR 0.98; 95% CI 0.84–1.14). **Serious AEs** probably no difference. PSM likely similar.<sup>[[9]](#ref9)[[10]](#ref10)</sup> |
| **Grasso 2016 meta** (21 studies) | Continence improved at **3–7 d, 30 d, and 90 d** post-catheter removal with significantly lower anastomotic leakage. **No benefit at 180 d or beyond.**<sup>[[11]](#ref11)</sup> |
| **Cui 2017 meta** | **Posterior reconstruction alone** provided the most consistent benefit. Anterior suspension alone showed no benefit. Combined anterior + posterior had limited additional benefit.<sup>[[12]](#ref12)</sup> |
| **Student 2017 (ARVUS RCT)** | n = 66; levator-incorporating ARVUS technique: **86.7% vs 61.3% continence at 12 mo** (p = 0.04) — the outlier positive trial; needs external validation.<sup>[[13]](#ref13)</sup> |

**Bottom line.** Rocco reconstruction is a **low-risk modification** that may accelerate early continence recovery (first 1–3 months) without affecting long-term outcomes. Adds minimal operative time, no reported complications. Most high-volume robotic surgeons include some form of it as standard practice.<sup>[[9]](#ref9)[[11]](#ref11)[[14]](#ref14)</sup>

## 2. Ureteroileal Anastomosis — Bricker vs Wallace

Two dominant techniques for ureteral anastomosis to the ileal conduit or neobladder: **Bricker (separate, end-to-side)** and **Wallace (conjoined, end-to-end)**. The evidence is nuanced — per-patient stricture rates are similar, but there is a **critical difference in bilateral stricture risk** because Wallace converts two independent renal units into a single anastomotic site.

| Study | Design | n | Bricker stricture | Wallace stricture | Insight |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Al-Nader 2024** (multi-institutional, propensity-matched) | 418 (209 each) | 12% patients | 14.4% patients (p = 0.56) | **Bilateral stricture: 1 Bricker vs 15 Wallace** (p = 0.00); significantly more affected renal units with Wallace (10.7% vs 6.2%)<sup>[[15]](#ref15)</sup> |
| **Krafft 2022** (retrospective) | 135 | 5.9% renal units | 16.6% renal units (p &lt; 0.001) | Bilateral stricture 69.2% in Wallace (p &lt; 0.001); prior chemo and Clavien ≥ III predict stricture<sup>[[16]](#ref16)</sup> |
| **Kouba 2007** (single-surgeon) | 186 | 3.7% ureters | **0% ureters** (p = 0.015) | Wallace had zero strictures; Bricker group had higher BMI<sup>[[17]](#ref17)</sup> |
| **Evangelidis 2006** (retrospective) | 198 | 1.85% | 4.46% (p = 0.21) | No significant difference<sup>[[18]](#ref18)</sup> |
| **Carreno 2025** (robotic) | 96 | 13.8% chronic stricture | 0% (p = 0.055) | Trend favoring Wallace in robotic setting<sup>[[19]](#ref19)</sup> |
| **Ren 2025** (single-surgeon, BMI-matched) | 461 | 1.1% hydronephrosis | 0% (p = 0.148) | Wallace: **shorter OR time** (281 vs 302 min; p &lt; 0.001) and LOS<sup>[[20]](#ref20)</sup> |

### The critical insight

**Per-patient stricture rates are comparable**, but the **Wallace technique shares a single anastomotic site between both kidneys**. A stricture at the conjoined plate affects **both renal units simultaneously**. In the Al-Nader multi-institutional analysis this converted to **significantly more affected renal units (10.7% vs 6.2%; p = 0.00)** and a much higher rate of bilateral obstruction.<sup>[[15]](#ref15)[[16]](#ref16)</sup>

**Independent predictors of ureteroenteric stricture:** prior pelvic radiotherapy, T4 disease, node-positive disease, prior chemotherapy, and 90-day Clavien ≥ III complications.<sup>[[15]](#ref15)[[16]](#ref16)</sup>

### Suturing principles for ureteroileal anastomosis (common to both)

- **Spatulate the ureter** to increase the anastomotic circumference and reduce stricture risk.<sup>[[21]](#ref21)[[22]](#ref22)</sup>
- **Tension-free, watertight, stented** — the fundamental triad of ureteral reconstruction applies regardless of technique.<sup>[[22]](#ref22)</sup>
- **Absorbable sutures only.** Non-absorbable sutures in the urinary tract are a predictable nidus for stone formation.<sup>[[23]](#ref23)</sup>
- Interrupted **4-0 or 5-0 absorbable** sutures are standard for both Bricker and Wallace; continuous lock-stitch techniques have been described with acceptable results.<sup>[[24]](#ref24)[[25]](#ref25)</sup>

## 3. Urethroplasty — Technique Evidence

The AUA Urethral Stricture Disease Guidelines (2023) and the Jasionowska 2022 systematic review form the anchor evidence base:<sup>[[26]](#ref26)[[27]](#ref27)</sup>

| Technique | Typical indication | Success rate | Evidence |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Excision and primary anastomosis (EPA)** — transecting | Short bulbar stricture (&lt; 2 cm) | **89.7%** (IQR 7.0) | Highest single-technique success in the Jasionowska SR<sup>[[26]](#ref26)</sup> |
| **Non-transecting (vessel-sparing) EPA** | Short bulbar stricture | **90–98%** | Comparable to traditional EPA; preserves bulbar arteries — potential erectile-function advantage<sup>[[28]](#ref28)</sup> |
| **Dorsal onlay buccal mucosa** | Bulbar / pan-urethral strictures ≥ 2 cm | **86.6%** | Best-supported substitution technique; largest evidence base<sup>[[26]](#ref26)</sup> |
| **Endoscopic treatment** (DVIU / dilation) | Very short, simple strictures only | ~20% (strictures &gt; 4 cm); ~40% overall | **Markedly inferior** to BMG urethroplasty — AUA recommends urethroplasty as initial treatment for bulbar strictures ≥ 2 cm<sup>[[27]](#ref27)</sup> |

**AUA 2023 key recommendations:**<sup>[[27]](#ref27)</sup>

- **Oral mucosa (buccal or lingual)** = first-choice graft material (Expert Opinion)
- **Buccal ≈ lingual mucosa** in outcomes (Strong Recommendation; Grade A) — 12-study meta RR 1.03; 95% CI 0.96–1.10
- **Urethroplasty > endoscopic treatment** for strictures ≥ 2 cm bulbar — the success-rate gap is decisive

For the graft-harvest technique details, see [Buccal mucosa](/docs/foundations/surgical-principles/grafts/buccal-mucosa) and the [Grafts hub](/docs/foundations/surgical-principles/grafts-gu-reconstruction).

## 4. Ureteral Reimplantation — Shared Principles

Regardless of technique — **Cohen cross-trigonal**, **Lich-Gregoir extravesical**, or **Politano-Leadbetter** — the suturing principles are:<sup>[[21]](#ref21)[[22]](#ref22)[[29]](#ref29)</sup>

| Principle | Detail |
|---|---|
| **Spatulate the ureter** | Increases anastomotic diameter → reduced stricture risk |
| **Mucosa-to-mucosa approximation** | Urothelial continuity → reduced fibrosis |
| **Tension-free anastomosis** | Psoas hitch or Boari flap when length deficit requires |
| **Interrupted absorbable sutures** | 4-0 or 5-0 polyglactin or poliglecaprone — standard for reimplant |
| **Stented** | Double-J ureteral stent 4–6 weeks to maintain patency during healing |
| **Top-down suturing** (robotic extravesical) | Starting detrusor reapproximation at the superior aspect elevates the ureter out of harm's way → less instrument contact and trauma<sup>[[30]](#ref30)</sup> |

**Reported success across techniques: 91–98%.**<sup>[[21]](#ref21)[[29]](#ref29)</sup>

## 5. Suture Material in the Urinary Tract — the Most Urologic Fact

The single most important materials fact for the reconstructive urologist: **sutures exposed to urine serve as a nidus for stone formation.** Non-absorbable sutures predictably promote urolithiasis. **Absorbable sutures are mandatory** for any closure in contact with the urinary stream.<sup>[[23]](#ref23)</sup>

### The PDS-in-urine surprise

A series of 1980s and 1990s in-vitro and animal studies produced a finding most surgeons do not know:

- **el-Mahrouky 1987** — **PDS lost all breaking strength after only 3 days in sterile urine.** Polyglycolic acid (PGA) retained **36% at 10 days**; **chromic catgut maintained nearly full strength.** In *Proteus*-infected urine, **both PDS and PGA lost all strength within 1 day**, while chromic catgut lost only 5.8% at 10 days.<sup>[[31]](#ref31)</sup>
- **Cohen 1987** and **Stewart 1990** — animal models generally favored PDS over chromic for most bladder-closure scenarios, but not all.<sup>[[32]](#ref32)[[33]](#ref33)</sup>
- **Wainstein 1997 pyeloplasty model** — polyglactin (Vicryl) produced the **least inflammation** of the tested materials.<sup>[[34]](#ref34)</sup>

**Practical implications:**

| Scenario | Implication |
|---|---|
| **Bladder closure in sterile urine** | Polyglactin (Vicryl) is the workhorse — 21-day strength retention matches bladder healing<sup>[[23]](#ref23)[[33]](#ref33)</sup> |
| **Standard VUA** | Poliglecaprone (Monocryl) or barbed poliglecaprone / polyglyconate — smooth passage, minimal tissue drag |
| **PDS in the urinary stream** | **Caution** — rapid strength loss in urine. Use for fascial closure rather than intravesical stitches |
| **Closure in known Proteus-infected urine** | **Chromic catgut** paradoxically outperforms synthetic absorbables in strength retention, despite greater inflammatory response<sup>[[31]](#ref31)</sup> |
| **Any closure contacting urine** | **Absorbable only** — non-absorbable sutures cause stones<sup>[[23]](#ref23)</sup> |

See [Sutures](/docs/foundations/surgical-principles/sutures) for the full materials framework, barbed-suture section, and procedure-by-procedure selection table.

## Practical Pearls

- **For VUA during robotic prostatectomy**, **barbed suture (V-Loc) in a running fashion** is the current standard — reduces anastomosis time by ~ 4–10 min with equivalent continence and stricture rates. **Run the line loose** to avoid the overtightening extravasation pitfall.<sup>[[5]](#ref5)[[7]](#ref7)[[8]](#ref8)</sup>
- **Posterior musculofascial reconstruction (Rocco)** accelerates early continence recovery (first 1–3 months) but does not affect long-term continence. Minimal added time, no reported complications — reasonable default addition.<sup>[[9]](#ref9)[[11]](#ref11)</sup>
- **For ureteroileal anastomosis**, Bricker and Wallace have comparable per-patient stricture rates, but **Wallace carries a significantly higher risk of bilateral obstruction** because the conjoined plate shares a single anastomotic site. Discuss this with patients when the technique is elective.<sup>[[15]](#ref15)[[16]](#ref16)</sup>
- **Prior pelvic radiation, T4 disease, node-positive disease, prior chemo, and Clavien ≥ III complications** all independently predict ureteroenteric stricture.<sup>[[15]](#ref15)[[16]](#ref16)</sup>
- **Absorbable sutures are mandatory** for any closure in contact with urine. PDS should be used with caution given its **rapid strength loss in urine** (loses all breaking strength in 3 days sterile, 1 day infected). In known *Proteus* infection, chromic catgut paradoxically outperforms synthetic absorbables.<sup>[[23]](#ref23)[[31]](#ref31)</sup>
- **For urethroplasty**, **dorsal onlay BMG** has the best evidence base among substitution techniques (86.6%). **Non-transecting EPA** has the highest success rates (90–98%) for short bulbar strictures with potential erectile-function preservation.<sup>[[26]](#ref26)[[28]](#ref28)</sup>
- **Ureteral reimplantation principles** — spatulate, mucosa-to-mucosa, tension-free, stented, interrupted absorbable — carry across Cohen, Lich-Gregoir, and Politano-Leadbetter. Success 91–98%.<sup>[[21]](#ref21)[[29]](#ref29)</sup>
