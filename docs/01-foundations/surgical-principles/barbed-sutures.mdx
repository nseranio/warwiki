# Barbed Sutures

Barbed sutures self-anchor along the suture line, reducing the need to maintain tension while tying knots. That feature may shorten a running closure, but it does **not** establish a preferred product, gauge, or closure pattern for every genitourinary reconstruction. Material, tissue, repair geometry, and the operator's ability to assess approximation remain procedure-specific. For the broader materials framework, see [Sutures](/docs/foundations/surgical-principles/sutures).

:::caution[Evidence boundary]
The best accessible comparative evidence in this review concerns vesicourethral anastomosis (VUA) and partial-nephrectomy renorrhaphy. Evidence for ureteral/UPJ reconstruction, bladder closure, urinary diversion, and cross-specialty complications is smaller, indirect, or not fully retrievable here. This is a reference article, not an operative protocol.
:::

## What the mechanism changes

| Potential advantage | Corresponding limitation |
| --- | --- |
| The line holds its position between passes and can reduce knot tying. | A pass cannot simply be slid back and forth like smooth monofilament; reassess tissue approximation before advancing. |
| It can shorten a running repair in selected comparative studies. | A shorter suture or ischemia time is a process outcome, not by itself proof of superior healing, patency, continence, or renal function. |
| It can distribute traction across a running line. | Product architecture, absorption profile, tissue quality, and the specific repair are not interchangeable. Do not extrapolate a bench or animal result into a universal gauge or product rule. |

## Vesicourethral anastomosis after minimally invasive prostatectomy

The accessible 2015 systematic review included three randomized and six observational studies (786 patients) comparing **unidirectional** barbed with conventional suture for VUA. It found shorter anastomosis time (mean difference −3.98 minutes) and total operative time (−10.06 minutes), but no statistically significant difference in leak, catheter duration, or continence at the reported follow-up intervals.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup> The authors describe the intended VUA as watertight, optimally apposed, and tension-free; that does **not** support deliberately leaving a gap between the edges.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup>

The pooled leak estimate was imprecise (7.08% vs 3.37%; OR 2.17, 95% CI 0.86–5.50), and the review notes variation in technique and limited long-term data. Therefore, barbed suture is an available option for an experienced VUA team—not a site-wide “standard of care” conclusion or a substitute for a validated anastomotic technique.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup>

### Practical safety framing

- Follow the procedure-specific technique, device instructions, and institutional practice.
- Aim for apposition without ischemic traction; if approximation is unsatisfactory, correct it rather than relying on a generic “run loose” or intentional-gap rule.
- Do not infer a continence, stricture, or leak advantage from reduced suturing time alone.

## Renorrhaphy during partial nephrectomy

In a 2018 systematic review/meta-analysis of eight **cohort** studies (431 patients; no eligible randomized trials), barbed suture was associated with lower warm-ischemia time (mean difference −6.55 minutes) and operative time (−11.29 minutes). Estimated blood loss and transfusion were not significantly different; the authors reported heterogeneous blood-loss data and no pooled renal-function conclusion.<sup>[[2]](#ref2)</sup> Confounding by tumour complexity, surgeon preference, and learning curve remains important.

A later single-centre randomized study assigned 50 selected T1 RAPN patients to double-needle barbed suture or Vicryl. It reported shorter mean warm-ischemia time (11.76 vs 14.56 minutes) and differences in short-term laboratory outcomes, but it was small, limited to selected T1 tumors, and explicitly called for larger multicentre trials. Its two operative-video supplements were not reviewed in this recovery pass.<sup>[[3]](#ref3)</sup>

**Interpretation:** barbed suture is a reasonable technique to evaluate within an established renorrhaphy workflow. Present evidence does not establish a universal product preference, clip strategy, gauge conversion, or renal-function benefit.

## Upper-tract and other urinary reconstruction

Published reports and reviews describe conflicting pyeloplasty experiences, including concerning small series, but the primary full texts needed to verify the prior page's product-specific failure rates and mechanism claims were not accessible in this review. The page therefore does not convert those reports into a blanket prohibition—or a product-selection rule—for UPJ, ureteral, or ureteroenteric repair.

For a small-calibre urinary anastomosis, the safer evidence-based posture is to use the established operation-specific method and material selected by the operating surgeon, avoid importing renorrhaphy or VUA performance data, and follow patients with the usual clinical and imaging surveillance. Evidence gaps should be discussed locally before adopting a new device or technique.

Animal and bench studies of bladder closure, exposed material in urine, stiffness, and tissue reaction can generate hypotheses. They cannot quantify human stone, migration, stricture, or closure-failure risk for a particular reconstruction. The original page's fixed gauge-upsize instruction and broad product hierarchy have therefore been removed.

## Abdominal exposure and suture tails

Case reports and a literature/MAUDE review have linked exposed barbed tails to small-bowel obstruction after abdominal or pelvic operations.<sup>[[4]](#ref4)</sup> The exact incidence and comparative risk are not established by that evidence. When a barbed line is used near mobile bowel, use the device's instructions for use and the operation-specific closure method, and avoid leaving unintended exposed suture material where it can contact bowel.

## Decision summary

| Setting | What the evidence can support | What it cannot support |
| --- | --- | --- |
| VUA | Modest time savings with no demonstrated pooled difference in the reported leak, catheter, or continence outcomes. | A universal standard-of-care designation, intentional gapped closure, or superior functional outcome. |
| Renorrhaphy | Time-related benefit is plausible; a small randomized study and older cohort synthesis support further comparative evaluation. | A universal device, clip, gauge, or long-term renal-function rule. |
| UPJ/ureter/ureteroenteric repair | Do not extrapolate VUA or renal-parenchymal data to a tubular urinary anastomosis. | A blanket ban, preferred brand, or quantitative failure claim without retrievable primary evidence. |
| Bladder/bowel-adjacent closure | Handle exposed material thoughtfully and follow the specific procedure/device guidance. | Human complication rates inferred from animal, bench, case-report, or surveillance data. |
