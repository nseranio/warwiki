# Principles of Genitourinary Reconstruction

Genitourinary reconstruction applies the foundational principles of plastic and reconstructive surgery to the unique demands of the urinary tract and external genitalia. Beyond the general framework — replace like with like, tension-free closure, optimize vascularity — GU reconstruction requires attention to principles specific to the urinary environment: watertight closure under continuous urine exposure, graft biology in a persistently wet mucosal field, and deliberate tissue rest before operative repair.

---

## Watertight Anastomosis

Creating a watertight anastomosis is essential during urinary tract reconstruction, and its importance cannot be overemphasized.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[2]](#ref2)[[3]](#ref3)</sup> Urinary tract anastomoses — whether performed in interrupted or running fashion based on the surgeon's preference — must ensure a circumferential **mucosa-to-mucosa approximation** to minimize the risk of urinary leakage. Care must be taken to avoid placing sutures with excessive force or too close together, as this produces ischemia at the anastomotic site — a principal cause of subsequent fistula or stricture formation.<sup>[[3]](#ref3)</sup>

A urethral catheter or ureteral stent is typically used to facilitate alignment and maintain the anastomosis during the early healing phase.

**Consequences of anastomotic failure:**<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[2]](#ref2)</sup>

- **Prolonged urothelial regeneration** — urinary leakage before epithelialization is complete delays primary re-epithelialization without altering the underlying regeneration pattern
- **Urinoma** — extravasated urine in the perisurgical space, predisposing to infection and fibrosis
- **Abscess formation**
- **Fistula** — the most serious complication; results from chronic urine extravasation and tissue necrosis
- **Obstruction** — from periureteral or periurethral fibrosis secondary to urine leakage

:::note Technical Principles
- **Interrupted vs. running suture:** Interrupted sutures allow individual tension control and adjustment for tissue discrepancy; running sutures are faster but less forgiving at points of tension mismatch
- **Suture caliber:** The smallest suture achieving adequate mucosal apposition should be used — excessive suture bulk increases foreign body reaction, tissue strangulation, and ischemia
- **Catheter/stent drainage:** Reduces intraluminal pressure during healing and provides a guide for anastomotic alignment; removal timing depends on anastomotic complexity and tissue quality
:::

## Graft Take: Imbibition and Inosculation

Understanding graft physiology is critical for the reconstructive urologist, as grafting — particularly with buccal mucosa — is central to urinary tract reconstruction. A graft is tissue transferred from a donor site to a recipient site **without its native blood supply**. Graft survival depends entirely on re-establishment of perfusion by two sequential processes:<sup>[[4]](#ref4)[[5]](#ref5)</sup>

### Imbibition (0–48 hours)

Passive diffusion of nutrients and metabolic byproducts between the graft and the recipient wound bed. The graft absorbs plasma exudate from the recipient site, maintaining graft cell viability until a vascular supply is re-established. During this phase, grafts appear edematous and pale; any fluid accumulation between graft and bed directly inhibits this passive exchange.

### Inosculation (48 hours – 1 week)

Active formation of new vascular connections: host capillaries grow into the graft substance (neovascularization), and residual graft vessels align with and connect to host vessels (direct capillary anastomosis). By day 5–7, blood flow through the graft is re-established and the graft appears pink and viable.

### Factors Affecting Graft Take

| Factor | Effect |
|---|---|
| **Recipient bed vascularity** | Primary determinant of take; avascular, irradiated, or ischemic beds have markedly impaired take |
| **Graft-bed apposition** | Dead space (hematoma, seroma, urinoma) between graft and bed physically prevents imbibition and inosculation — the most common cause of graft failure |
| **Immobilization** | Movement shears developing capillary connections; 48–72 hours of immobilization is standard practice |
| **Infection** | Bacterial colonization impairs inosculation and lowers tissue oxygen tension |
| **Graft thickness** | Thinner grafts (STSG, thin buccal mucosa) take more readily due to shorter diffusion distance during imbibition |
| **Graft defatting** | Removing excess submucosal/subcutaneous fat reduces diffusion distance and improves contact with the recipient bed |

:::note Omentum as Vascularized Graft Backing
In buccal mucosal graft (BMG) ureteroplasty, an omental flap serves a dual critical role: it provides a vascularized backing that drives graft neovascularization, and its porous structure permits drainage of any fluid accumulation from beneath the graft — directly preventing the dead space that impairs inosculation. Without vascularized backing, BMG ureteroplasty has inferior outcomes.<sup>[[5]](#ref5)</sup>
:::

## Spatulation

Spatulation refers to creating a longitudinal incision at the cut end of a tubular structure — ureter, urethra, or vessel — to widen and ovalize the anastomotic opening prior to anastomosis. It is a fundamental maneuver in urological reconstruction wherever tubular structures are joined end-to-end.

**Rationale:**
- **Increases anastomotic circumference**, reducing the risk of stricture from circular scar contraction
- **Converts circular to elliptical anastomosis**, distributing radial tension more evenly and eliminating the purse-string effect that narrows a circular anastomosis
- **Provides greater mucosal surface area** for precise, mucosa-to-mucosa suturing

**Technique in ureteral anastomosis:**
The ureter is spatulated on complementary sides — one limb spatulated anteriorly, one posteriorly, offset at 180° — creating a "fish-mouth" configuration at each end. The standard length of spatulation is 0.5–1.5 cm; wider spatulation (up to 2 cm) is used in areas of prior ischemia, irradiation, or fibrosis to ensure an adequate anastomotic caliber. Applied to ureteroureterostomy, ureteropyelostomy, and ureteroneocystostomy.

**Technique in urethral anastomosis:**
In excision and primary anastomosis (EPA) urethroplasty, the distal urethra is typically spatulated ventrally to create a broad mucosal cuff for anastomosis to the proximal urethral stump, reducing the risk of anastomotic stricture at the repair site.

## Urethral Rest

**Urethral rest** is the deliberate removal of all hardware traversing the urethra — including indwelling urethral catheters and intermittent self-catheterization (ISC) programs — combined with suprapubic urinary diversion where necessary, for a defined period prior to urethroplasty.

The principle recognizes that chronic instrumentation of a strictured or inflamed urethra perpetuates periurethral inflammation and fibrosis. Operating through active inflammation obscures the true extent and character of the stricture, may underestimate fibrotic involvement, and likely worsens healing. Urethral rest allows the urethra to transition from an actively inflamed to a mature, quiescent state — enabling accurate surgical assessment and a more favorable healing environment.

In a study of 128 anterior urethroplasty patients, 28 (21%) underwent preliminary urethral rest (median 3 months) following recent urologic procedures:<sup>[[6]](#ref6)</sup>

- **75%** of rested patients underwent successful excision-based (anastomotic) repair
- Stricture recurrence: **14%** in rested group vs. **10%** in non-rested group — comparable outcomes
- Urethral rest allowed clear identification of severely fibrotic segments that might otherwise have been underestimated at the time of surgery

**Clinical indications:**
- Recent urethral instrumentation (dilation, ISC, failed endoscopic treatment)
- Uncertainty about true stricture extent due to acute periurethral inflammation
- Inadequately recovered urethral tissue prior to complex reconstruction
- Optimization prior to redo urethroplasty

**Duration:** Typically **6–12 weeks**, achieved via suprapubic catheter (SPC) with complete removal of urethral hardware.

:::note
Urethral rest is a reconstructive tool, not merely a waiting period. Operating on mature, quiescent scar — rather than actively inflamed tissue — may convert a case requiring complex substitution urethroplasty into one amenable to the simpler excision-based repair.
:::

## Ureteral Rest

**Ureteral rest** is the removal of all hardware (double-J ureteral stent or percutaneous nephroureteral tube) from across a ureteral stricture for a minimum of **4 weeks** prior to ureteral reconstruction. The rationale mirrors urethral rest: indwelling hardware maintains a chronic inflammatory milieu at the stricture, obscures the true boundaries of the disease, and may perpetuate periureteral fibrosis — complicating the subsequent dissection and increasing the likelihood of requiring graft augmentation.

In a multi-institutional retrospective study (CORRUS collaborative) of 234 patients undergoing robotic reconstruction for proximal and middle ureteral strictures, 194 patients (82.9%) underwent ureteral rest while 40 did not:<sup>[[7]](#ref7)</sup>

| Outcome | Ureteral Rest (n=194) | No Rest (n=40) | p-value |
|---|---|---|---|
| Success rate | 90.7% | 77.5% | 0.027 |
| Estimated blood loss | 50 mL | 75 mL | &lt;0.001 |
| BMG graft utilization | 20.1% | 37.5% | 0.023 |

The reduction in BMG utilization with ureteral rest is particularly meaningful: when the ureter has been allowed to recover from chronic instrumentation, the stricture is shorter and less fibrotic — more often amenable to primary ureteroureterostomy rather than requiring graft augmentation. Rested strictures are also more clearly demarcated, facilitating precise excision.

**Duration:** Minimum **4 weeks**; typically achieved by placing a nephrostomy tube proximal to the stricture while removing any stent hardware crossing it.

:::note
Ureteral rest is not passive waiting — it is active preparation. Surgeons performing ureteral reconstruction should anticipate that rested strictures will have less periureteral fibrosis, bleed less intraoperatively, and require less complex reconstruction.
:::
