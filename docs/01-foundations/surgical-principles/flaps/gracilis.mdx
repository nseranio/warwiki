# Gracilis Flap

The gracilis provides vascularized medial-thigh tissue for perineal fistula interposition, support of a compromised urethral graft bed, and selected pelvic or genital defects. A proximally pedicled muscle flap usually reaches the perineum without microvascular anastomosis. Whether it supplies enough bulk, skin and reach depends on the defect and the patient's anatomy.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[2]](#ref2)</sup>

See [Flaps in GU Reconstruction](/docs/foundations/surgical-principles/flaps-gu-reconstruction) for flap selection and [The Leg & Thigh](/docs/foundations/anatomy-physiology/lower-extremity/leg-thigh) for regional anatomy.

## Anatomy That Determines the Operation

| Structure | Operative relevance |
|---|---|
| Muscle | Superficial medial-thigh adductor, with proximal attachment to the pubic body/inferior pubic ramus and distal insertion on the proximal medial tibia as part of the pes anserinus. Distinguish its distal tendon from sartorius and semitendinosus. |
| Dominant vascular pedicle | A proximal pedicle from the deep femoral/adductor arterial system; descriptions include medial circumflex femoral contributions. **A named artery or fixed surface distance does not replace identification of the actual pedicle.** |
| Accessory pedicles | Distal branches, commonly from the superficial femoral system. These may need division to mobilize a proximally based flap, after its dominant supply is identified and protected. |
| Venous drainage | Paired venae comitantes; proximal convergence may provide a larger vein when free transfer is planned. |
| Motor nerve | Branch of the obturator nerve, usually from its anterior division, entering near the dominant pedicle and branching longitudinally within the muscle. |

Anatomical studies use different landmarks and methods. The gracilis is a Mathes–Nahai type II muscle, with one dominant pedicle and several minor pedicles.

- **Macchi (CT angiography, 40 patients, mean age 63 years).** Mean muscle length was 41 ± 2.1 cm. The dominant pedicle entered approximately 10 ± 1 cm from the ischiopubic attachment, with a mean caliber of 2.5 ± 0.5 mm. It arose directly from the deep femoral artery in 45% and from the artery to the adductors in 46%, and it was larger when it arose directly from the deep femoral artery. The mean number of distal accessory pedicles was 1.8 (range 1–4), and the first accessory pedicle had a mean caliber of 2.0 mm.<sup>[[3]](#ref3)</sup>
- **Morris (14 cadavers).** The musculotendinous unit measured 44 ± 2 cm, including up to 6 ± 2 cm of tendon. The main pedicle entered 10 ± 1 cm from the pubic attachment (diameter 1.5–2.5 mm), and one to three small branches of the superficial femoral artery supplied the distal muscle. The obturator nerve entered with the major pedicle and ran within the muscle in two or three longitudinal branches parallel to the arterial branches; the authors conclude that this consistent anatomy suits segmental functional muscle transfer.<sup>[[4]](#ref4)</sup>
- **Magden (30 dissections).** The deep femoral artery was the dominant pedicle in 87% and the medial circumflex femoral artery in 13%. Distal pedicles from the superficial femoral artery were present in all specimens (double in 77%) and entered about 266 mm from the pubic tubercle.<sup>[[5]](#ref5)</sup>
- **Whetzel (cadaver study).** The pedicle was described as the medial femoral circumflex artery, with connections to fasciocutaneous perforators from the superficial femoral artery that supply the overlying skin.<sup>[[9]](#ref9)</sup>

Naming of the dominant pedicle therefore differs between studies. These are study averages, not a guaranteed usable flap length or interchangeable measurements from the pubic tubercle, groin crease and ischium.

### Match Nerve Handling to the Reconstruction

- **Static interposition or coverage:** decide whether to preserve or divide the motor branch as part of the selected technique. Denervation can cause atrophy; subsequent volume loss matters when filling a cavity.
- **Innervated pedicled transfer:** preserve the required motor connection in continuity when contraction is part of the intended function.
- **Distant free functional transfer:** plan vascular anastomoses and motor-nerve coaptation. This differs from simply rotating a pedicled muscle into the perineum.<sup>[[4]](#ref4)[[7]](#ref7)[[8]](#ref8)</sup>

## Pedicled Harvest and Transfer Principles

The exact dissection depends on muscle-only versus myocutaneous design, required reach and the recipient repair. The following is an operative planning framework; the dedicated procedure and flap design determine the details.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[2]](#ref2)</sup>

1. **Assess both donor and recipient sites.** Review medial-thigh operations, vascular disease, prior irradiation, tissue loss and available alternatives. Position and prepare the thigh so the tendon and perineum are accessible without excessive abduction or pressure injury. Selective vascular imaging can help when anatomy or previous surgery raises concern; routine CT angiography has not been established for every uncomplicated pedicled transfer.<sup>[[3]](#ref3)[[2]](#ref2)</sup>
2. **Identify the muscle and dominant pedicle directly.** Use the adductor longus and the medial-thigh anatomy to guide exposure. Preserve the fascia needed for any planned skin paddle and do not strip it indiscriminately.<sup>[[6]](#ref6)[[9]](#ref9)</sup>
3. **Mobilize only the tissue needed.** Distal tendon release and selective division of accessory vessels permit rotation while the dominant proximal supply remains intact. Tailor further proximal release or extended pedicle dissection to the required reach, with nerve handling matched to the functional plan.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[2]](#ref2)</sup>
4. **Create a generous transfer tunnel.** Avoid twisting, traction, compression or an acute turn of the pedicle. Check reach and perfusion with the leg in its expected postoperative position.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[2]](#ref2)</sup>
5. **Inset for the recipient problem.** For fistulas, independently repair the involved organs and place viable muscle between the closures. For urethral support, provide a vascular bed without constricting the lumen. For pelvic defects, fill the appropriate dead space and obtain skin closure without undue tension.<sup>[[10]](#ref10)[[11]](#ref11)[[2]](#ref2)</sup>
6. **Plan wound and drainage care around the repair.** Check donor and recipient wounds for hematoma, infection, ischemia and leakage. Drain removal, catheter duration and activity restrictions depend on output, healing, recipient anatomy and the reconstruction.<sup>[[12]](#ref12)[[13]](#ref13)[[2]](#ref2)</sup>

The [microsurgeon.org atlas](https://www.microsurgeon.org/gracilismuscle) is an additional anatomical resource; its free-flap harvest and recipient-specific postoperative instructions need adaptation to a pedicled GU reconstruction.

### Positioning and Marking

- Position the patient supine with the leg prepped to the groin, the thigh abducted and externally rotated and the knee slightly flexed (frog-leg position).
- Palpate the adductor longus with the thigh abducted. The gracilis lies about two to three finger breadths posterior to it, which is the surface guide commonly taught for the incision. Mark the axis from the pubic attachment toward the medial tibial condyle.
- The dominant pedicle enters about 10 cm from the ischiopubic attachment, so the incision must extend distal to this point.<sup>[[3]](#ref3)[[4]](#ref4)</sup>
- A separate distal incision near the medial tibial condyle can be used to divide the tendon when full muscle length is needed. Identify the gracilis tendon at its insertion and distinguish it from sartorius and semitendinosus.

### Standard Open Harvest Sequence

The following is the commonly described sequence. Individual surgeons vary the details.

1. Incise skin, subcutaneous tissue and the muscle fascia to expose the gracilis belly. Elevate the fascia off the muscle anteriorly, and keep the fascia in continuity with any planned skin paddle.<sup>[[9]](#ref9)</sup>
2. Identify the septum between the gracilis and the adductor longus and enter the fascial cleft. The dominant pedicle lies on the medial aspect of the gracilis in the cleft between the adductor longus and the adductor magnus.<sup>[[6]](#ref6)</sup>
3. Trace the pedicle proximally. Dividing the small branches to the adductor muscles and passing the gracilis deep to the adductor longus gives more reach (extended dissection).<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[6]](#ref6)</sup>
4. Free the muscle from its soft-tissue attachments except the pedicle. Divide the distal accessory pedicles after the dominant supply is identified.
5. Divide the distal tendon at its insertion or through a distal counter-incision.
6. Preserve or divide the obturator nerve branch according to the reconstruction plan (see nerve handling above).
7. Tunnel the muscle to the perineum through a generous subcutaneous tunnel or skin bridge, checking the pedicle for twisting and tension.
8. Close the thigh in layers over a closed-suction drain.

### Postoperative Care

- The thigh drain is commonly left in place until output is low, often 24 to 72 hours or less than 30 mL per day, and practice varies.
- Ambulation is encouraged as the recipient site allows. The donor site itself does not usually require restricted weight-bearing, and Ducic's extended-dissection series reported no restriction of ambulation or thigh abduction.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup>

### Useful Variants

| Variant | Role and limitation |
|---|---|
| Extended pedicle dissection | Ducic's series applied extended dissection to 19 pedicled perineal/groin reconstructions in high-risk patients; all reconstructions were successful, with one late donor-site infection. Passing the muscle beneath adductor longus can improve reach; this requires deliberate protection of the vascular supply. The authors report a greater arc of rotation and no restriction of ambulation or thigh abduction.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup> |
| Bilateral gracilis | Adds tissue when one muscle is insufficient and preserves the abdominal donor site. A 50-patient pelvic-oncology series described an interwoven configuration; complete wound healing was reported in **42/49 surviving patients** at follow-up. Donor-site and perineal complication rates were 14% and 22%, and flap integrity was maintained on follow-up imaging.<sup>[[14]](#ref14)</sup> |
| Myofasciocutaneous flap | Includes the associated fascia and skin. Whetzel–Lechtman's anatomical work and 12-patient/18-flap experience support preserving the fascial vascular network. The 18 flaps in previously irradiated patients were up to 8 cm wide and 30 cm long, with no partial or complete flap necrosis and minor complications in 6 of 12 patients (mean follow-up 12.1 months). Muscle survival does not guarantee survival of an extended skin paddle.<sup>[[9]](#ref9)</sup> |
| Bilobed design | Adds an adjacent soft-tissue arm. The original six-reconstruction report establishes a technical option, not comparative superiority or an unrestricted skin territory.<sup>[[15]](#ref15)</sup> |
| Robotic harvest | Described for free gracilis transfer. Published early technical experience does not establish lower donor morbidity, lower cost or a preferred approach for routine pedicled perineal transfer.<sup>[[16]](#ref16)</sup> |

## Fistula Interposition

Gracilis is an established option for complex or recurrent rectourethral and selected rectovaginal fistulas, especially when the local tissues are scarred or irradiated. The exposure, diversion strategy and choice of interposition depend on fistula level, associated stenosis, tissue quality, prior repairs and bowel and bladder function.<sup>[[17]](#ref17)[[10]](#ref10)[[18]](#ref18)</sup>

### What the Outcome Numbers Mean

The 2023 Garoufalia systematic review included 25 studies and 658 patients with mixed complex perineal fistulas. Its pooled 79.4% healing rate included additional procedures, with substantial heterogeneity. In the five studies reporting initial repair separately, pooled success after the index gracilis operation was 62.8%, increasing to 84.5% after further treatment; those additional procedures were often advancement flaps rather than another gracilis transfer. The review rated healing evidence very low certainty. These figures are not a rectourethral-only success rate or an individualized prediction.<sup>[[19]](#ref19)</sup>

| Series | Findings and counseling limits |
|---|---|
| Wexner 2008 | Among **36 men with rectourethral fistulas** in a 53-patient mixed cohort, initial success was 78% and final clinical healing 97% after additional procedures. The final rate includes further treatment.<sup>[[17]](#ref17)</sup> |
| Vanni 2010 | **74 repairs, 68 using gracilis**, with selective buccal onlay. At mean 20-month follow-up, single-stage closure was 100% in 35 nonirradiated cases and 84% in 39 radiation/ablation cases. In the latter group, 31% required permanent fecal diversion. Closure and reversal of diversion are separate outcomes.<sup>[[18]](#ref18)</sup> |
| Sbizzera 2022 | Closure in **20/21 patients** after prostate-cancer treatment. Of 18 postoperative questionnaire respondents, **11 reported significant urinary incontinence**. A closed fistula does not establish continence, and this postoperative rate alone cannot determine how much dysfunction the repair caused.<sup>[[10]](#ref10)</sup> |
| Higashino 2021 | 38 colorectal or urinary-tract-related fistulas repaired with vascularized tissue (gracilis in 19, gluteal fold flap in 13). Fistulas were cured in 31/38 (13/17 rectovaginal, 14/16 urorectal, p = 0.656), with a major leak in nine. Mixed flaps and fistula types.<sup>[[42]](#ref42)</sup> |
| Zmora 2006 | Nine patients (five rectovaginal, three rectourethral, one pouch-vaginal), all with fecal diversion. Seven healed after stoma closure at median 14 months.<sup>[[43]](#ref43)</sup> |
| Park 2022 | Retrospective comparison: recurrence 8% in 24 gracilis repairs versus 50% in 12 controls. Supports an association with less recurrence, but nonrandom treatment selection limits causal inference.<sup>[[20]](#ref20)</sup> |

For rectovaginal fistulas, Wexner's series reported healing in 75% of non-Crohn's fistulas and 33% of Crohn's-associated fistulas, with 76% of the women having had prior failed repairs.<sup>[[17]](#ref17)</sup> Paprottka reported eradication of all 12 complex urogenital fistulas (five VRAM and eight gracilis flaps, in patients with at least two prior failed repairs) at mean 6.3 years. Continence was restored in all seven gracilis patients assessed, whereas the three VRAM patients had slight incontinence; the series is small and selected.<sup>[[44]](#ref44)</sup>

A 2026 systematic review of post-prostatectomy rectourinary fistulas identified 455 cases across 34 studies, with heterogeneous surgical outcomes. Its proposed management flowchart is an author-developed framework; it does not establish one approach or flap as best for all patients.<sup>[[21]](#ref21)</sup>

**Early leakage requires reassessment of the repair and drainage.** Chen's small prospective series reported initial healing in 14/19 mixed fistulas and eventual healing in 18/19 after selected irrigation-suction salvage. There was no control group for salvage, and one patient required diversion with persistent fistula. This does not justify routine irrigation of every leak or delaying needed source control.<sup>[[13]](#ref13)</sup>

## Urethral Reconstruction

### Same-Stage Buccal Graft Support

The muscle can provide a vascularized bed beneath a ventral buccal mucosa graft in selected long strictures with compromised local tissue. Rozanski–Vanni's updated retrospective cohort included 30 patients, with patency in 23/30 at mean 32-month follow-up and seven patients requiring an artificial urinary sphincter postoperatively. The earlier 20-patient report and updated 30-patient experience should not be added as independent cohorts; “20-year experience” describes the treatment period, not 20-year follow-up for every patient.<sup>[[22]](#ref22)[[11]](#ref11)</sup>

Palmer's earlier report from the same institution (20 patients, mean stricture length 8.2 cm; 45% radiation etiology, 45% prior urethroplasty, 15% prior UroLume stent) had success in 16/20 (80%) at mean 40 months, with 5 patients (25%) needing an artificial urinary sphincter. Recurrence was defined as inability to pass a 16 Fr cystoscope, compared with 17 Fr in the updated cohort.<sup>[[22]](#ref22)</sup> A single 1996 case report described a 12-cm bulbar and penile urethral defect after Fournier's gangrene reconstructed with a proximally pedicled gracilis flap alone; it healed without stricture or leak, and urethral biopsies showed uroepithelial migration across the defect.<sup>[[45]](#ref45)</sup>

See [Ventral BMG with Gracilis Support](/docs/surgical-techniques/04a-urethral-reconstruction/vanni-zinman-ventral-bmg-gracilis) for the urethral operation.

### Staged Prelaminated Gracilis–Buccal Composite

This is a distinct salvage technique for a devastated bulbar urethra. In the original case, stage 1 created a dorsal buccal plate on the corporal bed and a temporary perineal outlet, while a second buccal graft was quilted onto distal gracilis. Eight weeks later, the vascularized muscle–mucosa composite was transferred to form the ventral plate.<sup>[[2]](#ref2)</sup>

The subsequent two-center series contained five patients, with no reported recurrence at mean 61-month follow-up and a mean 6.2-week interval between stages. This supports feasibility in selected salvage cases; it does not define a mandatory interval or establish comparative superiority. Discuss permanent perineal outlet and urinary diversion alongside reconstruction.<sup>[[23]](#ref23)</sup>

See [Laminated Gracilis Flap Urethroplasty](/docs/surgical-techniques/04a-urethral-reconstruction/laminated-gracilis-flap) for the staged concept and follow-up.

## Pelvic, Perineal and Genital Coverage

### After APR or Pelvic Exenteration

Gracilis may be useful when preserving the abdominal wall matters, abdominal donor sites are unavailable, or the defect can be filled with one or both thigh muscles. VRAM and other regional flaps remain options when greater bulk, skin or reach is required.<sup>[[24]](#ref24)[[14]](#ref14)[[1]](#ref1)</sup>

Selected perineal reconstruction series:

| Series | Design | Findings |
|---|---|---|
| Chong 2015 | 16 consecutive APRs (10 with pelvic exenteration), all after neoadjuvant chemoradiation; 6 bilateral flaps | One major perineal wound complication, from a pelvic anastomotic leak, managed with dressing changes. Three patients had skin separation greater than 5 mm. No operative debridement, revision, perineal hernia or gross dehiscence.<sup>[[41]](#ref41)</sup> |
| Singh 2016 | 40 patients, single surgeon, mean follow-up 2 years | Donor-site complications 12.5% (5) and recipient-site complications 40% (16); minor 25%, major 17.5%; no 30-day deaths.<sup>[[37]](#ref37)</sup> |
| Jenkins 2023 | 50 patients, bilateral gracilis reconstruction after extralevator APE | Complete perineal wound healing 86% (42/49); donor-site complications 14%, perineal 22%; 30-day mortality 2%.<sup>[[14]](#ref14)</sup> |
| Rinkinen 2024 | 101 patients, mean follow-up 75 months | Early major complications 7.9%, late major 12.9%, minor 32.7%; 91.1% had no donor-site complication.<sup>[[12]](#ref12)</sup> |

Single-surgeon and single-center series predominate, so the rates are not directly comparable.

The 2022 VRAM–gracilis proportional meta-analysis mainly pooled separate cohorts rather than direct comparisons. Its overall donor-complication analysis included only 56 gracilis and 240 VRAM patients, with inconsistent outcome definitions and important heterogeneity. It reported pooled overall donor-site complication proportions of 16.0% (95% CI 5.8–29.5%) for gracilis and 57.6% (95% CI 38.7–75.4%) for VRAM, with wide intervals and no significant difference in minor or major recipient-site complications or in flap necrosis. Some wound-dehiscence estimates favored VRAM. These data do not establish a universal risk ranking or equivalence. Select the flap for the defect, donor anatomy, planned stomas and surgical experience.<sup>[[24]](#ref24)</sup>

For persistent perineal wounds after proctectomy in IBD, the 2026 Pelly systematic review found 25 publications, no randomized trials and very low certainty for muscle-flap outcomes. It could not pool healing rates meaningfully. Define the sinus, adjacent bowel involvement and deep infection before selecting local repair, gracilis, a larger flap or combined abdominal and perineal treatment. An attractive percentage from a small series is not a validated treatment algorithm.<sup>[[25]](#ref25)</sup>

### Vulvovaginal and Penoscrotal Defects

Myocutaneous gracilis can replace selected vulvar or vaginal tissue, including neovaginal reconstruction after exenteration. Historical experience includes substantial skin necrosis, wound breakdown and prolapse. Goals, required lining, available tissue, hair-bearing skin and alternative flaps guide selection.<sup>[[26]](#ref26)[[27]](#ref27)[[28]](#ref28)</sup>

- **Copeland 1989** compared 107 women with gracilis neovaginal reconstruction at total pelvic exenteration with 44 without reconstruction. Reconstruction did not increase operating time, blood loss or length of stay. Neovaginal prolapse occurred in 65% before 1980 (25% severe) and fell to 16% (6% severe) after modifications: smaller flaps, anchoring the neovagina to the levator and retropubic fascia, and ligating the neurovascular pedicle when needed for mobilization. Two-thirds of patients were free of wound breakdown or necrosis after the modifications, and severe necrosis fell from 24% to 13%.<sup>[[28]](#ref28)</sup>
- **Short gracilis myocutaneous flaps** are based on the muscle's pubic origin and have greater mobility than the classic flap. Chen's 16 myocutaneous and 1 muscular flaps for vulvoperineal, inguinal, perineal and ischial defects all had surviving muscle, with partial necrosis of the distal third of the skin in six patients and superficial infection in seven.<sup>[[26]](#ref26)</sup> Burke's 18 single flaps in 17 women had skin necrosis in three and minor wound separation or edge necrosis in five, with a mean hospital stay of 18.4 days.<sup>[[27]](#ref27)</sup>

After Fournier's gangrene, muscle may fill a residual deep cavity, while skin grafts or local or perforator flaps provide surface coverage as needed. Hsu reported eight patients with Fournier's gangrene (defects 12 × 7 to 30 × 15 cm) treated with a unilateral gracilis myofasciocutaneous advancement flap, with one hematoma and one donor-thigh abscess. Lee reported seven patients (six Fournier's gangrene, one extramammary Paget's disease) treated with gracilis combined with an internal pudendal artery perforator flap, with all flaps surviving. These are reconstructive options after adequate debridement and infection control, not substitutes for those steps.<sup>[[29]](#ref29)[[30]](#ref30)</sup> In a retrospective comparison, filling the perianal dead space of Fournier's gangrene with gracilis was associated with a shorter interval from surgery to discharge (5.1 versus 12.7 days, p = 0.022) and a shorter hospital stay (23.5 versus 31 days) than soft-tissue reconstruction alone.<sup>[[46]](#ref46)</sup> The short gracilis flap, based on the muscle origin at the pubis, was described in two Fournier's gangrene patients for scrotal reconstruction, with greater mobility, no bulky pedicle zone and later atrophy.<sup>[[47]](#ref47)</sup>

## Graciloplasty for Continence: Historical and Specialized Uses

Urinary or fecal graciloplasty seeks a functional outlet, a different objective from static fistula interposition. Its outcomes should not be used to predict success of ordinary gracilis coverage.

| Application | Evidence boundary |
|---|---|
| Adynamic urinary wrap | Guo's 24-patient series (after prostatectomy, TURP or posterior urethroplasty) reported 18 cures, 4 improved and 2 failures at mean 31.5 months, with higher postoperative maximum urethral pressure, but five of six patients with severe incontinence did not achieve a good result. The uncontrolled cohort does not establish equivalence to standard continence surgery.<sup>[[31]](#ref31)</sup> Chancellor's 1997 pilot of three men after prostatectomy and radiotherapy reported improved continence at 6 to 24 months, with mean Valsalva leak point pressure rising from 26.3 to 83.0 cm H2O.<sup>[[8]](#ref8)</sup> |
| Electrically stimulated urinary wrap | Janknegt's 1995 pilot included seven patients: three continent, one partially continent and three treatment failures. This remains a small historical feasibility experience.<sup>[[32]](#ref32)</sup> |
| Free-flap urinary neosphincter | Anatomical and canine experiments demonstrated feasibility. Animal outlet-pressure measurements are not human continence outcomes.<sup>[[33]](#ref33)[[34]](#ref34)</sup> |
| Dynamic fecal graciloplasty | Baeten reported continence in 38/52 patients. In the later multicenter study, 85/128 graciloplasty patients met a study-specific success endpoint, which was not uniformly complete continence; major wound complications were common. These historical results require specialist interpretation and do not make graciloplasty routine first-line treatment.<sup>[[35]](#ref35)[[36]](#ref36)</sup> |

## Morbidity and Follow-Up

Track flap viability, wound healing, fistula closure, urethral patency, continence and quality of life separately. Also record whether repeat procedures or ongoing diversion were needed.<sup>[[10]](#ref10)[[11]](#ref11)[[19]](#ref19)</sup>

The donor site can develop dehiscence, seroma, hematoma, infection, sensory symptoms or functional complaints. Preserving other thigh adductors often limits disability, but absence of persistent gait impairment or edema in a small series is not a guarantee. In a later 101-patient perineal-reconstruction cohort, eight patients had early major complications, 13 had late major complications and 33 had minor complications; the study extended earlier institutional experience rather than supplying an independent population to add to it.<sup>[[37]](#ref37)[[12]](#ref12)</sup>

In Singh's 40-patient series, obesity (OR 7.5) and active smoking (OR 9.3) were associated with minor complications (p = 0.01 for each), and neoadjuvant chemoradiation (OR 21.4, p = 0.04) with any complication.<sup>[[37]](#ref37)</sup>

Optimize smoking, nutrition, diabetes and other modifiable risks; factor prior chemoradiation and donor and recipient tissue quality into planning. Associations reported in a small cohort do not provide a validated individual risk score. Follow-up should address both wounds, the reconstructed organ and the patient's functional goals.<sup>[[37]](#ref37)[[12]](#ref12)[[10]](#ref10)</sup>

## Other Reconstructive Uses

Free gracilis is also used for limb coverage, breast reconstruction and functional reanimation. Transverse upper gracilis provides limited breast volume; functional transfers require a suitable motor source and rehabilitation. Facial-reanimation reviews found heterogeneous reporting and could not establish one optimal donor nerve; spontaneous smiling has been reported after masseteric innervation, but assessment methods and results vary. Results from these different operations should not be pooled into GU flap-survival or donor-risk estimates.<sup>[[38]](#ref38)[[39]](#ref39)[[7]](#ref7)[[40]](#ref40)</sup>

Selected non-GU experience for context: a series of 204 free gracilis transfers for lower-limb reconstruction found no significant difference in major complications between defects smaller and larger than 150 cm² (15.72% versus 10.91%, p = 0.506) when the muscle was enlarged by epimysial dissection;<sup>[[38]](#ref38)</sup> the distally pedicled gracilis salvaged the knee in 8 of 9 patients with complex proximal knee wounds;<sup>[[48]](#ref48)</sup> and for breast reconstruction, Schoeller's 111 patients with 154 transverse gracilis flaps had three complete flap losses and a concealed donor scar,<sup>[[49]](#ref49)</sup> and the fleur-de-lis design was used in 17 patients (31 flaps) with 100% flap success and donor-site dehiscence in 19.3%, reduced with a negative-pressure dressing.<sup>[[50]](#ref50)</sup>
