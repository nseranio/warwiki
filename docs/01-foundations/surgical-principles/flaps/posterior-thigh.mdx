# Posterior Thigh Flap (Gluteal-Thigh / Friedman-Reece)

The **posterior thigh flap** is a fasciocutaneous option for complex pelvic and perineal reconstruction. Its design may recruit the **descending branch of the inferior gluteal artery (IGA)**, **profunda femoris perforating arteries**, or both, depending on which pedicles are preserved; sensate harvest via the **posterior femoral cutaneous nerve (PFCN)** is a separate design consideration. It can avoid an abdominal flap and laparotomy.<sup>[[5]](#ref5)[[3]](#ref3)</sup> In Friedman and Reece's retrospective series, 26 of 27 flaps survived in 19 patients, while 53% of patients experienced early wound-healing complications. The series does not isolate whether complications were caused by patient factors, reconstruction or both.<sup>[[5]](#ref5)</sup>

For the genital-context summary see [Scrotal Reconstruction Techniques](/docs/surgical-techniques/04e-genital-reconstruction/scrotal-reconstruction); for related gluteal-fold and propeller variants see [IGAP / gluteal-fold flap](/docs/foundations/surgical-principles/flaps/igap-gluteal-fold) and [propeller flap](/docs/foundations/surgical-principles/flaps/propeller-flap).

## History

| Year | Contribution |
|---|---|
| **1980 / 1985** | Hurwitz & Zwiebel described the **gluteal-thigh flap** as bilateral deepithelialized fasciocutaneous flaps transferred to chronic perineal wounds through the ischiorectal space on pliable musculofascial pedicles. The authors had studied the flap in over 40 patients; six consecutive patients with nonhealing perineal-pelvic sinuses were followed for 8 months to 5 years without wound recurrence.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup> |
| **1984** | Achauer applied the flap for immediate vaginal reconstruction after gynecologic resection (n = 7, 9 flaps); identified sitting discomfort and PFCN paresthesias and recommended pivoting distal to the ischium.<sup>[[2]](#ref2)</sup> |
| **1995** | Rubin, Whetzel, Stevenson — cadaveric vascular foundation (10 cadavers, 14 specimens): posterior thigh skin nourished primarily by **1st / 2nd profunda perforating arteries** with secondary supply from the **IGA descending branch**; flap survives even after prior IGA ligation.<sup>[[3]](#ref3)</sup> |
| **2002** | Windhofer (118 cadaveric gluteal regions) — IGA descending branch present in **91%**; PFCN in a **common connective-tissue sheath with the descending branch in 72%**, with nerve loops around the vessel in 29%.<sup>[[4]](#ref4)</sup> |
| **2010** | **Friedman & Reece** — landmark MD Anderson clinical series of n = 27 flaps in 19 patients for complex pelvic / perineal oncologic defects.<sup>[[5]](#ref5)</sup> |

## Vascular Anatomy

### 1. Inferior gluteal artery (IGA) descending branch

- Exits the pelvis through the greater sciatic foramen below piriformis.
- Descending branch ("arteria comitans nervi ischiadici") courses distally along the posterior thigh, typically in a common sheath with the PFCN (72% of cases).<sup>[[4]](#ref4)</sup>
- Present in 91% of cadaveric specimens; when absent, perfusion is taken over by medial / lateral femoral circumflex or PFA perforators.<sup>[[4]](#ref4)</sup>
- IGA cutaneous territory 177 ± 38 cm² (substantially larger than SGA at 69 ± 56 cm²).<sup>[[7]](#ref7)</sup>
- IGAP pedicle mean total length 13.36 cm (longer than SGAP at 9.80 cm).<sup>[[8]](#ref8)</sup>

### 2. Profunda femoris artery (PFA) perforators

- 1st, 2nd and 3rd perforating arteries provide fasciocutaneous branches via an extensive fascial plexus; Rubin's data position these as the primary posterior-thigh supply, with the IGA descending branch as secondary.<sup>[[3]](#ref3)</sup>
- Third perforator courses through the short head of biceps femoris: mean diameter 1.68 mm, mean pedicle length 10.28 cm.<sup>[[9]](#ref9)</sup>
- Dual IGA and PFA supply is the basis of the flap's survival even when one system is sacrificed.<sup>[[3]](#ref3)</sup>

### 3. Interconnecting fascial plexus

Pan and Taylor's lead-oxide angiosome study defined the source-vessel territories of the buttock and thigh, including the gluteal, profunda femoris and femoral circumflex systems that contribute to posterior thigh skin.<sup>[[11]](#ref11)</sup>

## Sensory Innervation — Posterior Femoral Cutaneous Nerve

- Origin: anterior and posterior divisions of S1–S3; exits below piriformis with the IGA.<sup>[[12]](#ref12)</sup>
- Courses superficial to and between medial and lateral hamstrings.
- Supplies posterior thigh skin and a variable posterior calf area.
- In a common sheath with the IGA descending branch in 72%; nerve loops around the vessel in 29%. This close relationship constrains arc of rotation when the nerve is preserved.<sup>[[4]](#ref4)</sup>
- PFCN perforating branches in the posterior thigh: mean diameter 1.34 ± 0.35 mm; mean length 8.82 ± 5.78 mm.<sup>[[13]](#ref13)</sup>

**Three options:**

- Include the PFCN for a sensate flap (important for perineal or scrotal reconstruction where protective sensation matters).
- Exclude the PFCN to maximize arc of rotation (Achauer's recommendation for subtotal vaginal reconstruction).<sup>[[2]](#ref2)</sup>
- **Sacrifice.** Posterior thigh hypoesthesia is the main donor sensory complaint; all six patients assessed after inferior gluteal musculocutaneous flaps for breast reconstruction had some hypoesthesia in the PFCN territory, even with the nerve preserved.<sup>[[14]](#ref14)</sup>

## The Friedman & Reece Landmark Series<sup>[[5]](#ref5)</sup>

| Variable | Value |
|---|---|
| Patients / flaps | 19 / 27 |
| Configurations | 11 unilateral + 8 bilateral (16 flaps) |
| Setting | Complex pelvic / perineal wounds after colorectal / gynecologic ablation; often after prior radiation; VRAM and gracilis unavailable or unsuitable |
| **Flap survival** | **96.3%** (26 / 27) |
| Total flap failure | 3.7% (1 / 27) |
| Ultimate primary wound healing | 100% (19 / 19) |
| Early wound-healing complications | **53%** (10 / 19) |
| Secondary procedures | 37% (7 / 19); only 1 patient required a secondary flap |

**Authors' conclusion.** An option when laparotomy is best avoided or both urinary and fecal diversion have already been performed. The retrospective series does not attribute the early wound complications solely to the underlying oncologic or radiation population.

---

## Indications

**Primary (Friedman / Reece emphasis):**

- Perineal defects after APR or pelvic exenteration for colorectal / gynecologic malignancy.
- Previously irradiated perineum.
- VRAM unavailable (prior abdominal surgery; need to preserve rectus for stoma; robotic / laparoscopic approach without laparotomy).
- Gracilis insufficient (large dead space or bulk requirement).
- Patients with both urinary and fecal diversion.

**Broader:** sacral pressure / radiation defects; recurrent ischial pressure ulcers; pelvic / perineal oncologic resection; vulvovaginal reconstruction; trochanteric defects; scrotal reconstruction as part of broader perineal coverage; buttock sarcoma defects.<sup>[[6]](#ref6)[[15]](#ref15)[[16]](#ref16)[[17]](#ref17)[[18]](#ref18)[[19]](#ref19)[[20]](#ref20)[[21]](#ref21)</sup>

## Surgical Technique

### Positioning

- Prone (preferred for isolated perineal / sacral defects).
- Lithotomy (when harvested simultaneously with an abdominal procedure).
- Repositioning is the main logistical constraint.

### Design

- Skin paddle over the posterior thigh, centered on the ischial-tuberosity-to-mid-popliteal-fossa axis.
- Width up to approximately 10 cm for primary donor closure; wider flaps generally need a donor-site skin graft.<sup>[[6]](#ref6)</sup> Ordenana's wide flaps averaged 12 cm.<sup>[[10]](#ref10)</sup>
- Configurations: V-Y advancement (Friedman / Reece default), rotation or transposition, propeller (90–180° on a skeletonized perforator), or fully islanded.<sup>[[10]](#ref10)[[22]](#ref22)</sup>

### Elevation

1. Incise the lateral border; dissect lateral to medial in the subfascial plane.
2. Identify the PFA perforators (1st, 2nd and 3rd) between hamstrings; identify the IGA descending branch proximally.
3. Select the dominant perforator(s); ligate non-dominant.
4. Identify the PFCN; include, exclude or sacrifice it based on the goal.
5. Complete the medial dissection; elevate the flap entirely in the subfascial plane.
6. Advance / rotate / tunnel through the ischiorectal fossa into the perineal defect.

### Hurwitz modification for deep pelvic dead space

Bilateral flaps deepithelialized and transferred through the ischiorectal space on musculofascial pedicles to obliterate pelvic dead space. Hurwitz and Zwiebel note that proper filling of the pelvic space requires adequate debridement, including release of a bottleneck narrowing at the sacral promontory when present.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup>

### Donor closure

Primary closure up to approximately 10 cm; wider flaps require STSG. Donor-site morbidity is generally minimal and temporary apart from posterior-thigh hypoesthesia.<sup>[[6]](#ref6)[[14]](#ref14)[[15]](#ref15)</sup>

## Complications

### Early wound-healing (53% in Friedman / Reece)<sup>[[5]](#ref5)</sup>

- Wound dehiscence is the most common complication and usually needs conservative management or minor revision.
- Surgical site infection is common in the irradiated perineum.
- Partial flap necrosis occurred in 22% of Saito's oncology cohort.<sup>[[15]](#ref15)</sup>
- **Total flap failure** is rare (3.7%).<sup>[[5]](#ref5)</sup>
- Seroma or hematoma is occasional.
- Perineal hernia has been reported after gluteal-flap perineal reconstruction; in Bowers' series of partial myocutaneous gluteal flaps after ELAPE, 5 of 49 patients had herniation, and the technique was modified in response.<sup>[[23]](#ref23)</sup>

### Donor site

- **Posterior thigh hypoesthesia** was the main donor-site morbidity after inferior gluteal musculocutaneous flaps in a small breast-reconstruction series (6 patients evaluated), even with the PFCN preserved.<sup>[[14]](#ref14)</sup>
- Sitting discomfort and paresthesias (Achauer's caveat) are mitigated by pivoting distal to the ischium.<sup>[[2]](#ref2)</sup>
- Otherwise minimal. Li reported no functional donor-site impairment in 9 free flaps, and Saito reported no donor-site morbidity in 9 oncology patients.<sup>[[9]](#ref9)[[15]](#ref15)</sup>
- Scar well-concealed on the posterior thigh.

---

## Advantages

1. **Avoids laparotomy.** Preserves the abdomen and rectus.<sup>[[5]](#ref5)[[24]](#ref24)</sup>
2. **No abdominal donor-site dissection** for the posterior-thigh harvest (unlike VRAM); this does not establish zero hernia risk from the patient's other operations.<sup>[[25]](#ref25)</sup>
3. **Dual blood supply.** IGA and PFA supply allows survival after prior IGA ligation.<sup>[[3]](#ref3)</sup>
4. **Large skin paddle**; bilateral harvest possible.<sup>[[5]](#ref5)</sup>
5. **Bulk for dead-space obliteration** when deepithelialized.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup>
6. **Sensate** when PFCN is included.<sup>[[4]](#ref4)</sup>
7. **Minimal functional donor morbidity.** Hamstrings are preserved.<sup>[[9]](#ref9)[[15]](#ref15)</sup>
8. **Suitable in irradiated patients.** The donor site lies outside the pelvic radiation field.
9. **Bilateral harvest** for very large defects.

---

## Limitations

1. **Repositioning** required if ablation was supine / lithotomy.
2. **High early wound-complication rate** (53% of patients in the Friedman series); its causes were not isolated.
3. **Posterior thigh hypoesthesia** is a possible donor-site issue; its frequency depends on the design and nerve handling and is not established as universal.
4. **Sitting discomfort** when pivoting at / proximal to the ischial tuberosity.
5. **Less familiar** to many reconstructive surgeons than VRAM / gracilis.
6. **IGA descending branch absent in about 9%.** PFA perforators are the safety net.
7. **Width-limited** for primary donor closure (approximately 10 cm).

---

## Modifications and Related Designs

- **Wide Posterior Gluteal-Thigh Propeller Flap (WPGTPF), Ordenana 2021.** Widens the flap from ischial tuberosity to greater trochanter, incorporating both IGA descending branch and PFA perforators; propeller rotation 90–180°. In the cadaver arm the IGA descending branch was dominant in 72.7% and the first or second PFA perforators in 22.7%; in the 10 patients, all flaps survived.<sup>[[10]](#ref10)</sup>
- **Gluteal Propeller Perforator Flaps (G-PPF), Chrelias 2023** (n = 20 APR). 100% defect coverage; 55% complication rate (30% delayed healing, 15% flap complications).<sup>[[22]](#ref22)</sup>
- **V-Y gluteal fasciocutaneous advancement (Myers 2019).** Bilateral V-Y for perineal closure after robotic APR (n = 31, 51 flaps); 100% flap survival; 42% minor wound complications.<sup>[[24]](#ref24)</sup>
- **U-shaped gluteal-thigh flap (Komuro 1993).** Extends the design along the IGA axis for large sacral defects within the approximately 10 cm width constraint.<sup>[[6]](#ref6)</sup>
- **Free posterior thigh perforator flap (Li 2017).** Based on the third PFA perforator; 12 × 7 to 20 × 13 cm; pedicle 10.28 cm on average, artery 1.68 mm; 7/9 complete survival.<sup>[[9]](#ref9)</sup>
- **Venous-supercharged freestyle posterior thigh flap (Fukunaga 2014).** Biceps-femoris freestyle perforator with venous supercharging when the IGA descending branch is sacrificed during sarcoma resection.<sup>[[26]](#ref26)</sup>

## Comparison with Alternative Perineal-Reconstruction Flaps

| Flap | Pedicle | Sensate | Avoids laparotomy | Dead-space fill | Donor-hernia risk | Survival |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Posterior thigh (Friedman / Reece)** | IGA + PFA perforators | Yes (PFCN) | **Yes** | Excellent (bilateral deepi) | None | **96.3%** (26/27 flaps) |
| [VRAM](/docs/foundations/surgical-principles/flaps/vram) | DIEA | No | **No** | Excellent | Yes | Varies by series |
| [Gracilis](/docs/foundations/surgical-principles/flaps/gracilis) | MCFA | Yes (obturator) | Yes | Limited | None | Varies by series |
| IGAM | IGA | Variable | Yes | Good | None | Varies by series |
| [ALT](/docs/foundations/surgical-principles/flaps/anterolateral-thigh) | LCFA descending | Variable (LFCN) | Yes | Good | None | Varies by series |
| Profunda artery perforator (PAP) | PFA | Yes (PFCN) | Yes | Moderate | None | Stable coverage in 15/15; 53% complications<sup>[[27]](#ref27)</sup> |

Galbraith 2023 (n = 122): VRAM, gluteal, and thigh flaps gave comparable outcomes for perineal closure after advanced pelvic oncologic resection; infection rates were lower in the flap group despite higher radiotherapy exposure.<sup>[[25]](#ref25)</sup> In a later retrospective comparison of 116 APR or exenteration patients, unilateral or bilateral inferior gluteal artery perforator flaps and VRAM flaps had similar minor (57% versus 49%, p = 0.43) and major (45% versus 36%, p = 0.35) complication rates.<sup>[[28]](#ref28)</sup>
