# Lotus Petal Flap

The **lotus petal flap (LPF)** is a fasciocutaneous flap based on perforators of the internal pudendal artery (IPA), harvested from the gluteal fold or perineal crease, and named for the cumulative bilateral flap designs that resemble the petals of a lotus flower.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[2]](#ref2)</sup> First described by Yii and Niranjan in 1996, it is widely used for vulvoperineal reconstruction. Reported advantages are reliable vascularity, a hidden donor scar, thin pliable tissue, preserved sensation and primary donor-site closure.<sup>[[3]](#ref3)[[4]](#ref4)</sup> Applications include vulvar cancer, ELAPE pelvic-floor reconstruction, scrotal reconstruction and neovagina creation.<sup>[[3]](#ref3)[[4]](#ref4)</sup>

This page is the foundations-level deep dive on the gluteal-fold IPA-perforator lotus petal. The closely related but distinct medial-thigh / posterior-labial-artery flap is covered separately on the [Singapore (Pudendal Thigh) Flap](/docs/foundations/surgical-principles/flaps/singapore-pudendal-thigh) page; lotus-petal use as a propeller variant is covered in the [Propeller Flap](/docs/foundations/surgical-principles/flaps/propeller-flap) page.

---

## Nomenclature and Identity

Related pudendal-perforator flaps have overlapping historical names, but their harvest site, vascular dissection and movement pattern can differ:<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[2]](#ref2)</sup>

- **Lotus petal flap** (Yii and Niranjan, 1996). Named for the bilateral petal-like design
- **Gluteal fold flap / gluteal sulcus flap.** Named for the donor site
- **Internal pudendal artery perforator (IPAP) flap.** Named for the vascular pedicle
- "Singapore flap" is sometimes applied to either the lotus petal or the medial-thigh pudendal-thigh flap (Wee and Joseph 1989); WARWIKI keeps these separate pages because the donor sites and primary indications differ. The Wee-Joseph medial-thigh flap is the [Singapore (Pudendal Thigh) Flap](/docs/foundations/surgical-principles/flaps/singapore-pudendal-thigh).

The flap can be configured as a transposition, propeller, or V-Y advancement, making it one of the most versatile local-flap concepts in the perineal region.<sup>[[5]](#ref5)</sup>

## Vascular Anatomy

The lotus petal flap is based on perforators from the internal pudendal artery that emerge in the anogenital triangle:<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[6]](#ref6)</sup>

- **Hashimoto** (2001, as cited by Giroux) described 3–5 perforators in the perineal anogenital triangle, with the ischial tuberosity as the landmark for the safe medial boundary of dissection.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup> Hashimoto 2014 reported 71 free-style IPAP flaps in 45 patients (67 survived completely, 4 partial necrosis, no total loss), located with handheld Doppler.<sup>[[5]](#ref5)</sup>
- **Giroux 2021.** Color Doppler in 15 subjects identified 24 perforators, with a mean ischial-tuberosity-to-perforator distance of 27.3 mm. The indexed abstract prints a vessel-diameter threshold of “greater than 5 mm,” which needs checking against the original methods before using a threshold. The authors concluded routine ultrasound mapping and formal perforator dissection are not always required for their landmark-based design; this does not apply automatically to an isolated-perforator flap.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup>
- **Jin 2009.** Cadaveric microdissection of the perineum (11 cadavers, 22 sides) found 4 relatively constant perforating arteries (branches of the superficial external pudendal, obturator and posterior scrotal or labial arteries; not all are internal pudendal branches). They overlap and form upper, middle and lower vascular networks in the deep fascia. Four cutaneous nerves were identified in the region, which supports the concept of sensate flaps.<sup>[[6]](#ref6)</sup>

The dense, overlapping perforator network explains the flap's reliability. Multiple perforators can be captured within a single skin paddle, providing redundant supply.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[6]](#ref6)</sup>

## Design and Surgical Technique

### Classic design (Yii and Niranjan)

Elliptical skin island centered over the gluteal fold, long axis parallel to the fold, base medial (toward the perineum); transposed into the vulvar or perineal defect.<sup>[[3]](#ref3)[[7]](#ref7)</sup>

![Schematic of the lotus petal flap design around the vulva, with the internal pudendal artery and the flap vascular pedicle in the gluteal fold](/img/figures/lotus-petal-grossi-2021-1.jpg)

*Schematic of lotus petal flap reconstruction: petal-shaped flap outlines arranged around the vulva and perineum, the internal pudendal artery branching toward the perineum (right) and the flap territory with its perforator-based vascular pedicle in the gluteal fold (left, dashed outline). From Grossi U, et al. Tech Coloproctol. 2021;25(1):141-142, Fig. 1 (CC BY 4.0).<sup>[[24]](#ref24)</sup>*

### Modified (thinned) design — Warrier 2004

A thinned cutaneous flap harvested without formal identification of the blood supply, relying on the dense perforator territory:<sup>[[3]](#ref3)</sup>

- Easier transposition due to reduced bulk
- Better aesthetic match to vulvar / perineal tissue
- No partial or complete failures in the initial 8-patient / 12-flap series

### Supra-fascial variant — Bodin 2015

Raised above the fascia (rather than including it), further reducing bulk while maintaining perfusion through the dense subcutaneous perforator network. Particularly suited for posterior perineal cavity filling and skin resurfacing.<sup>[[8]](#ref8)</sup>

### Tunneled variant

The flap is passed through a subcutaneous tunnel to reach the defect, avoiding an external skin bridge. In a published tunneled design, the tunnel was made wide enough to admit the flap without compressing its pedicle.<sup>[[27]](#ref27)</sup> Confalonieri 2017 reported the LPF, particularly the tunneled variant, to be superior to V-Y advancement in functional and cosmetic results for primary vulvar malignancies (retrospective, single center).<sup>[[9]](#ref9)</sup>

### Key design parameters

- Flap dimensions typically 3 × 4 cm to 13 × 12 cm depending on defect size<sup>[[5]](#ref5)</sup>
- Harvested unilaterally or bilaterally. Bilateral flaps are used for total vulvectomy or neovagina<sup>[[2]](#ref2)[[10]](#ref10)</sup>
- Donor site closes primarily in virtually all cases, with the scar hidden in the natural gluteal fold<sup>[[7]](#ref7)[[8]](#ref8)</sup>

## Urogynecologic Applications

### Vulvar reconstruction after oncologic resection — primary indication

Vulvar reconstruction is the application with the largest LPF cohorts. In a cross-sectional survey, 22 of 38 contacted women completed questionnaires a median of 38.5 months after LPF reconstruction; sexual-function scores were lower than in age-matched healthy women, and respondents reported pain during sexual activity. The comparison cannot isolate the flap’s effect from oncologic treatment.<sup>[[28]](#ref28)</sup>

- **Hellinga 2018.** Largest complications series: 93 patients, 137 lotus petal flaps:<sup>[[11]](#ref11)</sup>
  - **30.1%** of patients had no complications; 51.7% had Clavien-Dindo I–II complications; 18.3% required intervention under general anesthesia (Clavien-Dindo IIIb), usually debridement (64.7% of the IIIb group).<sup>[[11]](#ref11)</sup>
  - Donor-site morbidity 14.0%
  - Complication rates did not differ between vulvar, perineal, and vulvoperineal reconstruction
  - Patients younger than 60 years had a significantly higher complication rate than older patients (p = 0.015; OR 0.235, 95% CI 0.073–0.754, for the older group)
- **Argenta 2013.** 59 consecutive patients, 80 lotus petal flaps for vulvar carcinoma / melanoma (66.1%), vulvar dysplasia (20.3%), colorectal disease (8.5%), hidradenitis suppurativa (5.1%). Mean defect 29 cm². Complications 36% (superficial wound separation 15% most common). No complete flap loss; partial loss occurred in 7/80 flaps (8.8%). Four patients required delayed revision, two for partial flap loss and two for stricture or stenosis.<sup>[[4]](#ref4)</sup>
- **Confalonieri 2017.** Retrospective comparison reporting overall complications of V-Y 21% versus LPF 13%, without a significant overall difference (p = 0.588). The authors favored the tunneled variant for functional and cosmetic results in primary vulvar malignancies. The indexed abstract inconsistently lists 284 women, 234 V-Y plus 128 LPF recipients, and event fractions of 27/128 and 14/106; use neither apparent arm denominator as settled until the full table is checked.<sup>[[9]](#ref9)</sup>
- **Sawada 2004.** 5 patients with mean vulvoperineal defects of 157.9 cm² covered with bilateral LPF. All flaps survived without fatal necrosis. No pain, abnormal sensation, or impact on excretion or hip mobility; donor scars hidden in the gluteal fold.<sup>[[7]](#ref7)</sup>

### Vulvar reconstruction algorithm — Negosanti 2015

A defect-based classification from 22 consecutive patients:<sup>[[10]](#ref10)</sup>

| Type | Resection extent | Negosanti's proposed flap choice |
|---|---|---|
| **IA** | Half-vulvar resection, vagina preserved | **Unilateral LPF** |
| **IB** | Total vulvar resection, vagina preserved | **Bilateral LPF** |
| **II** | Vulvar + vaginal resection, large pelvic dead space | **Pedicled DIEP** (LPF insufficient for bulk) |

In Negosanti's proposed algorithm from 22 patients, unilateral or bilateral LPF can reconstruct vulvar-only defects (Types IA and IB); the authors preferred pedicled DIEP for type II defects with vaginal resection and substantial pelvic dead space.<sup>[[10]](#ref10)</sup> The 2023 ESGO guideline bases reconstructive choice on patient and tumor characteristics and team experience (level IV, grade B), without naming a first-line flap.<sup>[[26]](#ref26)</sup>

### Pelvic-floor reconstruction after extralevator abdominoperineal excision (ELAPE)

- **Hellinga 2016.** 28 patients with LPF reconstruction after ELAPE; 89.3% had preoperative RT, 28.6% biological mesh augmentation. No total flap loss; 21.4% no complications; 46.4% minor (Clavien-Dindo I–II); 32.1% reoperation (Clavien-Dindo IIIb), 3 needing a second LPF. Median time to wound healing 14 weeks. The flap also enabled neovagina creation when needed.<sup>[[12]](#ref12)</sup>
- **Hellinga 2020.** Cross-sectional QoL follow-up (15 of 23 eligible patients completed questionnaires; 11 examined; the authors advise caution given the small sample). QoL did not differ significantly between LPF patients and primary-perineal-closure controls despite presumably larger resections in the LPF group. 33% of LPF patients were sexually active postoperatively (versus 87% preoperatively), no perineal herniation was found, and hip-joint ROM was bilaterally reduced versus normal values.<sup>[[13]](#ref13)</sup>

### Posterior perineal reconstruction

**Bodin 2015.** 10 supra-fascial LPF procedures (6 patients; unilateral in 2, bilateral in 4) for posterior perineal cavity filling and skin resurfacing. 4 patients had prior RT. No wound complications or flap necrosis occurred over 20.5 months of follow-up. Mean LOS 11.3 days; healing time 35.2 days.<sup>[[8]](#ref8)</sup>

### Perioperative mobility after IPAP flap reconstruction

In a randomized trial of 51 patients after IPAP flap reconstruction of irradiated abdominoperineal resection defects, an adapted early-mobilization program (n = 25) versus 5 days of bed rest (n = 26) improved independent ambulation on day 5 (68.0% versus 38.5%; p = 0.035). No statistically significant difference was detected in healing time (p = 0.06) or complication rates (68.0% versus 80.8%; p = 0.296); equivalence was not tested.<sup>[[22]](#ref22)</sup>

### Rectovaginal fistula

A modified IPAP flap interposition has been described for low rectovaginal fistula: in a retrospective series of 16 patients, all healed without recurrence over a mean follow-up of 14.1 months.<sup>[[23]](#ref23)</sup>

### Complex pelvic-perineal reconstruction (combined flaps)

For extensive defects requiring more tissue than a single LPF can provide:

- **Contedini 2015.** Pedicled ALT flap combined with bilateral lotus petal flaps for a complex pelvic-perineal defect after pelvic exenteration. The ALT provided bulk for pelvic dead-space obliteration; bilateral LPFs resurfaced the perineum. Good result with no major complications.<sup>[[14]](#ref14)</sup>
- **Bini & Stavrianos 2025.** 34 patients with pelvic or perineal reconstruction after advanced tumor resection using VRAM, gracilis, IGAP, IPAP and LPFs. Wound dehiscence occurred in four patients (two gracilis, one IPAP, one LPF); surgical-site infection occurred in two (one gracilis, one ORAM). 15/34 achieved 5-yr disease-free survival; the authors attribute the low survival to tumor aggression and recurrence.<sup>[[15]](#ref15)</sup>

### Neovagina creation

Bilateral lotus petal flaps can be used for neovagina formation. Höckel and Dornhöfer noted that bilateral local axial-pattern flaps (including LPF) are needed for full neovagina formation, although tubularization of a single musculocutaneous flap may suffice in some cases.<sup>[[2]](#ref2)</sup> The Hellinga ELAPE series confirmed LPF-enabled neovagina creation as part of pelvic-floor reconstruction.<sup>[[12]](#ref12)</sup>

## Urologic Applications

### Scrotal reconstruction after Fournier's gangrene

Payne 2009 described lotus petal flaps for scrotal reconstruction in a single male patient following necrotizing fasciitis after circumcision:<sup>[[16]](#ref16)</sup>

- Bilateral lotus petal flaps were transposed to create a neoscrotum with adequate volume for testicular cover
- Combined with Integra dermal regeneration template for penile-shaft resurfacing
- The LPF "integrated well with the remaining scrotal sac to produce a good volume neoscrotum"
- Erectile capability preserved with the Integra-covered penile shaft

The LPF was originally designed for vulvovaginal defects. In this case it provided tissue of appropriate thickness and pliability to recreate a scrotal pouch.

In the broader Fournier's literature, a 2026 SR of 619 patients / 625 flaps reported flap loss in 1.6% of cases, with medial-thigh, pudendal-thigh, ALT and gracilis flaps most commonly used.<sup>[[17]](#ref17)</sup> The review identifies pudendal-thigh flaps among the common options; a separate report describes one LPF scrotal reconstruction.<sup>[[17]](#ref17)[[16]](#ref16)</sup>

### Perineal reconstruction — IPAT and IPAP variants

The **internal pudendal artery turnover (IPAT) flap**, a simplified IPAP (lotus petal) variant, was described by Nassar 2021 in 38 consecutive patients for perineal reconstruction:<sup>[[18]](#ref18)</sup>

- Requires no visualization or dissection of perforating vessels
- At 3 months, 37 flaps in the 38-patient cohort were fully healed; no flap failures or partial losses.<sup>[[18]](#ref18)</sup>
- Complications occurred in 10/38 patients (9 minor, 1 return to the operating room for washout after a urinary leak).<sup>[[18]](#ref18)</sup>
- Effective for deep three-dimensional defects following perineal excisions

The authors describe it as simpler than perforator dissection and useful at the end of long oncologic resections; the series is retrospective and from one surgeon.

Loreti 2023 reported 11 IPAP-based flaps (8 rotation, 2 advancement island, 1 propeller) after abdominoperineal resection. All 11 flaps survived with no immediate postoperative major complications; 8/11 patients (73%) had previously irradiated tissues.<sup>[[19]](#ref19)</sup>

### Presacral defect reconstruction

**Van der Meer 2014** described a partially de-epithelialized lotus petal perforator flap for an extended chronic presacral defect after radiotherapy and rectal cancer resection. Wound healing was achieved at 12 days (single case). The authors concluded the LPF is "a relatively simple and successful choice" for this indication.<sup>[[20]](#ref20)</sup>

## Complications

In Hellinga's single-center cohort of 93 patients receiving 137 flaps (patient-level complication categories, not a pooled estimate):<sup>[[11]](#ref11)</sup>

| Category | Rate |
|---|---|
| No complications | 30.1% |
| Minor (Clavien-Dindo I–II) | 51.7% |
| Major (Clavien-Dindo IIIb) | 18.3% |
| Donor-site morbidity | 14.0% |
| Flap loss | Not quantified in the accessible Hellinga abstract; review original tables before assigning a rate |

**Risk factors:** patients younger than 60 had more complications than older patients in this cohort (p = 0.015); the OR of 0.235 describes the older group.<sup>[[11]](#ref11)</sup>

A separate review of pelvic-exenteration reconstruction discusses VRAM and other choices, but differences in defect size, irradiated tissue and source populations prevent a direct LPF-versus-VRAM survival or donor-morbidity ranking from these observational series.<sup>[[21]](#ref21)</sup> A 2025 APR review reported perineal hernia in 7/47 patients (14.9%) with hernia data in a mixed gluteal fasciocutaneous group; this is not an LPF-specific rate.<sup>[[29]](#ref29)</sup>

## Comparison with Other Perineal / Vulvar Reconstruction Options

A 2024 systematic review placed conventional lotus-petal flaps among local fasciocutaneous flaps in its non-perforator group. Across 49 studies, short-term complication rates did not differ significantly between mixed perforator (276 patients) and non-perforator (1,564 patients) groups; these data do not establish an LPF-specific advantage, and long-term quality-of-life data were scarce.<sup>[[25]](#ref25)</sup>

| **Perforator dissection** | No (landmark-based) | No | Yes (skeletonization) | No (named vessel) | No (named vessel) |
| **Sensate** | Potentially; postoperative sensory preservation was not measured in the cited outcome series<sup>[[4]](#ref4)[[6]](#ref6)</sup> | Variable | Variable | No | No |
| **Donor scar** | Hidden in gluteal fold | Visible (perineal / thigh) | Hidden in gluteal fold | Abdominal (significant) | Medial thigh |

## Key Advantages

1. **Simplicity in selected designs.** Giroux considered anatomic-landmark harvesting possible without routine ultrasound or formal perforator dissection; isolated-perforator designs use a different approach.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[18]](#ref18)</sup>
2. **Useful vascular territory.** The overlapping IPA perforator network permits varied designs, but a pooled zero-loss rate across the heterogeneous series has not been established.<sup>[[4]](#ref4)[[5]](#ref5)[[11]](#ref11)</sup>
3. **Hidden donor scar** in the natural gluteal fold.<sup>[[7]](#ref7)[[8]](#ref8)</sup>
4. **Sensory anatomy.** Cutaneous nerves traverse the perineal flap territory and may be preserved during harvest; the cited anatomic and small clinical reports do not establish maintained sexual sensation after reconstruction.<sup>[[2]](#ref2)[[6]](#ref6)[[7]](#ref7)</sup>
5. **Tissue match.** Gluteal-fold skin is described as closely matching vulvar / perineal skin in thickness, texture, color and pliability.<sup>[[3]](#ref3)[[7]](#ref7)</sup>
6. **Versatility.** Transposition, propeller, V-Y, or tunneled configurations; uni- or bilateral; combinable with distant flaps for complex defects.<sup>[[5]](#ref5)[[10]](#ref10)[[14]](#ref14)</sup>

## Key Limitations

1. **Limited bulk for some defects.** Negosanti preferred pedicled DIEP for type II vulvovaginal defects with substantial pelvic dead space, while broader exenteration reconstructions require individualized flap selection. Selected ELAPE defects have been reconstructed with LPF, and the IPAT variant has been described for deep three-dimensional perineal defects.<sup>[[10]](#ref10)[[21]](#ref21)[[12]](#ref12)[[18]](#ref18)</sup>
2. **Minor wound complications are common.** About 50% of patients had Clavien-Dindo I–II complications, reflecting the challenging perineal wound-healing environment.<sup>[[11]](#ref11)[[12]](#ref12)</sup>
3. **Prior radiotherapy.** Assess gluteal-fold donor-tissue quality and pelvic dead-space volume when selecting a flap. In one ELAPE series, LPFs were used after preoperative radiotherapy in 25/28 patients; a flap with more bulk or a different donor site may be selected for a large defect or poor-quality local tissue.<sup>[[15]](#ref15)[[12]](#ref12)[[21]](#ref21)</sup>

## Technical Pearls

- **Map the ischial tuberosity** as the medial boundary of safe dissection (Hashimoto's anatomic landmark).<sup>[[5]](#ref5)</sup>
- **Select mapping to match the design and tissue.** Giroux's landmark-based approach need not require routine ultrasound, whereas free-style isolated-perforator designs commonly use handheld Doppler; planning for irradiated and reoperative fields depends on the flap design and tissue condition.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[5]](#ref5)</sup>
- **Choose the variant by defect.** Warrier-thinned for surface vulvar coverage, Bodin supra-fascial for posterior cavity filling, tunneled (Confalonieri) for primary vulvar malignancies, IPAT (Nassar) when avoiding perforator dissection altogether.
- **Plan bilaterally** for total vulvectomy, neovagina or large midline defects. Closure tension is shared and the bilateral petal design gives a midline result.
- **Combine with distant flaps** when bulk is required: bilateral LPF plus ALT (Contedini) or LPF as the surface-cover layer over a deep VRAM or gracilis.<sup>[[14]](#ref14)</sup>
