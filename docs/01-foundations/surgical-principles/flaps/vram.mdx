# Vertical Rectus Abdominis Myocutaneous (VRAM) Flap

The VRAM flap is an established abdominal-based pedicled option for pelvic and perineal reconstruction after extensive oncologic resection, including selected urologic exenteration and complex fistula repairs. It brings vascularized tissue and, when needed, a skin paddle into an irradiated field and can fill pelvic dead space. Its abdominal-wall donor morbidity and the size, route and tissue requirements of the defect must be weighed against other flaps.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[2]](#ref2)[[3]](#ref3)</sup>

See the overview article for comparative context: [Flaps in GU Reconstruction](/docs/foundations/surgical-principles/flaps-gu-reconstruction), and the anatomy references: [Abdominal Wall](/docs/foundations/anatomy-physiology/pelvis-support/abdominal-wall) and [Pelvic Vascular Anatomy](/docs/foundations/anatomy-physiology/pelvis-support/pelvic-vascular-anatomy).

External technique atlas: [microsurgeon.org — Rectus Muscle Flap](https://www.microsurgeon.org/rectus).

## Gross Anatomy

The paired rectus abdominis muscles provide abdominal flexion and support the intra-abdominal contents.

| Feature | Value |
|---|---|
| Origin | Pubic symphysis + pubic crest |
| Insertion | 5th–7th costal cartilages |
| Length | Up to **30 cm** |
| Width | Up to **10 cm** |
| Enclosed by | Rectus sheath (anterior + posterior) |

### The Arcuate Line

A critical landmark for VRAM harvest: **below the arcuate line, the posterior rectus sheath is absent** (all aponeurotic layers pass anterior to the muscle). Above the arcuate line, the muscle is enclosed anteriorly and posteriorly. Two implications:

1. Below the arcuate line, no posterior fascial defect is created at harvest — donor-site integrity here depends entirely on the intact anterior sheath.
2. Above the arcuate line, careful anterior-sheath closure (± mesh) is essential to prevent incisional hernia.

### Tendinous Inscriptions

Three to four transverse fibrous inscriptions tether the muscle to the overlying anterior sheath. At these points, the fascia is tightly adherent and must be sharply dissected during harvest to avoid damaging muscle fibers.

---

## Vascular Anatomy

The rectus abdominis has a **dual blood supply** — inferior and superior epigastric systems — that communicate through choke vessels within the muscle. For pelvic and perineal reconstruction, the flap is **inferiorly based** on the deep inferior epigastric artery.

### Dominant Pedicle — Deep Inferior Epigastric Artery (DIEA)

- **Origin:** external iliac artery, just above the inguinal ligament.
- **Course:** enters the deep surface of the rectus abdominis near its lower third, ascends along the deep muscle surface, anastomoses with the superior epigastric system.
- **Artery caliber:** **2–4 mm** at origin.
- **Pedicle length:** **5–7 cm** easily obtainable; additional length via intramuscular dissection.
- **Venae comitantes:** paired, merging at the external iliac; one typically larger and comparable to arterial caliber.

In Kim's retrospective series, postoperative imaging in **60 evaluable patients** suggested preserved DIEA flow in 98.3%; this is not a prospective perfusion or flap-survival rate for all VRAM operations.<sup>[[3]](#ref3)</sup>

### Superior Epigastric System

- Arises from the internal mammary artery as it crosses the costal margin.
- Supplies the superior rectus.
- **Sacrificed during inferior-based VRAM harvest** to mobilize the flap toward the pelvis.

### Skin Paddle Perforators

The skin paddle is supplied by **musculocutaneous perforators** through the anterior rectus sheath — principally in the paraumbilical region (rich perforator zone). This is why the skin paddle typically extends in a vertical orientation along the rectus axis.

---

## Flap Design

### Inferiorly Based VRAM (Standard for Urologic Reconstruction)

- **Skin paddle:** vertical, 8–12 cm wide, extending from the costal margin down toward the pubis. Length adjusted to defect size.
- **Muscle component:** full rectus or a muscle-sparing variant (see below).
- **Reach:** perineum, pelvis, groin, proximal thigh via subcutaneous tunnel or through a prepared pelvic/retropubic corridor.

### Superiorly Based

Rarely used in urology — primarily for chest-wall reconstruction.

### Free VRAM

Rare urologic indication. Reserved for specific distant reconstruction needs.

### Transverse Rectus Abdominis Myocutaneous (TRAM) Variant

The **TRAM flap** is the same parent flap with the **skin paddle oriented transversely** across the lower abdomen (rather than vertically). It shares the rectus muscle, DIEA / DSEA dual-pedicle anatomy with VRAM but has its own distinct historical lineage (Hartrampf 1982), zone classification (Hallock LDF; Holm clinical revision), muscle-sparing TRAM classification (MS-0 / MS-1 / MS-2 → DIEP MS-3), vascular-enhancement procedures (delay / supercharging / turbocharging), and comparative DIEP-vs-TRAM literature. See the dedicated [TRAM flap](/docs/foundations/surgical-principles/flaps/tram) foundations page for the full treatment.

## Harvest Technique

### Open Standard Harvest

1. **Incision** — vertical paramedian over the rectus muscle axis (or low transverse depending on surgeon preference). Length determined by the muscle segment required.
2. **Subcutaneous dissection** to the anterior rectus sheath; ligate any perforators encountered. Cautery risks damaging skin-paddle perforators — prefer fine ligatures.
3. **Anterior rectus sheath opened longitudinally.** Elevate sheath medially and laterally with Allis clamps.
4. **Tendinous inscriptions** — tightly adherent to sheath; tease off muscle sharply with electrocautery.
5. **Identify the DIEA laterally** at the inferior-lateral muscle border, in the areolar fat plane. Trace the pedicle down to its origin at the external iliac.
6. **Divide the muscle origin** at the pubis (finger-encircle, then cautery) while protecting the pedicle.
7. **Divide the muscle superiorly** (costal margin level). **Ligate or cauterize superior epigastric vessels securely** — they will retract and bleed if missed.
8. **Isolate the flap on the DIEA pedicle.** Tunnel subcutaneously or directly via the pelvis to the recipient site.
9. **Reconstruct the donor abdominal wall** with attention to fascial loss, closure tension, contamination, prior incisions and stoma position. Primary closure, reinforcement or a more complex repair may be appropriate; a hernia rate from an observational cohort does not establish routine mesh for every harvest.<sup>[[3]](#ref3)[[26]](#ref26)</sup>
10. **Drain** the donor site (suction) — especially when a skin paddle creates dead space above the fascia.

### Oblique Rectus Abdominis Myocutaneous (ORAM) Variant

The **ORAM flap** orients the skin paddle obliquely rather than vertically, offering a **greater arc of rotation, thinner skin paddle, less bulk, and limited fascial harvest**, with comparable complication rates to standard VRAM. Useful when the defect requires lateral reach (groin, proximal thigh) without the bulk of a full muscle paddle.<sup>[[19]](#ref19)</sup>

### Muscle-Sparing VRAM (MS-VRAM) Variants

Developed to reduce donor-site morbidity:<sup>[[8]](#ref8)</sup>

- **Modified long vertical flap** — extensive coverage with preserved muscle width.
- **Wrapping flap** — circumferential defects.
- **De-epithelialized flap** — fills dead space without external skin coverage.
- **Cork flap** — a limited-tissue variant described in the MS-VRAM series; its reported satisfaction and recovery measures are cohort-specific, not proof of superiority for every defect.

### Extended VRAM (eVRAM)

Large pelviperineal defects where standard VRAM is insufficient. In a 44-patient series: **91% successful reconstruction**, 3 flap failures, 39% perineal wound complications (8 requiring reoperation), 11% donor-site problems.<sup>[[9]](#ref9)</sup>

### Robotic Harvest

Robotic **muscle-only rectus harvest** is an emerging option in selected pelvic cases. Described approaches use abdominal ports, develop the posterior rectus plane, identify and preserve the inferior epigastric pedicle and deliver the muscle into the pelvic defect. Port placement, sheath harvest and need for reinforcement differ by technique; this is not interchangeable with a skin-bearing VRAM. Preserving the anterior sheath may reduce incision-related morbidity, but harvesting rectus muscle does not leave the abdominal wall intact.<sup>[[18]](#ref18)[[20]](#ref20)[[21]](#ref21)</sup>

- **Davila 2022** compared 16 robotic rectus muscle flaps with 20 traditional repairs (12 VRAM and eight gracilis). Major complications were similar; the 55% versus 31% minor-complication difference was not statistically significant. Six robotic patients received biologic mesh reinforcement.<sup>[[20]](#ref20)</sup>
- **Asaad 2021** described rectus muscle harvest after robotic extirpative surgery, with an anterior-sheath-sparing approach in selected patients.<sup>[[21]](#ref21)</sup>

## Critical Flap-Inset Considerations

Protecting the pedicle from twisting, compression and tension during transfer and inset is important. Trapero and colleagues attribute compromised viability in their experience principally to inset or postoperative-care problems; Kim's imaging observation cannot establish the cause of all flap failures.<sup>[[3]](#ref3)[[10]](#ref10)</sup>

**One published technical proposal adapts rotation to the defect and patient position:**<sup>[[10]](#ref10)</sup>

| Defect location | Rotation | Result |
|---|---|---|
| **Anterior perineum** | **270° in the sagittal plane**, as proposed by Trapero et al. | Positions the cranial flap toward the anterior defect in their method |
| **Posterior perineum** | **180° in the coronal plane**, as proposed by Trapero et al. | Positions the cranial flap posteriorly in their method |

In either case, meticulous attention to pedicle orientation to **avoid kinking, compression, or tension** on the DIEA pedicle is paramount.

## GU Reconstructive Applications

### Pelvic Exenteration and Radical Cystectomy

The principal urologic indication. Total or anterior pelvic exenteration creates a non-collapsible pelvic cavity particularly problematic in the irradiated field. VRAM addresses two critical issues:<sup>[[3]](#ref3)</sup>

- **Fills pelvic dead space**, with the aim of reducing fluid collection and bowel descent; it does not guarantee prevention of infection or other complications.
- **Introduces non-irradiated, well-vascularized tissue** into the radiated field.

The published VRAM literature is heavily weighted toward colorectal/pelvic oncologic surgery. In Kim's 60-patient imaging cohort, 75% of operations were for rectal cancer; this is **not** a pooled indication rate for all VRAM reconstructions.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[3]](#ref3)</sup>

### Complex Urogenital Fistula Repair

**Selected recurrent urogenital fistulas** after failed repairs may require vascularized interposition. Paprottka's retrospective 12-patient series used **five VRAM and eight gracilis flaps** across the cases. Final fistula repair was achieved in the series; among ten living patients surveyed, three VRAM patients reported slight incontinence and seven gracilis patients reported restored continence. The small, selected groups do not establish a flap-specific continence advantage or guarantee restoration of function.<sup>[[4]](#ref4)</sup>

For **complex / refractory urethrovaginal fistulas** specifically (after failed Martius or other simpler interpositions), **Bruce 2000** described rectus abdominis muscle flap interposition between the closed fistula and the vaginal suture line as a salvage technique; the rectus bulk provides robust vascularized tissue coverage where local options have already failed.<sup>[[22]](#ref22)</sup>

### Vaginoplasty After Anterior Exenteration

For radical cystectomy with partial or total vaginectomy for bladder or urethral carcinoma, the VRAM can be **tubed or used as a skin paddle to reconstruct the vagina and perineum** at the same setting. Parsons 2003 described this approach for urologic-malignancy anterior exenteration, achieving simultaneous defect coverage and neovaginal reconstruction.<sup>[[23]](#ref23)</sup>

### Bladder Neck Closure in Neurogenic Incontinence

The rectus abdominis muscle flap can be **interposed between the bladder neck and urethral stump** as an adjunct to bladder-neck closure to prevent vesicourethral fistula — an alternative to omental interposition when omentum is unavailable or inadequate. Smith 2010 reported a preliminary pediatric neurogenic-incontinence series with promising results.<sup>[[24]](#ref24)</sup>

### Epispadias / Exstrophy Reconstruction

Rectus abdominis muscle and fascia have been used to provide **abdominal-wall support, coverage of the bladder neck and proximal urethra, and mons reconstruction** in epispadias / exstrophy. Horton 1988 described this application as part of the multistage exstrophy reconstruction.<sup>[[25]](#ref25)</sup>

### Urosymphyseal Fistula (USF)

A difficult indication where the extent of resection and availability of omentum affect flap selection. In Escandón's **retrospective 56-patient** series (34 omental, 11 VRAM, 11 primary repair):<sup>[[5]](#ref5)</sup>

- **Omental flap** — 90-day sepsis 3%; adjusted early recurrence was lower than with primary repair, but not demonstrated to be lower than with VRAM
- **VRAM** — 90-day sepsis 27%; recurrence 27.3% in the reported group
- **Primary repair** — 90-day sepsis 18%; recurrence 30% in the reported group

The groups were small and selected for different operations. These observations do not establish a universal omental-first rule or reserve VRAM only for absent omentum; see the [omental flap](/docs/foundations/surgical-principles/flaps/omental) discussion.

### Genital Reconstruction

Inferiorly based VRAM has been used for **phalloplasty in selected cases** (7-patient series) when other options (RFFF, ALT) are unavailable or contraindicated.<sup>[[6]](#ref6)</sup> Not a first-line phalloplasty flap.

### Groin and Proximal Thigh Defects

After radical inguinal lymphadenectomy or tumor resection, VRAM can reach selected groin and proximal-thigh defects. A retrospective cohort/systematic review reports useful coverage but substantial complications; its observational mesh subgroups do not establish that mesh has no effect on hernia risk.<sup>[[7]](#ref7)</sup>

### Coverage of Major Vessels / Visceral Repairs

The rectus bulk is useful for **covering major vessels or visceral repairs** in the pelvic basin — obturator vessels after lymphadenectomy, visceral suture lines after complex resection, or pubectomy sites after osteomyelitis debridement.

## Outcomes

### Systematic Review — 1,827 Patients<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup>

| Outcome | Rate |
|---|---|
| Mean perineal / recipient-site morbidity | **27%** |
| Complete flap loss | **1.8%** |
| Perineal hernia | 0.2% |
| Mean donor-site morbidity | **15%** |
| Abdominal dehiscence | 5.5% |
| Incisional hernia (donor) | 3.3% |

These are means across heterogeneous reports, not patient-specific risks; recipient and donor definitions and follow-up varied.

### 10-Year Series (60 Evaluable of 77 Operated)<sup>[[3]](#ref3)</sup>

- Donor-site hernia: **16.7%**
- Perineal hernia: 3.3%
- DIEA flow suggested on available postoperative imaging: **98.3%**

### Proctor 10-Year Experience<sup>[[11]](#ref11)</sup>

- Clavien-Dindo ≥3 at donor site: 6%
- Clavien-Dindo ≥3 at flap / perineal site: 18%
- **Smoking** (P=0.003) and **neoadjuvant radiotherapy** (P=0.047) associated with flap complications in the reported analysis; applicability depends on cohort and adjustment
- Flap complications significantly prolonged hospital stay

### Factors Associated With VRAM Use Rather Than Primary Closure<sup>[[12]](#ref12)</sup>

In Nichols's 158-patient retrospective anorectal APR cohort, 29 received VRAM. The following were associated with **selection for flap reconstruction**, not proven requirements or independent predictors of complications:

- Current smoking
- Anal tumor location
- Larger perineal skin resection and neoadjuvant radiation

Prior abdominal surgery was associated with complications in Nelson's separate VRAM-versus-mixed-thigh series; it should not be attributed to the Nichols selection study.<sup>[[16]](#ref16)</sup>

## Comparative Outcomes

### VRAM vs Gracilis

Stein 2019 matched-cohort study:<sup>[[13]](#ref13)</sup>

- No difference in minor complications (44% gracilis vs 48% VRAM, P=0.8)
- No difference in major complications (19% vs 13%, P=0.53)
- Similar wound healing time
- **Muscle-only gracilis** heals faster than musculocutaneous gracilis

Vulvo-perineal meta-analysis:<sup>[[14]](#ref14)</sup>

- VRAM higher pooled donor-site complication proportions (57.6% vs 16.0% gracilis) across different vulvoperineal series; this is not a randomized head-to-head estimate
- Similar recipient-site outcomes
- No difference in minor or major recipient complications

**Practical takeaway:** both flaps are viable in selected defects. The comparative series found no statistically significant difference in its main complication outcomes; the proportional meta-analysis suggests more reported VRAM donor morbidity but combines heterogeneous cohorts. Paprottka's fistula series is too small to rank continence by flap.<sup>[[4]](#ref4)[[13]](#ref13)[[14]](#ref14)</sup>

### VRAM vs IGAM (Inferior Gluteal Artery Myocutaneous)

130-patient pelvic-exenteration series (97.7% previously irradiated):<sup>[[15]](#ref15)</sup>

- **IGAM had fewer recorded adverse outcomes** than VRAM, including flap failure, return to theatre and wound dehiscence, in this center's retrospective comparison
- Defect characteristics and flap selection may confound comparison; this does not establish an all-patient IGAM-first rule

### VRAM vs Mixed Thigh Flaps<sup>[[16]](#ref16)</sup>

- Thigh flaps had greater major complications (42% vs 15%)
- Higher cellulitis, pelvic abscess, major dehiscence
- This was an observational 114-VRAM versus 19-thigh comparison. The thigh group was not an isolated ALT/VL cohort, and the result does not establish VRAM superiority for every pelvic defect

### VRAM vs IGAP (Inferior Gluteal Artery Perforator)<sup>[[17]](#ref17)</sup>

- No significant difference in minor or major complications
- Both were used with no significant difference detected in that retrospective cohort; absence of a detected difference is not proof of equivalence
- IGAP preserves abdominal wall integrity

## Risk Factors and Patient Selection

**Factors to assess:**<sup>[[11]](#ref11)[[12]](#ref12)</sup>

- **Smoking** — associated with flap complications on univariate analysis in Proctor's cohort (P=0.003); support cessation when feasible
- **Neoadjuvant radiotherapy** — associated on univariate analysis in that cohort (P=0.047), but is often part of cancer treatment rather than a modifiable reconstructive choice
- **Prior abdominal surgery** — assess for prior TRAM/DIEP harvest or vascular ligation, which may compromise the DIEA pedicle

**Potential limitations requiring individual vascular and donor-wall assessment:**

- Prior TRAM/DIEP harvest on the intended side or known injury to its epigastric supply
- Prior iliac or inferior epigastric vessel ligation
- Severe abdominal-wall weakness, major hernia or multiple prior laparotomies

**Consider alternatives:**

- **IGAM or IGAP** when donor-abdominal morbidity, positioning and defect geometry favor gluteal tissue
- **Gracilis** when its volume and reach meet the defect's needs
- **Omental flap** when viable omentum provides appropriate interposition for a urosymphyseal fistula

---

## Donor-Site Management

The donor abdominal wall is an important source of morbidity, alongside recipient-site complications.

- **Anterior-sheath/fascial repair:** plan around the actual harvested sheath, tension, prior incisions and stoma; suture and closure pattern are selected for the defect. PDS is an absorbable suture, not a nonabsorbable one.
- **Mesh reinforcement:** assess case by case. A 58-patient randomized trial did not demonstrate prevention of one-year CT-detected hernia with collagen mesh (8/24 vs 12/24, P=0.38); a retrospective 100-patient study found no added hernia benefit when primary closure was achieved and a higher rate of infection requiring intervention in its mesh group. Neither study decides every complex or bridged closure.<sup>[[26]](#ref26)[[27]](#ref27)</sup>
- **Drain:** use according to the extent of dissection, dead space and local protocol; a fixed safe removal time is not established here.
- **Anterior sheath below arcuate line** — the only aponeurotic layer; closure vulnerability is highest here.
- **Long-term hernia surveillance** at follow-up visits.

---

## Postoperative Imaging Findings

Normal VRAM appearance on CT/MRI:<sup>[[2]](#ref2)</sup>

- Homogeneous soft-tissue density obliterating pelvic dead space
- Enhancement pattern following the DIEA
- Gradual fatty replacement of muscle over time (expected)

Complications to recognize:

- Fluid collections → infection or seroma
- Lack of enhancement → flap necrosis
- Herniation at donor or recipient sites

---

## Key Takeaways

1. **VRAM is the workhorse pedicled flap for pelvic / perineal reconstruction** after radical urologic oncology, particularly pelvic exenteration with radiation.
2. **Inferiorly based** on the DIEA; confirm its integrity and flap reach in the individual patient. Kim's 98.3% imaging observation applied to a 60-person evaluable cohort.
3. **Inset matters:** avoid pedicle torsion, compression and tension. The 270°/180° rotations are a published technical proposal, not universal angles.
4. **Counsel about donor and recipient morbidity.** Reported abdominal-wall hernia rates vary markedly with cohort, definition, imaging and follow-up; routine mesh benefit is unproven.
5. **Assess smoking, prior radiation, previous abdominal surgery, stomas and donor-wall integrity** as part of defect-specific planning.
6. **Select the flap for needed skin, bulk, vascularity and reach** rather than ranking VRAM, gluteal, thigh and omental options across unlike defects.
7. **Muscle-sparing and robotic variants** are described for selected patients; functional and donor-wall benefits still require technique-specific evaluation.
