# Deep Inferior Epigastric Perforator (DIEP) Flap

The **DIEP flap** is a skin-and-fat perforator flap based on the deep inferior epigastric artery (DIEA) and perforators passing through the rectus abdominis muscle. It preserves the rectus muscle, although perforator harvest usually requires a limited fascial incision and intramuscular dissection, and it does not eliminate abdominal-wall morbidity. It is used predominantly as a free flap for breast reconstruction. It is also used as a pedicled flap for selected vulvar, perineal and vaginal defects, particularly after pelvic exenteration. Höckel describes DIEA perforator tissue as one reconstructive option. In Negosanti's 22-patient series, the authors proposed a two-flap algorithm: DIEP for complex type-II defects and lotus petal flap for type-I defects.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[2]](#ref2)[[25]](#ref25)</sup>

For the broader GU flap menu see [Flaps for GU Reconstruction](/docs/foundations/surgical-principles/flaps-gu-reconstruction). For the parent muscle-flap page see [VRAM](/docs/foundations/surgical-principles/flaps/vram). For the SEPA-based mons pubis variant see [EPAP](/docs/foundations/surgical-principles/flaps/epap), and for the broader perforator framework see [SCIP / perforator flap](/docs/foundations/surgical-principles/flaps/scip).

## Concept — DIEP vs TRAM / VRAM

The DIEP flap is a muscle-sparing alternative to TRAM and VRAM. Those myocutaneous flaps harvest rectus muscle, whereas DIEP follows perforators through the muscle and preserves the muscle and most of its innervation and fascia. It still requires a fascial opening and can be followed by abdominal bulge or hernia, and mesh may be used in selected closures. In a retrospective breast-reconstruction comparison, postoperative bulge or hernia was 3.1% with DIEP versus 21.2% with pedicled TRAM, and mesh was used in 8.1% versus 44.2%.<sup>[[33]](#ref33)</sup> Total DIEP operative time was 234 minutes longer. These outcomes do not describe pedicled pelvic DIEP transfer or its harvest time.<sup>[[3]](#ref3)[[33]](#ref33)</sup>

| Configuration | Use |
|---|---|
| **Free flap** | Dominant breast-reconstruction application; pedicle divided and microsurgically anastomosed to chest recipient vessels |
| **Pedicled flap** | Selected vulvovaginal and perineal defects; a 17-patient male series also described large post-Fournier penoscrotal repair, with all flaps surviving but three partial necroses. The flap remains attached to its DIEA pedicle and rotates inferiorly through the abdominal wall toward the defect.<sup>[[41]](#ref41)</sup> |

## Vascular Anatomy — The Deep Inferior Epigastric Artery

### Origin and course (Myung 2018, n = 368 hemiabdomens)<sup>[[5]](#ref5)</sup>

- The DIEA arises from the external iliac artery in the medial direction.
- It courses caudally and then turns cephalad; the average descending length before the turning point is 11.29 mm.
- As the origin angle decreases (toward caudal), the initial-descent distance increases (r = 0.382, p &lt; 0.01).

### Branching pattern (Rozen 2010 CTA, n = 500 hemiabdomens)<sup>[[6]](#ref6)[[7]](#ref7)</sup>

| Type | Description | Prevalence (in vivo) | Prevalence (cadaveric) | Perforator transverse distance |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 0 | Absent DIEA | Rare | Not previously described | — |
| **I** | Single trunk (no bifurcation) | Higher than cadaveric | 29%<sup>[[36]](#ref36)</sup> | Intermediate |
| **II** | Bifurcating trunk (medial + lateral branches) | Most common | 57%<sup>[[36]](#ref36)</sup> | **Shortest** (less muscle sacrifice) |
| **III** | Trifurcating trunk in Rozen's classification | Lower than cadaveric | Moon and Taylor: 14% with three or more major branches<sup>[[36]](#ref36)</sup> | **Greatest** (more muscle sacrifice) |
| **IV** | Four-trunk DIEA | Rare | Not previously described | — |

- There is no overall concordance in branching patterns between contralateral sides of the same abdominal wall, except for Type I (51% concordance, p = 0.04).<sup>[[6]](#ref6)</sup>
- **Bifurcating (Type II)** perforators traverse the shortest transverse distances in cadaveric specimens, and longer transverse distances correlate with greater rectus muscle sacrifice.<sup>[[7]](#ref7)</sup>
- **Trifurcating (Type III)** perforators have the greatest transverse distances, which correlates with greater rectus muscle sacrifice during dissection.<sup>[[7]](#ref7)</sup>

### Perforator anatomy

| Study | Finding |
|---|---|
| Ireton 2014 SR (60 articles)<sup>[[8]](#ref8)</sup> | Musculocutaneous perforators most common (33–100%); extramuscular perforators (0–67.6%) — paramedian or tendinous types; extramuscular subtypes are the most desirable for dissection (minimal muscle disruption) |
| El-Mrakby 2002 (20 cadavers)<sup>[[9]](#ref9)</sup> | Average **5.4 large perforators (&gt; 0.5 mm) per dissected case**; lateral division and lateral perforators more dominant (80%) than medial (20%); most perforators located laterally and below the umbilicus, mean 4 cm from umbilicus |
| Meyerov 2025 CTA (n = 180)<sup>[[10]](#ref10)</sup> | Average **2.9 ± 1.6 perforators / hemiabdomen**; larger-caliber vessels closer to the umbilicus (p &lt; 0.005) |
| Bailey 2010 single-dominant-medial-row (3D)<sup>[[11]](#ref11)</sup> | Dominant perforator within a **3 cm radius of the umbilicus**, average size &gt; 1.8 mm; medial row near the umbilicus contains the largest perforators in the entire DIEA system |

### Perforasome perfusion (Schaverien 2008)<sup>[[12]](#ref12)</sup>

- **Medial row perforators.** These give a central elliptical perfusion pattern; injection of a medial row perforator consistently perfused zone IV in 10 cadaveric flaps. Large-diameter subdermal-plexus linking vessels connect the medial row perforators.
- **Lateral row perforators.** These predominantly perfuse the lateral flap and reach medial-row territory by recurrent flow through the subdermal plexus; injection of a lateral row perforator did not perfuse zone IV.
- A zone-discarding rule depends on the selected perforator and observed perfusion. These breast-flap territories should not be applied as a fixed resection mandate to every pedicled pelvic DIEP.<sup>[[10]](#ref10)[[12]](#ref12)</sup>

### Hartrampf perfusion zones

| Zone | Territory | Classical Hartrampf perfusion for medial-row DIEP; zones II and III reverse with a lateral-row perforator<sup>[[12]](#ref12)[[34]](#ref34)</sup> |
|---|---|---|
| I | Ipsilateral periumbilical | Best |
| II | Contralateral periumbilical | Good |
| III | Ipsilateral lateral | Moderate |
| IV | Contralateral lateral | Worst |

### Vascular territory of the DIEA (Boyd / Taylor / Corlett 1984)<sup>[[13]](#ref13)</sup>

- The DIEA is more significant than the superior epigastric artery in supplying the anterior abdominal-wall skin.
- Highest concentration of major perforators is in the paraumbilical area.
- Perforators feed a subcutaneous network that radiates from the umbilicus like spokes of a wheel.
- Choke connections are present inferiorly with the SIEA, inferolaterally with the SCIA and superolaterally with lateral cutaneous intercostal branches.

### Venous anatomy

- Two venae comitantes accompany the DIEA in 90% of cases.<sup>[[9]](#ref9)</sup>
- **Superficial inferior epigastric vein (SIEV).** This is a secondary venous drainage route, and the flap can be reliably thinned in the plane inferior to the SIEV.<sup>[[14]](#ref14)</sup>

### Sensory innervation

- The lower abdominal DIEP skin island contains cutaneous branches of lower intercostal nerves; harvest may disrupt them, so sensation at pelvic inset is uncertain.<sup>[[2]](#ref2)[[37]](#ref37)</sup> In 30 nonneurotized breast DIEP flaps, sensation improved between 6 and 12 months, but that study did not measure pelvic sensory recovery.<sup>[[38]](#ref38)</sup>

## The Pedicled DIEP Flap for Vulvar / Perineal / Vaginal Reconstruction

### Concept

In the pedicled configuration, the DIEP flap remains attached to its DIEA pedicle. The flap is harvested from the lower abdominal wall, the pedicle is dissected retrograde from the perforator through the rectus muscle to the DIEA origin at the external iliac artery, and the flap is then rotated inferiorly through the abdominal wall (retropubic tunnel or laparotomy incision) into the pelvic or perineal defect.<sup>[[15]](#ref15)[[20]](#ref20)</sup>

### Indications across vulvar algorithms

The 2023 ESGO vulvar-cancer guideline recommends access to reconstructive surgical expertise within the multidisciplinary team and selection of the reconstructive technique by patient and tumor characteristics and the team’s experience (evidence IV, recommendation B). It does not name a preferred flap.<sup>[[42]](#ref42)</sup>

| Algorithm | Position |
|---|---|
| **Höckel 2008**<sup>[[2]](#ref2)</sup> | Named option for vulvovaginal reconstruction; abdominal-wall skin on DIEA perforators; insensate; rectus parent flap (VRAM/TRAM) replaced by its muscle-sparing perforator evolution |
| **Negosanti 2015** (n = 22)<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup> | Authors' local **two-flap algorithm**: type IA → monolateral LPF; type IB → bilateral LPF; **type II (wider vulvar/vaginal resection and pelvic dead space)** → pedicled DIEP. This is their series-based proposal, not proof that two flaps suit every patient. |
| **Caretto 2023 secondary recon** (n = 66)<sup>[[21]](#ref21)</sup> | DIEP among the most frequently employed flaps for secondary reconstruction |
| **Toulouse 2025**<sup>[[22]](#ref22)</sup> | Location-based algorithm that favors perforator flaps and reserves musculocutaneous flaps for selected cases. |

## Surgical Technique — Pedicled DIEP

### Preoperative planning

- **CTA of the abdominal wall** can map perforator location and course for complex planning; neither universal necessity nor 100% diagnostic accuracy for all candidates is established by the cited small technical series.<sup>[[14]](#ref14)[[15]](#ref15)</sup>
- CTA defines perforator location, number, caliber, intramuscular course and DIEA branching pattern. For pelvic exenteration, coordinate the harvest side with the planned urinary and digestive diversions and stomas.<sup>[[39]](#ref39)</sup>
- **Perforator selection** considers caliber, intramuscular course, perfusion territory and reach; the medial-row measurements cited here came from a breast-reconstruction series, not a rule for all pedicled pelvic cases.<sup>[[11]](#ref11)</sup>
- Handheld Doppler can supplement CTA intraoperatively.

### Pedicled vertical DIEP for vaginal reconstruction — Ferron 2015 Toulouse technique<sup>[[15]](#ref15)</sup>

1. **Position.** Supine lithotomy.
2. **Midline laparotomy.** For pelvic exenteration, DIEP harvest uses the same abdominal incision.
3. **Perforator identification.** Identify one or two medial perforators on the anterior rectus sheath, based on preoperative CTA.
4. **Flap design.** Design a vertical skin island on the lower abdominal wall, centered on the selected perforators; the vertical orientation allows tubing for a circumferential neovagina.
5. **Suprafascial dissection.** Elevate lateral to medial in a suprafascial plane until perforators are identified entering the anterior rectus sheath.
6. **Fascial incision.** Incise the anterior rectus sheath around the selected perforators while preserving safe dissection and closure. The 6.6 versus 11.1 cm comparison cited elsewhere on this page is from a breast free-flap harvest study and is not from Ferron's pedicled vaginal series.<sup>[[23]](#ref23)</sup>
7. **Intramuscular dissection.** Spread the rectus muscle fibers in the direction of their orientation, ligate or divide muscle branches, and preserve the muscle fibers intact.
8. **Pedicle dissection.** Follow the DIEA toward its external-iliac origin as needed for the actual reach. The 12.6 cm mean pedicle length in Kim's breast cohort is not a fixed length or requirement for the pelvic transfer.<sup>[[23]](#ref23)</sup>
9. **Flap mobilization.** Mobilize the flap on its DIEA pedicle.
10. **Flap transfer.** Rotate the flap inferiorly through the laparotomy or through a retropubic or paravesical tunnel into the pelvic cavity. Before inset, check that the pedicle is not twisted along the chosen route.<sup>[[40]](#ref40)</sup>
11. **Neovagina formation.** Tube the vertical skin island (skin inward), bring it to the prerectal pocket and anastomose it to the vaginal introitus.<sup>[[24]](#ref24)</sup>
12. **Donor closure.** Repair the anterior rectus sheath and abdominal skin according to the defect and the laparotomy. Muscle preservation does not guarantee that mesh is never needed.<sup>[[33]](#ref33)</sup>

Median time for DIEP harvest and vaginal reconstruction together was 60 minutes in Ferron's 10 DIEP cases; this is not an isolated harvest-time benchmark.<sup>[[15]](#ref15)</sup>

### Pedicled DIEP for vulvar surface reconstruction — Muneuchi 2005<sup>[[16]](#ref16)</sup>

- In a 71-year-old with large vulvar cancer, the DIEP flap was harvested through the abdominal incision, which avoided an additional scar.
- Less invasive, simple procedure; morphologically / functionally satisfactory; no dysuria, no abdominal hernia.
- The authors consider DIEP the first choice for vulvar reconstruction following radical vulvectomy (a single case report).

### Thinned pedicled DIEP — Fang 2011 (n = 12)<sup>[[14]](#ref14)</sup>

- The flap was thinned in the plane inferior to the SIEV, based on preoperative CTA.
- 3 vulval / vaginal tumor, 5 congenital vaginal agenesis, 4 perineal Paget's.
- 10/12 flaps transplanted without complications; 1 partial necrosis (large 24 × 8.5 cm transverse flap); 1 dehiscence (re-sutured).
- The authors conclude that DIEP can be reliably debulked inferior to the SIEV with minimal necrosis risk (12 flaps).

### Pre-expanded DIEP — Monsivais 2017<sup>[[17]](#ref17)</sup>

- Monsivais and colleagues describe pre-expansion as a staged option that may enlarge the coverage area; the proposed vascular and donor-site benefits remain unquantified for pelvic reconstruction.<sup>[[17]](#ref17)</sup>
- Requires staged procedures with risk of extrusion / infection.
- Useful for very large defects of perineum / vulva / buttock.

## DIEP vs TRAM / VRAM — Head-to-Head

### Qiu 2013 — TRAM vs DIEP for vaginal reconstruction after PE (n = 28)<sup>[[4]](#ref4)</sup>

| Outcome | DIEP (n = 7) | TRAM (n = 21) |
|---|---|---|
| Total flap loss | **0/7** | **3/21**, plus five partial losses |
| Mean harvest time | **63 min** | 105 min |
| Abdominal-wall complications | **0%** | 19% (despite mesh in all) |
| External-genitalia appearance | Normal | Normal |

The authors concluded that the DIEP flap seems to be an option that could replace the TRAM flap. This nonrandomized 7-versus-21 comparison cannot establish universal superiority or equate partial necrosis with total flap loss; the time and morbidity observations are specific to these cohorts.

### VRAM donor-site morbidity (Radwan SR 2021, n = 1,827)<sup>[[26]](#ref26)</sup>

| Outcome | Review-reported rate |
|---|---|
| Perineal flap morbidity | 27% |
| Complete flap loss | 1.8% |
| Donor-site morbidity | **15%** |
| Abdominal dehiscence | 5.5% |
| **Incisional hernia** | **3.3%** |
| Perineal hernia | 0.2% |

### VRAM vs gracilis (Eseme meta-analysis)<sup>[[27]](#ref27)</sup>

VRAM donor-site complications were 57.6% versus 16.0% for gracilis in the cited synthesis, and partial or total flap necrosis was similar. Pividori observed one major donor complication in 34 pedicled DIEP patients and no hernia during reported follow-up. These separate procedure cohorts provide no direct DIEP–VRAM morbidity comparison.<sup>[[25]](#ref25)[[27]](#ref27)</sup> A 2024 synthesis of female vulvoperineal reconstructions found no detected difference in short-term complication frequency between perforator (n = 276) and nonperforator (n = 1,564) cohorts; it was not a DIEP-specific comparison.<sup>[[35]](#ref35)</sup>

## Operative Efficiency

### Haddock & Teotia 2020 process analysis (n = 147 DIEP flaps, breast)<sup>[[28]](#ref28)</sup>

Eight maneuvers were timed: skin-to-perforator identification, perforator decision making, perforator dissection, pedicle dissection, flap harvest, preparation for microsurgery, venous anastomosis and arterial anastomosis.

| Surgeon configuration | Mean flap-harvest time |
|---|---|
| **Faculty surgeons** | **54.8 min** |
| Faculty + senior resident / fellow | 98.3 min |
| Supervised chief resident | 178.8 min (p &lt; 0.001) |

### Single-perforator antegrade technique — Feingold 2009<sup>[[29]](#ref29)</sup>

- Flap-harvest time on the order of one hour with antegrade technique.
- Significantly less stress; minimized fascial incisions.
- Strategy based on single-perforator harvest and discontinuous fascial incisions.

### Short-fasciotomy technique — Kim 2023 (n = 304 total: 124 short-fasciotomy, 180 conventional)<sup>[[23]](#ref23)</sup>

- Mean fascial incision: 6.6 cm (short-fasciotomy) versus 11.1 cm (conventional).
- Mean pedicle length 12.6 cm.
- No flap loss in either group.
- Rate of abdominal bulge / hernia significantly lower with short-fasciotomy.

### Minimally invasive harvest

| Approach | Series | Key result |
|---|---|---|
| Laparoscopic (Hivelin 2018)<sup>[[18]](#ref18)</sup> | Anatomic + clinical | Mean anterior rectus sheath incision **12 cm → 3 cm** (anatomic) / 5 cm (clinical); average laparoscopic harvest **50 min** in the anatomic subjects |
| Laparoscopic (Shakir 2020)<sup>[[30]](#ref30)</sup> | Clinical | In 33 breast-reconstruction patients (57 flaps), mean fascial incision was 2.0 cm; 60% recovered without narcotics and mean stay was 2.5 days. Two pedicle transections required perforator-to-pedicle anastomosis, and one flap required salvage for venous congestion |
| Robotic (Wittesaele 2022)<sup>[[19]](#ref19)</sup> | n = 10 | Mean robot-assisted operating time **86 min**; mean total operative time 479 min; no flap losses |

## Advantages

| Advantage | Detail |
|---|---|
| Muscle preservation | Rectus muscle generally preserved; fascia is incised around perforators and repaired, with donor-site morbidity still possible<sup>[[3]](#ref3)[[33]](#ref33)</sup> |
| Potentially lower donor-site morbidity | Pividori: 1/34 major donor event and no observed hernia in a pelvic series; breast cohorts show lower abdominal bulge/hernia with DIEP than pedicled TRAM, not a zero-risk guarantee<sup>[[25]](#ref25)[[33]](#ref33)</sup> |
| Small pelvic comparison | Qiu: no total loss in seven DIEP versus three total and five partial losses among 21 TRAM; the groups were nonrandomized<sup>[[4]](#ref4)</sup> |
| Efficiency in selected series | Qiu measured mean **63 vs 105 min** harvest (DIEP vs TRAM); Ferron reported a **median 60 min** for DIEP harvesting and vaginal reconstruction combined<sup>[[4]](#ref4)[[15]](#ref15)</sup> |
| Large skin island | In Fang’s mixed perineal-reconstruction series, one transverse flap measured 24 × 8.5 cm and developed distal partial necrosis<sup>[[14]](#ref14)</sup> |
| Versatile design | Vertical (tubed neovagina) or transverse (surface coverage); thinnable<sup>[[14]](#ref14)[[15]](#ref15)[[24]](#ref24)</sup> |
| Utilizes existing abdominal incision | In PE via midline laparotomy — same incision, no additional scar<sup>[[16]](#ref16)</sup> |
| Mappable vascular anatomy | DIEA present in virtually all patients; Meyerov reported a mean 2.9 ± 1.6 perforators per hemiabdomen in 180 breast-flap CTA patients; Bailey found that the dominant medial-row perforator lay within a 3-cm radius of the umbilicus on average across 36 dissected cadaveric and clinical abdominal flaps. Individual anatomy still requires assessment<sup>[[10]](#ref10)[[11]](#ref11)</sup> |
| Fills pelvic dead space | Tissue volume to obliterate dead space after PE. In a small retrospective series of primary neovagina reconstruction (mostly rectus flaps), pelvic abscess occurred in 0/16 versus 6/29 patients without reconstruction (P = 0.050)<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[32]](#ref32)</sup> |
| Donor-wall conservation | Muscle sparing may simplify closure, but a fascial incision is still made and some patients need reinforcement<sup>[[33]](#ref33)</sup> |
| Prior radiation in reported cohort | Pividori: 29/34 patients had prior radiotherapy; 11/34 had at least one major recipient-site complication. The series did not compare outcomes by radiation exposure<sup>[[25]](#ref25)</sup> |
| Pre-expansion possible | Tissue expansion ↑ coverage for very large defects<sup>[[17]](#ref17)</sup> |
| Observed pelvic outcomes | No perineal or incisional hernia in Pividori's 34-person series during its reported follow-up; not a lifetime zero-risk claim<sup>[[25]](#ref25)</sup> |

## Limitations

| Limitation | Detail |
|---|---|
| Technically demanding | Perforator dissection through the rectus muscle requires microsurgical expertise and a steep learning curve; failure to localize perforators led to conversion to VRAM in 2/13 (15%) in Ferron<sup>[[15]](#ref15)</sup> |
| Protective sensation | The abdominal skin island can lose its native cutaneous innervation during harvest; normal protective sensation in a reconstructed vulva or vagina should not be assumed.<sup>[[2]](#ref2)</sup> |
| Branching variability | Type III (trifurcating) DIEA occurred in 14% of Moon and Taylor’s cadaveric series; Rozen’s cadaveric study found the greatest transverse perforator distance with Type III, correlating with greater rectus muscle sacrifice during perforator dissection<sup>[[6]](#ref6)[[7]](#ref7)</sup> |
| Prior abdominal surgery | Prior TRAM / DIEP / vascular interventions may compromise the DIEA pedicle |
| Total / partial flap loss | Pividori: two total and one partial loss in 34 patients; **31/34 had neither**, but partial loss should not be counted as complete flap failure<sup>[[25]](#ref25)</sup> |
| Smoking / elevated BMI | In free DIEP breast cohorts, smoking was associated with more partial flap loss (3.2% versus 0.9% in 4,577 flaps) and wound-healing revision; fat necrosis requiring revision occurred in 9/496 normal-BMI, 30/452 overweight, and 8/170 obese patients. These cohorts do not quantify risk in pedicled pelvic DIEP.<sup>[[43]](#ref43)[[44]](#ref44)</sup> |
| Neovaginal sensibility and sexual function | Sensibility was not established by the cited small series; the two sexually active patients reported satisfactory penetrative intercourse.<sup>[[20]](#ref20)</sup> |
| Hair-bearing | Lower abdominal donor may be hair-bearing — relevant for tubed neovagina (intra-vaginal hair) |
