# Island Groin Flap (SCIA-Axial) and Its Perforator Evolution

The **island groin flap** is the axial fasciocutaneous flap pedicled on the **superficial circumflex iliac artery (SCIA)**, a named branch of the femoral artery. McGregor and Jackson's 1972 description of the groin flap introduced the **axial pattern flap** paradigm; Daniel and Taylor's 1973 free-flap transfer of the groin flap was the **first successful microsurgical free tissue transfer in history**.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[3]](#ref3)</sup> Its modern perforator-based evolution — the **Superficial Circumflex Iliac Artery Perforator (SCIP) flap** (Koshima 2004) — overcomes the traditional flap's short pedicle, small vessels, variable anatomy, and bulkiness, and Goh & Hong's 210-flap series established the suprafascial SCIP as **"the thinnest skin flap presently available."**<sup>[[5]](#ref5)[[6]](#ref6)[[7]](#ref7)</sup>

For the perforator deep-dive see [SCIP flap](/docs/foundations/surgical-principles/flaps/scip); for genital-context use see [Scrotal Reconstruction Techniques](/docs/surgical-techniques/04e-genital-reconstruction/scrotal-reconstruction).

## History

| Year | Contribution |
|---|---|
| **1972** | McGregor & Jackson — first description of the groin flap as an **axial pattern flap** on the SCIA.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup> |
| **1973** | Daniel & Taylor — **first successful free fasciocutaneous microvascular transfer** using the groin flap; the birth of microsurgical free tissue transfer.<sup>[[3]](#ref3)</sup> |
| Late 1980s–90s | Groin flap falls out of favor for free-flap use because of short pedicle, small vessels, variable anatomy, bulkiness.<sup>[[4]](#ref4)[[5]](#ref5)</sup> |
| **2004** | Koshima — **SCIP flap**: perforator-based, thinner, longer pedicle.<sup>[[6]](#ref6)</sup> |
| **2007** | Hsu — formally framed SCIP as the "evolution of the free groin flap."<sup>[[5]](#ref5)</sup> |
| **2015** | Goh & Hong — **largest SCIP series (210 flaps)**; "thinnest available skin flap"; new workhorse for moderate-size cutaneous defects.<sup>[[7]](#ref7)</sup> |

## Vascular Anatomy

### Source — SCIA

- Origin: **femoral artery in ~85%** (CTA, n = 142); shared common trunk with the superficial epigastric artery in ~18%; budding laterally **~1.5 cm below the inguinal ligament**.<sup>[[8]](#ref8)[[9]](#ref9)</sup>
- Origin from external iliac or deep femoral artery in the remainder.<sup>[[10]](#ref10)</sup>
- Courses laterally, parallel to the inguinal ligament, toward the **ASIS**.

### Bifurcation into superficial and deep branches

**1. Superficial (medial) branch.**

- Courses **superficial to the deep fascia** in the intra-adiposal plane.<sup>[[8]](#ref8)[[12]](#ref12)</sup>
- Originates from the SCIA in **94%** of cases; penetrates the deep fascia within a 4.2 × 2 cm oval **4.5 cm lateral and 1.5 cm superior to the pubic tubercle**.<sup>[[8]](#ref8)</sup>
- Direct dermal anchor in 56% / axial pattern in 44%; **91.9% of SCIP pedicles** show an axial pattern cephalad to the ASIS on ultrasound.<sup>[[8]](#ref8)[[12]](#ref12)</sup>
- Mean caliber **2.0 mm**; perforasome **178.6 cm²**.<sup>[[13]](#ref13)</sup>

**2. Deep (lateral) branch.**

- Courses **deep to the deep fascia** between sartorius and iliacus toward the ASIS.<sup>[[14]](#ref14)</sup>
- Bifurcation in **85%** of specimens within 2 cm of: **6 cm from the pubic tubercle toward the ASIS and 3 cm caudal**.<sup>[[14]](#ref14)</sup>
- Branches to **sartorius (up to 8 cm from ASIS) and iliac bone (up to 1.5 cm from the iliac crest)** — basis for composite osteocutaneous / musculocutaneous chimeric harvests.<sup>[[14]](#ref14)</sup>
- Mean caliber **2.1 mm**; perforasome **156.2 cm²**.<sup>[[13]](#ref13)</sup>
- Consistent **transverse branch ~25 mm below the ASIS** — a reliable surgical landmark for the deep branch.<sup>[[15]](#ref15)</sup>

**3. Inferolateral perforators (Zhang & Zeng 2023).** Form an "F" configuration with the main branch, extend directly into the dermal plexus, and enable **superthin flap harvest without microdissection defatting**; found ~2 cm caudal to the inguinal ligament.<sup>[[4]](#ref4)</sup>

### Anastomotic and reciprocal patterns

- Descending deep branches anastomose with the **ascending LCFA** in ~21% — a choke connection to the ALT territory.<sup>[[13]](#ref13)</sup>
- **SCIA ↔ SIEA reciprocal dominance** — when one is small, the other is typically large; critical for combined SCIP + SIEA / DIEP flap planning.<sup>[[16]](#ref16)</sup>

## Venous Drainage (Jeong 2026)

- **SCIV + venae comitantes** observed in **73%** (22/30) of cadaveric specimens; SCIV classified into two types by VC connection.<sup>[[16]](#ref16)</sup>
- Clinical series of 64 patients: only **3 perioperative complications** (2 total losses, 1 minor hematoma); **no venous congestion**.<sup>[[16]](#ref16)</sup>
- SCIV may drain into the deep circumflex iliac vein in some variants — important during harvest.<sup>[[17]](#ref17)</sup>

---

## Sensory Innervation

- **Lateral femoral cutaneous nerve (LFCN)** — principal sensory nerve of the lateral groin territory; passes beneath the inguinal ligament **2.1 ± 1.3 cm medial to the ASIS** (range 0.2–6.4 cm); absent in 6%.<sup>[[18]](#ref18)</sup> Can be included in the SCIP (particularly the deep-branch variant) for **sensate** reconstruction.<sup>[[19]](#ref19)</sup>
- **Ilioinguinal / genitofemoral** branches supply the medial groin / inguinal region with significant variability (bilateral symmetry only 40.6%).<sup>[[20]](#ref20)</sup>
- Turan 2023 — pedicled **sensate osteocutaneous** groin flap with LFCN inclusion for forearm / hand; protective 2-point discrimination achieved by 18 mo in 7/7 patients.<sup>[[21]](#ref21)</sup>

## Design Variants

| Variant | Key feature |
|---|---|
| **Traditional pedicled groin (McGregor)** | Skin paddle along SCIA axis to ASIS; **"two-finger" rule** (Chuang &gt; 200 flaps, no design-related complications); requires 3–4 wk pedicle division when used as a staged hand flap.<sup>[[5]](#ref5)[[22]](#ref22)</sup> |
| **Island groin (SCIA-axial)** | Completely islanded on SCIA — transposed or rotated without skin bridge. Workhorse for **scrotal / perineal** reconstruction.<sup>[[23]](#ref23)</sup> |
| **SCIP — superficial branch** | Suprafascial; thinnest variant; mean flap 86 cm² (17.5–216 cm²); 6.3 × 13.5 cm.<sup>[[7]](#ref7)</sup> Pedicle ~6.9 cm with intraflap dissection.<sup>[[24]](#ref24)</sup> |
| **SCIP — deep branch** | Subfascial; longer pedicle; access to sartorius / iliac bone for composite harvest.<sup>[[14]](#ref14)</sup> |
| **Extended SCIP (Fernandez-Garrido 2022)** | Medial / cranial to ASIS on superficial branch; exit through Hesselbach's fascia within a 21-mm-radius circle 18 mm medial and 17 mm distal to ASIS in 90%; success 92.3% in n = 39; complications 17.9%.<sup>[[25]](#ref25)</sup> |
| **SCIP propeller (Boissière 2019)** | 72 flaps for abdominopelvic defects; mean 20.2 × 8.2 cm; mean rotation 163.3°; 2 venous-congestion losses; **no donor complications**.<sup>[[26]](#ref26)</sup> |
| **Freestyle axial groin (Chao 2015)** | 35 patients; 2–4 SCIA branches identified intraoperatively (mean 3.09); 1 × 1.5 cm → 11 × 30 cm; 100% survival.<sup>[[27]](#ref27)</sup> |
| **Superthin / expanded (Atik 2006)** | Tissue expansion × 3 wk before harvest for **total scrotal coverage** matching scrotal-skin characteristics.<sup>[[28]](#ref28)</sup> |

## Operative Technique — Island Groin Flap for Scrotal Reconstruction (Sahai & Singh 30-yr)<sup>[[23]](#ref23)</sup>

**Preoperative.** Handheld Doppler maps the SCIA origin (~1.5 cm below the inguinal ligament, lateral to femoral artery); axis drawn from SCIA origin to ASIS; flap dimensions based on the defect.

**Elevation.**

1. Outline along the SCIA axis.
2. Begin incision at the **lateral border**.
3. Dissect lateral → medial in the **subfascial plane** (traditional island) or **suprafascial plane** (SCIP variant).
4. Identify and preserve the SCIA pedicle; ligate all other vessels.
5. **Completely island** the flap — divide the skin bridge to the groin.
6. Transpose / rotate onto the scrotal defect.
7. Inset around the testes to create a neoscrotum.

**Donor closure.** Primary in virtually all cases; scar concealed in the **inguinal crease**.<sup>[[5]](#ref5)[[23]](#ref23)</sup>

**Technical points.**

- Procedure performable under **spinal anesthesia**.<sup>[[23]](#ref23)</sup>
- For total scrotal loss (&gt; 50%), combine with **bilateral superior-medial pedicle thigh flaps**.<sup>[[23]](#ref23)</sup>
- Flap can be **thinned / defatted** to match scrotal skin thickness.<sup>[[28]](#ref28)</sup>

### Modern free SCIP (Hong / Goh)<sup>[[7]](#ref7)[[29]](#ref29)</sup>

1. **CTA or ultrasound** preoperatively to identify SCIA origin, bifurcation, and dominant perforator.<sup>[[12]](#ref12)</sup>
2. **Medial exploratory incision** first to identify the perforator.
3. **Suprafascial elevation** in a freestyle approach.
4. Identify and preserve the dominant perforator; design the flap around it.
5. **Intraflap dissection** can extend the pedicle to ~6.9 cm.<sup>[[24]](#ref24)</sup>
6. Microsurgical anastomosis — small vessels (&lt; 1 mm) often require **supermicrosurgical** technique.<sup>[[6]](#ref6)</sup>

## Clinical Applications

### 1. Scrotal reconstruction — the signature pedicled use

- **Sahai & Singh 2021** — 29 patients over 30 yr (25 Fournier's + 4 avulsion); **100% survival**; satisfactory results; &gt; 50% scrotal defects covered by a single groin flap, with bilateral superior-medial thigh flaps added for total scrotal loss.<sup>[[23]](#ref23)</sup>
- **B 2023** — 3 pedicled SCIP for perineo-scrotal Fournier's; **100% survival**; largest defect 22 × 10 cm; authors proposed SCIP as a potential "gold standard" for perineo-scrotal coverage.<sup>[[30]](#ref30)</sup>
- **Atik 2006** — expanded superthin groin for total scrotal loss; thin, durable, matches scrotal skin.<sup>[[28]](#ref28)</sup>
- Alammar 2026 SR of Fournier's flap reconstruction (619 patients) — groin-based flaps among the commonly utilized options; overall flap-loss rate **1.6%**.<sup>[[31]](#ref31)</sup>

### 2. Hand and upper-extremity reconstruction (pedicled)

- **Goertz 2012** — 85 patients (49 examined); 1 flap loss (1.2%); mean 4.6 operations per patient; **82% would repeat**; mean DASH 23 ± 17; pedicle division at 24 ± 5 d.<sup>[[32]](#ref32)</sup>
- **Al-Qattan 2016** — current indications: complex defects in children &lt; 2 yr, microsurgery contraindication, salvage when free flaps fail.<sup>[[33]](#ref33)</sup>
- **Turan 2023** — pedicled **sensate osteocutaneous** groin for forearm / hand / thumb; 7/7 survival; bone 1 × 1.5 × 3.5 → 1 × 1.5 × 5 cm; protective sensation at 18 mo.<sup>[[21]](#ref21)</sup>

### 3. Lower-extremity reconstruction (free SCIP)

- **Goh & Hong 2015** — 210 SCIP flaps (176 lower limb); total loss **4.8%**; debulking 2.4%; only 2 donor complications.<sup>[[7]](#ref7)</sup>
- **Pereira 2023** — 101 SCIP flaps (80 lower / 21 upper); total loss **5.9%**; debulking 6.9%; mean flap 90.5 cm² (25–212 cm²).<sup>[[34]](#ref34)</sup>

### 4. Head and neck reconstruction (free SCIP)

- **Scaglioni 2022** — 22 patients (17 simple + 5 chimeric); **95.5% success**; minor donor complications 9%.<sup>[[35]](#ref35)</sup>

### 5. Vulvar reconstruction

- **Gentileschi 2022** — 34 SCIP flaps in 32 patients post-vulvar-cancer surgery; SCIP **always feasible**; mean flap 128.8 cm²; only 3 wound complications; minimal donor morbidity.<sup>[[36]](#ref36)</sup>

### 6. Perineal reconstruction

- **Boissière 2019** — 72 SCIP propeller flaps for abdominopelvic defects; mean 20.2 × 8.2 cm; 2 flap necroses; no donor complications.<sup>[[26]](#ref26)</sup>
- **Grishkevich 2010** — groin flap for **postburn perineal obliteration**: simultaneous perineal, inguinal, anal reconstruction; in children, the flap grows preventing contracture recurrence.<sup>[[37]](#ref37)</sup>

## Composite and Chimeric Configurations

- **Osteocutaneous SCIP.** Deep branch supplies the iliac bone (up to 1.5 cm from crest) and cephalad sartorius (up to 8 cm from ASIS).<sup>[[14]](#ref14)</sup> Zubler 2023 — 6 osteocutaneous SCIPs; mean bone 4.9 × 3 cm, skin 14.3 × 6.3 cm; all survived; one venous-congestion revision.<sup>[[47]](#ref47)</sup> Walczak 2025 — 3 osteocutaneous SCIPs for maxillary reconstruction.<sup>[[48]](#ref48)</sup>
- **Chimeric groin (Chao 2016).** 18 patients in C-C, M-C, O-C configurations; SCIA gives 3–4 identifiable branches with visible pulsation enabling multiple independent paddles; all 18 flaps survived.<sup>[[11]](#ref11)</sup>
- **Quadruple-component SCIP (Yamamoto 2016).** Sartorius muscle + deep fascia + inguinal lymph node + skin/fat on a single SCIA pedicle — complex ankle reconstruction with exposed total-ankle-arthroplasty implant.<sup>[[19]](#ref19)</sup>
- **SCIP + SIEA / DIEP (Yoshimatsu 2020).** For defects up to 20 × 20 cm; single arterial anastomosis when SCIA and SIEA share a common trunk; two anastomoses otherwise.<sup>[[49]](#ref49)</sup>

## Complications

**Flap-related.** Total loss 0–5.9% across series (highest in free SCIP extremity reconstruction); **venous congestion** is the principal cause of failure (SCIV-drainage anatomy is key); partial necrosis is uncommon; debulking required in 2.4–6.9% of modern SCIP series.<sup>[[7]](#ref7)[[16]](#ref16)[[34]](#ref34)</sup>

**Donor site.** Consistently the major advantage — complications in only 1.0–4.0% of large SCIP series.<sup>[[7]](#ref7)[[34]](#ref34)</sup> No functional impairment (sartorius / iliacus preserved unless intentionally harvested). Concealed inguinal-crease scar. Modern SCIP harvest reduces LFCN-territory numbness vs the traditional groin flap.<sup>[[5]](#ref5)</sup>

**Lymphedema.** Theoretical concern given inguinal-node proximity; SCIP harvest does **not** perform node dissection and lymphedema has not been reported as a significant SCIP complication, unlike formal inguinal lymphadenectomy (12–55%).<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[50]](#ref50)[[51]](#ref51)[[52]](#ref52)</sup> Scaglioni 2023 — novel pedicled SCIP + **lymphovenous anastomosis** between recipient-site lymphatic vessels and flap superficial veins; simultaneous defect reconstruction + lymph flow-through to reduce lymphatic complications.<sup>[[53]](#ref53)</sup>

## Advantages

1. **Historic reliability** — 50+ years of clinical experience; the first free flap in history.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[3]](#ref3)</sup>
2. **Thinnest skin flap available** (suprafascial SCIP).<sup>[[7]](#ref7)</sup>
3. **Concealed donor scar** in the inguinal crease.<sup>[[5]](#ref5)[[23]](#ref23)</sup>
4. **Primary donor closure** in virtually all cases.<sup>[[5]](#ref5)[[7]](#ref7)</sup>
5. **Minimal donor morbidity** — no functional impairment, no lymphedema.<sup>[[6]](#ref6)[[7]](#ref7)</sup>
6. **Non-hair-bearing skin** for hand, foot, genital reconstruction.<sup>[[5]](#ref5)</sup>
7. **Composite / chimeric capability** — iliac bone, sartorius muscle, inguinal lymph node, deep fascia, multiple independent skin paddles.<sup>[[11]](#ref11)[[14]](#ref14)[[19]](#ref19)[[47]](#ref47)</sup>
8. **Versatile configurations** — pedicled (island, propeller, V-Y), free, superthin, osteocutaneous, chimeric.<sup>[[7]](#ref7)[[11]](#ref11)[[23]](#ref23)[[26]](#ref26)</sup>
9. **Spinal anesthesia possible** for pedicled use.<sup>[[23]](#ref23)</sup>
10. **Large perforasome** (156–179 cm²) supports flaps up to 25 × 8 cm.<sup>[[5]](#ref5)[[13]](#ref13)</sup>

---

## Limitations

1. **Short pedicle** (3–4 cm traditional; ~6.9 cm with intraflap dissection; up to 13 cm with full SCIA dissection) — still shorter than ALT or DIEP.<sup>[[4]](#ref4)[[5]](#ref5)[[24]](#ref24)</sup>
2. **Small vessel diameter** (~ 1 mm at perforator) — supermicrosurgery often required as a free flap.<sup>[[6]](#ref6)</sup>
3. **Variable anatomy** — SCIA origin, bifurcation, and perforator distribution vary; CTA / ultrasound recommended.<sup>[[8]](#ref8)[[12]](#ref12)[[13]](#ref13)</sup>
4. **Bulkiness** of the traditional subfascial groin flap in obese patients (eliminated by the suprafascial SCIP).<sup>[[4]](#ref4)[[5]](#ref5)</sup>
5. **Hand-to-groin immobilization** 3–4 wk for pedicled hand reconstruction; mean 4.6 operations including thinning and revisions.<sup>[[32]](#ref32)[[33]](#ref33)</sup>
6. **Limited volume** — thin for resurfacing but insufficient for deep dead-space obliteration or total breast reconstruction.<sup>[[6]](#ref6)[[7]](#ref7)</sup>
7. **Inguinal-node proximity** — care to avoid disrupting lymphatic channels during dissection.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup>
