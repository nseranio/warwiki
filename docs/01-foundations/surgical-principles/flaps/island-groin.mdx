# Island Groin Flap (SCIA-Axial) and Its Perforator Evolution

The **island groin flap** is an axial fasciocutaneous flap pedicled on the superficial circumflex iliac artery (SCIA), usually a branch of the femoral artery. The 1972 groin-flap descriptions helped establish axial-pattern skin-flap design. The 1973 Taylor–Daniel free ilio-femoral transfer arose from the same region but was based on the superficial inferior epigastric artery (SIEA), not the SCIA, according to a historical clarification of their original report. Graziano and colleagues call it the first fasciocutaneous vascularized free tissue transfer. It was not an SCIA island-flap operation.<sup>[[2]](#ref2)[[44]](#ref44)</sup> The superficial circumflex iliac artery perforator (SCIP) flap (Koshima 2004) is a distinct, perforator-based evolution. Goh and colleagues described their suprafascial SCIP as the thinnest skin flap presently available in a 210-flap series. That is their characterization, not a comparison with every flap design.<sup>[[4]](#ref4)[[5]](#ref5)[[6]](#ref6)</sup>

For the perforator deep-dive see [SCIP flap](/docs/foundations/surgical-principles/flaps/scip); for genital-context use see [Scrotal Reconstruction Techniques](/docs/surgical-techniques/04e-genital-reconstruction/scrotal-reconstruction).

## History

| Year | Contribution |
|---|---|
| **1972** | McGregor and Jackson described the groin flap as an axial pattern flap on the SCIA.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup> |
| **1973** | Taylor & Daniel first fasciocutaneous free tissue transfer, based on the SIEA after the SCIA proved too small for anastomosis; distinct from the SCIA-based groin flap.<sup>[[2]](#ref2)[[44]](#ref44)</sup> |
| Following decades | The groin flap loses popularity for free-flap use because of its short pedicle, small vessels, variable anatomy and bulk.<sup>[[3]](#ref3)[[4]](#ref4)</sup> |
| **2004** | Koshima described the SCIP flap, nourished by a perforator of the SCIA system with a short (3–4 cm) vascular segment. Reported advantages were shorter flap elevation, possible primary thinning and a concealed donor site.<sup>[[5]](#ref5)</sup> |
| **2007** | Hsu and colleagues presented the SCIP flap as an evolution of the free groin flap in 12 patients, with a reported arterial pedicle of 3–13 cm.<sup>[[4]](#ref4)</sup> |
| **2015** | Goh and colleagues reviewed 210 selected SCIP flaps and proposed a very thin resurfacing option for moderate-size cutaneous defects.<sup>[[6]](#ref6)</sup> |

## Vascular Anatomy

### Source — SCIA

- Origin: the femoral artery in 84.8% (CTA, 142 patients). In an 11-limb cadaver study the SCIA and superficial epigastric artery generally arose through a common trunk budding laterally from the femoral artery about 1.5 cm below the inguinal ligament.<sup>[[7]](#ref7)[[8]](#ref8)</sup>
- In a 100-cadaver study, 81.8% of SCIAs originated from the femoral artery, with the remainder arising elsewhere, for example from the external iliac artery.<sup>[[9]](#ref9)</sup>
- Courses laterally, parallel to the inguinal ligament, toward the ASIS.

### Bifurcation into superficial and deep branches

**1. Superficial (medial) branch.**

- Courses superficial to the deep fascia in the intra-adiposal plane.<sup>[[7]](#ref7)[[11]](#ref11)</sup>
- The medial perforator originates from the SCIA in 94% of cases and penetrates the deep fascia within a 4.2 × 2 cm oval 4.5 cm lateral and 1.5 cm superior to the pubic tubercle.<sup>[[7]](#ref7)</sup>
- After it passes the superficial fascia, the medial perforator anchors directly into skin in 56% and travels in an axial pattern beyond the ASIS in 44% (CTA). An ultrasound mapping series reported an axial pattern extending cephalad to the ASIS in 91.9% of SCIP pedicles.<sup>[[7]](#ref7)[[11]](#ref11)</sup>
- In a cadaver study of 14 flaps, the major perforator had a mean caliber of 2.0 mm and the perforasome a mean area of 178.6 cm².<sup>[[12]](#ref12)</sup>

**2. Deep (lateral) branch.**

- Courses deep to the deep fascia between sartorius and iliacus toward the ASIS.<sup>[[13]](#ref13)</sup>
- The bifurcation lies within 2 cm of a point 6 cm from the pubic tubercle toward the ASIS and 3 cm caudal in 85% of specimens.<sup>[[13]](#ref13)</sup>
- Its branches perfuse the sartorius (up to 8 cm from the ASIS) and the iliac bone (up to 1.5 cm from the iliac crest), which is the basis for composite osteocutaneous and musculocutaneous chimeric harvests.<sup>[[13]](#ref13)</sup>
- The major perforator had a mean caliber of 2.1 mm and the perforasome a mean area of 156.2 cm² (14 cadaver flaps).<sup>[[12]](#ref12)</sup>
- A transverse branch of the deep branch was found in all 20 dissected groins, on average 25.5 ± 13.0 mm (range 5–50 mm) caudal to the ASIS. It served as a landmark for deep-branch dissection in 27 reconstructions.<sup>[[14]](#ref14)</sup>

**3. Inferolateral perforators (Zhang & Zeng 2023).** In the authors' experience these perforators form an "F" configuration with the main branch and extend directly into the dermal plexus, so superthin flaps can be harvested without microdissection defatting. An exploratory incision about 2 cm caudal to the inguinal ligament finds them.<sup>[[3]](#ref3)</sup>

### Anastomotic and reciprocal patterns

- Descending branches of the deep branch anastomosed with the ascending branch of the lateral circumflex femoral artery in 3 of 14 cadaver flaps (about 21%), a connection to the ALT territory.<sup>[[12]](#ref12)</sup>
- The SCIA and SIEA show reciprocal dominance, which matters when a SCIP flap is combined with an SIEA or DIEP flap.<sup>[[15]](#ref15)</sup>

## Venous Drainage (Jeong 2026)

- The superficial circumflex iliac vein (SCIV) and accompanying venae comitantes were observed in 73% (22/30) of cadaveric specimens. The SCIV was classified into two types by its connection with the venae comitantes.<sup>[[15]](#ref15)</sup>
- In a clinical series of 64 patients, three perioperative complications occurred (two total flap losses and one minor hematoma) with no venous congestion.<sup>[[15]](#ref15)</sup>
- In some dissections the SCIV drains into the deep circumflex iliac vein, which matters during harvest.<sup>[[16]](#ref16)</sup>

## Sensory Innervation

- The lateral femoral cutaneous nerve (LFCN) is the principal sensory nerve of the lateral groin territory. It passes beneath the inguinal ligament 2.1 ± 1.3 cm medial to the ASIS (range 0.2–6.4 cm) and was not found in 6% of cadavers.<sup>[[17]](#ref17)</sup> It can be included in the SCIP flap, particularly the deep-branch variant, for sensate reconstruction; an LFCN flap accompanied the deep-branch SCIP in four of 27 reconstructions in one series.<sup>[[14]](#ref14)</sup>
- The ilioinguinal and genitofemoral nerves supply the medial groin and inguinal region with marked variability. A cadaver study described four cutaneous branching patterns, bilaterally symmetric in 40.6% of cadavers.<sup>[[19]](#ref19)</sup>
- Turan 2023 used a pedicled sensate osteocutaneous groin flap with LFCN inclusion for forearm, hand and thumb defects. All seven flaps survived and protective sensation was obtained by 18 months.<sup>[[20]](#ref20)</sup>

## Design Variants

| Variant | Key feature |
|---|---|
| **Traditional pedicled groin (McGregor)** | Skin paddle along the SCIA axis to the ASIS, designed with the "rule of two finger widths" (Chuang: over 200 pedicled and free flaps without design-related complications). Requires pedicle division at about 3–4 weeks when used as a staged hand flap.<sup>[[4]](#ref4)[[21]](#ref21)</sup> |
| **Island groin (SCIA-axial)** | Completely islanded on the SCIA and transposed or rotated without a skin bridge. Described for scrotal and perineal reconstruction.<sup>[[22]](#ref22)</sup> |
| **SCIP — superficial branch** | Suprafascial; described by Goh as the thinnest variant; mean flap 86 cm² (17.5–216 cm²), 6.3 × 13.5 cm.<sup>[[6]](#ref6)</sup> Mean pedicle 6.9 cm (range 6–8 cm) with a long-pedicle design.<sup>[[23]](#ref23)</sup> |
| **SCIP — deep branch** | Subfascial; longer pedicle; access to sartorius / iliac bone for composite harvest.<sup>[[13]](#ref13)</sup> |
| **Extended SCIP (Fernandez-Garrido 2022)** | Designed medial and cranial to the ASIS on the superficial branch. The branch exits through Hesselbach's fascia within a 21-mm-radius circle 18 mm medial and 17 mm distal to the ASIS in 90%. Reconstruction succeeded in 92.3% of 39 patients; complications occurred in 17.9%.<sup>[[24]](#ref24)</sup> |
| **SCIP propeller (Boissière 2019)** | 72 flaps for abdominopelvic defects; mean 20.2 × 8.2 cm; mean rotation 163.3°; two flaps lost to venous congestion; no donor-site complications.<sup>[[25]](#ref25)</sup> |
| **Freestyle axial groin (Chao 2015)** | 35 patients (34 free, 1 pedicled); 2–4 SCIA branches identified intraoperatively (mean 3.09); flaps from 1 × 1.5 cm to 11 × 30 cm; all survived.<sup>[[26]](#ref26)</sup> |
| **Superthin / expanded (Atik 2006)** | Tissue expansion for 3 weeks before harvest in three patients with total scrotal loss; the expanded flap was described as thin and durable.<sup>[[27]](#ref27)</sup> |

## Operative Technique — Island Groin Flap for Scrotal Reconstruction (Sahai & Singh 30-yr)<sup>[[22]](#ref22)</sup>

**Preoperative.** Handheld Doppler maps the SCIA origin, about 1.5 cm below the inguinal ligament and lateral to the femoral artery. The axis is drawn from the SCIA origin to the ASIS, and flap dimensions follow the defect.

**Elevation.**

1. Outline along the SCIA axis.
2. Begin the incision at the lateral border.
3. Dissect from lateral to medial in the subfascial plane (traditional island) or the suprafascial plane (SCIP variant).
4. Identify and preserve the SCIA pedicle; ligate all other vessels.
5. Completely island the flap by dividing the skin bridge to the groin.
6. Transpose / rotate onto the scrotal defect.
7. Inset around the testes to create a neoscrotum.

**Donor closure.** Primary in virtually all cases, with the scar concealed in the inguinal crease.<sup>[[4]](#ref4)[[22]](#ref22)</sup>

**Technical points.**

- The operation was performed under spinal anesthesia in this series.<sup>[[22]](#ref22)</sup>
- A single groin flap covered defects of more than 50% of the scrotum. For total scrotal loss, the authors combined it with two superior-medial-pedicle thigh flaps.<sup>[[22]](#ref22)</sup>
- The flap can be thinned to match scrotal skin thickness; Atik used tissue expansion for this purpose.<sup>[[27]](#ref27)</sup>

### Modern free SCIP (Hong / Goh)<sup>[[6]](#ref6)[[28]](#ref28)</sup>

1. Obtain CTA or ultrasound preoperatively to identify the SCIA origin, bifurcation and dominant perforator.<sup>[[11]](#ref11)</sup>
2. **Medial exploratory incision** first to identify the perforator.
3. **Suprafascial elevation** in a freestyle approach.
4. Identify and preserve the dominant perforator; design the flap around it.
5. **Intraflap dissection** can extend the pedicle to a mean of 6.9 cm.<sup>[[23]](#ref23)</sup>
6. Perform the microsurgical anastomosis. Small vessels (&lt; 1 mm) often require supermicrosurgical technique.<sup>[[5]](#ref5)</sup>

## Clinical Applications

### 1. Scrotal reconstruction — the signature pedicled use

- Sahai and Singh (2021) reported 29 selected patients (25 with Fournier's gangrene and 4 with avulsion injuries), all with good aesthetic results. One groin flap covered defects of more than half the scrotum. Flap survival was not reported as a separate endpoint.<sup>[[22]](#ref22)</sup>
- BS and colleagues (2023) used a pedicled SCIP flap in three patients with perineoscrotal defects after Fournier's gangrene. All flaps survived without significant complications. The authors suggested the flap could become a preferred option, which three cases cannot establish.<sup>[[29]](#ref29)</sup>
- Atik (2006) used an expanded superthin groin flap in three patients with total scrotal loss and described it as thin and durable.<sup>[[27]](#ref27)</sup>
- Alammar (2026) reviewed flap reconstruction after Fournier's gangrene (619 patients, 625 flaps). Medial thigh, pudendal thigh, ALT and gracilis flaps were the most common designs, and flap loss was 1.6% pooled across flap types. The figure is not specific to the island groin flap.<sup>[[30]](#ref30)</sup>

### 2. Hand and upper-extremity reconstruction (pedicled)

- Goertz (2012) treated 85 patients (49 examined) with pedicled groin flaps. One flap was lost (1.2%), patients had a mean of 4.6 operations, and 82% of those examined would undergo the procedure again. Mean DASH score was 23 ± 17 and pedicle division took place at 24 ± 5 days.<sup>[[31]](#ref31)</sup>
- Al-Qattan (2016) listed current indications for pedicled groin and abdominal flaps in the hand: complex defects in children younger than 2 years, digital stump coverage before toe-to-hand transfer, high-voltage electric burns, mutilating hand injuries, length preservation in multiple digital amputations and multiple defects of the digits, hand or forearm.<sup>[[32]](#ref32)</sup>
- Turan (2023) used the pedicled sensate osteocutaneous groin flap for forearm, hand and thumb defects. All seven flaps survived, bone segments ranged from 1 × 1.5 × 3.5 to 1 × 1.5 × 5 cm, and protective sensation was obtained at 18 months.<sup>[[20]](#ref20)</sup>

### 3. Lower-extremity reconstruction (free SCIP)

- Goh (2015) reported 210 SCIP flaps (176 lower limb). Total loss occurred in 10/210 (4.8%), debulking in five patients (2.4%) and donor-site complications in two.<sup>[[6]](#ref6)</sup>
- Pereira (2023) reported 101 SCIP flaps (80 lower limb, 21 upper limb). Total loss occurred in 6 flaps (5.9%), debulking in 7 patients (6.9%) and donor-site complications in 4; mean flap size was 90.5 cm² (25–212 cm²).<sup>[[33]](#ref33)</sup>

### 4. Head and neck reconstruction (free SCIP)

- Scaglioni (2022) treated 22 patients (17 simple and 5 chimeric flaps), 21 (95.5%) successfully, with minor donor-site complications in two (9%). Mean follow-up was 5.3 months.<sup>[[34]](#ref34)</sup>

### 5. Vulvar reconstruction

- Gentileschi (2022) used 34 SCIP flaps in 32 patients after vulvar-cancer surgery. The flap was feasible in every patient, mean flap size was 128.8 cm², and three patients had wound complications. Mean follow-up was 30 months.<sup>[[35]](#ref35)</sup>

### 6. Perineal reconstruction

- Boissière (2019) used 72 SCIP propeller flaps for abdominopelvic defects (mean 20.2 × 8.2 cm). Two flaps necrosed and there were no donor-site complications.<sup>[[25]](#ref25)</sup>
- Grishkevich (2010) used the groin flap for postburn perineal obliteration in four patients, with simultaneous perineal, inguinal and anal reconstruction. In children the flap continues to grow, which prevents contracture recurrence.<sup>[[36]](#ref36)</sup>

## Composite and Chimeric Configurations

- **Osteocutaneous SCIP.** The deep branch supplies the iliac bone (up to 1.5 cm from crest) and cephalad sartorius (up to 8 cm from ASIS).<sup>[[13]](#ref13)</sup> Zubler (2023) reported six osteocutaneous SCIP flaps with a mean bone segment of 4.9 × 3 cm and skin paddle of 14.3 × 6.3 cm. All survived and one needed emergency revision for venous congestion.<sup>[[37]](#ref37)</sup> Walczak (2025) described osteocutaneous SCIP flaps for maxillary reconstruction.<sup>[[38]](#ref38)</sup>
- **Chimeric groin (Chao 2016).** Eighteen patients received cutaneous-cutaneous (12), musculocutaneous (1) or osteocutaneous (5) chimeric groin free flaps. Three to four SCIA branches (mean 3.33) could be used for flap design, and all flaps survived.<sup>[[10]](#ref10)</sup>
- **Quadruple-component SCIP (Yamamoto 2016).** A case report of sartorius muscle, deep fascia, inguinal lymph node and skin and fat on one SCIA pedicle for an ankle defect with an exposed total-ankle-arthroplasty implant.<sup>[[18]](#ref18)</sup>
- **SCIP with SIEA or DIEP (Yoshimatsu 2020).** Six patients with defects larger than 113 cm² (mean 18.5 × 15.7 cm) were covered by combining a SCIP flap with an SIEA flap, or with a DIEP flap when the SIEA was smaller than 0.5 mm.<sup>[[39]](#ref39)</sup>

## Complications

**Flap-related.** Total loss and revision rates vary across noncomparable pedicled and free series. Goh reported 10/210 total losses and five debulkings in a mixed-site SCIP cohort, and Pereira 6/101 total losses. In Jeong's separate 64-patient series two flaps were lost and no venous congestion was recorded, so a principal cause of failure cannot be inferred from it.<sup>[[6]](#ref6)[[15]](#ref15)[[33]](#ref33)</sup>

**Donor site.** Donor-site complications occurred in about 1.0% (2/210) and 4.0% (4/101) of two large SCIP series.<sup>[[6]](#ref6)[[33]](#ref33)</sup> The sartorius and iliacus are preserved unless intentionally harvested, and the scar is concealed in the inguinal crease. Hsu and colleagues reported a smaller area of donor-site numbness with the SCIP flap than with the free groin flap.<sup>[[4]](#ref4)</sup>

**Lymphedema.** Inguinal lymphatic channels should not be disrupted during harvest; SCIP harvest is not a formal node dissection. Lymphedema rates from melanoma lymphadenectomy populations are not SCIP rates, and the absence of events in a small series does not show zero risk.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[40]](#ref40)[[41]](#ref41)[[42]](#ref42)</sup> Scaglioni (2023) described a preliminary pedicled SCIP flap combined with lymphovenous anastomosis; a preventive effect has not been compared against outcomes without it.<sup>[[43]](#ref43)</sup>

## Advantages

1. **Long clinical history.** SCIA groin-flap experience dates from the 1970s and is distinct from the Taylor–Daniel SIEA free transfer.<sup>[[2]](#ref2)[[44]](#ref44)</sup>
2. **Thin suprafascial option (SCIP).** Goh describes the SCIP as the thinnest skin flap presently available, an author conclusion from a selected series.<sup>[[6]](#ref6)</sup>
3. **Concealed donor scar** in the inguinal crease.<sup>[[4]](#ref4)[[22]](#ref22)</sup>
4. **Primary donor closure** in virtually all cases.<sup>[[4]](#ref4)[[6]](#ref6)</sup>
5. **Potentially limited donor morbidity** with appropriate design; no series can guarantee no numbness or lymphedema.<sup>[[5]](#ref5)[[6]](#ref6)</sup>
6. **Non-hair-bearing skin** for hand, foot, genital reconstruction.<sup>[[4]](#ref4)</sup>
7. **Composite and chimeric capability.** The flap can carry iliac bone, sartorius muscle, inguinal lymph node, deep fascia and multiple independent skin paddles.<sup>[[10]](#ref10)[[13]](#ref13)[[18]](#ref18)[[37]](#ref37)</sup>
8. **Versatile configurations.** Pedicled (island, propeller, V-Y), free, superthin, osteocutaneous and chimeric configurations have been described.<sup>[[6]](#ref6)[[10]](#ref10)[[22]](#ref22)[[25]](#ref25)</sup>
9. **Spinal anesthesia possible** for pedicled use.<sup>[[22]](#ref22)</sup>
10. **Large perforasome** (156–179 cm²) supports flaps up to 25 × 8 cm.<sup>[[4]](#ref4)[[12]](#ref12)</sup>

---

## Limitations

1. **Short pedicle.** The perforator segment is 3–4 cm in Koshima's description, the mean is 6.9 cm with a long-pedicle design, and Hsu reported pedicles of 3–13 cm. This remains shorter than the ALT or DIEP pedicle.<sup>[[4]](#ref4)[[5]](#ref5)[[23]](#ref23)</sup>
2. **Small vessel diameter.** Koshima described anastomosis of vessels smaller than 1.0 mm, and cadaveric perforator calibers of about 2 mm have also been measured. Supermicrosurgery is often required for free transfer.<sup>[[5]](#ref5)[[12]](#ref12)</sup>
3. **Variable anatomy.** SCIA origin, bifurcation and perforator distribution vary, and CTA or ultrasound mapping has been used to define them.<sup>[[7]](#ref7)[[11]](#ref11)[[12]](#ref12)</sup>
4. **Bulkiness** of the traditional subfascial groin flap in obese patients, which the suprafascial SCIP reduces.<sup>[[3]](#ref3)[[4]](#ref4)</sup>
5. **Hand-to-groin immobilization** 3–4 wk for pedicled hand reconstruction; mean 4.6 operations including thinning and revisions.<sup>[[31]](#ref31)[[32]](#ref32)</sup>
6. **Limited volume.** The flap is thin for resurfacing but insufficient for deep dead-space obliteration or total breast reconstruction.<sup>[[5]](#ref5)[[6]](#ref6)</sup>
7. **Inguinal-node proximity.** Dissection should avoid disrupting lymphatic channels.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup>
