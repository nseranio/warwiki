# Rhomboid (Limberg) Flap

The **rhomboid (Limberg) flap** is a local transposition flap designed to redistribute closure tension when straight-line repair would be difficult or distort nearby structures. Limberg's geometric design is widely used in sacrococcygeal pilonidal surgery and can be adapted to selected vulvar, genital, and perineal defects when adjacent tissue and its perfusion are suitable.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[2]](#ref2)</sup>

This page is the foundations-level deep dive. Site-specific pages — vulvar reconstruction, scrotal reconstruction, Fournier's reconstruction — link back here.

---

## Geometric Principle

The Limberg flap is built around a **rhombus (equilateral parallelogram)** with angles of **60° and 120°**. The defect is either created as or converted into a rhomboid shape, and the flap is designed as follows:<sup>[[2]](#ref2)[[3]](#ref3)[[4]](#ref4)</sup>

1. The **short diagonal** of the rhombus is extended by a length equal to one side of the rhombus.
2. A second incision is made from the end of this extension, **parallel to the adjacent side**, also equal in length to one side.
3. This creates a second rhombus adjacent to the defect.
4. The flap is elevated, **transposed** into the defect, and the donor site is closed primarily.

**Four possible Limberg flaps** can be designed around a rhomboid defect. Select the donor site by available skin laxity, the **line of maximal extensibility (LME)**, scar orientation, local perfusion, and the structures that must not be displaced. A geometric option is not automatically the safest option in every anatomic field.<sup>[[2]](#ref2)[[3]](#ref3)</sup>

### Key geometric properties

| Property | Detail |
|---|---|
| **Geometric proportions** | In the standard design, flap sides are planned from the rhombus side length; this is a design rule, not proof of vascular safety.<sup>[[2]](#ref2)[[4]](#ref4)</sup> |
| **Perfusion** | The usual local cutaneous flap depends on its tissue base and local vascularity. Assess prior incisions, radiation, undermining, and inset tension rather than applying a universal base-to-length cutoff.<sup>[[5]](#ref5)[[6]](#ref6)</sup> |
| **Stress redistribution** | In a **finite-element model**, Rajabi and colleagues' optimized design reduced peak von Mises stress by **43%** versus standard Limberg and **53%** versus Dufourmentel. These are simulated stresses, not measured clinical complication reductions.<sup>[[7]](#ref7)</sup> |

### Limberg vs. Dufourmentel

The **Dufourmentel flap** (1962) is the principal modification of the Limberg:<sup>[[8]](#ref8)[[9]](#ref9)</sup>

- Uses a broader base than the classical Limberg design; actual perfusion still depends on the patient and operative field
- Accommodates defects with angles other than the strict 60° / 120° of the Limberg design
- A 2024 Cochrane review's **direct modified-versus-standard Limberg comparison** in pilonidal disease drew on **one trial of 204 participants**; estimates for recurrence and wound outcomes were imprecise, so the review did not establish equivalence.<sup>[[10]](#ref10)</sup>
- Both are classified as "rhombic transposition flaps" and share the same fundamental geometric principle

## Pilonidal Sinus Disease — the signature application

The Limberg flap is most extensively studied in **sacrococcygeal pilonidal sinus disease (PSD)**, an off-midline closure application. After rhomboid excision, a neighboring cutaneous/fasciocutaneous transposition flap is moved into the defect; this is **not** a gluteus-maximus muscle rotation flap.<sup>[[11]](#ref11)[[15]](#ref15)</sup>

### Outcomes in PSD

| Outcome | Result |
|---|---|
| **Network meta-analysis of 39 RCTs (5,061 patients)** | Modified Limberg was among favorable PSD procedures for recurrence in an indirect treatment network; rankings are not proof it is best for every patient.<sup>[[12]](#ref12)</sup> |
| **Meta-analysis of 11,730 RCT patients** | Limberg / Dufourmentel recurrence **0.6%** at 12 mo and **1.8%** at 24 mo<sup>[[13]](#ref13)</sup> |
| **Global series** | Limberg / Dufourmentel recurrence **0.3%** at 12 mo (95% CI 0.2–0.4)<sup>[[14]](#ref14)</sup> |
| **Arnous RCT (n = 60)** | Limberg flap vs. primary midline closure: **0% vs. 20%** recurrence (p &lt; 0.02); complications were **30% vs. 43.3%** (p = 0.4), while cosmetic satisfaction favored primary closure.<sup>[[15]](#ref15)</sup> |
| **Karydakis vs. Limberg meta (15 RCTs, 2,000 patients)** | No significant differences in pain, LOS, healing time, or recurrence<sup>[[11]](#ref11)</sup> |

Wound-complication rates with Limberg flap in PSD range from **0% to 26.5%** — primarily hematoma / seroma and dehiscence from the large area of tissue mobilization.<sup>[[11]](#ref11)</sup>

PSD is a colorectal indication. Its outcome rates cannot be transferred to genital or perineal reconstruction, although its design and off-midline closure lessons are relevant.

## Urologic Applications

### Epispadias-exstrophy complex — penile elongation and genital reconstruction

The earliest described urologic application is in the **epispadias-exstrophy complex**. Kramer and Jackson (1986) described **bilateral rhomboid flaps** for penile elongation and genital reconstruction in 10 young males:<sup>[[16]](#ref16)</sup>

- The technique accurately defines the penopubic angle
- The authors described protection of deeper structures, including the verumontanum and ejaculatory ducts; this small series was not a comparative safety trial
- The authors reported satisfactory cosmetic and functional results in their 10 cases, without a controlled comparison

The flap transposes tissue from the suprapubic region onto the dorsal penile shaft, lengthening the penis while redistributing skin tension — the same geometric logic used in pilonidal disease applied to a different anatomic plane.

### Scrotal and perineal reconstruction

The rhomboid flap is a possible local option for a **selected scrotal or perineal skin defect** when neighboring tissue can reach without undue tension. A 2025 systematic review reported plastic-surgery involvement in **33% of scrotal SCC** and **67% of extramammary Paget's disease** Mohs cases; those percentages **do not measure rhomboid-flap use**.<sup>[[17]](#ref17)</sup>

### Perineal reconstruction after Fournier's gangrene

[V-Y advancement](/docs/foundations/surgical-principles/flaps/v-y-advancement) and pudendal-thigh flaps are described more often in Fournier's reconstruction; a rhomboid flap may be considered for a smaller, well-defined defect if viable adjacent tissue remains. The **1.6% pooled flap-loss figure** in a 2026 review of **619 patients / 625 flaps** combined several flap designs and **is not a rhomboid-specific rate**.<sup>[[18]](#ref18)</sup>

## Urogynecologic Applications

### Vulvar reconstruction after oncologic resection

Local rhomboid flaps are among the options described for selected vulvar defects, especially when a neighboring donor site offers enough laxity and coverage without distorting the introitus or urethra.

Höckel and Dornhöfer proposed a defect-classification approach that includes Limberg flaps among multiple reconstructive choices. Their **bilateral rhomboid** configurations are examples for selected commissural or peripheral defects, not a universal rule for every defect in those regions.<sup>[[6]](#ref6)</sup>

- **Anterior or posterior commissural defects** when local tissue movement preserves anatomy
- **Peripheral vulvar defects** with an appropriate adjacent donor site
- Unilateral or bilateral designs according to defect extent, prior treatment, perfusion, and the authors' scheme

Höckel et al.'s **vulvar field resection (VFR)** trial reported 94 women treated per protocol and 46 moderate-to-severe postoperative complications overall. These are outcomes of the **entire single-group treatment pathway**, not outcomes of a rhomboid-flap subgroup or evidence for a specific location-to-flap rule.<sup>[[19]](#ref19)</sup>

Local perfusion, donor-site laxity, previous operations and radiation, and the distance needed for transposition constrain the design. Larger or compromised defects may call for a different local, regional, or perforator-based option rather than simply enlarging a rhomboid flap.<sup>[[6]](#ref6)</sup>

### Comparative outcomes in vulvar cancer — rhomboid vs. V-Y vs. gluteal-thigh

A single-center **nonrandomized** retrospective by Tock et al. (2019) described flap outcomes in 61 women undergoing radical vulvar surgery. The groups differ by the defects and procedures for which each design was selected; their associations are not proof that one flap causes a shorter stay or fewer reoperations.<sup>[[20]](#ref20)</sup>

| Parameter | Gluteal-thigh flap (GTF) | Rhomboid flap (RF) | V-Y flap (VYF) | p |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Median hospital stay | 24 days | 17 days | **14 days** | 0.002 |
| 60-day reoperation | 69% | 41% | **25%** | 0.04 |
| Most common complication | Partial necrosis | Partial necrosis | Partial necrosis | — |
| Overall / recurrence-free survival | Comparable | Comparable | Comparable | NS |

The authors favored V-Y flaps for larger defects in their practice. The shorter observed stays and lower reoperation proportions should **not** be read as adjusted comparative effectiveness. A rhomboid flap remains a possible option for a smaller, well-defined defect with suitable adjacent tissue.<sup>[[20]](#ref20)</sup>

A prospective study of **136 locoregional flaps in 69 women** reported **126/136 (92.6%)** with no-to-mild complications at day 7 and **113/136 (83.1%)** at days 8–30. Rhomboid flaps were only one design in the pooled cohort; these are **not rhomboid-specific rates**. The 12-month symptom results came from respondent subsets, not every enrolled woman.<sup>[[21]](#ref21)</sup>

### Defect-based vulvar reconstruction algorithm

Published location-based schemes, including a recent **single-center Toulouse algorithm**, offer planning examples; they should be adapted to the actual defect, perfusion, oncologic margins, prior radiation, and patient goals rather than applied as mandatory assignments.<sup>[[6]](#ref6)[[22]](#ref22)[[23]](#ref23)</sup>

- **Small posterior / commissure defects:** local rhomboid, V-Y, or other nearby tissue may be feasible
- **Lateral / hemivulvectomy defects:** consider medial-thigh or pudendal-region tissue according to reach and perfusion
- **Anterior defects:** protect urethral and clitoral position; pubolabial options may be useful
- **Total or extended defects:** regional or musculocutaneous flaps may be needed to bring adequate tissue into the field

## Comparison with Other Geometric Flap Techniques

| Feature | Rhomboid (Limberg) | [Z-plasty](/docs/foundations/surgical-principles/flaps/z-plasty) | [Y-V plasty](/docs/foundations/surgical-principles/flaps/y-v-plasty) | [V-Y advancement](/docs/foundations/surgical-principles/flaps/v-y-advancement) |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Mechanism** | Transposition into a rhomboid defect | Transposition of two triangular flaps | Advancement along contracture axis | Advancement into a defect |
| **Primary effect** | Defect coverage with tension redistribution | Scar reorientation and ideal geometric length gain | In selected procedures, lengthening by advancing a Y-shaped flap | Tissue advancement into a defect |
| **Lengthening** | Not its primary goal | ~73% **theoretical** gain for a 60° design; actual gain varies | Procedure- and tissue-dependent | Procedure- and tissue-dependent |
| **Vascularization** | Usually local cutaneous tissue-base perfusion | Local cutaneous tissue-base perfusion; tip compromise is possible | Depends on tissue and procedure | Subcutaneous, fasciocutaneous, or perforator-based |
| **Best for** | Small–moderate defects with adequate adjacent tissue | Circumferential contractures, scar reorientation | Stenotic segments (BNC, UPJ) | Perineal / vulvar defect filling, penile lengthening |
| **Urologic niche** | Epispadias-exstrophy, scrotal reconstruction | Hypospadias revision, phimosis, frenuloplasty | Bladder-neck contracture, UPJ obstruction | Penile lengthening, Fournier's reconstruction |
| **Urogynecologic niche** | Small posterior vulvar defects | Transverse vaginal septum, vaginal constriction | — | Vulvovaginal oncologic defects, perineal reconstruction |

The rhomboid flap is a useful **geometric local transposition** option when neighboring tissue can close the defect without excessive tension or distortion. It does not require microsurgical anastomosis, but the required donor-site closure and vascularity still need individualized assessment. Larger or compromised vulvar, perineal, or genital defects may require other local, regional, or perforator-based designs; **V-Y is a movement pattern, not one vascular category**.<sup>[[6]](#ref6)[[20]](#ref20)[[22]](#ref22)</sup>

## Technical Pearls

- **Compare the four possible donor sites** against skin laxity, the **line of maximal extensibility**, perfusion, and the direction in which closure would pull critical structures.<sup>[[3]](#ref3)</sup>
- **Plan a rhomboid defect deliberately** when using classical Limberg geometry; do not extend an oncologic or functional resection solely to force an ideal drawing.
- **Assess the flap base and final inset**, not a supposed universal 1:1 vascular-safety rule. Reconsider the design if closure would be tight or tissue perfusion is compromised.
- **For commissural vulvar defects**, assess whether one or two local flaps can preserve midline contour and introital function in the individual case.<sup>[[6]](#ref6)</sup>
- **In irradiated or scarred fields**, assess viable donor tissue carefully and consider a regional or musculocutaneous option when local perfusion is inadequate.
- **Rajabi's finite-element result** is a design hypothesis about modeled stress; it does not establish a clinically safer modified flap without outcome data.<sup>[[7]](#ref7)</sup>
