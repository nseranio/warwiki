# Propeller Flap

The propeller flap is an island flap that reaches its recipient site through axial rotation (90–180°) around its nourishing pedicle.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[2]](#ref2)</sup> The Tokyo consensus, reached at a 2009 meeting and published in 2011, classifies subcutaneous-pedicled, perforator-pedicled and supercharged designs; not every propeller flap pivots on a single skeletonized perforator.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup> Rotation can bring local tissue to penoscrotal, perineal, vulvar and selected urethral defects without necessarily requiring free transfer. Donor morbidity and vascular risk depend on the particular design and site.

This page is the foundations-level deep dive on the propeller principle in GU reconstruction. The variant-specific [PMTP Propeller Flap](/docs/foundations/surgical-principles/flaps/pmtp-propeller) page covers the deep-femoral-perforator flap in technique-level detail.

---

## Geometric Principle

The propeller flap is conceptually distinct from the geometric flaps in the rest of this section ([Z-plasty](/docs/foundations/surgical-principles/flaps/z-plasty), [rhomboid (Limberg)](/docs/foundations/surgical-principles/flaps/rhomboid-limberg), [bilobed](/docs/foundations/surgical-principles/flaps/bilobed)). The propeller is designed in freestyle fashion around a single identified perforator vessel, without a fixed geometric template:<sup>[[2]](#ref2)[[3]](#ref3)</sup>

| Step | Detail |
|---|---|
| **Perforator identification** | Preoperative handheld Doppler or multidetector-row CT angiography. MDCT provides high-resolution 3D images of the perforator's position and subcutaneous course<sup>[[4]](#ref4)</sup> |
| **Skin-island design** | Elliptical / lenticular island centered on the perforator. The perforator divides the flap into a **larger "propeller blade"** (directed toward the defect) and a **smaller blade** (rotates into the donor site or is closed primarily). Blade ratio adjustable to defect size<sup>[[2]](#ref2)[[3]](#ref3)</sup> |
| **Rotation** | The flap is elevated as a complete island, the perforator is skeletonized, and the skin paddle is rotated **90–180°** around the perforator axis — analogous to a propeller blade spinning on its shaft. A 180° rotation places the larger blade directly into the defect<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[5]](#ref5)</sup> |

### Tokyo Consensus classification<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup>

- **Subcutaneous-pedicled propeller flap.** Rotates on a subcutaneous tissue pedicle
- **Perforator-pedicled propeller flap.** Rotates on a skeletonized perforator vessel (most common in GU reconstruction)
- **Supercharged propeller flap.** Adds a venous anastomosis to augment outflow

### Why propeller differs from other local flaps

Perforator-pedicled propeller flaps offer flexible local design around a suitable mapped vessel. Unlike the rhomboid or bilobed geometric templates, the skin island can be tailored to the defect and the actual vascular territory; this does not mean every perforator or donor field is usable.<sup>[[2]](#ref2)</sup>

## Urologic Applications

### Penoscrotal resurfacing — internal pudendal artery perforator (IPAP) propeller flap

The most significant urologic application is the IPAP propeller flap from the gluteal fold for extensive penoscrotal reconstruction:<sup>[[6]](#ref6)</sup>

- **10 consecutive patients** with extensive penoscrotal defects (Fournier's gangrene, foreign-body injection, trauma, cancer)
- The IPAP was identified with handheld Doppler and served as the pivot. The flap was internally rotated &gt; 90° in tension-free fashion, with the long axis centered on the gluteal fold
- Mean flap dimensions 6.7 × 11.7 cm
- **No major complications** over mean 19.7 mo follow-up; one case of partial distal necrosis healed spontaneously
- All patients satisfied with cosmetic and functional results
- The gluteal-fold donor site provides a natural-appearing scrotal pouch with a hidden donor scar and optimal skin thickness and pliability

The gluteal-fold skin closely matches scrotal skin in thickness, texture, and color, which is an aesthetic advantage.

### Perineoscrotal reconstruction after Fournier's gangrene

Several propeller configurations have been described:

- **Posteromedial thigh (PMT) propeller flap.** Scaglioni 2015: a 9 × 23 cm PMT propeller flap based on two profunda-femoris perforators for a 10 × 12 cm scrotal defect after Fournier's gangrene with complete flap survival.<sup>[[7]](#ref7)</sup>
- **Vertical posteromedial thigh (vPMT) propeller flap.** Wishart 2021: 21 pedicled vPMT flaps in 12 patients (3 scrotal, 8 perianal, 5 vulvar). 18/21 rotated in propeller fashion. Flap sizes 5–9 × 18–35 cm. All flaps survived; all donor sites primarily closed.<sup>[[8]](#ref8)</sup>
- **Propeller PMTP flap.** Kwon 2021: 8 patients with extensive perineal defects (infection and skin cancer) reconstructed with PMTP propeller flaps rotated 180°. Mean flap 256.5 cm². All flaps survived without major complications over mean 22.4 mo.<sup>[[9]](#ref9)</sup> (See [PMTP Propeller Flap](/docs/foundations/surgical-principles/flaps/pmtp-propeller) for technique-level detail.)

A 2026 review of 619 patients/625 mixed flap reconstructions after Fournier's gangrene reported 1.6% pooled flap loss. Medial-thigh, pudendal-thigh, ALT and gracilis designs were most commonly named; this is not a propeller-specific loss rate.<sup>[[10]](#ref10)</sup>

### Perineal-urethrostomy revision

Schulster et al. (2021) described a novel application, a posterior-thigh propeller flap for perineal urethrostomy (PU) revision in complex cases at high risk for stenosis:<sup>[[11]](#ref11)</sup>

- The stenotic PU was incised, and a propeller flap was designed around the posterior-thigh perforator closest to the defect
- The flap was elevated and rotated on its pedicle, with the apex placed directly into the urethrotomy defect and partially tubularized to a 30 Fr calibre
- At 17 months: patent PU, voiding well, low post-void residual

This single case illustrates an option for a challenging PU revision: a local propeller flap can import tissue from outside the scarred field when its perforator, reach and tissue properties are suitable. One patient's 17-month result does not establish reliable patency across other revisions.

## Urogynecologic Applications

### Vulvar reconstruction — IPAP propeller flap

The IPAP propeller flap is one of the most extensively studied perforator flaps for vulvar reconstruction:

- **Hashimoto 2014.** Largest series: 71 IPAP flaps in 45 patients for vulvar (36), buttock (10), vaginal (9), anal (6), and pelvic-cavity (6) reconstruction. Propeller flaps used in 35 cases, transposition in 3, V-Y in 7. 67/71 flaps survived completely (94.4%); 4 partial necrosis, no total failures.<sup>[[12]](#ref12)</sup>
- **Han 2016 "gull-wing flap".** IPAP-based propeller flap rotated 150–180° internally to reconstruct both labium and external vaginal wall with sufficient volume for 3D vulvovaginal reconstruction after tumor excision.<sup>[[13]](#ref13)</sup>
- **Shin 2022.** Retrospectively described 16 patients/27 new PPSF flaps, and compared aesthetic ratings with an earlier PPPF group. All PPSF flaps survived in the reported cohort; the abstract does not give a combined survival denominator for both designs. PPSF had better reported symmetry, labial-shape and total scores in the nonrandom comparison; selection and scoring details require the full paper.<sup>[[14]](#ref14)</sup>

### Vulvar reconstruction algorithms — location-based selection

The Toulouse Algorithm (Ricotta et al., 2025) is a location-based framework for vulvar cancer reconstruction; it should not be treated as a universal first-line recommendation for every propeller design over musculocutaneous reconstruction.<sup>[[15]](#ref15)</sup> Potential perforator sources include IPAP, deep femoral or PAP, medial circumflex femoral and external pudendal systems, with selection depending on defect and donor anatomy.

Han 2023 proposed a simplified algorithm based on the vulvo-thigh junctional crease (visual landmark of the inferior pubic ramus): defects medial to the crease are assigned to IPAP flaps (66%); defects lateral to it, to profunda artery perforator or transverse upper gracilis flaps (34%). IPAP flaps had significantly fewer wound complications (12.9% versus 37.5%, p = 0.04).<sup>[[16]](#ref16)</sup>

Huang 2015 reported 27 perforator flaps in 16 patients for vulvar reconstruction (4 IPAP propeller). 100% flap survival, all donor sites primarily closed.<sup>[[17]](#ref17)</sup>

### The lotus-petal flap — a propeller variant

The [lotus-petal flap](/docs/foundations/surgical-principles/flaps/lotus-petal) is a gluteal-fold IPA-perforator fasciocutaneous flap that can be configured as a propeller (as well as a transposition or V-Y advancement). The Confalonieri 2017 V-Y versus LPF series, the Hellinga 2016 ELAPE series, and the Bodin 2015 supra-fascial-LPF series are covered in depth on the dedicated lotus-petal page; they are noted here only because the LPF in propeller configuration is the most studied IPA-perforator propeller flap used in perineal reconstruction.<sup>[[18]](#ref18)[[19]](#ref19)[[20]](#ref20)[[21]](#ref21)</sup>

### Perineal reconstruction after pelvic exenteration

For large defects after pelvic exenteration, Witte's review included 1,988 patients across 58 mainly observational studies; among the 910 APR patients, 91% received a rectus-abdominis flap. This is a description of published reconstruction patterns, not evidence that VRAM is superior to a propeller flap for a matched defect. Propeller perforator flaps are described as alternatives when their tissue volume, reach and perfusion suit the defect.<sup>[[22]](#ref22)</sup>

The IPAP propeller was used in 1 of 11 perineal reconstructions after APR by Loreti 2023, alongside 8 rotation flaps and 2 advancement island flaps, all based on the internal pudendal perforator system. All 11 flaps survived without major complications, even in irradiated patients (73%).<sup>[[23]](#ref23)</sup>

## Complications and Management

A review of 1,315 propeller flaps in 1,242 patients across multiple body regions found the following; these rates should not be transferred directly to GU or perineal reconstruction:<sup>[[24]](#ref24)</sup>

- Overall complication rate approximately 22.6%
- Most frequent: partial flap necrosis and venous congestion
- Significantly higher complications in patients &gt; 70 years
- Higher complication rates in the lower limbs (approximately 31.8%) than other regions

### Venous congestion — the Achilles heel

Venous congestion can result from pedicle torsion or compression. The following reports describe potential responses in specific settings, not a routine protocol for every GU propeller flap:<sup>[[25]](#ref25)[[26]](#ref26)[[27]](#ref27)[[28]](#ref28)</sup>

- **Venous supercharging.** Chaput 2018 compared consecutive groups of 30 standard and 30 venous-supercharged lower-limb propeller flaps. Congestion was 36.7% versus 6.7%, and distal necrosis 30% versus 3.3%, with longer operative time for supercharging. This nonrandom, mainly traumatic lower-limb comparison does not establish the effect in perineal flaps.<sup>[[26]](#ref26)</sup>
- **Local subcutaneous LMWH injection.** Pérez 2014 retrospectively described salvage attempts in 15 mixed free or regional congested flaps when venous outflow could not be restored surgically. Although all were at least partially salvaged, 13 patients required transfusion (mean five units of packed red cells among those transfused), and hematoma and anemia-related syncope occurred. This is a high-morbidity specialist salvage report, not routine propeller-flap care.<sup>[[27]](#ref27)</sup>
- **Flap geometry.** Huang's 2026 meta-analysis covered 402 distal-leg, ankle and hindfoot propeller flaps. Associations for 40–80 cm² area and width &gt;4.5 cm do not define universal perineal dimensions; its flap-to-defect ratio &lt;2.5 association was not statistically significant (*p* = 0.15), so it is not a validated threshold.<sup>[[28]](#ref28)</sup>

## Comparison with Other Flap Techniques

This table contrasts design features, not matched outcome rates; a gracilis muscle flap and VRAM have different reach, bulk and donor-site risks despite sharing a column.

| Feature | Propeller flap | [Rhomboid (Limberg)](/docs/foundations/surgical-principles/flaps/rhomboid-limberg) | [Bilobed flap](/docs/foundations/surgical-principles/flaps/bilobed) | [V-Y advancement](/docs/foundations/surgical-principles/flaps/v-y-advancement) | Musculocutaneous (VRAM / gracilis) |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Vascularization** | Subcutaneous or perforator pedicle; may be supercharged | Random pattern | Random or perforator | Subcutaneous / perforator | Named vessel (axial) |
| **Arc of rotation** | 90–180° | ~60° | 90–100° | None (linear) | Variable |
| **Tissue bulk** | Thin, adjustable | Minimal | Minimal | Moderate | Substantial |
| **Donor-site morbidity** | Depends on flap size and closure | Depends on defect and closure | Depends on defect and closure | Depends on defect and closure | Depends on muscle and donor site |
| **Defect-size capacity** | Small to large | Small to moderate | Small to moderate | Small to moderate | Large |
| **Example perineal / genital use** | Selected penoscrotal, vulvar or perineal defects | Selected small posterior vulvar defects | Selected multi-compartment defects | Selected vulvar or perineal defects | Deep-volume or pelvic-floor defects, neovagina |
| **Key advantage** | Freestyle design; thin, pliable; hidden donor scar | Simple geometry | Tissue relay from lax area | No rotation needed | Bulk for dead-space obliteration |
| **Key limitation** | Venous congestion with rotation | Size-limited (random) | Complex design | Limited reach | Donor-site morbidity |

## When to Reach for the Propeller Flap

- Extensive penoscrotal resurfacing where local advancement is insufficient and the gluteal-fold donor matches scrotal skin character (IPAP propeller).<sup>[[6]](#ref6)</sup>
- Vulvar oncologic reconstruction when a thin, pliable, sensate flap is preferred over musculocutaneous bulk (IPAP propeller, gull-wing, lotus-petal, perineal-perforator switch).<sup>[[12]](#ref12)[[13]](#ref13)[[14]](#ref14)[[19]](#ref19)</sup>
- Extensive perineal defects after Fournier's, ELAPE, or pelvic exenteration when VRAM is unavailable or undesired (PMTP / vPMT propeller, lotus-petal, IPAP).<sup>[[7]](#ref7)[[8]](#ref8)[[9]](#ref9)[[20]](#ref20)[[23]](#ref23)</sup>
- **Perineal urethrostomy revision** in scarred, multiply-operated fields where a distant local propeller imports healthy tissue outside the injury zone.<sup>[[11]](#ref11)</sup>

**Reconsider the design** when the defect requires more deep-space volume than a fasciocutaneous island supplies, or when donor perfusion, irradiation, prior surgery or venous outflow make rotation unsafe. A muscle-bearing or other flap may be preferable, but that decision is defect- and patient-specific.

## Technical Pearls

- **Plan the vascular pivot** with examination and an appropriate mapping method; handheld Doppler, ultrasound or CT angiography can be useful depending on location and prior operations. Confirm the actual perforator and usable rotation arc at dissection.<sup>[[4]](#ref4)</sup>
- **Preserve venous outflow during dissection.** The extent of perforator skeletonization or retained soft-tissue cuff is design-specific; there is no universal 2–3 cm cuff rule.
- **Check perfusion and outflow during rotation**; pedicle kinking, compression or tension may require changing the arc or flap plan.
- **Consider outflow options** when the expected rotation or donor field increases venous risk; the comparative supercharging data cited above are from lower-limb reconstruction, not a perineal prophylaxis trial.<sup>[[25]](#ref25)[[26]](#ref26)</sup>
- **Inset without avoidable tension** while preserving the pedicle and venous drainage.
- **Match donor tissue to the defect.** Gluteal fold, posteromedial thigh and perineal perforator systems offer different reach, thickness and donor scars; choose according to actual vascular anatomy and reconstructive needs.
