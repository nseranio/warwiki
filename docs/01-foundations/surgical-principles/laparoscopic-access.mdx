# Laparoscopic Access

Primary access is a distinct risk period in minimally invasive GU reconstruction. Entry can injure adherent bowel, abdominal-wall vessels or retroperitoneal great vessels before the intended procedure begins. Choose the **site, instrument and technique together**, considering previous surgery, anatomy, the planned reconstruction and operator experience. No approach guarantees freedom from major injury.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[2]](#ref2)[[3]](#ref3)</sup>

## Entry Techniques

| Method | Sequence and useful features | Important limitations |
|---|---|---|
| **Veress needle** | Needle placement → confirmation and pneumoperitoneum → primary trocar. | Both needle and trocar can injure viscera or vessels. Failed entry and extraperitoneal insufflation occur more often than with direct trocar insertion in comparative trials. |
| **Open (Hasson)** | Controlled incision through the abdominal wall and peritoneum → blunt cannula → insufflation. | Adherent bowel may be immediately beneath the peritoneum. Open entry does not eliminate injury; gas leakage and a thick wall can make access difficult. |
| **Direct trocar insertion (DTI)** | Trocar insertion without prior pneumoperitoneum → immediate inspection → insufflation. | Requires controlled insertion by a trained operator. Fewer failed entries do not establish universal superiority for rare major injuries or a hostile abdomen. |
| **Optical entry** | A compatible laparoscope displays tissue layers as the access device advances. Devices may be used with or without prior insufflation according to their instructions. | Visualization is not a protective shield against adherent bowel or uncontrolled advancement. Optical trocars and threaded visual cannulas are different devices. |

These distinctions are supported by Cochrane and society guidance; the comparison should not be reduced to a zero-risk “winner.”<sup>[[2]](#ref2)[[3]](#ref3)[[4]](#ref4)</sup>

### What comparative evidence establishes

- **Cochrane 2019:** direct trocar insertion reduced failed entry versus Veress. Evidence for differences in visceral and vascular injury was limited by rare events, imprecision and trial quality. Many studies excluded patients with previous surgery or high BMI, limiting applicability to complex reconstruction.<sup>[[2]](#ref2)</sup>
- **Pairwise meta-analysis, 2023:** 25 trials involving 6,950 patients favored DTI for several entry outcomes. Heterogeneity and sparse major injuries limit a universal safety ranking; pooled entry-time estimates were particularly heterogeneous.<sup>[[5]](#ref5)</sup>
- **Network meta-analysis, online 2025 / journal issue 2026:** reported signals favoring direct entry for major/minor complications and open entry for vascular injury. Its networks had different denominators: **7,584 participants in 29 studies for major injuries**, versus **8,984 in 33 for minor injuries**. Sparse-event rankings and zero observed events do not prove zero risk or establish the preferred method for every high-risk patient.<sup>[[6]](#ref6)</sup>

A failure to detect a difference is also not proof that methods are equivalent. Maintain proficiency in an alternative entry plan when the initial method is unsuitable or unsuccessful.

## Planning and Performing Entry

### Before insertion

Review previous incisions, stomas, mesh, hernias and available imaging. Choose an entry site with a plausible safe route to the intended working space. Empty the bladder and decompress the stomach when indicated, particularly for left-upper-quadrant entry. Check the access device and insufflator, and ensure that conversion and hemorrhage-control equipment are available.<sup>[[3]](#ref3)</sup>

### Veress placement and confirmation

Use an adequate skin opening and controlled advancement. At an umbilical site, the insertion angle depends on habitus and anatomy; ISGE describes approximately **45° in non-obese women toward 90° in obesity**, not a fixed angle for every patient or entry site. Routine lifting of the lower abdominal wall has not been shown to reduce injury and may increase failed entry. Avoid lateral sweeping of an inserted needle.<sup>[[3]](#ref3)[[4]](#ref4)</sup>

**Low initial insufflation pressure** is more useful than isolated tactile or saline tests. ISGE uses an initial pressure **≤8 mmHg** as a placement indicator; pressure must be interpreted with flow and the clinical situation. High or unexpected pressure, poor flow, asymmetric distension or blood/enteric aspiration should trigger reassessment. “Two pops,” a negative aspiration test or a hanging drop alone cannot establish safe placement or exclude an injury.<sup>[[7]](#ref7)[[8]](#ref8)[[3]](#ref3)</sup>

Avoid repeated blind attempts at the same site. Reassess early and change the site or method when entry is unsuccessful; ISGE specifically recommends an alternative after three failed umbilical Veress attempts.<sup>[[3]](#ref3)</sup>

### Insufflation and trocar placement

Use pressure and the patient's physiology to guide insufflation rather than a fixed gas volume. ISGE's transient high-pressure entry recommendation concerns **healthy women** and requires prompt reduction after trocar placement; it should not be transferred automatically to patients with compromised cardiopulmonary function or prolonged robotic positioning.<sup>[[3]](#ref3)</sup>

After initial access, inspect the entry region and underlying viscera. Place secondary ports **under direct laparoscopic vision**, identifying abdominal-wall vessels and the actual underlying anatomy. Surface transillumination does not reliably locate deep epigastric vessels. Fixed distances from the midline and the instruction “always lateral to rectus” are not substitutes for visualization.<sup>[[9]](#ref9)[[3]](#ref3)</sup>

## Entry Sites

| Site | When it may help | Limits and safeguards |
|---|---|---|
| **Umbilicus** | A thin abdominal-wall access point in many patients. | Umbilical hernia, mesh or suspected adhesions may change the plan. Vessel depth and level vary with habitus and position. |
| **Palmer's point** | Approximately 3 cm below the left costal margin in the midclavicular line; an option when umbilical adhesions or failed entry are concerns. | Avoid with suspected LUQ adhesions, relevant previous gastric/splenic surgery, gastric distension, splenomegaly or portal-hypertensive vessels. Decompress the stomach and assess the actual anatomy. |
| **Supraumbilical / other alternative site** | May improve access for selected body habitus, masses or previous scars. | Imaging studies describe average distances, not guaranteed safety margins. Select the site and direction for that patient. |

The historical **8-mm aortic-bifurcation measurement describes its caudal level relative to the umbilicus**, not an 8-mm skin-to-aorta depth. It must not be used to determine a safe insertion depth.<sup>[[10]](#ref10)[[11]](#ref11)[[12]](#ref12)[[3]](#ref3)</sup>

## Entry-Related Complications

### Major vascular injury

Suspect great-vessel injury with unexplained hemodynamic deterioration, brisk bleeding or an expanding retroperitoneal hematoma. Alert anesthesia, activate hemorrhage support and obtain vascular help. A trocar may tamponade an injury: do not remove it blindly before a control plan is established. **Uncontrolled bleeding or instability requires prompt open exposure and hemorrhage control.** Selected laparoscopic repairs reported in stable patients do not justify delaying conversion.<sup>[[13]](#ref13)</sup>

See [Vascular Management & Damage Control](/docs/foundations/surgical-principles/vascular-damage-control).

### Abdominal-wall vessel injury

Control bleeding under direct vision. Options include compression/trocar tamponade, energy when safely applicable, or suture ligation; persistent bleeding or a postoperative hematoma may require surgical or interventional-radiology treatment. Inspect ports as they are removed because a cannula can temporarily hide bleeding. Avoid blind full-thickness sutures that could capture bowel.<sup>[[9]](#ref9)[[14]](#ref14)</sup>

### Bowel injury

Inspect a suspected injury completely and seek appropriate surgical help. Management depends on depth, mechanism, tissue viability, location, contamination and patient stability. A superficial serosal abrasion with intact viable bowel is different from an enterotomy, devascularization or thermal injury; not every abrasion needs repair, and not every perforation needs resection.

Missed injuries can present after an initially reassuring recovery, including several days later. Increasing pain, distension, persistent ileus, fever, tachycardia or systemic deterioration merit urgent assessment. A normal white count does not exclude injury. Stable patients may need prompt contrast-enhanced CT and surgical review; peritonitis, sepsis with a suspected uncontrolled source or instability requires urgent source control. Do not wait for a fixed 96-hour window or a particular symptom constellation.<sup>[[15]](#ref15)[[16]](#ref16)[[17]](#ref17)[[18]](#ref18)</sup>

Use [Intraoperative Bowel Handling & Injury Management](/docs/foundations/surgical-principles/bowel-handling-injury-management) for the repair, resection and delayed-recognition pathway.

### Gas embolism

CO₂ can enter an injured vessel during initial insufflation or later dissection. Clinical severity ranges from subclinical echocardiographic bubbles to collapse; a single incidence number cannot describe both. Sudden changes in end-tidal CO₂, hypoxemia, hypotension or arrhythmia warrant immediate evaluation. End-tidal CO₂ may briefly rise or fall; a “mill-wheel” murmur is not required.<sup>[[19]](#ref19)</sup>

**Stop insufflation, release pneumoperitoneum and initiate coordinated anesthetic resuscitation with 100% oxygen.** Treat shock and perform standard CPR when indicated. The 2025 AHA guideline considers **level supine or recovery positioning** reasonable; CPR requires supine positioning. It does not make routine head-down positioning mandatory. In adults, aspiration may be reasonable for known, accessible **right-ventricular gas**; it is not a reason to delay resuscitation.<sup>[[20]](#ref20)[[19]](#ref19)</sup>

Seek hyperbaric expertise urgently for life-threatening gas embolism, particularly suspected arterial/cerebral involvement. AHA considers hyperbaric oxygen reasonable, while acknowledging limited evidence and uncertainty across gas types and mechanisms. It is not an automatic treatment for every transient CO₂ microbubble finding.<sup>[[20]](#ref20)</sup>

A randomized liver-resection study found fewer high-grade **TEE-detected gas emboli** at 10 versus 15 mmHg. That is a liver-dissection result, not proof that 10 mmHg is the safest pressure for primary trocar insertion in GU reconstruction.<sup>[[21]](#ref21)</sup>

### Trocar-site hernia

The EHS/AHS guideline suggests closing fascial defects **≥10 mm**, especially umbilical and single-incision sites. Assess the final defect after extraction or stretching. Evidence for the optimal closure method is limited. A small trial of prophylactic mesh at the umbilical extraction site after selected high-risk cholecystectomies does not establish routine port-site mesh in urologic reconstruction.<sup>[[22]](#ref22)[[23]](#ref23)</sup>

See [Incisions & Closure](/docs/foundations/surgical-principles/incisions-closure) for incision selection, closure and interpretation of hernia rates.

## High-Risk Patients

| Situation | Practical approach |
|---|---|
| **Obesity** | Assess wall thickness, needle/trocar length, pannus position and trajectory. Optical, open and Veress methods each have limitations. Average imaging distances do not mean that abdominal organs are beyond the reach of the needle.<sup>[[24]](#ref24)[[3]](#ref3)</sup> |
| **Extensive prior surgery** | Anticipate adhesions and choose access away from likely adherent viscera. Open or optical entry can help visualize wall layers but can still injure bowel attached immediately underneath. Alternative-site case series do not establish universal safety. |
| **Portal hypertension / cirrhosis** | Review cross-sectional imaging and consider ultrasound mapping when abdominal-wall collaterals are a concern. Plan with anesthesia and hepatology according to liver severity, portal hypertension and the operation. No access device substitutes for disease-specific risk assessment.<sup>[[25]](#ref25)[[26]](#ref26)</sup> |
| **Very low BMI** | Great vessels may be close to the wall. Limit force and insertion depth, and choose an entry route suited to the anatomy; a routine fixed angle or wall lift does not guarantee protection.<sup>[[3]](#ref3)</sup> |

A small randomized study of subxiphoid optical access in previously operated patients compared it with a **combined Palmer's-point Veress plus subumbilical optical strategy**. It was not a simple comparison of two needle sites, and liver injuries occurred with subxiphoid access. Treat it as a selected technical option rather than a general recommendation.<sup>[[27]](#ref27)</sup>

## Operative Pearls

1. Plan an alternative site and technique before repeated failed entry.
2. Interpret pressure, flow and anatomy together; no placement test excludes all injuries.
3. Inspect the initial entry and place secondary ports under direct vision.
4. Respond to suspected vascular, bowel or gas-embolism complications promptly, with conversion or resuscitation as required.
5. Inspect port sites for bleeding and close appropriate final fascial defects.
