# Knot Security: Throws, Knot Configuration and Technique

**Knot security** is the ability of a tied knot to hold without slipping or untying; in bench testing a knot is classed as secure when the suture breaks before the knot slips, or when slippage stays below a set limit of about 2 to 3 mm.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[2]](#ref2)[[7]](#ref7)</sup> In 710 of 720 loaded suture loops the knot, not the strand, was the point of failure, and knot strength ranged from 5% to 99% of the unknotted suture's strength across 12 knots and 12 materials.<sup>[[3]](#ref3)</sup> Security varies with suture material and size, knot configuration, number of throws, position in the suture line and tying technique.<sup>[[2]](#ref2)[[4]](#ref4)</sup>

Suture materials are compared on [Sutures](/docs/foundations/surgical-principles/sutures); knotless running closure is on [Barbed Sutures](/docs/foundations/surgical-principles/barbed-sutures).

## Terms

| Term | Definition |
| --- | --- |
| Throw | One half-knot: one strand passed around the other. A surgeon's double throw wraps twice. Most studies count a double throw as one throw; Trimbos 1984 and the Romeo laparoscopic studies count each wrap separately.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[5]](#ref5)</sup> |
| Square (flat, reef) knot | Successive throws in opposite rotation, written 1=1.<sup>[[6]](#ref6)</sup> |
| Granny knot | Successive throws in the same rotation, written 1×1.<sup>[[6]](#ref6)</sup> |
| Surgeon's knot | A double first throw followed by single throws, written 2=1 or 2=1=1.<sup>[[7]](#ref7)</sup> |
| Post | The strand held under tension while the other strand is thrown around it. Throws laid on a post slide down it as half-hitches.<sup>[[8]](#ref8)[[7]](#ref7)</sup> |
| Identical sliding knot | The same tying action repeated with the same hand on the same post (S=S=S).<sup>[[8]](#ref8)</sup> |
| Nonidentical (crossed) sliding knot | Forehand and backhand throws alternated with the same hand on the same post (S×S×S).<sup>[[8]](#ref8)</sup> |
| Parallel sliding knot | The strand held under tension is changed on each throw while the hands alternate (S//S//S).<sup>[[8]](#ref8)</sup> |
| Blocking half-hitch | A half-hitch thrown after the active and passive strands are exchanged; it stops further sliding.<sup>[[9]](#ref9)</sup> |

Knot notation differs between research groups: Tera and Åberg introduced a code in 1976, Trimbos and colleagues use =, × and //, the Romeo and Koninckx laparoscopic studies use H (half-knot) and S (half-hitch) with a rotation suffix, and O'Keefe and colleagues proposed a unifying nomenclature in 2025.<sup>[[3]](#ref3)[[10]](#ref10)[[11]](#ref11)[[12]](#ref12)</sup>

Across five materials in sizes 4-0 to #5, at least 5 flat square throws met a binomial 95% confidence interval for security of 0.84 to 1.0 and 4 throws an interval of 0.76 to 0.99; FiberWire required 6 throws at either level.<sup>[[21]](#ref21)</sup> In a 0-gauge comparison of polyester, polydioxanone, polypropylene and polyglactin 910, knots with 4 throws came untied more often than knots with 5 or 6, and tensile strength did not differ between 4, 5 and 6 throws.<sup>[[23]](#ref23)</sup> In 0 polyglactin 910 tied with 5 throws, cut to 3 mm tails and soaked in saline, 27 of 56 square knots and 14 of 32 surgeon's knots untied; no silk, polydioxanone or chromic knot untied in the same test.<sup>[[7]](#ref7)</sup>

In no. 1 sutures snugged against 1 N, 2-throw square knots held 11% to 51% of the suture's breaking force (polyglycolic acid: 100%), and every knot reached the breaking force after at most two additional throws.<sup>[[16]](#ref16)</sup>

## Position in the Suture Line

The knot that ends a continuous line ties a loop of two strands to a single strand and needs more throws than an interrupted knot.

| Suture (2-0, snug square knot) | Interrupted | Start of continuous line | End of continuous line |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Chromic gut | 3 | 4 | 5 |
| Polyglycolic acid (Dexon Plus) | 3 | 3 | 5 |
| Polyglactin 910 (Vicryl) | 3 | 3 | 6 |
| Polydioxanone (PDS) | 4 | 5 | 7 |
| Nylon (Ethilon) | 4 | 5 | 6 |
| Polypropylene (Prolene) | 3 | 3 | 5 |

*Minimum throws at which 20 of 20 knots broke without slipping, after 24 hours in canine serum at 37 °C.*<sup>[[15]](#ref15)</sup>

- **Large-gauge suture.** In USP 2 polydioxanone and USP 2 and 3 polyglactin 910, start knots needed at least 6 throws and end knots at least 7 to prevent unravelling; in polydioxanone square end knots, 1 of 10 still slipped at 6 and at 7 throws and none at 8.<sup>[[24]](#ref24)</sup>
- **Coated polydioxanone.** With 2-0 polydioxanone coated in plasma or fat, square start knots needed 4 throws in plasma and 5 in fat, and square end knots needed 5 in both.<sup>[[25]](#ref25)</sup>
- **Aberdeen knot.** An Aberdeen knot of 3 throws and 1 turn was secure in plasma- and fat-coated polydioxanone.<sup>[[25]](#ref25)</sup> In a separate study, 3 throws and 2 turns gave the strongest Aberdeen knot, and adding throws or turns did not increase strength.<sup>[[26]](#ref26)</sup>

## Surgeon's Knot Versus Square Knot

- **5-0 polyglactin 910.** A 3-throw square knot slipped in 10 of 12 tests; 3-throw knots that included a surgeon's double throw did not, and a 3-throw surgeon's knot had the same tensile strength as a 5-throw single-throw knot.<sup>[[27]](#ref27)</sup>
- **3-0 to 5-0 sutures.** Square and surgeon's knots generally needed 4 throws and sliding knots 5; 3-0 polyglactin 910 surgeon's knots were secure at 3 throws, against 5 for square knots.<sup>[[13]](#ref13)</sup>
- **0 polyglactin 910.** The 2=1 surgeon's knot slipped in 6 of 10 tests and the 2=2 knot in none.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup>
- **0-gauge sutures, 5 throws.** Failure load and the rate of untying did not differ between surgeon's and square knots.<sup>[[7]](#ref7)</sup>
- **Dry laparoscopic bench knots in 2-0 polyglactin 910.** The surgeon's flat knot (H2H1s) opened below 10 N in 5% of tests; 97.5% of symmetric H2H2 knots and 100% of asymmetric H2H2 and both H3H2 orientations met the study's no-opening-below-30 N criterion.<sup>[[5]](#ref5)</sup>

## Sliding Knots and Post Switching

A sliding knot forms whenever one strand is held under more tension than the other; each throw then runs down the tensioned strand. Its security depends mainly on whether successive throws repeat the same action and whether the post is changed.

| 3-0 suture, 5 throws | Identical (same post, same action) | Nonidentical (same post, alternating forehand and backhand) | Parallel (post changed each throw) |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Polyglactin 910 (Vicryl), loop holding capacity<sup>[[8]](#ref8)</sup> | 10.8 N | 34.0 N | 58.4 N |
| Four braided absorbables, loop holding capacity<sup>[[8]](#ref8)</sup> | 3.2 to 11.4 N | 18.6 to 37.6 N | 50.4 to 59.3 N |
| Polyglactin 910 (Vicryl), knots slipping more than 2 mm<sup>[[10]](#ref10)</sup> | 80% | 20% | 0% |
| Polydioxanone (PDS), knots slipping more than 2 mm<sup>[[10]](#ref10)</sup> | 70% | 0% | 0% |

*Loop holding capacity from van Leeuwen 2012 and slippage from Trimbos 1986; 10 knots per group in both.*

- **Throw count with identical throws.** Identical sliding knots of 5 throws slipped in 40% to 80% of tests in four of five materials; parallel 5-throw knots never slipped in any of the five.<sup>[[10]](#ref10)</sup>
- **Throw count with nonidentical throws.** In 0 and 2-0 polydioxanone and polyglactin 910, 3-throw nonidentical sliding knots untied in 50% to 90% of tests and 6-throw knots in 20% to 30%.<sup>[[28]](#ref28)</sup> In 0 and 2-0 glycomer 631 monofilament, 8 of 20 and 9 of 20 nonidentical 6-throw knots untied against none of the parallel knots, and 1 or 2 of 20 nonidentical knots still untied at 10 throws.<sup>[[29]](#ref29)</sup>
- **Square knots versus sliding knots.** Three-throw sliding knots and two-throw square and surgeon's knots performed poorly; in smaller-diameter sutures, sliding knots with one extra throw performed the same as square knots.<sup>[[30]](#ref30)</sup>
- **Blocking half-hitches.** In 0 polyglactin 910, two sliding half-hitches followed by one blocking half-hitch held 52 N or 98 N depending on the rotation of the throws; with a second asymmetric blocking half-hitch every suture broke, and with a second symmetric blocking half-hitch 8 of 10 knots slid open.<sup>[[9]](#ref9)</sup>
- **Monofilament sequences.** In tensiometer testing, secure monofilament knots needed 6 throws (two symmetric sliding half-hitches and four asymmetric blocking half-hitches), whereas 4- or 5-throw sequences were secure with braided sutures.<sup>[[6]](#ref6)[[11]](#ref11)</sup> Five half-hitches with three blocking throws (SSSbSbSb) were recommended for laparoscopic use.<sup>[[31]](#ref31)</sup>
- **Alternating posts.** A systematic review of 14 studies found knots with throws on alternating posts more secure and less variable than knots with opposite throws on the same post.<sup>[[32]](#ref32)</sup> Arthroscopic sliding knots are backed up with reversed half-hitches on alternating posts; in braided polyester, SMC, Field and Giant sliding knots resisted cyclic loading after 1 additional half-hitch and the Duncan loop after 2.<sup>[[33]](#ref33)</sup>
- **Trainee knots.** Of knots tied by 25 surgical trainees, 12.6% in Vicryl and 5.6% in Prolene opened below 5 N, and every one of these was a slip knot of up to 6 throws.<sup>[[34]](#ref34)</sup>

## Tying Technique

- **Tying force.** In USP 2 polydioxanone, 5-throw square knots held when each throw was set with 5 N or more; 2 of 7 failed at 1 or 2 N and 7 of 7 at 0.5 N. Polyethylene-core 5-throw knots resolved in every test even at 50 N per throw.<sup>[[35]](#ref35)</sup>
- **Pulling direction.** In 3-0 silk tied by 368 residents, knots laid by pulling in alternate directions were stronger at every count from 2 to 7 throws; at 7 throws, 7.7% of alternately pulled knots untied against 19.6% of knots pulled in a constant direction.<sup>[[36]](#ref36)</sup>
- **Traction.** In laparoscopic testing, tying each knot with higher traction increased the security of half-knot and half-hitch sequences.<sup>[[31]](#ref31)</sup>
- **Laparoscopic versus hand tying.** In knots tied in fresh postmortem human stomach, three half-hitches and surgeon's knots tied laparoscopically were weaker than the same hand-tied knots, and an additional throw increased security; hand-tied knots needed 4 half-hitches in nylon, polytetrafluoroethylene, braided polyester suture and polyamide and 3 in polyglactin 910, braided polyester fiber, silk and polydioxanone.<sup>[[37]](#ref37)</sup>
- **Fluid.** DynaCord needed 6 throws dry, 4 wet and 5 in fat to rupture without unravelling, against 7 in all three conditions for FiberWire.<sup>[[38]](#ref38)</sup>

## Knot Bulk

- **Volume and weight.** In USP 2 polydioxanone square start knots, mean knot weight increased from 20.3 milligrams at 4 throws to 32.0 milligrams at 8.<sup>[[24]](#ref24)</sup>
- **Extrusion.** In a swine subcuticular model with 4-0 braided absorbable surgeon's knots, cumulative suture extrusion over 5 weeks was 17% with 3 throws, 10% with 4 and 30% with 5 in lactomer (Polysorb), and averaged 31% in coated polyglactin 910 against 19% in lactomer.<sup>[[39]](#ref39)</sup>
- **Reviews.** A review of 86 studies reported 3 to 5 throws for square and surgeon's knots, 3 throws with 1 or 2 turns for the Aberdeen knot, and an optimal tail length of 3 mm.<sup>[[4]](#ref4)</sup>
