# Wetting Surgical Gloves for Knot Tying

Some surgeons moisten gloved fingertips with sterile saline to adjust suture handling during hand tying. The intent is to reduce friction between suture and glove, to help throws run down smoothly, and to limit the suture's tendency to abrade the glove, most often with monofilament and ultrahigh-molecular-weight polyethylene (UHMWPE) braids. This is a handling preference. No study shows that wetting gloves prevents glove perforation, anastomotic leakage or surgical-site infection, and the studies below primarily test suture and knot materials under laboratory conditions.

See [Sutures](/docs/foundations/surgical-principles/sutures) for material selection and [Surgical Gloving](/docs/foundations/perioperative-care/intraoperative-care/surgical-gloving) for barrier protection.

## Wet Suture Is Not the Same Intervention as Wet Gloves

| Study | What was actually tested | Interpretation |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Pietschmann 2011** | Arthroscopic knots in five No. 2 suture materials (PDS II, Ethibond, FiberWire, Orthocord, Herculine) under dry versus saline-soaked test conditions with cyclic loading; 30 knot slippages were reported dry and 14 wet (P < 0.027), mainly with UHMWPE sutures and sliding knots.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup> | Material- and test-specific bench evidence, not a clinical trial of moistening gloves. |
| **Savage 2023** | Four No. 2 suture materials and four arthroscopic knots, tested dry or after 24-hour saline soaking, under a monotonic tensile test.<sup>[[2]](#ref2)</sup> | Most sutures had lower tensile strength in the wet state, whereas the four knots performed similarly wet with ForceFiber. Knot, material and environment interacted, so moisture is not uniformly beneficial or even neutral. |
| **Coleridge 2017** | Veterinary bench testing of continuous-pattern ending knots in polyglactin 910 or PDS, exposed to electrolyte solution, serum or fat.<sup>[[3]](#ref3)</sup> | Effects depend on the tested combination. This does not establish a GU knot-tying protocol. |
| **Muffly 2012** | Saline versus petroleum exposure of sutures.<sup>[[4]](#ref4)</sup> | Petroleum exposure impaired performance relative to saline. This does not show that blood, povidone-iodine or every other fluid behaves like petroleum. |

These bench studies do not support a universal instruction to wet every monofilament or high-strength braid before tying, or to soak sutures for better clinical outcomes.

## What the Glove-Damage Studies Show

Enz and colleagues examined gloves used specifically for knot tying in 118 orthopedic operations (40 total hip arthroplasties, 42 total knee arthroplasties, 36 rotator cuff repairs). Damage occurred in gloves from 25% of hip, 36.6% of knee and 25% of rotator cuff operations. The pulling hand was affected in 46.2% of damaged hip cases, 75% of knee cases and 66.7% of rotator cuff cases, with lesions clustered at the middle, index and ring fingertips. These are operation-level proportions from one setting and glove product, not the expected perforation rate for GU surgeons. A bacterial pass-through test under simulated conditions (3 surgeons, 45 minutes of wear) recovered *Staphylococcus hominis* and *Bacillus cereus*. It did not measure postoperative infection or show that saline wetting prevents passage.<sup>[[5]](#ref5)</sup>

In an in vitro study in which one double-gloved surgeon tied arthroscopic knots with four high-tensile sutures, the overall glove perforation rate was 3.4% and was detected only by electroconductivity testing. All perforations occurred in 0.32-mm powdered gloves (6.8%); none occurred in 0.24-mm powder-free gloves. The inner glove perforated 5.7 times more often than the outer glove, and no set had simultaneous inner and outer perforation.<sup>[[8]](#ref8)</sup>

In a randomized experiment in which surgeons tied 1,466 knots single-gloved and double-gloved, double gloving reduced the knot quality score by 24% overall and by almost 50% with 4-0 suture (95% CI 13–93%). The experiment did not measure clinical anastomotic failure. Barrier protection is selected using the broader gloving evidence; a 2026 consensus statement built on four systematic reviews issued 10 recommendations on glove fit, double gloving, indicator gloves and glove-change frequency.<sup>[[9]](#ref9)</sup> Changing an outer glove has not been shown to restore knot performance in the knot-quality experiment.<sup>[[6]](#ref6)</sup>

## Practical Application

- If saline-moistened fingertips improve control, use a sterile source within the operative field and assess the actual knot's seating and tension. Do not add unvalidated lubricants or assume moisture improves security.
- Avoid wrapping tight suture loops around fingers or using excessive traction. A suspected glove breach requires a glove change; wetting cannot repair it. Follow the [gloving protocol](/docs/foundations/perioperative-care/intraoperative-care/surgical-gloving).
- Select the knot configuration and number of throws for the actual material, caliber and task. Laboratory findings do not justify one throw count for every GU suture. Loop security and final knot security are different properties.<sup>[[7]](#ref7)</sup>
- Saline-soaked chromic gut, nylon, silk and Vicryl (3-0 to 5-0) generally needed 4 throws for surgeon's and square knots and 5 for sliding knots, with no gain beyond 5 throws.<sup>[[10]](#ref10)</sup> Polyfilament sutures reached secure knots with four or five half-knot or half-hitch throws, whereas monofilament knots were less predictable and needed six throws with symmetrical sliding half-hitches or blocking half-hitches.<sup>[[7]](#ref7)</sup> For laparoscopic knots, H3H2 or five half-hitches are preferred, and half-knot combinations are secured with at least two blocking half-hitches.<sup>[[11]](#ref11)</sup>
- For robotic intracorporeal tying, console gloves do not contact the suture. Focus on instrument handling, loop formation and tissue tension; wetting the surgeon's gloves before docking is not an evidence-based knot-security intervention.

## Where Knot Handling Matters in Reconstruction

The following are settings in which fine monofilament or high-tensile sutures are tied under tension. They are examples of where suture handling matters, not evidence that wetting gloves improves the result.

- **Anastomotic urethroplasty** with 4-0 or 5-0 PDS, where the working space is tight.
- **Vesicourethral anastomosis** after prostatectomy and salvage urethroplasty.
- **High-tensile sutures** for sling fixation, pubovaginal sling fascia and prosthesis-tubing tie-downs.
- **Robotic or laparoscopic intracorporeal knots**, which are hard to revise once tied.
