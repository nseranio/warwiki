# Locking Stitch (Running-Locked Suture)

The **locking stitch** is a modification of a continuous (running) suture in which the needle passes through the loop of the preceding stitch before advancing to the next bite, limiting how freely tension redistributes along the line. It is a traditional option for bleeding vaginal or cervical mucosa. It is not the default for every perineal or episiotomy repair.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[3]](#ref3)</sup>

---

## What Locking Does

- **Limits tension redistribution.** Compared with a plain running suture, each locked loop maintains more local tension; excessive tension can constrict tissue.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup>
- **Can help hemostasis.** Local compression is why a locking stitch may be used for oozing vaginal or cervical mucosa, although it is not required for every repair.<sup>[[2]](#ref2)[[3]](#ref3)</sup>
- **Has a traditional shortening rationale, not proven prevention.** The older rationale for locking the vaginal mucosal layer was to prevent vaginal shortening. The Cochrane review states that good-quality evidence for that theory is lacking.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup>

---

## When to Lock

- **Bleeding vaginal mucosa during laceration repair.** A traditional option when local hemostasis is needed; the vaginal-shortening rationale remains unproven.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[2]](#ref2)</sup>
- **Bleeding vaginal and cervical lacerations.** ACOG describes running-locking or interrupted sutures when repair is needed. For a cervical laceration, the first stitch is placed above the apex; subsequent interrupted or continuous locking sutures may incorporate the full cervical thickness outward through the raw edges. These technique choices are largely expert opinion, not comparative trial findings.<sup>[[3]](#ref3)</sup>
- **Any mucosal closure where hemostasis is the priority.** Locking "can be used if needed for hemostasis" and is not mandatory for every mucosal closure.<sup>[[2]](#ref2)</sup>

## When *Not* to Lock

- **Routine perineal muscle and skin closure.** Continuous non-locking repair is commonly used; avoid overtightened locked loops in tissue likely to swell. The trials below compare whole repair techniques, not an isolated lock in one layer.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup>
- **When edema is anticipated.** A non-locking continuous suture allows more tension redistribution; this is a mechanical rationale, not a trial-proven outcome for every tissue.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup>

---

## The Evidence Trend: Lock Selectively, Not by Default

Although locking the vaginal mucosa is *traditional*, a Cochrane review of 16 trials (8,184 women enrolled) found continuous repair associated with less pain up to 10 days postpartum than interrupted repair (RR 0.76, 95% CI 0.66 to 0.88; nine trials).<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup> ACOG prefers continuous over interrupted suturing for second-degree lacerations.<sup>[[3]](#ref3)</sup> Many trials changed the vaginal stitch and the muscle and skin closure together, so these findings do not isolate the effect of locking alone. Suture-material choice is a separate question addressed by another Cochrane review, not evidence for or against a lock.<sup>[[4]](#ref4)</sup> In practice, locking is used selectively for hemostasis.

| | Lock | Don't lock |
|---|---|---|
| **Tissue** | Oozing vaginal or cervical mucosa | Routine perineal muscle and skin closure |
| **Goal** | Local hemostasis | Avoid excessive local tension and permit redistribution |
| **Typical use** | Selected bleeding vaginal/cervical lacerations | Continuous non-locking second-degree repair when hemostasis permits |
