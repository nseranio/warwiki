# Locking Stitch (Running-Locked Suture)

The **locking stitch** is a modification of a continuous (running) suture in which the needle passes through the loop of the preceding stitch before advancing to the next bite, limiting how freely tension redistributes along the line. It is a traditional option for bleeding vaginal mucosa or a bleeding cervical laceration. It is not the default for every perineal or episiotomy repair.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[3]](#ref3)</sup>

---

## What Locking Does

- **Limits tension redistribution.** Compared with a plain running suture, each locked loop maintains more local tension; excessive tension can constrict tissue.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup>
- **Can help hemostasis.** Local compression is why a locking stitch may be used for oozing vaginal mucosa or a bleeding cervical laceration, although it is not required for every repair.<sup>[[2]](#ref2)[[3]](#ref3)</sup>
- **Has a traditional shortening rationale, not proven prevention.** The older rationale for locking the vaginal mucosal layer was to prevent vaginal shortening. The Cochrane review states that good-quality evidence for that theory is lacking.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup>

---

## When to Lock

- **Bleeding vaginal mucosa during laceration repair.** A traditional option when local hemostasis is needed; the vaginal-shortening rationale remains unproven.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[2]](#ref2)</sup>
- **Bleeding vaginal and cervical lacerations.** When a vulvar or vaginal laceration needs repair, ACOG recommends a running-locking or interrupted suture that incorporates the underlying tissue, with 2-0 chromic or polyglactin by expert opinion. For a cervical laceration, the first stitch is placed above the apex; subsequent interrupted or continuous locking sutures may incorporate the full cervical thickness outward through the raw edges. These technique choices are largely expert opinion, not comparative trial findings.<sup>[[3]](#ref3)</sup>
- **Second-degree obstetric repair with mucosal bleeding.** During running closure of the vaginal mucosa and underlying fascia, locking can be used if needed for hemostasis.<sup>[[2]](#ref2)</sup>

## When *Not* to Lock

- In second-degree obstetric repair, the perineal muscle is reapproximated with continuous nonlocking sutures. Well-apposed perineal skin may be left unsutured; if skin closure is chosen, avoid overtightened locked loops.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[2]](#ref2)[[3]](#ref3)</sup>
- In episiotomy or second-degree perineal repair, a continuous nonlocking suture is intended to distribute tension along the line as tissue swells.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup>

---

## The Evidence Trend: Lock Selectively, Not by Default

Although locking the vaginal mucosa is *traditional*, a Cochrane review included 16 trials (8,184 women enrolled); its meta-analysis of nine trials (4,231 women) found a lower risk of pain up to 10 days postpartum with continuous than interrupted perineal closure (all layers or perineal skin only; RR 0.76, 95% CI 0.66 to 0.88).<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup> ACOG prefers continuous over interrupted suturing for second-degree lacerations (Level B).<sup>[[3]](#ref3)</sup> Many trials changed the vaginal stitch and the muscle and skin closure together, so these findings do not isolate the effect of locking alone. In practice, locking is used selectively for hemostasis.

| | Lock | Don't lock |
|---|---|---|
| **Tissue** | Oozing vaginal or cervical mucosa | Routine perineal muscle and skin closure |
| **Goal** | Local hemostasis | Avoid excessive local tension and permit redistribution |
| **Typical use** | Selected bleeding vaginal/cervical lacerations | Continuous non-locking second-degree repair when hemostasis permits |
