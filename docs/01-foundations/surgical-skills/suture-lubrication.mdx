# Suture Lubrication: Bacitracin, Petroleum, and Saline

Some surgeons run sutures through ointment, mineral oil, or petroleum jelly to ease handling. Direct evidence on this intraoperative practice is limited. A bench comparison found lower failure loads for knots exposed to petroleum gauze than to saline; that result does not by itself establish the effects of every oil or bacitracin ointment, or a difference in clinical wound outcomes.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup> Bacitracin irrigation, postoperative bacitracin wound care and antimicrobial-coated sutures involve different exposures and outcomes; none directly tests suture lubrication.

For the companion maneuver of wetting the glove instead of the suture, see [Glove Wetting for Knot Tying](/docs/foundations/surgical-skills/glove-wetting). For the broader material context (monofilament versus braided, triclosan-coated sutures), see [Sutures](/docs/foundations/surgical-principles/sutures).

---

## Why Lubricant Choice Matters at the Knot

In dry polypropylene sliding-knot models, resistance to slippage varied with friction, throw geometry, tying pretension and plastic deformation.<sup>[[2]](#ref2)</sup> A fluid on a suture can reach the knot as well as the glove–suture interface. Technique and the specific suture's instructions still matter.

## Evidence

### Petroleum-gauze exposure lowered knot failure load in one bench study

| Study | Design | Finding |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Muffly 2012**<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup> | 2-0 square knots in PDS, polyglyconate, glycolide/lactide copolymer and silk; tied before 12-hour exposure to Xeroform (bismuth/petrolatum gauze) versus saline soaking | Gauze-exposed knots failed at a lower pooled mean load (116.7 vs 123.8 N; p = 0.002; 285 knots); brief suture coating and petrolatum alone were not tested |
| **Johanns 2023**<sup>[[2]](#ref2)</sup> | Model experiments and finite-element simulation of surgical sliding knots | Knot strength depends on the interplay of friction and plastic deformation, tying pre-tension and the number of throws; the study did not compare lubricants |
| **Coleridge 2017**<sup>[[10]](#ref10)</sup> | Large-gauge equine-surgery knots exposed to balanced electrolyte solution, carboxymethylcellulose, equine serum or fat | Media exposure had no effect on knot holding capacity or improved it in some knot and suture combinations; an in-vitro veterinary study, not a human saline-lubrication trial |

A Henry Schein bacitracin zinc ointment label lists white petrolatum, mineral oil and paraffin as inactive ingredients.<sup>[[16]](#ref16)</sup> In Muffly's bench comparison of tied knots exposed to Xeroform gauze for 12 hours or soaked in saline, 6 of 285 untied and 279 broke during tensile testing.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup> Whether coating a particular suture with ointment changes its knot security is an extrapolation, not a measured result of that study.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup>

A separate seven-day immersion study (Alnaqi 2023, fusidic-acid ointment among several agents) measured suture-strand failure load, not knot security or brief intraoperative handling. Its results neither establish benefit nor rule out harm from lubricating a suture before tying.<sup>[[3]](#ref3)</sup>

### Bacitracin: in-vitro suture activity is not an outcome study

- **Adkins 2022.**<sup>[[4]](#ref4)</sup> Bacitracin-soaked sutures had measurable in vitro inhibition zones against *S. aureus* and MRSA versus unsoaked controls. The finding is in vitro only.
- **CDC 2017 SSI guideline.**<sup>[[5]](#ref5)</sup> It recommends against applying antimicrobial agents to the surgical incision to prevent SSI (Category IB) and says to consider triclosan-coated sutures (Category II). Neither recommendation is a direct comparison of products used to lubricate a suture.
- **SHEA/IDSA 2022 SSI update.**<sup>[[6]](#ref6)</sup> It states that bacitracin irrigation is contraindicated, citing anaphylactic-shock cases and US withdrawal of injectable bacitracin. Case reports describe anaphylaxis after bacitracin ointment application to abrasions and clean dermatologic surgical wounds.<sup>[[15]](#ref15)</sup>
- **Smack 1996 JAMA RCT.**<sup>[[11]](#ref11)</sup> In this trial, 922 dermatologic-surgery patients were randomized to postoperative wound application of white petrolatum or bacitracin. Among evaluable patients, infection was 9/440 (2.0%) versus 4/444 (0.9%; p = 0.37), and contact dermatitis was 0 versus 4 (p = 0.12).
- **Goswami 2019.**<sup>[[7]](#ref7)</sup> Polymyxin and bacitracin irrigation was ineffective at bacterial killing in vitro and inferior to povidone-iodine, while showing fibroblast cytotoxicity.
- **Roy 2024.**<sup>[[8]](#ref8)</sup> A single-institution retrospective review (188 women) of implant-based breast reconstruction across the period when FDA requested withdrawal of bacitracin for injection in 2020 found no association between bacitracin irrigation and infection.<sup>[[12]](#ref12)</sup> This observational comparison neither proves absence of benefit nor tests suture ointment.

### Engineered alternatives exist

Manufactured suture coatings offer different handling or antimicrobial properties; check the specific product instructions; coatings do not all behave alike:

- **Coated Vicryl (polyglactin 910)** uses a polyglactin 370 and calcium stearate coating; uncoated Vicryl variants also exist.<sup>[[14]](#ref14)</sup> An extraocular-muscle evaluation reported easier tissue passage with the coated form.<sup>[[17]](#ref17)</sup>
- **Silicone- and wax-coated multifilaments** are designed to reduce passage friction; knot behavior depends on material and technique.<sup>[[9]](#ref9)</sup>
- **Triclosan-coated sutures** (Vicryl Plus, PDS Plus, Monocryl Plus) are manufactured antimicrobial products for an SSI-prevention purpose. The CDC 2017 guideline says to consider them for SSI prevention (Category II, weak recommendation).<sup>[[5]](#ref5)</sup> A 2025 Surgical Infection Society guideline recommends triclosan-coated suture for adult abdominal-incision closure (Grade 1A).<sup>[[13]](#ref13)</sup>

## Summary Comparison

| Agent | Evidence for handling | Knot / infection evidence | Practical interpretation |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **Sterile saline** | Direct glide benefit not quantified here | Comparator in Muffly knot test; no SSI-prevention claim<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup> | If moisture is needed, use sterile technique and follow suture IFU; do not claim proven clinical superiority |
| **Petroleum gauze** | Handling benefit not measured | Lower mean bench knot-failure load than saline (116.7 vs 123.8 N, p = 0.002)<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup> | Avoid assuming that petroleum exposure is mechanically neutral for a knot-critical closure |
| **Mineral oil / bacitracin ointment** | Specific intraoperative coating effect untested in the cited knot study | Bacitracin in-vitro antimicrobial activity is not clinical SSI benefit; irrigation and postoperative-wound data are different settings<sup>[[4]](#ref4)[[6]](#ref6)[[11]](#ref11)</sup> | Do not use an unverified coating to claim knot or infection benefit; consider allergy history and IFU |
| **Triclosan-coated sutures** | Manufactured coating, not ad-hoc lubricant | CDC Category II: consider for SSI prevention; knot comparison to ointment not supplied<sup>[[5]](#ref5)</sup> | Consider when appropriate for infection prevention, not as a proven solution to handling friction |

---

## Bottom Line

- Do not rank saline, petroleum, mineral oil and bacitracin for clinical knot or wound outcomes from these heterogeneous bench, irrigation and postoperative studies.
- If a suture is difficult to handle, first check material and coating instructions and technique; sterile saline on the glove is a practical option, but its clinical advantage over alternatives has not been established here (see [Glove Wetting for Knot Tying](/docs/foundations/surgical-skills/glove-wetting)).
- Petroleum-gauze exposure lowered bench knot-failure load relative to saline. Bacitracin ointment on a suture has no demonstrated infection-prevention or handling advantage in the cited studies.
