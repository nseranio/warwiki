# Barbed (Knotless) Sutures

**Barbed sutures** are self-anchoring monofilament sutures with **helically arranged barbs cut into the suture surface** that grip tissue as the suture is passed — eliminating the need for knot tying and distributing tension uniformly along the closure.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[2]](#ref2)</sup> They are FDA-approved for soft-tissue approximation and have been adopted across reconstructive urology, urogynecology, gynecology, orthopedics, plastic surgery, and general-surgical body-wall closure.

For the reconstructive urologist, the most relevant barbed-suture applications are the **robot-assisted vesicourethral anastomosis**, **pyeloplasty**, **partial-nephrectomy renorrhaphy**, and **sacrocolpopexy mesh attachment** — all situations where knotless closure shortens suture time, eliminates the need for an assistant to hold tension, and prevents slippage during continuous running closures in confined spaces.

---

## History and Concept

The barbed suture was **inspired by the North American porcupine quill** — a biological structure that grips tissue directionally with barbs that resist backward motion. The original concept envisioned a bidirectional array of barbs in which one set anchored the other, avoiding the **constricting loop** that underlies the classical surgical principle of *"approximate, don't strangulate."* The constricting loop of traditional knots produces focal tissue ischemia — the most common pathway to wound necrosis and dehiscence.<sup>[[2]](#ref2)</sup>

Key milestones:<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[2]](#ref2)</sup>

- Original concept derived from porcupine quill biomechanics (mid-2000s)
- FDA approval for soft-tissue approximation
- Clinical release of unidirectional **V-Loc** (Covidien, now Medtronic) and bidirectional **Quill** (Angiotech, now Surgical Specialties)
- Widespread adoption in minimally invasive surgery, body contouring, and gynecologic/urologic reconstruction

## Types

### Unidirectional barbed sutures (V-Loc)

- **Barbs oriented in one direction** along the suture length
- **Welded terminal loop** at the trailing end anchors the suture on first pass
- Examples: **V-Loc** (V-Loc 90, V-Loc 180), Stratafix Spiral
- **Associated with higher rates of wound dehiscence and suture extrusion** in meta-analysis compared with bidirectional variants<sup>[[3]](#ref3)</sup>

### Bidirectional barbed sutures (Quill)

- **Barbs oriented in opposite directions from the midpoint** — one half faces each end
- Suturing begins at the **center** of the tissue and proceeds outward in both directions
- **No terminal loop required** — barbs anchor from center outward
- **More even distribution of tension** along the closure
- Examples: **Quill**, Quill Monoderm
- May have **lower complication rates** than unidirectional variants<sup>[[3]](#ref3)</sup>

---

## Mechanism of Action

| Principle | Effect |
|---|---|
| **Self-anchoring** | Barbs grip tissue on passage; no knot required |
| **No assistant needed to hold tension** | Surgeon can suture single-handed in deep or minimally invasive fields |
| **Slippage prevention** | Anastomotic integrity maintained without constant reassessment<sup>[[4]](#ref4)</sup> |
| **Uniform tensile distribution** | Even tension across the wound; no focal point of stress |
| **No constricting loop** | Avoids focal tissue ischemia — the ergonomic basis of the original design<sup>[[2]](#ref2)</sup> |

### Biomechanics

- **Tensile strength:** knotless barbed closures have **lower tensile strength than layered conventional closures** (46.5 N vs 79.3 N) but **both exceed clinical wound tension requirements**<sup>[[5]](#ref5)</sup>
- **Joint closure strength and permeability** comparable to standard sutures<sup>[[6]](#ref6)</sup>
- **Ultimate tensile strength and stiffness** greater than conventional sutures in Pulvertaft tendon weaves<sup>[[7]](#ref7)</sup>
- **Tissue reaction** mild and comparable to conventional monofilament sutures<sup>[[8]](#ref8)</sup>

## Clinical Applications in GU Reconstruction

### Vesicourethral Anastomosis (RARP)

The urologic application with the strongest evidence base. Zorn 2012 prospective randomized trial:<sup>[[4]](#ref4)</sup>

| Parameter | Barbed | Conventional | P |
|---|---|---|---|
| Anastomosis time | **13.1 min** | 20.8 min | &lt;0.001 |
| Nurse set-up time | **31 s** | 294 s | &lt;0.001 |
| Material cost (CAN$) | **$48.05** | $70.25 | — |
| Need to readjust suture tension | 6% | 24% | 0.03 |
| Continence at 1, 3, 6 months | Equivalent | Equivalent | NS |

Subsequent meta-analyses confirm **~5–10 minute savings** in anastomosis time and **~10 minute** overall operative time reduction, with **equivalent continence** and **no increase in anastomotic leak**.<sup>[[9]](#ref9)[[10]](#ref10)</sup>

### Pyeloplasty (robotic / laparoscopic)

- **Shorter operative time** and **shorter suture time** (277 s vs 364 s)<sup>[[11]](#ref11)</sup>
- **Reduced suture-line shortening** (–26.6% vs –40.7%)<sup>[[11]](#ref11)</sup>
- **92% success rate** with symptom resolution in contemporary series<sup>[[12]](#ref12)</sup>
- No significant difference in redo-pyeloplasty rates compared with non-barbed<sup>[[13]](#ref13)</sup>

### Renorrhaphy (partial nephrectomy)

- **Warm ischemia time reduced from 24.7 → 18.5 min** (P=0.008)<sup>[[14]](#ref14)</sup>
- **No suture slippage or tearing** reported
- Barbs hold **sliding-clip renorrhaphy** intact without redundant clips — particularly useful in robotic partial nephrectomy
- The reduced ischemia time is clinically meaningful for parenchymal preservation

### Ureteral and bladder reconstruction

- **Knotless ureteroneocystostomy** in robotic ureteral reconstruction
- **Augmentation cystoplasty** closure
- **Diversion anastomoses** where knot tying is cumbersome

## Clinical Applications — Gynecology and Urogynecology

### Vaginal cuff closure (laparoscopic / robotic hysterectomy)

Meta-analysis across multiple RCTs:<sup>[[15]](#ref15)[[16]](#ref16)</sup>

- **Suture time reduced 4–5 min**
- **Operative time reduced 8–38 min** depending on approach
- **No difference** in vaginal cuff dehiscence rates
- **Reduced granulation tissue** formation (2.6% vs 11.3%)
- **Reduced SSI** (RR 0.26)<sup>[[15]](#ref15)</sup>

### Cesarean section — uterine closure

Peleg 2018 RCT:<sup>[[17]](#ref17)</sup>

- Uterine closure time **reduced by 1.7 min**
- Lower need for **hemostatic sutures** (median 0 vs 1)
- **Reduced blood loss** during incision closure (221 vs 268 mL)
- The "fishbone" double-layer barbed technique associated with **lower uterine-niche / isthmocele incidence**<sup>[[18]](#ref18)</sup>

### Sacrocolpopexy — mesh attachment

- **Mesh attachment time reduced 11–16 min**<sup>[[19]](#ref19)</sup>
- **Comparable anatomic outcomes** at 12 months
- No difference in anatomic success between permanent and absorbable barbed sutures<sup>[[20]](#ref20)</sup>

---

## Clinical Applications — Other

### Fascial closure (midline laparotomy, abdominal wall)

- **Significantly reduced SSI: 1.9% vs 4.0%** (OR 0.41, P&lt;0.05)<sup>[[21]](#ref21)</sup>
- Shorter closure time
- No increase in incisional hernia

### Orthopedic — TKA wound closure

- **Closure time reduced 4.7 min**<sup>[[22]](#ref22)</sup>
- Net **$175 USD savings per case** accounting for OR time
- Equivalent wound complication rates

### Plastic / body contouring surgery

- Faster closure
- Reduced seroma formation in some series
- **Caution:** higher rates of wound dehiscence (OR 1.60) and suture extrusion (OR 3.97), **particularly with unidirectional sutures**<sup>[[3]](#ref3)</sup>
- Two-layer closure with barbed sutures associated with higher wound-separation rates than single-layer<sup>[[23]](#ref23)</sup>

## Complications

### Meta-analysis summary<sup>[[9]](#ref9)[[3]](#ref3)[[23]](#ref23)</sup>

| Complication | Barbed vs conventional | Effect size | Significance |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Overall postoperative complications** | Higher | OR 1.43 | P=0.03 |
| **Wound dehiscence** | Higher (esp. unidirectional) | OR 1.60 | P=0.02 |
| **Suture extrusion** | Higher (esp. unidirectional) | OR 3.97 | P=0.0001 |
| **Incisional infection** | No difference | — | NS |
| **Delayed wound healing** | No difference | — | NS |
| **Hematoma** | No difference | — | NS |

**Unidirectional &gt; bidirectional** for both wound dehiscence and suture extrusion — bidirectional Quill generally has a more favorable complication profile than unidirectional V-Loc in comparative data.<sup>[[3]](#ref3)</sup>

### The small-bowel obstruction (SBO) question

Early case reports raised concern that barbed sutures could cause SBO by **entrapping small bowel** on intraperitoneal knots.<sup>[[24]](#ref24)</sup> Key features of reported cases:

- Average presentation: 26.3 days postop (range 1–196 days)
- Common presentations: abdominal pain (89%), vomiting (33%), distension (28%)
- Often presented as **mesenteric volvulus on CT**
- Required operative exploration (laparoscopy often successful)

**But large institutional data refute a causal link.** The Sassun 2026 cohort of **20,260 intra-abdominal barbed-suture cases** found:<sup>[[25]](#ref25)</sup>

- 264 patients (1.3%) developed intestinal obstruction
- 102 (0.5%) underwent reoperation
- **No case of obstruction was attributable to barbed suture use**
- Alternative etiologies: adhesions, anastomotic leaks, abscesses, bleeding, hernias

**Current consensus:** SBO from barbed-suture entrapment is a rare event, and the large cohort data challenge the early concerns. Standard intraperitoneal precautions (burying cut ends, avoiding free intraperitoneal loops) remain appropriate.

### Brand-specific reactivity

- **V-Loc** — consistently **lowest tissue reaction scores**; significantly less erythema than Quill at postoperative day 10<sup>[[8]](#ref8)[[23]](#ref23)</sup>
- **Quill** — higher rates of excessive erythema along incision sites compared with V-Loc

## Technique Considerations

### Optimal technique

- **Single-layer closure** may be preferable to two-layer for barbed sutures — lower wound-separation rates<sup>[[23]](#ref23)</sup>
- **At least 6 passes per mesh leaflet** for sacrocolpopexy mesh attachment<sup>[[19]](#ref19)</sup>
- **Bidirectional** may offer advantages over unidirectional for body-wall and gynecologic applications<sup>[[3]](#ref3)</sup>
- **Trim excess suture flush with tissue** to minimize extrusion risk
- **Bury free ends within the closure** where possible to reduce intraperitoneal loose ends

### Learning curve

A present but modest learning curve — the tactile feedback of barb engagement is different from conventional suturing, and the suture cannot be "backed out" to adjust tension without barb disengagement.

### Tissue considerations

- Barbed sutures may induce **slightly more fibrosis** than conventional monofilament — relevant consideration in anastomoses where mucosal healing and supple tissue are priorities<sup>[[26]](#ref26)</sup>
- **Watertight anastomosis** routinely achieved without knot tying in urinary-tract applications

## Key Takeaways for the Reconstructive Urologist

1. **Vesicourethral anastomosis during RARP** is the highest-yield urologic application — meaningful time savings, equivalent continence, cost-neutral to cost-favorable
2. **Partial-nephrectomy renorrhaphy** — reduced warm ischemia time with no slippage
3. **Pyeloplasty** — faster suturing with reduced suture-line shortening
4. **Unidirectional vs. bidirectional matters** — bidirectional has better complication profile; consider for high-risk tissue
5. **SBO concerns from barbed-suture entrapment are not supported by large cohort data**
6. **Single-layer running technique** is generally preferred when using barbed suture; two-layer closures increase wound-separation risk in some contexts
7. **Bury free ends** within the closure whenever possible
