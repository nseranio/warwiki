# SEXI — Stance Exchange In situ

**Stance Exchange In situ (SEXI)** is the family of **needle-handling maneuvers that change needle orientation without removing it from the operative field**. Named and codified by **Hosseinpour, Hilton, and Nashef** at Papworth Hospital in a 2005 *Interactive CardioVascular and Thoracic Surgery* technical note, SEXI describes **five distinct maneuvers** by which the operating surgeon flips, swings, or pirouettes the needle between forehand and backhand stance using only the tools already in hand — needleholder and forceps — without ever taking the needle out of the wound to remount it.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup>

The clinical value is simple and practical: **needle remounting outside the operative field after every suture wastes time** and, in confined pelvic and perineal wounds typical of reconstructive urology and urogynecology, each remount is a hand-motion that breaks the rhythm of a running anastomosis or apical closure. The five SEXI maneuvers preserve that rhythm. Though the original paper is cardiothoracic, the techniques transfer directly to any fine, at-depth anastomotic work — vesicourethral anastomosis, ureteral reimplant, vaginal cuff, urethroplasty anastomotic bites.

---

## Why the maneuvers matter

Any continuous or interrupted anastomosis requires **alternating forehand and backhand stances**. The conventional approach — withdraw the needle, remount it in the driver at the opposite angle, then re-enter the field — adds 2–4 seconds per suture, blurs hand-eye rhythm, and forces the assistant to repeatedly pass the needle back. In a 30-bite vesicourethral anastomosis this is **a minute or more of cumulative dead time**, and in narrow perineal wounds the repeated re-entry can unseat atraumatic tissue positioning.

SEXI maneuvers trade the remount for an **in-wound re-orientation** using the needleholder and a forceps (usually the assistant's or the operator's second hand). Once learned, they are **faster, quieter, and more elegant** — the original authors' adjectives, and an accurate description.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup>

## The Five Maneuvers

All five assume the needle is held in a standard needleholder with the curve facing the direction of the next pass. The goal is to change **stance** — forehand vs backhand — without withdrawing the needle from the field.

### 1. Tissue Anchor

The simplest maneuver. **Place the needle tip lightly against a firm, non-critical tissue point** (a pledget, adventitia, a dry gauze edge, or — intra-abdominally — a bowel-wall seromuscular point you are not going to suture). **Loosen the needleholder grip just enough** that the needle can rotate; the anchored tip acts as a pivot. **Re-grip at the new stance.**

- **When to use:** quick, small-angle stance changes during a running line.
- **Risk:** damage to the anchoring tissue if the anchor is placed carelessly; keep the anchor off any organ that cannot take a needle-tip scratch.

### 2. Tak Maneuver

Used **mid-bite**, after the needle has passed through the first wall of the anastomosis and is protruding into the lumen. **Advance the needle partially through the second wall, then loosen the driver** and re-grip at the new angle. The **partial-purchase second-wall bite** holds the needle fixed while the grip is changed.

- **When to use:** running bowel or urinary-tract anastomosis where the second wall provides the anchor.
- **Caveat:** works only when you are sure the needle trajectory through the second wall is the trajectory you want. Rotating mid-bite changes the final exit point.

### 3. Saatvedt Swing

**Use the forceps to grasp the needle ~ 1 mm from its tip.** With the forceps tip holding the needle tip, **rotate the needle in a horizontal circle** while the needleholder grip is momentarily loosened. The needle swings around the forceps tip like a hand on a clock. **Re-grip with the needleholder at the new stance.**

- **When to use:** larger rotation angles (&gt; 90°) where a tissue anchor or Tak is insufficient.
- **Setup:** requires forceps with fine, non-traumatic tips (Adson, DeBakey, or fine jeweler's forceps). Standard Gerald forceps are adequate.

### 4. Large Flip

**Approach the needle at an oblique angle with the needleholder jaws open**, catch it at the desired new position, then **rotate** — effectively flipping the needle from forehand to backhand stance (or vice versa) in one driver-jaw motion. No forceps required.

- **When to use:** full forehand-to-backhand stance reversal when you do not have a second instrument free or want a single-hand move.
- **Difficulty:** highest of the five. Requires a feel for where the needle body will be after the flip.

### 5. Pirouette

**Combined Saatvedt swing + Large flip, executed with only the needleholder** (no forceps). The surgeon rotates the needle body through approximately 180° using wrist rotation alone. The needle is swept through a vertical arc and caught at the new stance.

- **When to use:** highest level of the five — a sustained running anastomosis where the surgeon never lets go of the needle driver.
- **Difficulty:** practice required. This is the "expert" SEXI maneuver and the one most worth deliberate drilling on a benchtop practice pad before using in the OR.

## How to Learn Them

The original SEXI paper is a technical note with line diagrams — short, concrete, and worth reading once.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup> Beyond that:

- **Benchtop practice with a tissue-pad model** and a fine 4-0 suture on a taper needle — start with Tissue Anchor and Tak (the two easiest), progress to Saatvedt Swing, then to Large Flip, finally to Pirouette.
- The **BBASS (Black Belt Academy of Surgical Skills)** curriculum linked from the [surgical skills index](/docs/foundations/surgical-skills) includes needle-handling drills that reinforce the motor patterns SEXI requires.
- Most reconstructive surgeons use **Tak and Saatvedt Swing** habitually without knowing the names. Large Flip and Pirouette are the two that benefit from explicit deliberate practice.
