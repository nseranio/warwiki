# Self-Locking Knots

**Self-locking knots** are a family of knots that secure a running suture through **friction and geometry** rather than a separate tail-on-tail square tie. They produce **smaller, flatter, more secure** closures than conventional square or surgeon's knots — and in multiple in-vitro studies outperform conventional knots across nearly every combination of material and gauge tested.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[2]](#ref2)</sup>

The most important members of this family for the reconstructive urologist:

- **Aberdeen knot** — terminal self-locking knot for ending a running closure
- **Forwarder knot** — alternative terminal knot with superior knot-holding capacity under tension
- **Middler / loop-lock technique** — mid-run tensioning knot for long running closures

---

## Why Self-Locking Knots

In classical square-knot technique, the knot is a separate mechanical element on top of the suture line. In a self-locking knot, **the final construction is part of the running suture itself** — the suture passes through its own loop(s), and friction holds it in place. The consequences:

- **Smaller final knot** — less tissue bulk, less palpability, better cosmesis
- **Higher knot-holding capacity** — studies show Forwarder and Aberdeen end knots outperform square/surgeon's knots under tension<sup>[[3]](#ref3)</sup>
- **No bulky tail-on-tail tie** needed — useful when one tail is short or when the closure is in a deep pocket where tying would be awkward
- **Compatible with monofilament and braided suture** — with proper throw count

The main trade-off is that self-locking knots **take longer to learn** and require more deliberate hand choreography than a reflex square tie.

---

## Aberdeen Knot — the Standard Terminal Knot

The Aberdeen is the most widely taught self-locking knot in general surgery. It is used at the **end** of a running closure to secure the final loop without a tail-on-tail tie.

See the [dedicated Aberdeen knot page](/docs/foundations/surgical-skills/knot-tying/aberdeen-knot) for step-by-step technique.

**Key points (summary):**

- Constructed from the final loop of the running suture
- Loop is passed through itself 3–4 times in sequence
- Stott "Ultimate Aberdeen" configuration = **3 throws + 2 turns** is the strongest tested variant<sup>[[4]](#ref4)</sup>
- **Stronger and more secure than a surgeon's knot** for ending a running suture line<sup>[[4]](#ref4)</sup>
- Buried, flat, consistent — the defining advantages

---

## Forwarder Knot — Terminal Knot for High-Tension Closures

The **Forwarder knot** is a terminal self-locking knot designed specifically for closures where the suture line will sit under meaningful tension. In large-gauge comparative bench studies, Forwarder end knots provided **increased knot-holding capacity and relative knot security** compared with square or surgeon's knots — making it a reasonable choice for fascial or high-tension closures where the Aberdeen may underperform.<sup>[[3]](#ref3)</sup>

### Construction principle

- Constructed from the final loop of the running suture, similar to Aberdeen
- Differs in the **pass-through pattern** — the loop is passed through itself in a specific sequence that locks by creating additional friction against the previous throw
- **Three to four sequential passes** form the locking configuration

### When to use

- Running fascial closure with tail constraints
- Closures under ongoing tension (high BMI patient, Valsalva stress)
- As an alternative to Aberdeen when additional security is preferred

### Technique pearl

The Forwarder's additional friction comes at the cost of **extra suture consumption** — plan for a longer tail before initiating the terminal knot compared with an Aberdeen. Practice on bench models before first clinical use.

## Middler Knot / Loop-Lock — Mid-Run Tensioning

Running closures occasionally need to be **locked mid-run** — for example, when crossing an anatomic landmark, when tension must be transferred to a new segment, or when breaking the closure into two tension zones. The **Middler knot** (and the closely related **loop-lock technique**) accomplish this without interrupting the running suture.<sup>[[5]](#ref5)</sup>

### Loop-lock principle

During the middle of a running closure, the working strand is used to form a controlled loop that locks against the previous throw:

- The suture is looped around the needle holder (both limbs held by the same hand)
- The free end is grasped through the loop with the needle holder
- The loop and needle holder are pulled apart so the loop seats against the previous throw
- The loop is released and the knot completed while maintaining tension<sup>[[5]](#ref5)</sup>

This creates a locking element mid-run that prevents the running closure from loosening proximally if distal tension is released or adjusted.

### When to use

- Very long running closures (e.g., midline fascial)
- Running closures crossing high-tension zones where proximal loosening is a risk
- When pausing the closure to reassess tension in one segment

### Terminology note

Named variants of mid-run locking knots (including **"Middler"**) appear in surgical-education materials and small-animal and bariatric-surgery literature. The **loop-lock technique** is the most formally described in the peer-reviewed literature.<sup>[[5]](#ref5)</sup>

## Other Self-Locking Knots

### Running noose / sliding self-locking knot

Constructed by the scrub nurse extracorporeally and passed into the wound; pulled tight to form a self-locking first throw. Widely used in **laparoscopic** and bariatric surgery to avoid intracorporeal knot tying at the start of a running closure.<sup>[[2]](#ref2)[[6]](#ref6)</sup>

### SMC knot and related arthroscopic slider knots

Arthroscopic self-locking sliding knots (SMC, Nicky's, Tennessee slider) are used in orthopedic / shoulder reconstruction. They share the self-locking principle but are tuned for arthroscopic cannula delivery. **Rare in GU reconstructive practice** but mentioned for completeness.

## Comparison

| Knot | Role | Best for | Construction complexity |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Aberdeen** | Terminal | Standard running closure endpoint | Moderate — 3–4 pass-throughs |
| **Ultimate Aberdeen (3+2)** | Terminal | Maximum security Aberdeen variant | Slightly higher |
| **Forwarder** | Terminal | High-tension closures; fascia | Moderate — similar to Aberdeen |
| **Middler / loop-lock** | Mid-run | Long running closures; tension zones | Moderate |
| **Running noose (sliding)** | Initial | Lap / MIS extracorporeal first throw | Low — scrub-tied |
| **Square knot** | Conventional | Everything else | Low |
| **Surgeon's knot** | Conventional | Tension, slippery suture | Low–moderate |

## Technical Pearls for Self-Locking Knots

1. **Practice on bench-top models first.** The pass-through patterns are easy to get wrong mid-case, and a failed self-locking knot at the end of a running closure means redoing the entire line.
2. **Count your passes.** Three is the minimum for most self-locking knots; four adds security with modest additional suture consumption.
3. **Maintain tension during construction.** Every pass should be seated taut before the next; slack during construction leaves the finished knot loose.
4. **Suture material matters.** Monofilament sutures (Prolene, PDS) require an extra pass for knot security; braided sutures (Vicryl, Ethibond) hold reliably at the minimum pass count.
5. **Visualize before you cut.** Confirm the knot is seated and holds tension before cutting the tail; a premature cut on an under-seated self-locking knot cannot be rescued.
