# Self-Locking Knots

**Self-locking knots** are a family of knots that secure a running suture through **friction and geometry** rather than a separate tail-on-tail square tie. They produce **smaller, flatter, more secure** closures than conventional square or surgeon's knots — and in multiple in-vitro studies outperform conventional knots across nearly every combination of material and gauge tested.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[2]](#ref2)</sup>

The most important members of this family for the reconstructive urologist:

- **Aberdeen knot** — terminal self-locking knot for ending a running closure
- **Forwarder knot** — alternative terminal knot with superior knot-holding capacity under tension
- **Loop knot** — starting knot for abdominal closure; 2–3× knot-holding capacity of square/surgeon's knots
- **Nice knot** and **Modified Nice knot** — doubled-suture sliding knots for high-tension orthopedic-style and soft-tissue applications
- **Cow hitch** — stiffest tested configuration for high-strength suture material (UHMWPE)
- **Middler / loop-lock technique** — mid-run tensioning for long running closures

---

## Knot Biomechanics — Terminology and Security

**Knot security depends on knot configuration, suture material, number of throws, and tying technique — not on suture size.**<sup>[[7]](#ref7)[[8]](#ref8)</sup>

### Terminology

| Term | Definition |
|---|---|
| **Half-knot (H)** | Two suture ends crossed and pulled through; defined by number of throws (H1 = single throw, H2 = double wrap, H3 = triple wrap)<sup>[[9]](#ref9)</sup> |
| **Half-hitch (S)** | One end wrapped around the other; can be **sliding (s)** or **blocking (b)** |
| **Symmetric knot** | Opposite rotation between throws — e.g., **square knot** (more secure) |
| **Asymmetric knot** | Same rotation between throws — e.g., **granny knot** (less secure; should be avoided) |
| **Loop security** | Force required to keep tissue together after the first half-knot |
| **Knot security** | Force at which the completed knot opens or the suture breaks |

### Loop security values (polyfilament suture)<sup>[[9]](#ref9)</sup>

| Configuration | Loop security |
|---|---|
| H2 | 10 N |
| H3 | 18 N |
| SSs (sliding half-hitches) | 28 N |
| SSsSsSs | 48 N |

*Monofilament sutures have approximately 30–50% lower loop security values than polyfilament.*

### General principles

- **Minimum 4 throws** for a secure square or surgeon's knot<sup>[[7]](#ref7)</sup>
- **Additional throws beyond 5 do not increase security** in most configurations<sup>[[7]](#ref7)</sup>
- **2 throws are never indicated** as a final knot<sup>[[7]](#ref7)</sup>
- **Polyfilament &gt; monofilament** for knot-holding capacity
- **Knot security is independent of suture size** — a 4-0 and a 2-0 with the same material and configuration behave similarly<sup>[[7]](#ref7)</sup>

The granny knot (asymmetric, same-rotation) is consistently the **weakest** of the standard knots and tends to slip — it should be avoided in clinical practice.<sup>[[10]](#ref10)</sup>

## Why Self-Locking Knots

In classical square-knot technique, the knot is a separate mechanical element on top of the suture line. In a self-locking knot, **the final construction is part of the running suture itself** — the suture passes through its own loop(s), and friction holds it in place. The consequences:

- **Smaller final knot** — less tissue bulk, less palpability, better cosmesis
- **Higher knot-holding capacity** — studies show Forwarder and Aberdeen end knots outperform square/surgeon's knots under tension<sup>[[3]](#ref3)</sup>
- **No bulky tail-on-tail tie** needed — useful when one tail is short or when the closure is in a deep pocket where tying would be awkward
- **Compatible with monofilament and braided suture** — with proper throw count

The main trade-off is that self-locking knots **take longer to learn** and require more deliberate hand choreography than a reflex square tie.

## Aberdeen Knot — the Standard Terminal Knot

The Aberdeen is the most widely taught self-locking knot in general surgery. It is used at the **end** of a running closure to secure the final loop without a tail-on-tail tie.

See the [dedicated Aberdeen knot page](/docs/foundations/surgical-skills/knot-tying/aberdeen-knot) for step-by-step technique.

**Key points (summary):**

- Constructed from the final loop of the running suture
- Loop is passed through itself 3–4 times in sequence
- Stott "Ultimate Aberdeen" configuration = **3 throws + 2 turns** is the strongest tested variant<sup>[[4]](#ref4)</sup>
- **Stronger and more secure than a surgeon's knot** for ending a running suture line<sup>[[4]](#ref4)</sup>
- Buried, flat, consistent — the defining advantages

## Forwarder Knot — Terminal Knot for High-Tension Closures

The **Forwarder knot** is a terminal self-locking knot designed specifically for closures where the suture line will sit under meaningful tension. In large-gauge comparative bench studies, Forwarder end knots provided **increased knot-holding capacity and relative knot security** compared with square or surgeon's knots — making it a reasonable choice for fascial or high-tension closures where the Aberdeen may underperform.<sup>[[3]](#ref3)</sup>

### Construction principle

- Constructed from the final loop of the running suture, similar to Aberdeen
- Differs in the **pass-through pattern** — the loop is passed through itself in a specific sequence that locks by creating additional friction against the previous throw
- **Three to four sequential passes** form the locking configuration

### When to use

- Running fascial closure with tail constraints
- Closures under ongoing tension (high BMI patient, Valsalva stress)
- As an alternative to Aberdeen when additional security is preferred

### Technique pearl

The Forwarder's additional friction comes at the cost of **extra suture consumption** — plan for a longer tail before initiating the terminal knot compared with an Aberdeen. Practice on bench models before first clinical use.

## Middler Knot / Loop-Lock — Mid-Run Tensioning

Running closures occasionally need to be **locked mid-run** — for example, when crossing an anatomic landmark, when tension must be transferred to a new segment, or when breaking the closure into two tension zones. The **Middler knot** (and the closely related **loop-lock technique**) accomplish this without interrupting the running suture.<sup>[[5]](#ref5)</sup>

### Loop-lock principle

During the middle of a running closure, the working strand is used to form a controlled loop that locks against the previous throw:

- The suture is looped around the needle holder (both limbs held by the same hand)
- The free end is grasped through the loop with the needle holder
- The loop and needle holder are pulled apart so the loop seats against the previous throw
- The loop is released and the knot completed while maintaining tension<sup>[[5]](#ref5)</sup>

This creates a locking element mid-run that prevents the running closure from loosening proximally if distal tension is released or adjusted.

### When to use

- Very long running closures (e.g., midline fascial)
- Running closures crossing high-tension zones where proximal loosening is a risk
- When pausing the closure to reassess tension in one segment

### Terminology note

Named variants of mid-run locking knots (including **"Middler"**) appear in surgical-education materials and small-animal and bariatric-surgery literature. The **loop-lock technique** is the most formally described in the peer-reviewed literature.<sup>[[5]](#ref5)</sup>

## Loop Knot — the Starting Knot

The **loop knot** is the self-locking counterpart to the Aberdeen — used at the **beginning** of a running closure rather than the end. In comparative testing with gauge 1 polyglyconate suture, the loop knot produced **knot-holding capacity 2–3× greater** than square, surgeon's, or Aberdeen knots.<sup>[[11]](#ref11)</sup>

### Construction

- The working strand is passed through a small preformed loop at the end of the suture
- The loop is cinched as the suture is pulled through, creating a self-locking hold
- The running closure then proceeds from this anchored starting point

### Role in abdominal closure

The classical recommendation — best supported by Fong 2008 ANZ Journal of Surgery data — is:

- **Start abdominal fascial closure with a loop knot**
- **Finish with an Aberdeen, square, or surgeon's knot**

The combination **loop knot start + Aberdeen finish** minimizes knot volume at both ends of the closure while maximizing security at the highest-risk moment (the unanchored proximal end) and the cut tail (the distal end).<sup>[[11]](#ref11)</sup>

## Nice Knot — Doubled-Suture Sliding Knot

The **Nice knot** is a modern self-locking knot constructed from a **doubled suture in a sliding knot configuration**.<sup>[[12]](#ref12)[[13]](#ref13)</sup> Originally popularized in orthopedic and rotator-cuff surgery, the Nice knot has migrated into soft-tissue applications where high-tension security is needed — including ACL graft preparation, ankle syndesmosis, acromioclavicular joint separation, and coracoid bone block fixation.<sup>[[13]](#ref13)</sup>

### Construction principle

1. The suture is passed around the target tissue or through the bone channel
2. Both free ends are handled together as a **doubled strand**
3. A sliding half-hitch is constructed from the doubled configuration
4. Tension is applied by pulling the standing limbs — the knot **slides down** to the tissue surface
5. The knot **self-locks** when fully cinched; additional half-hitches can be added as locking throws

### Biomechanical data<sup>[[14]](#ref14)[[15]](#ref15)</sup>

| Property | Nice knot |
|---|---|
| **Stiffness** | **169 N/mm** (second only to cow hitch at 185 N/mm) |
| **Size** | 6.1 mm (vs. 9.5 mm for equivalent half-hitches) |
| **Dynamic elongation** | Decreased vs. conventional knots (P &lt; 0.05)<sup>[[12]](#ref12)</sup> |
| **ACL graft elongation** | **Less than Prusik knot** in cadaver quadriceps tendon studies<sup>[[15]](#ref15)</sup> |

### Advantages<sup>[[13]](#ref13)</sup>

- **Strength** — high-tension applications
- **Adjustability** — slides into position before locking
- **Simplicity** — single knot configuration
- **Versatility** — applicable across tissue types and bone-suture constructs
- Can **replace metallic wires or cables** for bone fixation in selected applications

### Failure mode

The failure mode is **suture rupture at the point where the suture passes through the loop at the base of the knot** — not knot slippage.<sup>[[16]](#ref16)</sup> Strength and integrity are **variable between surgeons**, reflecting the dependence on consistent construction technique.

### Applications in GU-adjacent reconstruction

The Nice knot is not standard in GU soft-tissue reconstruction but is worth knowing for:

- **High-tension fascial repair** where a bone anchor-equivalent is desired
- **Pelvic-floor reconstruction** where a small, strong, self-locking knot is needed
- **Pubic-symphysis or pelvic-ring work** in complex post-traumatic reconstruction

## Modified Nice Knot

The **Modified Nice knot** adds **additional half-hitches** on top of the primary Nice knot configuration, theoretically reinforcing the primary knot.<sup>[[16]](#ref16)</sup>

- Similar biomechanical profile to the original Nice knot
- **Same failure mode** — suture rupture at the loop base, not knot slippage
- Offered as a security-enhancement variant when the primary Nice knot is deemed inadequate for the tension application

---

## Cow Hitch — the Stiffest Configuration

The **cow hitch** (double-stranded looped knot configuration) is the **stiffest knot tested** in biomechanical comparison of high-strength suture configurations.<sup>[[14]](#ref14)</sup>

### Biomechanical data

| Property | Cow hitch |
|---|---|
| **Stiffness** | **185 N/mm** (stiffest, P &lt; 0.05 vs. alternatives) |
| **Strength** | Up to **3× stronger** than single-stranded knots |
| **Size** | 7.6 mm (smaller than 9.5-mm half-hitches) |
| **Loop security** | **158 N** (vs. 85 N for half-hitch; P &lt; 0.05) |
| **Complexity** | Technically simple construction |

### Best use case

The cow hitch is **best suited to exploit the mechanical capabilities of modern high-strength suture material** (UHMWPE / FiberWire / FiberTape) — materials whose strength exceeds what conventional knot configurations can translate into clinical security.<sup>[[14]](#ref14)</sup> It is a niche but growing option in orthopedic, spine, and complex pelvic-ring reconstruction.

### Relevance to GU reconstruction

Not standard in routine GU work but worth knowing for:

- **Mid-urethral sling anchor applications** — where high-strength suture is used
- **Sacrocolpopexy mesh anchor points** — in selected contexts
- Any reconstruction using UHMWPE or equivalent high-strength material

## Other Self-Locking Knots

### Running noose / sliding self-locking knot

Constructed by the scrub nurse extracorporeally and passed into the wound; pulled tight to form a self-locking first throw. Widely used in **laparoscopic** and bariatric surgery to avoid intracorporeal knot tying at the start of a running closure.<sup>[[2]](#ref2)[[6]](#ref6)</sup>

### SMC knot and related arthroscopic slider knots

Arthroscopic self-locking sliding knots (SMC, Nicky's, Tennessee slider) are used in orthopedic / shoulder reconstruction. They share the self-locking principle but are tuned for arthroscopic cannula delivery. **Rare in GU reconstructive practice** but mentioned for completeness.

## Technical Pearls for Self-Locking Knots

1. **Practice on bench-top models first.** The pass-through patterns are easy to get wrong mid-case, and a failed self-locking knot at the end of a running closure means redoing the entire line.
2. **Count your passes.** Three is the minimum for most self-locking knots; four adds security with modest additional suture consumption.
3. **Maintain tension during construction.** Every pass should be seated taut before the next; slack during construction leaves the finished knot loose.
4. **Suture material matters.** Monofilament sutures (Prolene, PDS) require an extra pass for knot security; braided sutures (Vicryl, Ethibond) hold reliably at the minimum pass count.
5. **Visualize before you cut.** Confirm the knot is seated and holds tension before cutting the tail; a premature cut on an under-seated self-locking knot cannot be rescued.
