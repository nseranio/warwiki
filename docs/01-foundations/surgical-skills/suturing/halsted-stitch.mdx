# Halsted Stitch

The **Halsted stitch** is an **interrupted seromuscular inverting** suture — named for **William Stewart Halsted** (1852–1922, founding chair of surgery at Johns Hopkins). Halsted's teaching emphasized meticulous, atraumatic, interrupted seromuscular suturing for bowel and vascular work — a principle that shaped modern anastomotic technique.

## Technique

- Interrupted variant of the [Lembert stitch](/docs/foundations/surgical-skills/suturing/lembert-stitch) — seromuscular inverting bite
- Each throw tied individually, cut, and the next placed

## Key Uses

- **Interrupted outer layer** of two-layer bowel closure
- **Interrupted vascular anastomosis** (in Halsted's era)
- **Reinforcement** stitches at anastomotic corners
- Any situation where interrupted seromuscular inversion is preferred over continuous

## Halsted's Legacy

Halsted's emphasis on the seromuscular interrupted stitch — along with his principles of atraumatic tissue handling, meticulous hemostasis, and anatomical planes — defined American surgical training. His techniques underpin not only bowel anastomosis but vascular, biliary, and urologic reconstruction.
