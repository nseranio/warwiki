# Healthcare Finance & Payment Models

This article covers the finance and payment-structure knowledge the reconstructive urologist needs to function as a clinical operator: the revenue cycle, RVUs, cost structure, payer mix, payment models (fee-for-service, bundled, value-based), RBRVS, and what hospital administrators actually do with the money.

See also: [Clinical Operator Mindset](/docs/resources/hidden-curriculum/overview), [Billing & Coding](/docs/resources/hidden-curriculum/billing-coding).

---

## The Revenue Cycle

Every clinical encounter follows the same financial pipeline:

**Charge → Coding → Claim → Adjudication → Payment → Collections**

1. **Charge capture.** The operation or encounter generates CPT and ICD-10 codes.
2. **Coding.** Professional and facility coders review the documentation and assign codes for billing.
3. **Claim submission.** Typically via clearinghouse to payer.
4. **Adjudication.** Payer reviews, may deny, underpay, or approve.
5. **Payment posting.** EOB / ERA is reconciled.
6. **Patient responsibility.** Deductible, coinsurance, balance billed.
7. **Collections.** Accounts receivable, follow-up, bad debt.

The surgical service makes money when documentation is accurate, codes are optimized but compliant, denials are appealed, and the net collection rate (revenue actually collected ÷ expected revenue) is kept high. The biggest preventable leaks: missed modifiers, undocumented complexity, and denials not appealed within filing deadlines.

## RVUs — Work, Practice Expense, Malpractice

The **Resource-Based Relative Value Scale (RBRVS)** is the CMS-established system that determines physician reimbursement for nearly every CPT code.<sup>[[9]](#ref9)</sup> A procedure's total RVU is the sum of three components:

| Component | What it represents |
|---|---|
| **Work RVU (wRVU)** | The physician's time, skill, mental effort, judgment, stress |
| **Practice expense (PE) RVU** | Staff, rent, equipment, overhead |
| **Malpractice (MP) RVU** | Liability-insurance cost |

**Reimbursement calculation:**<sup>[[17]](#ref17)</sup>

```
Payment = [(wRVU × work GPCI) + (PE RVU × PE GPCI) + (MP RVU × MP GPCI)] × conversion factor
```

The **conversion factor** is the dollar multiplier CMS sets annually. For CY2026 it is $33.4009 for most clinicians and $33.5675 for qualifying APM participants (CMS CY2026 relative value file, RVU26C).<sup>[[10]](#ref10)</sup> CMS also applied a −2.5% efficiency adjustment to the work RVUs and corresponding intraservice time of selected non-time-based services in CY2026. Time-based codes such as E/M and additional listed codes are exempt, so its effect varies by procedure.<sup>[[11]](#ref11)</sup> The conversion factor also moves through budget neutrality: when CMS projects a payment change above $20 million, it offsets the change across the schedule, and specialty societies argue this has cut procedural payment (Hall 2026).<sup>[[12]](#ref12)</sup> **Geographic Practice Cost Index (GPCI)** adjusts for regional cost differences.

**Selected urologic wRVUs for orientation:**

| Procedure (CPT) | CY2026 wRVUs |
|---|---|
| Cystourethroscopy, diagnostic (52000) | 1.49 |
| TURP (52601) | 9.75 |
| HoLEP (52649) | 12.68 |
| Robotic or laparoscopic radical prostatectomy (55866) | 21.90 |
| Cystectomy with ileal conduit (51590) | 35.42 |
| Cystectomy with continent diversion (51596) | 43.15 |
| Urethroplasty, 1-stage anterior urethra (53410) | 17.24 |
| Urethroplasty, 2-stage prostatic or membranous urethra (53420 + 53425) | 14.79 + 16.64 |
| Vesicovaginal fistula repair, vaginal (57320) or abdominal (51900) | 8.66 / 14.26 |
| AUS insertion (53445) | 12.68 |
| IPP insertion (54405) | 14.16 |

Values are Medicare work RVUs from the CY2026 relative value file (RVU26C, July 2026 release) and change each January. Verify with the [CMS Physician Fee Schedule](https://www.cms.gov/medicare/medicare-fee-for-service-payment/physicianfeesched) or the coding team before using for specific decisions.

In a 2020 editorial, McIntyre and Beach argued that the RBRVS and other policy decisions over the preceding 25 years disadvantaged orthopaedic surgeons in valuing their surgical work and warned that continued reliance on the RBRVS could further reduce surgical reimbursement.<sup>[[9]](#ref9)</sup> This is the political context for every fee-schedule conversation.

## Professional Fee vs Facility Fee

Every surgical episode generates two separate revenue streams:

- **Professional fee.** Paid to the surgeon (or the surgeon's employer) for the operative work; uses the RBRVS professional fee schedule.
- **Facility fee.** Paid to the hospital or ASC for the space, equipment, nursing, and supplies; Medicare uses the ASC payment system for ASCs, OPPS for hospital outpatient departments, and IPPS with MS-DRGs for acute inpatient hospital stays.<sup>[[18]](#ref18)[[19]](#ref19)[[20]](#ref20)</sup>

A TURP case at a hospital may generate approximately $650 in professional fee and approximately $2,800 in facility fee. A cystectomy may generate $3,000 professional fee and $25,000+ facility fee (by MS-DRG). Facility fees are typically 3–10× the professional fee for procedural work, the main reason hospital systems want procedural volume.

## Payer Mix — Why It Matters

Different payers pay different amounts for the same CPT code:

| Payer | Typical reimbursement vs Medicare |
|---|---|
| **Commercial / private** | 143% of Medicare for professional services in a 2024 national estimate<sup>[[25]](#ref25)</sup> |
| **Medicare** | Reference (~100%) |
| **Medicaid** | 75% of Medicare in the 2024 national fee-for-service physician-fee index using the original service basket; varies by state and service<sup>[[26]](#ref26)</sup> |
| **Self-pay / uninsured** | Highly variable; often discounted |
| **Workers' compensation** | Varies by state and service; fee-schedule methods differ |

A practice with 70% commercial payer mix has radically different economics from one with 70% Medicare and Medicaid. Understanding the payer mix is the single most important step in understanding why one institution is fighting for a service line and another is not.

## Fixed vs Variable Costs — Why Hospital Finance Is Counterintuitive

In Roberts and colleagues’ 1993 cost analysis of one large urban public teaching hospital, 84% of costs were classified as fixed (including salaries, buildings, and equipment) and 16% as variable (including medications and supplies).<sup>[[4]](#ref4)</sup>

**Implications:**

- Adding one more case on an otherwise scheduled OR day barely raises cost; incremental margin is high.
- Cutting a case from a scheduled day barely reduces cost; margin is left on the table.
- **Short-term cost reduction** effectively only touches variable items: supplies, implants, drugs, length of stay (variable labor).
- **Long-term cost reduction** requires changing fixed-cost structure: staffing ratios, block-time consolidation, service-line closure.

This is why hospital administrators fight over OR block utilization: empty block time is pure lost margin.

## Payment Models

### Fee-for-Service (FFS) — the Default

The traditional model: providers are paid for each service. Incentives:

- Volume over value
- Potentially misaligned with cost containment
- Complications can paradoxically increase revenue (longer stay, more services)

In one tertiary hospital’s retrospective analysis of 821 Medicare elective colectomies, 277 patients (33.7%) had negative margins under observed fee-for-service payment; under the authors’ modeled bundled-payment scenario, 342 (41.7%) would have had negative margins.<sup>[[5]](#ref5)</sup>

### Bundled Payments

**Bundled Payments for Care Improvement (BPCI)** began in 2013 with several payment designs.<sup>[[5]](#ref5)[[6]](#ref6)[[7]](#ref7)[[21]](#ref21)</sup> Its BPCI Advanced successor (2018–2025) used episode target prices with retrospective reconciliation while Medicare continued fee-for-service billing. An inpatient episode included the anchor stay plus 90 days after discharge; an outpatient episode included the anchor procedure plus 90 days after completion.<sup>[[22]](#ref22)</sup>

**Mechanics:**

- After aggregate reconciliation, the model participant could receive a payment when eligible episode spending was below its target, subject to quality adjustment.
- After aggregate reconciliation, the model participant could owe CMS a repayment when eligible episode spending exceeded its target.
- The original BPCI Models 2–4 offered up to 48 clinical episodes; BPCI Advanced used a different catalog, with 29 inpatient, 3 outpatient, and 2 multi-setting categories in model years 6–8.<sup>[[23]](#ref23)[[24]](#ref24)</sup>

**Incentives created:**

- Coordinate care across the 90-day window.
- Reduce complications and readmissions.
- Shift work to lower-cost settings (SNF avoidance, outpatient rehabilitation).
- Standardize pathways to reduce variation.

**Outcomes so far:**<sup>[[8]](#ref8)</sup>

- **In the original BPCI initiative, 78% of hospitals saw payment decreases for major joint replacement** (a relatively standardized, high-volume procedure).<sup>[[8]](#ref8)</sup>
- In the original BPCI initiative, only 46% of hospitals saw payment decreases for congestive heart failure (less standardized, more medical complexity).<sup>[[8]](#ref8)</sup>
- The cited BPCI Advanced analysis found modest payment decreases for both medical and surgical episodes and did not establish that either episode type was better suited to bundling.<sup>[[8]](#ref8)</sup>

### Value-Based Care / Alternative Payment Models (APMs)

A broader category in which reimbursement is tied to quality and outcomes, not just service rendered. Includes:

- **Merit-Based Incentive Payment System (MIPS).** CMS adjustment up or down based on quality, cost, improvement, and interoperability metrics.
- **Accountable Care Organizations (ACOs).** Shared savings against benchmark if quality is maintained.
- **Hospital Value-Based Purchasing (HVBP).** Adjusts IPPS payments based on quality outcomes.
- **Hospital Readmissions Reduction Program (HRRP).** Penalizes hospitals with excess readmissions.

For urology specifically, cystectomy has been evaluated for bundled payment with mixed results; the heterogeneity of patient comorbidity and complication rates limits bundle viability relative to, say, joint replacement.

### Capitation

A single per-member-per-month fee covers all care for a population. **Kaiser Permanente** is the archetype. Strong incentive to prevent illness, maintain quality, avoid unnecessary care; risk is cost-shift to underprovision.

## Cost Structure of a Surgical Case

The Gani 2016 analysis of colectomy is a useful reference; the dominant cost drivers across surgical specialties are:<sup>[[5]](#ref5)</sup>

1. **Postoperative complications.** By a wide margin the largest single driver
2. **Length of stay beyond expected duration**
3. **Patient comorbidities and disease severity**
4. **Operative time and OR supply cost**
5. **Socioeconomic factors**

**Practical implications for the urologist:**

- ERAS protocols, VTE prophylaxis, infection bundles, and surgical technique that reduces complications are simultaneously quality interventions and margin interventions.
- Reducing LOS by 1 day after open radical cystectomy saves several thousand dollars of variable cost per case; multiplied across a service line, substantial net margin.
- Patient comorbidity (diabetes, frailty, malnutrition) is where prehabilitation meets finance.

## What Hospital Administrators Do

Hospital administrators and clinical leaders carry four principal functions:<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[2]](#ref2)[[3]](#ref3)</sup>

### 1. Strategic and Operational Leadership

Setting organizational direction, driving quality-improvement culture, monitoring performance, ensuring regulatory compliance. High-performing boards pay more attention to clinical quality metrics and use them more effectively, which correlates with better front-line management and higher quality of care.<sup>[[3]](#ref3)</sup>

### 2. Financial Management

Balancing **technical efficiency** (cost per unit of service) with **economic efficiency** (reimbursement relative to expenditures). Optimizing resource utilization within complex reimbursement structures. This is where contribution-margin analysis, payer-mix optimization, and cost accounting live.<sup>[[4]](#ref4)</sup>

### 3. System-Level Coordination

Creating the structural and cultural conditions for interprofessional care. Individual clinicians cannot implement system-wide changes alone; that is the administrator's domain.<sup>[[2]](#ref2)</sup>

### 4. Quality and Safety Oversight

Implementing quality monitoring, setting targets, providing feedback to clinical teams, and ensuring accountability. Increasingly, this function is tied directly to reimbursement through HVBP, HRRP, and MIPS.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[3]](#ref3)</sup>

The operational point for the surgeon: the administrator is not the enemy. The administrator's interests are usually aligned with the surgeon's interests when communicated in the administrator's language: RVUs, margin, length of stay, quality metrics, and regulatory risk.

## Key Practice Economic Realities

Condensed from the surgical-finance literature:<sup>[[4]](#ref4)[[5]](#ref5)</sup>

- **Hospital costs are predominantly fixed.** Variable costs are the short-term lever.
- **Facility fees typically dwarf professional fees** for procedural work.
- **In one Medicare elective colectomy cohort, 33.7% of cases had a negative net hospital margin under fee-for-service** (277/821 at one tertiary hospital).<sup>[[5]](#ref5)</sup>
- **In the same colectomy cohort, a modeled bundled payment yielded a negative net hospital margin in 41.7% of cases** (342/821); bundled payments shift complication-cost risk to the hospital.<sup>[[5]](#ref5)</sup>
- **Complications are simultaneously quality failures and financial hemorrhage** under every modern payment model.
- **Payer mix affects service-line viability.** Compare the service line’s payment rates with its costs before concluding whether more volume will offset losses.

## How Payment Policy Is Made

The professional fee for every reconstructive operation is set in a public process that surgeons and their societies can influence:

1. **Valuation.** The AMA RVS Update Committee (RUC), a multispecialty committee, describes the resources each physician service requires and submits RVU recommendations to CMS. Specialty societies supply the input, including surveys of their members. CMS makes all final payment decisions.<sup>[[14]](#ref14)</sup>
2. **Rulemaking.** CMS publishes a proposed Physician Fee Schedule rule each summer and takes public comments; for the CY2027 rule, comments were due September 14, 2026.<sup>[[15]](#ref15)</sup> The final rule, with the new conversion factor and any across-the-board adjustments, follows for the next calendar year.<sup>[[11]](#ref11)</sup> Budget neutrality links the two steps: an increase for one group of services is offset across the schedule.<sup>[[12]](#ref12)</sup>
3. **Coverage.** Medicare Administrative Contractors (MACs) process claims regionally and can issue local coverage policies, so device and procedure coverage can differ by region.<sup>[[16]](#ref16)</sup>
4. **Representation.** The AUA Coding and Reimbursement Committee represents urology on coding, terminology and reimbursement and seeks new or revised codes; the AUA Practice Management Committee advises on practice operations.<sup>[[16]](#ref16)</sup>

For an early-career surgeon the practical entry points are a RUC survey when the society circulates one for a procedure the surgeon performs, a comment on the proposed rule, and a report to the society's coding committee when a new operation has no adequate code. Legislative engagement and the wider advocacy structure are covered in [Clinical Operator Mindset](/docs/resources/hidden-curriculum/overview#engagement-and-advocacy).

## Getting Involved in Finance at Your Institution

1. **Ask for the monthly financial report**: wRVUs, payer mix, net collections, denial rate, no-show rate. If it doesn't exist, request it.
2. **Request to review coding and billing on the surgeon's own cases** for one month. Compare to peers in your specialty.
3. **Sit in on one service-line financial meeting per quarter.**
4. **Attend one hospital operational meeting** per month: quality committee, OR committee, finance subcommittee.
5. **Ask the administrator to explain one payment decision per visit**: why a particular CPT was denied, how the service compares to peers, what the bundle benchmark is for a particular episode.
6. **Propose one margin-improving intervention per year**: reduce no-show rate, consolidate OR block time, reduce LOS after cystectomy, standardize a post-op pathway, or trim surgeon preference cards and instrument trays so unused instruments are not opened (suggested in the 2026 AUGS sustainability white paper, which notes that many of its measures also lower cost).<sup>[[13]](#ref13)</sup>

These steps cost nothing, are welcomed by competent administrators, and are the shortest path to operational literacy.
