# Radiation and Hemorrhagic Cystitis

**Radiation cystitis** is bladder injury after pelvic radiotherapy; acute cystitis can occur during or soon after treatment, while late injury may cause storage symptoms, reduced capacity and compliance, and bleeding from fragile, telangiectatic urothelium.<sup>[[3]](#ref3)</sup> **Hemorrhagic cystitis** is diffuse bladder bleeding from any cause; in adult cancer survivors the usual causes are pelvic radiation and, less often, oxazaphosphorine chemotherapy or BK polyomavirus after hematopoietic stem cell transplantation.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[2]](#ref2)</sup> Late radiation cystitis has been reported in up to 10% of patients who receive pelvic radiotherapy, can present months to decades after treatment, and ranges from microscopic hematuria to transfusion-dependent bleeding and an end-stage, contracted bladder.<sup>[[3]](#ref3)[[4]](#ref4)</sup>

For the reconstructive surgeon, the condition matters at two points. The first is acute bleeding that fails catheter-based care and needs escalation through intravesical, hyperbaric, interventional and surgical options. The second is the end-stage irradiated bladder, where the decision is between continued bladder salvage and urinary diversion with or without cystectomy.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[5]](#ref5)</sup> Tissue biology after radiation, and hyperbaric oxygen physiology, are covered in [Radiation and Tissue Effects](/docs/foundations/surgical-principles/radiation-tissue-effects) and [Hyperbaric Oxygen Therapy](/docs/foundations/surgical-principles/hyperbaric-oxygen-therapy).

## Definition and Grading

Radiation cystitis is divided into acute cystitis during or shortly after treatment, which is usually self-limited, and late cystitis, which Smit and Heyns describe as developing 6 months to 20 years after treatment.<sup>[[3]](#ref3)</sup> For RTOG/EORTC toxicity reporting, late effects occur or persist more than 90 days after the start of radiotherapy.<sup>[[6]](#ref6)</sup> Late toxicity is graded by clinician-reported scales. NCI released CTCAE v6.0 in 2025; the table below retains v5.0 criteria for interpreting studies that used that version.<sup>[[39]](#ref39)</sup> RTOG/EORTC and CTCAE grades are not interchangeable, and a trial's "grade ≥2 GU toxicity" is a composite endpoint that includes frequency, dysuria, obstruction and incontinence as well as hematuria.<sup>[[6]](#ref6)[[7]](#ref7)</sup>

| Grade | RTOG/EORTC late bladder morbidity<sup>[[6]](#ref6)</sup> | CTCAE v5.0, cystitis noninfective<sup>[[7]](#ref7)</sup> |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | Slight epithelial atrophy, minor telangiectasia, microscopic hematuria | Microscopic hematuria; minimal increase in frequency, urgency, dysuria or nocturia; new-onset incontinence |
| 2 | Moderate frequency, generalized telangiectasia, intermittent macroscopic hematuria | Moderate hematuria; moderate increase in frequency, urgency, dysuria, nocturia or incontinence; urinary catheter placement or bladder irrigation indicated; limits instrumental activities of daily living |
| 3 | Severe frequency and dysuria, severe generalized telangiectasia (often with petechiae), frequent hematuria, capacity &lt;150 mL | Gross hematuria; transfusion, IV medication or hospitalization indicated; elective invasive intervention indicated<sup>[[6]](#ref6)[[7]](#ref7)</sup> |
| 4 | Necrosis, contracted bladder (capacity &lt;100 mL), severe hemorrhagic cystitis | Life-threatening consequences; urgent invasive intervention indicated<sup>[[6]](#ref6)[[7]](#ref7)</sup> |
| 5 | Death from toxicity | Death |

The grade guides the intensity of treatment, but it does not replace a description of the actual problem: transfusion requirement, clot retention, number of admissions, bladder capacity, upper-tract status and the patient's symptom burden.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup>

## Epidemiology and Latency

Reported incidence varies with the treatment field, dose, fractionation, concurrent therapy, the definition of cystitis and the length of follow-up. Severe hematuria is less common than any-grade cystitis.<sup>[[3]](#ref3)[[4]](#ref4)</sup>

| Radiation source | Evidence | Interpretation |
|---|---|---|
| **Prostate RT, population data** | In a Medicare-linked cohort, radiotherapy was associated with radiation cystitis (HR 131.47, 95% CI 52.48–329.35) and bladder cancer (HR 2.78, 95% CI 1.92–4.02) compared with untreated participants<sup>[[8]](#ref8)</sup> | Observational; the very large hazard ratio reflects a near-absent baseline rate in untreated men |
| **Prostate LDR brachytherapy boost** | In ASCENDE-RT, five-year cumulative grade 3 GU events were 18.4% after LDR boost and 5.2% after dose-escalated EBRT boost<sup>[[9]](#ref9)</sup> | Composite GU events that include more than hemorrhagic cystitis; both arms received pelvic RT and androgen deprivation |
| **Prostate SBRT** | In PACE-B (874 men), cumulative late RTOG grade ≥2 GU toxicity through five years was 26.9% after five-fraction SBRT and 18.3% after conventional or moderately hypofractionated RT<sup>[[10]](#ref10)</sup> | Composite endpoint; low- and intermediate-risk disease without androgen deprivation |
| **Cervical cancer RT** | In 1,784 patients treated with radiotherapy for stage Ib cervical cancer, hemorrhagic cystitis developed in 6.5%, at a median of 35.5 months from the start of radiotherapy (mean 58 months), with some cases appearing up to 20 years later<sup>[[11]](#ref11)</sup> | Older external-beam and brachytherapy techniques; demonstrates continuing risk over two decades |
| **Rectal, endometrial and other pelvic RT** | Included in pooled descriptions of late radiation cystitis after pelvic radiotherapy<sup>[[3]](#ref3)[[1]](#ref1)</sup> | Site-specific hemorrhagic cystitis rates are poorly defined; the bladder dose and volume matter more than the primary tumor |

Latency is long. Bleeding typically appears months to years after treatment, and the cervical cancer data show new events beyond 10 years.<sup>[[11]](#ref11)[[4]](#ref4)</sup> In a Mayo Clinic series of cystectomy for refractory hemorrhagic cystitis, 17 of 21 patients had prior prostate radiotherapy, and the median interval from radiation to cystectomy was 91 months.<sup>[[12]](#ref12)</sup> AUA/ASTRO/SUO guidance on salvage radiotherapy after prostatectomy identifies late hemorrhagic cystitis as a potential harm to be discussed when salvage treatment is considered.<sup>[[13]](#ref13)</sup>

## Pathophysiology

Radiation injures the urothelium, the microvasculature and the detrusor. The late lesion is progressive obliterative endarteritis with ischemia and tissue hypoxia, followed by submucosal and detrusor fibrosis. Fragile neovascular telangiectasias in the hypoxic mucosa bleed with minimal trauma, and fibrosis reduces capacity and compliance.<sup>[[14]](#ref14)[[15]](#ref15)</sup> The changes vary within the treatment field.<sup>[[15]](#ref15)</sup> Bleeding may come from discrete sites amenable to fulguration or from diffuse oozing across the bladder.<sup>[[4]](#ref4)</sup> Hyperbaric oxygen is used to treat this hypoxic, hypovascular state.<sup>[[16]](#ref16)</sup>

See [Radiation and Tissue Effects](/docs/foundations/surgical-principles/radiation-tissue-effects) for the broader tissue biology and its implications for grafts, flaps and anastomoses.

## Non-Radiation Hemorrhagic Cystitis in Survivors

| Cause | Mechanism and setting | Prevention and specific management |
|---|---|---|
| **Cyclophosphamide and ifosfamide** | Urotoxic metabolite acrolein injures the urothelium; bleeding can occur during treatment or later<sup>[[2]](#ref2)[[17]](#ref17)</sup> | ASCO protectant guidance supports mesna to reduce ifosfamide urothelial toxicity, and mesna with saline diuresis or forced saline diuresis for high-dose cyclophosphamide in the stem-cell transplantation setting; established hemorrhagic cystitis is managed with the same bladder-directed ladder as radiation cystitis<sup>[[17]](#ref17)[[2]](#ref2)</sup> |
| **BK polyomavirus after allogeneic HSCT** | Reported in 7–54% of adult allogeneic HSCT recipients; diagnosis requires cystitis, macroscopic hematuria and urinary BK viral load &gt;7 log10 copies/mL, with other causes excluded<sup>[[18]](#ref18)</sup> | Supportive care with hydration, platelet support as needed, catheter drainage and irrigation; evidence for antiviral therapy is limited; refractory cases may need the interventional options below<sup>[[18]](#ref18)</sup> |

These patients differ from radiation survivors. Thrombocytopenia, graft-versus-host disease, immunosuppression and concurrent infection change both the bleeding risk and the risk of intervention, and treatment is coordinated with the transplant or oncology team.<sup>[[18]](#ref18)[[2]](#ref2)</sup>

## Evaluation

Hematuria in an irradiated patient is not assumed to be radiation cystitis. Neither the radiation history nor bacteriuria establishes the diagnosis, and recurrent or new malignancy must be excluded.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup> Radiotherapy for prostate cancer is associated with later bladder cancer, which adds to this concern.<sup>[[8]](#ref8)</sup>

- **Hemodynamic status and bleeding severity.** Clots, retention, hemoglobin trend and transfusion requirement.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[4]](#ref4)</sup>
- **Coagulation and medications.** Platelet count, coagulation studies, renal function, and review of anticoagulant and antiplatelet therapy with the prescribing team; anticoagulation does not explain away hematuria.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup>
- **Urine studies.** Culture to treat concurrent infection; cytology as indicated.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup>
- **Upper-tract imaging.** CT urography or an equivalent study to exclude an upper-tract source and obstruction, preferably before antifibrinolytic therapy.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[19]](#ref19)</sup>
- **Cystoscopy.** Clot evacuation, identification of discrete bleeding sites or diffuse oozing, assessment of capacity, and biopsy of any lesion concerning for malignancy.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[4]](#ref4)</sup>
- **Fistula and necrosis.** Cystography or cross-sectional imaging when necrosis, perforation, fistula or osteitis is suspected; these findings change management and usually exclude formalin.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[20]](#ref20)</sup>

## Stepwise Management

Management depends on bleeding severity, bladder capacity, renal function, comorbidity and local expertise. The CUA best practice report separates initial care, persistent or recurrent bleeding, and refractory life-threatening bleeding; it does not require every patient to pass through each step in order.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup>

| Step | Intervention | Main role | Principal cautions |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | **Resuscitation** | Transfusion, correction of coagulopathy, review of anticoagulants<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[4]](#ref4)</sup> | Thromboembolic risk when anticoagulants are stopped is weighed with the prescriber |
| 2 | **Large-bore catheter and clot evacuation** | Bedside or operative clot evacuation; a large three-way catheter for drainage<sup>[[4]](#ref4)</sup> | Retained clot sustains bleeding and obstruction |
| 3 | **Continuous bladder irrigation (CBI)** | Saline irrigation after clot clearance<sup>[[4]](#ref4)[[1]](#ref1)</sup> | Recurrent clot retention |
| 4 | **Cystoscopic fulguration** | Treats discrete bleeding sites at the same anesthetic as clot evacuation<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[4]](#ref4)</sup> | Less useful for diffuse oozing |
| 5 | **Intravesical alum** | Persistent bleeding after CBI; no anesthesia required<sup>[[21]](#ref21)[[1]](#ref1)</sup> | Aluminum absorption, especially with renal impairment; bladder spasm; transient delirium |
| 6 | **Intravesical hyaluronic acid or chondroitin sulfate** | Persistent mild-to-moderate bleeding and symptoms; possible relapse prevention<sup>[[22]](#ref22)[[23]](#ref23)</sup> | Slow onset; limited evidence in severe acute bleeding |
| 7 | **Hyperbaric oxygen therapy (HBOT)** | Persistent or recurrent radiation cystitis in a stable patient<sup>[[24]](#ref24)[[25]](#ref25)</sup> | 30–40 sessions; access; does not provide emergency hemostasis |
| 8 | **Aminocaproic acid** | Oral or intravesical antifibrinolytic in selected cases<sup>[[19]](#ref19)</sup> | Exclude upper-tract bleeding; hard clots form if bladder clot is not cleared; thrombotic events |
| 9 | **Tranexamic acid** | Off-label antifibrinolytic adjunct<sup>[[26]](#ref26)</sup> | Upper-tract clot obstruction; thrombotic risk |
| 10 | **Intravesical formalin** | Refractory bleeding after less invasive treatment fails<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[20]](#ref20)</sup> | General or regional anesthesia; exclude perforation, assess for VUR and occlude refluxing ureteral orifices; fibrosis, contracture and upper-tract injury |
| 11 | **Selective internal iliac or superselective vesical artery embolization** | Refractory or life-threatening bleeding, especially in patients unfit for surgery<sup>[[27]](#ref27)[[28]](#ref28)</sup> | Recurrent bleeding; repeat procedures; periprocedural deaths reflect comorbidity |
| 12 | **Bilateral nephrostomy diversion** | Diverts urine from the bladder when bleeding persists<sup>[[29]](#ref29)</sup> | Long-term tubes; the defunctionalized bladder remains in situ |
| 13 | **Cystectomy and urinary diversion** | Refractory bleeding, end-stage bladder, fistula, intractable pain<sup>[[12]](#ref12)[[5]](#ref5)</sup> | High morbidity and mortality in this population |

### Resuscitation, clot evacuation and irrigation

Most episodes of radiation hemorrhagic cystitis respond to clot evacuation, catheter drainage and irrigation, with correction of coagulopathy.<sup>[[4]](#ref4)[[1]](#ref1)</sup> A large-caliber three-way catheter allows irrigation, but a bladder full of organized clot usually needs evacuation under anesthesia with a rigid sheath and an Ellik evacuator; cystoscopic fulguration of discrete bleeding points can be done at the same time.<sup>[[4]](#ref4)</sup> See [Three-Way Catheter](/docs/foundations/tools/biomaterials/urinary-catheters/three-way-catheter) and [Ellik Evacuator](/docs/foundations/tools/instruments/suction/ellik-evacuator).

### Intravesical alum

Alum (aluminum potassium or ammonium sulfate) is an astringent that precipitates protein at the bleeding surface. After bladder clot evacuation, 1% alum (50 g in 5 L sterile water) can be continuously irrigated at 250–300 mL/hour without anesthesia.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[19]](#ref19)[[21]](#ref21)</sup> In a retrospective Mayo Clinic series of 40 patients who had failed saline irrigation and clot evacuation, 24/40 (60%) needed no further treatment before discharge and 13/40 (32.5%) needed none at a median 17-month follow-up; seven began HBOT before alum was completed.<sup>[[21]](#ref21)</sup> Adverse events were reported in 15 of 40 (38%): bladder spasm in 14, urinary tract infection in 2, and transient delirium in 2, which the investigators attributed to aggressive anticholinergic treatment of spasms; one patient had an asymptomatic rise in blood aluminum that normalized.<sup>[[21]](#ref21)</sup> Assess renal function before alum irrigation and monitor mental status during treatment; consider serum aluminum testing when renal clearance is impaired or toxicity is suspected.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[21]](#ref21)</sup>

### Intravesical glycosaminoglycan replacement

Hyaluronic acid with or without chondroitin sulfate is intended to restore the urothelial glycosaminoglycan layer. In a small randomized trial, intravesical hyaluronic acid and HBOT had no statistically significant difference in hematuria improvement at 6, 12, or 18 months; pelvic pain decreased significantly in both groups through 18 months, while voiding frequency decreased significantly in both at six months but only in the hyaluronic acid group at 12 months.<sup>[[22]](#ref22)</sup> Retrospective data suggest a role in preventing relapse of hematuria, but evidence is limited and onset is slower than with alum or formalin.<sup>[[23]](#ref23)[[1]](#ref1)</sup>

### Hyperbaric oxygen therapy

HBOT has randomized trial evidence for chronic radiation cystitis. RICH-ART randomized 87 patients and analyzed 79; at 6–8 months the improvement in EPIC urinary total score was 10.1 points greater with HBOT than with standard care (95% CI 2.2–18.1).<sup>[[24]](#ref24)</sup> The trial excluded patients with recent bleeding requiring more than 500 mL of transfusion in four weeks, a permanent catheter, bladder capacity below 100 mL and bladder fistula.<sup>[[24]](#ref24)</sup> In the five-year report, after control patients had been offered HBOT, the modeled improvement in EPIC urinary score among 70 HBOT-treated patients was 19.1 points (95% CI 13.3–24.9); only 39 had five-year observations, and 9 of 70 received additional HBOT for recurrence.<sup>[[30]](#ref30)</sup> This supports durability in some patients but is not a five-year randomized comparison.

A 2024 meta-analysis of 14 studies (556 patients) estimated complete remission of radiation-cystitis symptoms, defined as disappearance of hematuria and other symptoms, in 55% (95% CI 51–59%) after HBOT; included studies were heterogeneous.<sup>[[25]](#ref25)</sup> A retrospective series of 60 patients associated HBOT started within six months of hematuria onset with higher response rates.<sup>[[31]](#ref31)</sup> The 2023 Cochrane review rated the evidence for late radiation injury of the bladder as low to moderate certainty and found mainly mild adverse events, such as barotrauma and transient myopia.<sup>[[16]](#ref16)</sup> Regimens, contraindications and logistics are on the [Hyperbaric Oxygen Therapy](/docs/foundations/surgical-principles/hyperbaric-oxygen-therapy) page.

### Antifibrinolytics: aminocaproic acid and tranexamic acid

Aminocaproic acid inhibits plasminogen activation and can be given orally or intravesically. Small series report high response rates, without data on durability. Upper-tract bleeding is excluded first, because stabilized clot can obstruct the ureter, and bladder clot is evacuated completely before treatment, because aminocaproic acid can produce hard clots that are difficult to remove. Thrombotic complications, myopathy and rhabdomyolysis have been reported, mostly with oral use.<sup>[[19]](#ref19)</sup>

Tranexamic acid has been studied off-label for hematuria. In a systematic review of seven studies (970 adults) with postoperative and nonoperative hematuria, four postoperative trials reported less blood loss or hemoglobin decline with TXA, and three nonoperative studies reported less visible hematuria or shorter symptom duration; transfusion findings varied.<sup>[[26]](#ref26)</sup> The same upper-tract clot precautions apply; see [Tranexamic Acid](/docs/foundations/pharmacology/intraoperative-adjuncts/tranexamic-acid).

### Intravesical formalin

Formalin fixes the bladder mucosa and occludes telangiectatic vessels. It is reserved for bleeding that has failed less invasive treatment because of its morbidity.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[4]](#ref4)</sup> Instillation is painful and is performed under general or regional anesthesia. Before each formalin instillation, obtain a cystogram to assess for perforation and vesicoureteral reflux; if reflux is present, occlude the refluxing ureteral orifice with a Fogarty balloon and confirm occlusion on repeat cystography. Upper-tract or peritoneal exposure can cause serious injury; reported complications include bladder fibrosis and contracture, ureteral obstruction and fistula.<sup>[[20]](#ref20)[[4]](#ref4)[[1]](#ref1)</sup>

In a 1989 literature analysis of 235 mixed-etiology hemorrhagic-cystitis cases, complete response rates were 71% with 1% formalin, 78% with 5%, and 83% with 10%; differences were not statistically significant, and higher concentrations were associated with greater morbidity.<sup>[[32]](#ref32)[[20]](#ref20)</sup> In a contemporary cohort of 8 patients with radiation cystitis treated with 1–4% formalin, 6 (75%) responded, with a median time to resolution of 4 days; intraoperative cystography showed reflux in 4 (50%).<sup>[[20]](#ref20)</sup> Of the 6 responders, 2 later needed cystectomy, one for recurrent hematuria and one for bladder neck contracture and bladder dysfunction.<sup>[[20]](#ref20)</sup> The high prevalence of reflux in that series supports routine cystography before every instillation.

### Selective embolization

For refractory or life-threatening bleeding in patients unfit for major surgery, the CUA best-practice report favors selective or superselective vesical-artery embolization when feasible (Grade 3C) to reduce ischemic complications of less selective internal-iliac embolization, including rare bladder necrosis.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[28]](#ref28)</sup> Across observational cohorts of 295 patients with intractable bladder bleeding from mixed causes, a systematic review reported bleeding-control rates of 43–100% after selective embolization; post-embolization syndrome was the most frequently reported complication.<sup>[[28]](#ref28)</sup> In a series of 20 patients with bladder or prostate bleeding, embolization was technically successful in 18 (90%) and controlled bleeding after the first procedure in 15 of 18; 4 of 14 survivors had late recurrence, and the 20% periprocedural mortality was attributed to underlying disease.<sup>[[27]](#ref27)</sup> In 9 patients with hemorrhagic cystitis, superselective vesical artery embolization stopped hematuria within 48 hours in all.<sup>[[33]](#ref33)</sup> These are small retrospective series with selected patients.

### Nephrostomy diversion

Bilateral percutaneous nephrostomy diverts urine away from the bladder. In a retrospective series of 24 patients with refractory hemorrhagic cystitis, hematuria resolved in 17 (71%), with a median time to resolution of 12 days, and the median severity grade fell from 3 to 1.<sup>[[29]](#ref29)</sup> Nephrostomy can serve a patient too unwell for cystectomy, but it leaves the bladder in situ, with the retained-bladder risks described below.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[34]](#ref34)</sup> See [Nephrostomy Tube](/docs/foundations/tools/biomaterials/ureteral-stents/nephrostomy-tube).

### Cystectomy and urinary diversion

For radiation hemorrhagic cystitis unresponsive to previously available therapy, urinary diversion with or without cystectomy is an option; cystectomy with diversion may also be considered for selected patients with an end-stage irradiated bladder, fistula or intractable pain.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[5]](#ref5)</sup> The patients are typically older and comorbid, and the field is irradiated. In the Mayo Clinic series of 21 patients, all of whom had failed clot evacuation, fulguration and irrigation, Clavien grade III–V complications occurred in 8 of 19 evaluable patients (42%) and 90-day mortality was 3 of 19 (16%).<sup>[[12]](#ref12)</sup> In a nine-center study of 100 men who underwent urinary diversion for urinary adverse events of prostate radiation, short- and long-term complication rates were considerable.<sup>[[35]](#ref35)</sup> Among 34 women diverted for late effects of gynecologic radiotherapy, 27/34 (79.4%) had a complication within 90 days and 9/34 (26.5%) had a high-grade complication; high-grade complication rates did not differ significantly by diversion type or concurrent cystectomy in this small cohort.<sup>[[36]](#ref36)</sup>

Patient-reported benefit has been described. In a prospective cohort of 55 patients diverted for radiation-related injury, 47 were included with paired pre- and postoperative surveys; completion varied by instrument (92% PROMIS, 68% GUPI, 89% decision-regret scale). Group scores showed improved global health, pain and quality of life with low decision regret over a median follow-up of 47.7 months; there was no comparison group.<sup>[[37]](#ref37)</sup>

Operative planning in the radiated field accounts for dense pelvic fibrosis, obliterated tissue planes around the bladder, rectum and vagina, and the location of irradiated bowel when a segment for diversion is chosen.<sup>[[5]](#ref5)</sup> See [Simple Cystectomy](/docs/surgical-techniques/04c-urinary-diversion/simple-cystectomy) and [Urinary Diversion](/docs/surgical-techniques/04c-urinary-diversion).

| HBOT, long term | RICH-ART follow-up, n = 70<sup>[[30]](#ref30)</sup> | Modeled +19.1 points at 5 years; 9/70 retreated | Single-group follow-up; 31/70 missing at 5 years |
| HBOT, radiation-cystitis symptoms | Meta-analysis, 14 studies, n = 556<sup>[[25]](#ref25)</sup> | Complete remission of all symptoms in 305/556 (55%; 95% CI 51–59%) | Heterogeneous, mostly observational |
| Hyaluronic acid | RCT against HBOT, n = 36<sup>[[22]](#ref22)</sup> | Hematuria improved in both groups, with no statistically significant between-group difference at 6, 12 or 18 months | Small sample; equivalence untested |
| Alum | Retrospective series, n = 40<sup>[[21]](#ref21)</sup> | No additional therapy before discharge in 24/40 (60%); 13/40 (32.5%) still needed none at median 17-month follow-up | Single center; selected patients |
| Formalin | Mixed-etiology case analysis, n = 235; radiation-only cohort, n = 8<sup>[[32]](#ref32)[[20]](#ref20)</sup> | Complete response 71–83% across historical cases; 6/8 during hospitalization in the radiation cohort | Historical and small series; morbidity underreported |
| Tranexamic acid | Systematic review, 7 studies, n = 970<sup>[[26]](#ref26)</sup> | Reduced bleeding; variable transfusion effect | Mixed etiologies; not specific to radiation |
| Embolization | Systematic review, n = 295; series<sup>[[28]](#ref28)[[27]](#ref27)</sup> | Initial control in most; late recurrence in 4/14 survivors in one series | Retrospective; heterogeneous techniques |
| Nephrostomy | Retrospective series, n = 24<sup>[[29]](#ref29)</sup> | Resolution in 17/24 (71%) | Single center; mixed etiologies |
| Cystectomy | Retrospective series, n = 21 (17 after prostate radiotherapy)<sup>[[12]](#ref12)</sup> | Clavien III–V complications 8/19 (42%); 90-day mortality 3/19 (16%) | Single center; frail, mixed-etiology cohort; outcomes reported for 19 patients |

## The Contracted Radiated Bladder and Reconstructive Endpoints

RTOG/EORTC grade 4 bladder morbidity includes necrosis, a contracted bladder (capacity &lt;100 mL), or severe hemorrhagic cystitis; end-stage radiation injury may also involve poor compliance, outlet stenosis, incontinence, fistula or pain.<sup>[[6]](#ref6)[[5]](#ref5)</sup> Bladder salvage by repeated hemostatic procedures does not restore capacity.<sup>[[6]](#ref6)[[5]](#ref5)</sup>

**Augmentation cystoplasty** has a limited role. It leaves the irradiated bladder remnant and outlet in place. A review of the end-stage irradiated bladder discusses augmentation as an organ-preserving option but concludes that cystectomy with urinary diversion is a safe and viable option in selected irradiated patients, with the goal of improving quality of life.<sup>[[5]](#ref5)</sup> Augmentation in a radiated bladder is therefore an individual decision made with knowledge of outlet function and the patient's ability to catheterize; see [Bladder Augmentation](/docs/surgical-techniques/04b-bladder-reconstruction/bladder-augmentation).

**Diversion type.** In a three-center cohort of women diverted for late adverse effects of gynecologic radiotherapy, most diversions were noncontinent: 26 of 34 (76.5%).<sup>[[36]](#ref36)</sup> In the multi-institutional prostate radiation series, diversions were urinary conduits or continent catheterizable pouches.<sup>[[35]](#ref35)</sup>

**Bladder in situ or cystectomy.** Supravesical diversion without cystectomy shortens the operation but leaves a defunctionalized bladder that can bleed, become infected or develop pyocystis. In a series of 30 patients diverted for benign or malignant disease with the bladder left in situ, 80% had at least one complication related to the bladder (67% pyocystis), 43% were rehospitalized and 30% needed reoperation, including cystectomy in 4.<sup>[[34]](#ref34)</sup> In a second series of 24 patients, whose indications included radiation and hemorrhagic cystitis, 54% had complications of the retained bladder, 33% had pyocystis and 25% later needed cystectomy.<sup>[[38]](#ref38)</sup> Both groups favored primary cystectomy when undiversion is not anticipated. This morbidity is weighed against the added operative risk of concurrent cystectomy in a frail, irradiated patient.<sup>[[12]](#ref12)[[36]](#ref36)</sup>

## Counseling and Follow-Up

- **Natural history.** Bleeding can recur years after a successful treatment; treatment response after alum, formalin, HBOT and embolization is often incomplete or temporary.<sup>[[21]](#ref21)[[30]](#ref30)[[27]](#ref27)</sup>
- **Timing of HBOT.** Earlier referral after hematuria onset is associated with better response, and HBOT requires daily sessions over several weeks.<sup>[[31]](#ref31)[[24]](#ref24)</sup>
- **Formalin consent.** Counsel about anesthesia, bladder contracture, upper-tract injury and the possibility of later cystectomy.<sup>[[20]](#ref20)[[4]](#ref4)</sup>
- **Diversion consent.** Discuss high complication and readmission rates, the expected stoma or catheterization burden, and whether the bladder will be removed.<sup>[[12]](#ref12)[[36]](#ref36)[[35]](#ref35)</sup> Pain relief and freedom from bleeding may be the main goals even when continence cannot be restored.<sup>[[37]](#ref37)</sup>
- **New hematuria.** Repeat evaluation for malignancy and upper-tract disease rather than attributing every episode to radiation.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[8]](#ref8)</sup>
- **Salvage radiotherapy.** Before salvage RT after prostatectomy, late hemorrhagic cystitis is included in counseling about urinary harms.<sup>[[13]](#ref13)</sup>
