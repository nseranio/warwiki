# Masculinizing Procedures

Masculinizing genital surgery creates male-appearing genitalia, allows voiding while standing, and — in staged procedures — enables penetrative intercourse. The two primary genital reconstruction options are **metoidioplasty** and **phalloplasty**, with most patients undergoing concurrent or staged gynecologic procedures (hysterectomy, vaginectomy, scrotoplasty). Surgery is complex, multistaged, and associated with high urethral complication rates — yet overall patient satisfaction remains consistently strong.[^10][^11][^12]

## Gynecologic Procedures

### Hysterectomy and Oophorectomy

Removal of the uterus (hysterectomy) and ovaries (bilateral salpingo-oophorectomy) is commonly performed as an early or concurrent step:

- Eliminates menses, reducing gender dysphoria related to cyclical bleeding
- Oophorectomy allows reduction or cessation of exogenous testosterone in many patients
- Performed laparoscopically in most cases; vaginal approach is an option in patients with prior vaginal delivery
- Must be preceded by fertility preservation counseling (oocyte retrieval) if desired

### Vaginectomy

Colpocleisis (partial or complete vaginal closure) may be performed at the time of metoidioplasty or phalloplasty, particularly when urethral lengthening is planned. Eliminates the vaginal canal and reduces infectious risk from the residual cavity. Creates a more anatomically male perineum.

## Metoidioplasty

Metoidioplasty releases and advances the testosterone-hypertrophied clitoris to create a neophallus. It is the less technically complex of the two genital reconstruction options and can often be completed in one or two stages.

### Technique

- Division of the suspensory ligament and clitoral hood releases the hypertrophied clitoris
- Ventral chordee is corrected to achieve straighter projection
- Urethral plate advancement allows urethral lengthening to the tip of the neophallus (optional)
- Labia minora flaps augment neophallus girth and create a penile shaft appearance
- Scrotoplasty using labia majora tissue, with or without testicular prostheses, completes reconstruction

### Dimensions and Functional Outcomes

- Average neophallus length: 4–5.5 cm[^11][^12]
- Erogenous sensation: excellent — typically rated 4.8/5[^11] (neurovascular supply preserved)
- Standing micturition: achievable with urethral lengthening, though fistula and stricture rates are significant (see Complications)
- Penetrative intercourse: generally not possible given neophallus size; erectile prosthesis cannot be placed
- Recovery: substantially shorter than phalloplasty; hospital stay typically 1–3 days

### Urethral Lengthening in Metoidioplasty

Urethral lengthening extends the native urethra to the neophallus tip to enable voiding while standing. This significantly increases surgical complexity:

- Requires formation of a neourethral tube from vaginal mucosa, buccal mucosa, or labial flaps
- Carries fistula rates of 15–25% and stricture rates of 10–20%
- Many patients opt for metoidioplasty without urethral lengthening and void via native perineal urethra

## Phalloplasty

Phalloplasty constructs an adult-sized neophallus using distant tissue flaps. It provides more complete genital transformation than metoidioplasty but involves greater surgical complexity, longer operative time, staged procedures, and higher complication rates.[^10][^11]

### Radial Forearm Free Flap (RFFF)

The RFFF is the most commonly performed phalloplasty technique.[^10][^13]

**Technique:**
- A fasciocutaneous flap from the non-dominant forearm is harvested on the radial artery pedicle
- A tube-within-a-tube (cricket bat) design creates both the neophallus shaft and the neourethral tube simultaneously
- Microvascular anastomosis to femoral or inferior epigastric vessels
- Nerve coaptation (medial antebrachial cutaneous or lateral antebrachial cutaneous nerve to dorsal clitoral nerve) provides erogenous reinnervation
- The forearm donor site requires skin graft coverage, leaving a visible scar

**Outcomes:**
- Average neophallus length: ~14.1 cm[^11]
- Erogenous sensation develops over 12–18 months; tactile sensation present in most
- Allows later implantation of erectile device and testicular prostheses

**Donor site morbidity:**
- Visible forearm scar is the primary patient concern; can be minimized with careful harvest technique and split-thickness graft
- Temporary loss of wrist extension and forearm strength; typically resolves within months

### Anterolateral Thigh Flap (ALT)

The ALT uses fasciocutaneous or myocutaneous tissue from the lateral thigh, based on perforators from the descending branch of the lateral circumflex femoral artery.

**Advantages:**
- Concealed donor site (beneath clothing)
- May be pedicled (no microvascular anastomosis required) or free
- Useful in patients refusing forearm scar or with unsuitable forearm vasculature

**Disadvantages:**
- Variable perforator anatomy
- Thicker flap in patients with higher BMI — may produce a bulky neophallus
- Sensory reinnervation less reliable than RFFF

### Other Flap Options

- **Superficial circumflex iliac artery perforator (SCIP) flap:** Low donor site morbidity, concealed scar in inguinal crease; growing in popularity[^13]
- **Musculocutaneous latissimus dorsi flap:** Reserved for revision or complex cases; significant donor site morbidity
- **Abdominal flap:** Pedicled option; historically used but now less common due to inferior functional outcomes

### Staging of Phalloplasty

Most phalloplasty programs follow a multistage approach:

| Stage | Timing | Procedure |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | Initial | Flap harvest, neophallus construction, urethral anastomosis to native urethra |
| 2 | 3–6 months | Glansplasty, urethral lengthening revision if needed |
| 3 | 6–12 months | Scrotoplasty, testicular prosthesis placement |
| 4 | 12–18+ months | Erectile device implantation (inflatable or malleable) |

Timing varies by program and individual healing. Urethral complications at stage 1–2 must be resolved before proceeding to implant surgery.

## Urethral Lengthening

Urethral lengthening — whether in metoidioplasty or phalloplasty — is the component associated with the highest complication burden in masculinizing surgery.

### Fistula

- Definition: communication between the neourethra and skin, causing urinary leakage
- Rate: 27–50% following phalloplasty with urethral lengthening[^11][^14]
- Most fistulas occur within the first 3 months postoperatively[^14]
- Management: small fistulas may close with urinary diversion (catheter); most require surgical closure with local tissue flap interposition
- Overall, 73% of patients with phalloplasty require at least one revision for urethral complications[^14][^10]

### Stricture

- Definition: narrowing of the neourethra causing obstructed voiding
- Rate: 32–63% following phalloplasty[^11][^14]
- Most commonly occurs at anastomotic sites (native-to-neourethra junction and neourethra-to-glans junction)
- Management: urethral dilation, direct visual internal urethrotomy, or urethroplasty
- Strictures at the anastomotic junction between native urethra and neourethra often require formal urethroplasty with grafts or flaps

## Prosthetic Implants

### Testicular Prostheses

- Silicone testicular implants placed within a constructed scrotum (labia majora advancement flap)
- Typically placed as a delayed stage after resolution of early wound complications
- Implant extrusion or rejection occurs in approximately 2% of cases[^11]
- Provides significant psychological benefit (genital self-image) independent of functional outcomes

### Erectile Devices

Erectile implants enable penetrative intercourse — not possible with neophallus anatomy alone:

- **Inflatable (hydraulic) devices:** 3-piece or 2-piece systems; provide natural-appearing erection/flaccidity cycle; higher mechanical complexity
- **Malleable (semi-rigid) devices:** Simpler; permanently semi-rigid; easier to implant and maintain

**Timing:** Implantation requires confirmed sensation in the neophallus (typically 12–18 months post-phalloplasty), resolution of all urethral complications, and stable skin envelope. Premature implantation significantly increases infection and extrusion risk.

**Infection:** Device infection rates in neophalli are higher than in cisgender implants due to altered vascularity and prior surgical trauma. Salvage protocols for infected devices (irrigation, exchange) are more challenging in this population.

## Complications Summary

### Metoidioplasty

| Complication | Rate |
|---|---|
| Urethral fistula | 15–25% |
| Urethral stricture | 10–20% |
| Wound dehiscence | 10–15% |
| Testicular implant extrusion | ~2% |
| Worsened mental health | ~19%[^11] |

### Phalloplasty

| Complication | Rate |
|---|---|
| Urethral fistula | 27–50%[^11][^14] |
| Urethral stricture | 32–63%[^11][^14] |
| Flap failure (partial or complete) | 1–5%[^11] |
| Testicular implant rejection | ~2%[^11] |
| Requires urethral revision surgery | ~73%[^14][^10] |

## Outcomes

Despite high urethral complication rates, patient-reported outcomes are strongly favorable:

- **79%** of phalloplasty/metoidioplasty patients report being totally satisfied[^12]
- **20%** report being mainly satisfied[^12]
- Postoperative genital self-image score (20.29) significantly exceeds preoperative score (13.04), approaching but slightly below cisgender male norms (21.97)[^11]
- Most complications occurring within 3 months are ultimately resolved and do not negate overall satisfaction[^14]
