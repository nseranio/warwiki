# Gender-Affirming Surgery — Overview

Gender-affirming surgery (GAS) encompasses surgical procedures designed to align an individual's physical anatomy with their gender identity. For many transgender and gender-diverse (TGD) patients, these procedures are medically necessary interventions that significantly reduce gender dysphoria and improve quality of life — not elective cosmetic surgery. Urologists and reconstructive surgeons are central to the care of TGD patients requiring genital surgery, urethral reconstruction, and management of surgical complications.

## Standards of Care

Modern GAS is governed by the **World Professional Association for Transgender Health (WPATH) Standards of Care**, currently in its eighth version (SOC8, 2022). SOC8 represents a shift from gatekeeping toward informed consent, emphasizing patient autonomy alongside clinical safety.

### Criteria for Genital Surgery (SOC8)

| Criterion | Detail |
|---|---|
| Sustained gender incongruence | Marked and persistent mismatch between gender identity and assigned sex |
| Informed consent capacity | Demonstrated understanding of risks, benefits, and alternatives |
| Medical and psychiatric stability | Significant comorbidities must be well-controlled |
| Hormone therapy | Typically ≥6–12 months of gender-affirming hormones unless medically contraindicated or not desired |
| Mental health referral | SOC8 recommends one letter; many insurers still require two |

SOC8 also eliminated the minimum age requirement for adults and moved away from mandatory "real-life experience" documentation, though individual programs may apply additional criteria based on clinical judgment.[^2]

### Endocrine Society Guidelines

The Endocrine Society guidelines (2017) add that gonadectomy or hysterectomy requires age ≥18 years, at least one year of consistent hormone therapy, satisfactory social role change, and documented satisfaction with hormonal effects — in addition to written agreement from a mental health professional and endocrinologist.[^8]

## Preoperative Preparation

### Permanent Hair Removal

Hair removal from donor skin is among the most time-intensive preoperative requirements:

- **Vaginoplasty (MTF):** Penile shaft and scrotal skin that will line the neovaginal canal must be completely depilated before surgery to prevent intravaginal hair growth, which can cause infection, stones, or obstruction. Both laser hair removal and electrolysis are used; electrolysis is definitive but slower.
- **Phalloplasty (FTM):** The forearm or thigh donor skin used to construct the neourethra must be hair-free. Hair within the urethra results in recurrent UTI, calculus formation, or stricture.

Hair removal typically requires multiple sessions over 6–18 months and should begin as early as possible in the surgical planning process.

### Medical Optimization

- **Smoking cessation:** Nicotine substantially impairs microvascular perfusion and wound healing. Free flap procedures (phalloplasty) carry a particularly elevated risk of partial or complete flap loss in active smokers. Most programs require ≥6–8 weeks of cessation prior to surgery.
- **Weight optimization:** Elevated BMI increases operative time, wound complication rates, and risk of flap failure. Many centers use BMI thresholds (often &lt;35 kg/m²) as relative criteria for major genital procedures.[^9]
- **Cardiovascular and metabolic control:** Diabetes, hypertension, and other medical comorbidities should be optimized. The Charlson Comorbidity Index ≥3 is among the expert-consensus relative contraindications to feminizing genital surgery.[^9]

### Fertility Preservation

Genital surgery involving gonadectomy (orchiectomy, oophorectomy) results in permanent loss of fertility. Patients should be counseled and offered referral for:

- **Sperm cryopreservation** (prior to estrogen therapy, which reduces sperm production, or prior to orchiectomy)
- **Oocyte or embryo cryopreservation** (prior to testosterone therapy or oophorectomy)
- **Uterine tissue preservation** (experimental)

Documentation of counseling — and patient decision to proceed or decline — should be part of the preoperative record regardless of whether preservation is pursued.[^1]

### Psychological Preparation

Patients benefit from preoperative counseling addressing:

- Realistic expectations for functional and aesthetic outcomes
- Surgical staging (many genital procedures require 2–3 stages)
- Commitment to postoperative protocols (particularly dilation after vaginoplasty)
- Long-term follow-up with the surgical team and primary care

## Shared Surgical Considerations

### Multidisciplinary Teams

Major genital surgery — particularly phalloplasty — typically requires plastic surgery, urology, and gynecology working in concert. Even at experienced centers, coordinated intraoperative roles reduce operative time and complication rates.[^4]

### Staging

Most comprehensive genital reconstructions are performed in stages. Phalloplasty commonly proceeds as:
1. Flap harvest and neophallus construction ± urethral lengthening
2. Glansplasty and urethral anastomosis
3. Scrotoplasty ± testicular prostheses
4. Erectile device implantation

Metoidioplasty can often be completed in one or two stages. Vaginoplasty is typically single-stage, though revision procedures are common.

### Anesthesia and Positioning

Genital surgery requires careful positioning, often in lithotomy or modified dorsal lithotomy. Prolonged cases carry risk of compartment syndrome, brachial plexus injury, and pressure necrosis — particularly in obese patients or procedures exceeding 6 hours.

## Special Populations

### Adolescents

SOC8 acknowledges that some gender-affirming procedures may be appropriate for adolescents after thorough evaluation, though genital surgery is typically not offered before adulthood. Chest surgery (mastectomy) may be considered earlier in select cases under SOC8 criteria.

### Elderly Patients

Advanced age alone is not a contraindication to GAS. Older TGD patients may have lived for decades with untreated gender dysphoria, and the psychological benefit of surgery can be profound. Preoperative assessment should account for polypharmacy, baseline mobility, cardiopulmonary reserve, and realistic recovery expectations.[^3]

### Incarcerated Individuals

Transgender prisoners face significant systemic barriers including denial of hormone therapy, housing in facilities inconsistent with gender identity, and denial of medically necessary GAS. Legal frameworks continue to evolve; access has most commonly been achieved through litigation rather than institutional policy.[^3]

### Patients With Prior Pelvic Surgery or Radiation

Previous pelvic surgery (particularly hysterectomy or prostatectomy) and pelvic radiation significantly alter the surgical field. Radiation in particular impairs tissue vascularity, wound healing, and functional outcomes. Vaginoplasty and urethroplasty in irradiated patients require greater use of vascularized tissue interposition (e.g., omental pedicle, gracilis flap) and carry higher complication rates.[^9]

## Outcomes

Despite high reported complication rates — particularly for urethral procedures in phalloplasty — overall patient satisfaction with GAS is consistently high across the literature:

- 94% of vaginoplasty patients report they would undergo surgery again[^7]
- 79% of phalloplasty/metoidioplasty patients report being totally satisfied[^6]
- Quality of life improves significantly across mental health, body image, social function, and sexual function domains following vaginoplasty[^7]
- Postoperative genital self-image scores following phalloplasty significantly exceed preoperative scores, though remain slightly below cisgender male norms[^5]

Patients and providers should discuss that surgical complications — while common — are generally manageable and do not negate the overall benefit of surgery for appropriately selected patients.
