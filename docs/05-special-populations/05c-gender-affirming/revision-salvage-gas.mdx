# Revision & Salvage GAS

Revision and salvage surgery following gender-affirming genital procedures is common — not exceptional. The majority of patients undergoing phalloplasty with urethral lengthening will require at least one additional procedure.[^3] Understanding the patterns of failure and the principles of revision allows the reconstructive urologist to counsel patients accurately preoperatively and manage complications systematically.

## When Revision Is Expected vs. Unexpected

| Category | Examples |
|---|---|
| Planned/staged revision | Glansplasty, scrotoplasty, erectile device placement — these are built into the surgical plan |
| Expected complication-driven revision | Urethral fistula or stricture after phalloplasty (73% of patients[^3]) |
| Unplanned reconstructive challenge | Flap failure, device infection, major wound breakdown |
| Aesthetic revision | Refinement of vulvar aesthetics, labia symmetry, glansplasty revision |

## Vaginoplasty Revisions

### Vaginal Stenosis

The most common indication for revision vaginoplasty. Results from inadequate dilation adherence, wound contracture, or insufficient initial skin volume.

- **Mild:** Progressive dilation with larger dilators; topical estrogen to improve skin pliability
- **Moderate:** Surgical scar release with Z-plasty or VY advancement at the introitus; skin graft or flap augmentation
- **Severe:** Intestinal or peritoneal interposition (converting from skin-lined to mucosa-lined canal); requires reopening of the perineal dissection plane

### Rectovaginal Fistula

A major complication occurring in 0.9–3% of vaginoplasties.[^1] Fistula typically results from intraoperative rectal injury during cavity creation.

- Fecal diversion (loop colostomy or ileostomy) is usually required to allow healing
- Primary repair: tissue interposition (labial fat pad, gracilis flap) to separate the fistula tract
- Timing: most surgeons defer definitive repair until diversion is in place and tissue inflammation has subsided (minimum 3 months)
- Colostomy reversal follows confirmed fistula closure

### Urethral Meatal Stenosis

Very common after vaginoplasty; presents with obstructed voiding, stream deviation, or spraying. Managed with serial urethral dilation or formal meatoplasty. Severe cases require formal urethroplasty.

### Aesthetic and Functional Refinements

- **Granulation tissue:** Persistent granulation in the neovaginal canal is treated with silver nitrate cauterization or topical triamcinolone
- **Labial asymmetry or excess:** Labiaplasty techniques
- **Clitoral hood revision:** Excess tissue may require excision for adequate clitoral exposure
- **Urethral repositioning:** Spraying urinary stream related to urethral angulation

## Phalloplasty Revisions

### Urethral Fistula

- Complication rate: 27–50%[^2][^3]; most occur in the first 3 months
- Small cutaneous fistulas: catheter drainage ± local wound care may allow spontaneous closure
- Persistent or large fistulas: excision of fistula tract with local flap closure; buccal mucosa or skin graft inlay if tissue is deficient
- Principle: the tissue used to close the fistula must be well-vascularized; do not reapproximate in a previously failed closure without tissue interposition

### Urethral Stricture

- Complication rate: 32–63%[^2][^3]; commonly occurs at anastomotic junctions
- **Dilation:** Short-segment, compliant strictures may respond to serial dilation
- **DVIU:** Limited role; high recurrence in neourethral strictures
- **Urethroplasty:** Excision and primary anastomosis for short bulbar-equivalent strictures; buccal mucosa onlay or inlay for longer segments

The neourethra in phalloplasty is anatomically analogous to the male urethra in many respects, and the principles of stricture management — including graft urethroplasty — apply directly. Urologists skilled in anterior urethroplasty are well-positioned to manage these complications.

### Partial Flap Loss

Partial necrosis of the phalloplasty flap may result in loss of distal neophallus, urethral disruption, or skin deficiency. Management depends on extent:

- Small areas of distal necrosis may be managed with wound care and secondary healing
- Larger defects require local rearrangement flaps or additional free tissue transfer
- Urethral disruption at the distal anastomosis requires staged reconstruction

### Complete Flap Failure

Rare (~1–5%) but catastrophic. Requires:
- Immediate return to the operating room for microvascular exploration / re-anastomosis (within 6 hours of ischemia)
- If salvage fails: staged reconstruction with a second donor site after recipient vessel recovery (typically 6–12 months)
- Patient counseling regarding metoidioplasty as an alternative if microsurgical redo is not feasible

### Erectile Device Complications

- **Infection:** Higher rates than in cisgender patients due to altered vascularity, prior surgical trauma, and scarred tissue bed
  - Salvage protocol (washout, catheter irrigation, device exchange) may preserve the implant in select cases
  - Explantation with delayed reimplantation (3–6 months minimum) is the standard approach for established infection
- **Mechanical failure:** Component leak, cylinder aneurysm, or pump malfunction — managed per standard penile prosthetics revision principles
- **Erosion:** Device eroding through neophallus skin; requires explantation and tissue reinforcement before reimplantation

## Principles of Revision Surgery

1. **Allow adequate healing time** between procedures. Rushing revision for fistula or wound breakdown invariably fails. Most revision urethroplasties should wait ≥3 months from the last procedure.
2. **Identify and address etiology** before re-operating. Stricture at the native-to-neourethra anastomosis requires the same rational approach as any anastomotic stricture.
3. **Use vascularized tissue interposition** for fistula closure in previously repaired sites — do not simply close in layers.
4. **Manage expectations.** Patients who require multiple revisions often have underlying tissue factors (ischemia, infection history, BMI) that will recur. Honest preoperative counseling before each revision prevents serial disappointment.
5. **Maintain the patient's relationship with the operative team.** Continuity of care through complications significantly affects patient psychological outcomes after GAS.
