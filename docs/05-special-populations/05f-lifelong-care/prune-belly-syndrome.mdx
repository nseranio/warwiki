# Prune Belly Syndrome

**Prune belly syndrome (PBS)**, also called **Eagle-Barrett** or **triad syndrome**, is a rare congenital disorder (incidence about 1 in 35,000–50,000) defined by the classic triad of **abdominal-wall musculature deficiency, urinary-tract dilation, and bilateral cryptorchidism** in males.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup> It is among the most complex conditions in transitional urology: survivors require lifelong multidisciplinary urological care that must be deliberately handed off from pediatric to adult providers.<sup>[[2]](#ref2)[[3]](#ref3)</sup>

In adults, PBS presents as a large hypotonic bladder that empties unpredictably, dilated upper tracts at chronic risk, fertility limited by cryptorchidism and accessory-gland hypoplasia, and a lax abdominal wall that may affect voiding efficiency. This article therefore sits within [Transitional Urology](/docs/special-populations/05f-lifelong-care/transitional-urology).

---

## Disease Overview and Spectrum

PBS is a spectrum disorder, from mild (near-normal life expectancy) to lethal (neonatal pulmonary hypoplasia).<sup>[[4]](#ref4)</sup> The urinary-tract abnormalities include large hypotonic bladders, dilated tortuous megaureters, vesicoureteral reflux (about 78%), and hydronephrosis.<sup>[[5]](#ref5)</sup> A histologic study of three PBS fetuses versus seven controls found less smooth muscle, type-III collagen and innervation. These findings help explain the phenotype but do not prove that functional improvement is impossible.<sup>[[6]](#ref6)</sup>

In a single-center cohort of 46 children, 39% had documented CKD and 17% had undergone renal transplantation.<sup>[[5]](#ref5)</sup> Prognostic markers for renal failure include nadir serum creatinine &gt;0.7 mg/dL, bilaterally abnormal kidneys on imaging, and clinical pyelonephritis.<sup>[[7]](#ref7)</sup>

---

## Renal Function and Transplantation

Renal preservation is the primary lifelong goal. Pyelonephritis and obstruction are potentially modifiable contributors to later renal decline. This distinction arose from a small historical pathologic series; congenital dysplasia and reduced nephron reserve remain relevant throughout life.<sup>[[8]](#ref8)</sup>

For those reaching ESRD, transplant outcomes are encouraging:

- A single-center series (13 transplants in 9 patients) reported graft survival of 90.9% at 5 years, 57.7% at 10 years and 38.5% at 15 years, not significantly different from 106 matched recipients with non-urologic causes (P = 0.134).<sup>[[9]](#ref9)</sup>
- Australian KRT registry data (37 patients) showed 1-, 5-, and 10-year first-graft survival of 91%, 71% and 51%, equivalent to or better than matched congenital-kidney-disease controls, and peritoneal dialysis was feasible despite the abdominal-wall deficiency.<sup>[[10]](#ref10)</sup>
- The ESPN/ERA-EDTA registry (88 boys) reported graft-loss risk comparable to other CAKUT groups and slightly higher mortality than in congenital obstructive uropathy (HR 1.96, 95% CI 1.03–3.74).<sup>[[11]](#ref11)</sup>
- The distensible abdominal wall can facilitate placement of an adult kidney in younger recipients.<sup>[[12]](#ref12)</sup>

## Lower Urinary Tract Management

Bladder dysfunction is the central and evolving challenge. Voiding is highly variable and changes over time: in a 34-patient urodynamic series, 44% voided spontaneously and 56% required clean intermittent catheterization (CIC). That status is not permanent, so serial urodynamic reassessment is needed instead of reliance on symptoms.<sup>[[13]](#ref13)</sup> The series (follow-up 6 months–19 years) described three voiding patterns, none correlating with residual volumes.<sup>[[13]](#ref13)</sup>

The flow patterns cannot determine who needs CIC. Serial PVR, infections, renal and upper-tract findings and, where necessary, pressure-flow assessment guide the decision. There is no validated universal PBS-specific PVR percentage mandating catheterization.<sup>[[13]](#ref13)</sup> When ongoing drainage is required and feasible, intermittent catheterization generally avoids the burdens of a chronic indwelling catheter; AUA/SUFU support comes from neurogenic, not PBS-specific, evidence.<sup>[[14]](#ref14)</sup> Do not prescribe Valsalva or Credé from the flow-curve shape alone: adjacent EAU neuro-urology guidance cautions against these methods unless urodynamics demonstrate safe pressures.<sup>[[36]](#ref36)</sup>

Surgical and pharmacologic options:

- **Monfort abdominoplasty** was associated with improved emptying in a 12-patient series (paired UDS in eight). PVR fell from 40.3% to 13% of capacity and UTIs from 5.7 to 1.2/year, with better continence and bladder sensation, even without concomitant urinary reconstruction. This suggests a possible functional benefit, but does not establish efficacy of adult revision abdominoplasty.<sup>[[20]](#ref20)</sup>
- **Reduction cystoplasty** lowers bladder volume (about 52%) and can help in childhood, but long-term capacities re-expand (740–2,300 cc) and it does not reliably improve voiding dynamics. It is most useful in the pediatric setting.<sup>[[15]](#ref15)[[21]](#ref21)</sup>
- **Selective ureteral tailoring and reimplantation** for severe VUR or upper-tract stasis, guided by longitudinal renal-function studies and not performed empirically.<sup>[[16]](#ref16)[[17]](#ref17)</sup>
- **Persistently unsafe storage or emptying** requires reassessment and specialist treatment. A pressure of 40 cm H₂O is a historical upper-tract risk marker, not a universal PBS threshold or automatic indication for augmentation. Treatment depends on compliance, detrusor activity, drainage and renal trajectory; evidence for neurogenic therapies should not be assumed to establish PUV or PBS-specific benefit.<sup>[[14]](#ref14)[[19]](#ref19)</sup>

### Urodynamic surveillance

PBS is not automatically neurogenic LUTD. Its adult surveillance is individualized using congenital anatomy, bladder function, infections, reflux and renal status. The AUA/SUFU adult NLUTD schedule can inform discussion, but its categories and intervals are not validated PBS-specific recommendations.<sup>[[18]](#ref18)</sup> Follow renal function and upper tracts regularly, more closely when abnormal; obtain multichannel UDS when pressure or compliance information will change management or deterioration may be clinically silent. Neither congenital megacystis nor prior reconstruction alone establishes a neurogenic high-risk category, and AUA/SUFU does not mandate a universal two-year UDS interval for PBS.<sup>[[14]](#ref14)[[18]](#ref18)</sup>

> **Practical point.** Voiding status in PBS changes over time. A patient voiding spontaneously in adolescence may later need CIC, and the reverse also occurs. Reassess serially.<sup>[[13]](#ref13)</sup>

## Reproductive and Sexual Health

This is a major transition-era concern. Most PBS males who underwent orchiopexy reach spontaneous puberty with satisfactory sexual function.<sup>[[22]](#ref22)</sup> However, retrograde ejaculation is common (7 of 9 in one cohort), and late orchiopexy historically produced sexually active but infertile men with azoospermia and elevated FSH.<sup>[[23]](#ref23)</sup>

More recent data are more optimistic. In 15 postpubertal patients (mean orchiopexy age 18 months), motile spermatozoa were found in 4/8 who submitted semen, with generally normal hormonal profiles, though prostate hypoplasia (66.6%) and unilateral seminal-vesicle absence (66.6%) were prevalent and likely contribute to infertility.<sup>[[24]](#ref24)</sup> **Intracytoplasmic sperm injection (ICSI)** has enabled successful pregnancies, and the Australian registry reported 3 PBS men achieving parenthood at a median age of 35.<sup>[[10]](#ref10)[[25]](#ref25)</sup> Early orchiopexy is advocated; current EAU guidance for undescended testes aims for 6–12 months and by 18 months at the latest, with technique tailored to anatomy and comorbidity.<sup>[[22]](#ref22)[[37]](#ref37)</sup>

## Malignancy Surveillance

As survival into adulthood improves, malignancy risk becomes increasingly relevant:

- **Testicular malignancy.** Inherent cryptorchidism carries germ-cell-tumor risk; a retroperitoneal germ cell tumor has been reported.<sup>[[26]](#ref26)</sup>
- **Bladder cancer.** Advanced bladder cancer has been reported in a 38-year-old PBS patient with a nonaugmented, nondefunctionalized bladder, supporting prompt investigation of new hematuria. A single case does not establish a routine cystoscopic cancer-screening program for all asymptomatic PBS patients.<sup>[[27]](#ref27)</sup>

Patients with prior augmentation or diversion require lifelong annual surveillance (focused history, exam, basic metabolic panel, upper-tract imaging) given metabolic, stone, perforation, and malignancy risk.<sup>[[14]](#ref14)</sup>

## Transition Process and Models of Care

A retrospective **congenital neurogenic-bladder** transition program reported successful transfer in 76/79 patients deemed ready, among 157 enrolled. Follow-up remained active in 55/76 transferred versus 63/171 adults without formal transition (72.4% vs 36.8%). These selected, nonrandomized groups support coordinated care but do not establish a PBS-specific causal effect.<sup>[[29]](#ref29)</sup> The International Children's Continence Society supports early, developmentally appropriate transition preparation, building self-management, health literacy, and independence via a readiness-checklist approach.<sup>[[30]](#ref30)</sup>

Elements:

- **Dedicated multidisciplinary clinics with transition coordinators**; structured staged models (e.g., T1–T4) with milestone-based progression.<sup>[[2]](#ref2)[[28]](#ref28)[[31]](#ref31)</sup>
- **Time alone with the adolescent** during consultations to build independence and address confidential issues.<sup>[[30]](#ref30)</sup>
- **Psychosocial-readiness assessment.** Health literacy and family support are primary predictors of success; address self-esteem, body image (abdominal-wall appearance), and reproductive counseling.<sup>[[2]](#ref2)[[32]](#ref32)[[33]](#ref33)</sup>

Barriers mirror the rest of transitional urology: limited patient education, scarce adult-urologist expertise in congenital disease, financial constraints, and geographic disparity.<sup>[[2]](#ref2)[[3]](#ref3)</sup>

## Quality of Life

In an online survey of 32 children with PBS, scores were significantly below those of healthy controls across all PedsQL domains: physical (66.3 vs 84.4), emotional (68.4 vs 80.9), social (63.1 vs 87.4), and school (53.0 vs 78.6; p &lt; 0.01 for each). Scores of 19 caregivers were lower than those of a healthy adult comparison group as well.<sup>[[34]](#ref34)</sup> Chronic constipation, pulmonary problems, and orthopedic issues affect over half of survivors, contributing to morbidity beyond the urinary tract.<sup>[[35]](#ref35)</sup> Body-image concerns and the psychosocial weight of infertility should be addressed through transition and into adult care.

## Key Principles

- **Renal preservation is the lifelong goal**; address infection, obstruction and bladder safety while recognizing persistent congenital renal vulnerability.<sup>[[8]](#ref8)</sup>
- **Bladder emptying changes over time.** Combine PVR, symptoms, renal imaging and indicated UDS; the 56% CIC estimate is from one historical 34-patient series.<sup>[[13]](#ref13)</sup>
- **Monfort abdominoplasty** was associated with lower PVR and fewer UTIs in a 12-patient series, in addition to its cosmetic effect.<sup>[[20]](#ref20)</sup>
- **Unsafe pressure or compliance requires treatment**, but no single pressure cut-off automatically mandates augmentation.<sup>[[14]](#ref14)[[19]](#ref19)</sup>
- **Counsel on fertility early.** Retrograde ejaculation and accessory-gland hypoplasia are common, but motile sperm (4/8 tested in one series) and ICSI-assisted parenthood are achievable; advocate early orchiopexy.<sup>[[22]](#ref22)[[24]](#ref24)[[25]](#ref25)</sup>
- **Transplant outcomes are encouraging.** Five-year graft survival was 91% in one single-center series and 71% in the Australian registry, and peritoneal dialysis was feasible despite the lax abdominal wall.<sup>[[9]](#ref9)[[10]](#ref10)</sup>
