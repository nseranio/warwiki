# Transitional Urology

**Transitional urology** is the subspecialty bridging pediatric and adult urologic care for patients with congenital or acquired genitourinary conditions requiring lifelong management. It encompasses the structured process by which adolescents and young adults (AYAs) assume increasing responsibility for their own health care, transfer from pediatric to adult providers, and receive ongoing surveillance for condition-specific complications.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[2]](#ref2)</sup> With improved survival, more individuals with complex congenital anomalies are reaching adulthood than ever before, creating an urgent need for dedicated adult expertise in conditions historically managed only by pediatric urologists.<sup>[[3]](#ref3)[[4]](#ref4)</sup>

---

## Definition and Scope

Transition is distinct from transfer: **transition** is the longitudinal process of preparing the patient for adult-centered care, while **transfer** is the discrete event of moving to an adult provider.<sup>[[5]](#ref5)</sup> The International Children's Continence Society (ICCS) position statement emphasizes that transition should begin in early adolescence and address medical, psychosocial, educational, and vocational needs.<sup>[[5]](#ref5)</sup>

Core conditions requiring transitional urologic care:<sup>[[2]](#ref2)[[6]](#ref6)[[7]](#ref7)</sup>

- **Neurogenic bladder** (spina bifida, spinal cord injury, tethered cord)
- **Posterior urethral valves (PUV)**
- **Bladder exstrophy-epispadias complex (BEEC)**
- **Disorders / differences of sex development (DSD)**
- **Hypospadias** (particularly proximal / complex)
- **Anorectal malformations** (cloacal anomalies)
- **Prune belly syndrome**
- **Vesicoureteral reflux and CAKUT** (congenital anomalies of the kidney and urinary tract)
- **History of cryptorchidism / orchiopexy**, and the postpubertal undescended testis
- Patients with augmentation cystoplasty, continent diversions, or catheterizable channels
- Survivors of pediatric genitourinary malignancy (for example, pelvic rhabdomyosarcoma) with treatment-related lower urinary tract dysfunction

---

## Universal Goals

Regardless of the underlying condition, the overarching objectives are:<sup>[[2]](#ref2)[[6]](#ref6)[[7]](#ref7)</sup>

1. **Preservation of renal function.** This is the main lifelong goal.
2. **Optimization of lower urinary tract function.** Safe storage and drainage.
3. **Urinary continence.** It supports social participation, employment, and quality of life.
4. **Sexual function and fertility.** Addressed proactively starting in adolescence.
5. **Surveillance for long-term surgical complications.** Especially after augmentation cystoplasty or diversion.
6. **Malignancy monitoring.** In augmented bladders, retained gonads (DSD), and a history of cryptorchidism or testicular microlithiasis.
7. **Psychosocial well-being and independence.** Self-management, mental health, vocational planning.

## The Transition Process

### When to Start

The joint AAP, AAFP and ACP clinical report recommends beginning transition planning by age 12–14, with transfer of care ideally between ages 18 and 21, depending on developmental readiness and medical stability.<sup>[[49]](#ref49)[[8]](#ref8)</sup> The Spina Bifida Association (SBA) 2023 guidelines provide age-stratified recommendations beginning in early adolescence.<sup>[[9]](#ref9)</sup> The EAU 2026 paediatric guideline advises addressing transition from age 12 and by age 16 at the latest, with gradual introduction of the adult urologist several years before transfer. It gives a weak recommendation to start transition at the onset of adolescence with paediatric and adult urologists working together, and a strong recommendation to develop a standardised transition program and to use a validated readiness tool. Its supporting evidence is level 4.<sup>[[48]](#ref48)</sup>

### Transition Readiness Assessment

Several tools have undergone validation studies, but measurement properties remain limited. A 2025 COSMIN review of 22 instruments graded all of them B because of insufficient or uncertain content validity and low-to-moderate quality of evidence, and none was clearly preferred:<sup>[[8]](#ref8)[[10]](#ref10)[[11]](#ref11)</sup>

- **Transition Readiness Assessment Questionnaire (TRAQ).** Most widely used; generic.
- **TRAQ-SB.** Spina bifida-specific version, validated up to age 25.<sup>[[9]](#ref9)</sup>
- **Good2Go Questionnaire.** Evaluates self-advocacy, knowledge, self-care, and social support.<sup>[[12]](#ref12)</sup>
- **TRxANSITION Scale**, **RTQ**, and **Got Transition tools**.<sup>[[10]](#ref10)</sup>

A retrospective Good2Go comparison (53 program participants versus 53 controls) found greater knowledge readiness (+12.78 points). Although some ER/admission comparisons improved, difference-in-differences analyses did not consistently establish reduced emergency utilization; this was not a randomized trial.<sup>[[12]](#ref12)</sup>

### Structured Transition Programs

A systematic review of 20 studies found that structured transition programs with dedicated multidisciplinary clinics were associated with improved patient adherence and fewer emergency visits. Psychosocial readiness and family involvement were the primary predictors of successful transition. Barriers include inadequate patient education, lack of transition coordinators, limited adult care access, and financial constraints.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup>

A 9-year evaluation of a staged transition clinic for congenital neurogenic bladder reported transfer in 76/79 patients considered ready, among 157 program participants. Follow-up remained active in 55/76 transferred patients versus 63/171 patients entering adult care without formal transition. The selected, nonrandomized groups do not establish a causal effect.<sup>[[13]](#ref13)</sup>

## Condition-Specific Considerations

### Spina Bifida / Neurogenic Bladder

> See the dedicated [Neurogenic Bladder](/docs/clinical-conditions/03d-nlutd/neurogenic-bladder) page for the full adult NLUTD workup, AUA/SUFU 2021 framework, and management ladder. The summary below is the transition-clinic orientation.

Spina bifida is the most common condition requiring transitional urology. Adult management issues:<sup>[[2]](#ref2)[[14]](#ref14)[[15]](#ref15)[[16]](#ref16)</sup>

- **Ongoing CIC and anticholinergic management.** Bladder dynamics can change with growth, puberty, and aging; urodynamic reassessment is often needed. As chapter-author practice rather than a validated schedule, reassessment triggers include a declining estimated GFR or new hydronephrosis on surveillance, a continence change (including new nocturnal enuresis or a shortened dry interval), or new or more frequent UTIs; bladder pain, frequency, urgency and incontinence can all reflect changing bladder function rather than true infection and should not be assumed to be recurrent UTI without confirmation.<sup>[[62]](#ref62)</sup>
- **Renal surveillance.** Proteinuria screening, renal function and upper-tract imaging, recognizing creatinine limitations with low muscle mass; use kidney-protective medication according to CKD phenotype and nephrology guidance rather than progressive CKD alone.
- **Augmentation cystoplasty complications.** Metabolic acidosis, stone formation, UTIs, perforation risk, and malignancy surveillance (see below).
- **Bowel management.** Fecal incontinence rated as the biggest issue by 50% of parents; continence is a major factor in social participation and employment.<sup>[[9]](#ref9)[[15]](#ref15)</sup>
- **Sexual health.** Dissatisfaction with sexual function is common; proactive counseling about fertility, contraception, and pregnancy planning is needed.<sup>[[9]](#ref9)</sup>
- **Latex allergy.** High prevalence; must be documented and communicated to adult providers.
- **Shunt management.** Adult neurosurgery referral for VP shunt concerns.<sup>[[9]](#ref9)</sup>

The SBA 2023 guidelines recommend a hand-off packet including medical summary, action plans for common complications (shunt malfunction, UTI, constipation, skin ulcers), and supply / equipment information.<sup>[[9]](#ref9)</sup>

**Disparities in access and outcomes.** In registry analyses of children and young adults with spina bifida, male sex, non-Hispanic Black race, and lack of private insurance were each independently associated with lower odds of bladder and bowel continence; among participants aged 5–21, Hispanic/Latino patients had lower urinary (38.6% versus 44.9%) and bowel (43.9% versus 55.8%) continence at their most recent clinic visit than others.<sup>[[51]](#ref51)[[52]](#ref52)</sup> Surgical management itself shows a more mixed pattern: across NSBPR centers, rates of bladder reconstruction surgery vary widely (12.1% to 37.9%) and are independently associated with treating center, spina bifida type, mobility, sex, and age, but not with race or insurance status after adjustment; rates of neurogenic-bowel surgery, by contrast, are independently associated with white race and private insurance in addition to clinical factors.<sup>[[53]](#ref53)[[54]](#ref54)</sup> A patient's continence outcomes and access to bowel surgery may therefore already be shaped by demographic and insurance factors before reaching an adult reconstructive urologist, relevant to the system and provider barriers below.

**Cost of lifelong care.** Adults with spina bifida in a privately insured population had average incremental medical expenditures of $13,339/year (ages 18-44) and $10,134/year (ages 45-64) above matched peers, three to six times the spending of adults without spina bifida; per-capita costs are highest in infancy.<sup>[[55]](#ref55)</sup> A statewide cohort found a mean hospital cost of $39,059 per infant with spina bifida in the first year of life.<sup>[[56]](#ref56)</sup> Caregivers of children with spina bifida worked an average of 7.5 to 11.3 fewer hours per week than Arkansas survey controls, depending on disability severity, translating to an estimated lifetime productivity cost of $133,755 (2002 dollars).<sup>[[57]](#ref57)</sup> Successful transition to adult care is associated with a shift toward ambulatory rather than emergency care: in one health system, children used more ambulatory visits per year than fully transitioned adults (7.25 versus 5.33), while adults who did not complete transition used more inpatient and emergency care.<sup>[[58]](#ref58)</sup>

### Posterior Urethral Valves (PUV)

> See the dedicated [Posterior Urethral Valves](/docs/special-populations/05f-lifelong-care/posterior-urethral-valves) page for the full adult workup, valve-bladder management ladder, renal-preservation framework, transplantation, and surveillance protocol. The summary below is the transition-clinic orientation.

PUV is a major cause of congenital male outlet obstruction. Renal outcomes vary by cohort and follow-up: a Finnish series reported ESRD in 44/193 patients and a Kaplan–Meier lifetime estimate of 28.5%<sup>[[18]](#ref18)</sup>. Although no new ESRD was observed after age 34 in that series, this is not a safe age to stop surveillance or proof that lifelong risk ends.<sup>[[17]](#ref17)[[18]](#ref18)</sup>

Adult issues:

- **Progressive CKD.** In a selected cohort of 52 PUV patients (35 children and 17 adults), the pooled median eGFR decline was 2.6 mL/min/1.73 m²/year; decline occurred in both age groups, and proteinuria and recurrent febrile UTIs were associated with faster progression.<sup>[[17]](#ref17)</sup>
- **"Valve bladder" syndrome.** Lower urinary tract dysfunction was documented in 15/39 (38%) adults with complete follow-up after childhood PUV treatment; valve-bladder dysfunction can evolve over years and manifest as poor compliance, detrusor overactivity, or myogenic failure.<sup>[[19]](#ref19)[[48]](#ref48)</sup>
- **Sexual function.** In one selected adult PUV cohort, none of 32 men with sexual-function data reported erectile dysfunction and 4/32 reported slow ejaculation; these findings do not establish population rates for either endpoint.<sup>[[19]](#ref19)</sup>
- **Fertility.** Generally preserved, though lower paternity rates are reported.
- A retrospective dedicated-clinic comparison found less CKD progression (12% versus 27%) and kidney replacement therapy (3% versus 20%); baseline and treatment differences preclude attributing these reductions solely to the clinic.<sup>[[20]](#ref20)</sup>

### Bladder Exstrophy-Epispadias Complex (BEEC)

> See the dedicated [Bladder Exstrophy-Epispadias Complex](/docs/special-populations/05f-lifelong-care/bladder-exstrophy-epispadias) page for the full adult continence-reconstruction ladder, continent diversion, malignancy surveillance, genital reconstruction, fertility, and pregnancy management. The summary below is the transition-clinic orientation.

Adults with BEEC face unique challenges related to continence, genital anatomy, sexual function, and fertility:<sup>[[21]](#ref21)[[22]](#ref22)[[23]](#ref23)[[24]](#ref24)[[25]](#ref25)</sup>

- **Continence.** In a selected 29-patient exstrophy-epispadias series, 83% achieved diurnal continence, while 76% required &gt;1 continence procedure.<sup>[[25]](#ref25)</sup>
- **Sexual function in males.** In selected series, 55% of 38 men had normal erectile function, while 79% of men in a 29-patient exstrophy-epispadias cohort reported sexual satisfaction and 63% reported normal ejaculation; 73% of 39 men after urinary diversion reported a stable relationship. Short functional penile length and dorsal curvature are anatomical concerns.<sup>[[21]](#ref21)[[22]](#ref22)[[24]](#ref24)[[25]](#ref25)[[48]](#ref48)</sup>
- **Sexual function in females.** A Finnish comparison found similar FSFI domain scores and a higher median total score among responding women than controls.<sup>[[21]](#ref21)</sup> A Dutch cohort of eight women found no difference from population comparators except for pain.<sup>[[23]](#ref23)</sup> In another selected series, 25% of women underwent introitoplasty.<sup>[[25]](#ref25)</sup> In a 2026 classic-bladder-exstrophy cohort, 20 of 27 eligible women responded; the authors reported that 42.6% met the FSFI dysfunction threshold and cautioned that standard scores may miss exstrophy-specific concerns.<sup>[[64]](#ref64)</sup>
- **Male fertility.** In a selected 39-man series after urinary diversion, oligoasthenoteratozoospermia was reported in 71%; in a separate adult series, mean ejaculate volume was 0.4 cc among four men who had semen analysis. Retrograde ejaculation has been described as a possible contributor to impaired fertility after exstrophy reconstruction.<sup>[[22]](#ref22)[[26]](#ref26)[[66]](#ref66)</sup> In two selected adult series, 11/39 men after urinary diversion and 2/16 men with classic bladder exstrophy reported fathering children; fertility counseling may include ART.<sup>[[22]](#ref22)[[24]](#ref24)[[26]](#ref26)</sup> Early sperm banking should be discussed.<sup>[[22]](#ref22)[[24]](#ref24)[[26]](#ref26)</sup>
- **Female fertility.** Generally preserved; pregnancy is possible but requires specialized obstetric management (cesarean delivery often recommended due to pelvic anatomy).<sup>[[27]](#ref27)</sup> The EAU 2026 guideline reports vaginal stenosis in 31%, pelvic organ prolapse and an increased risk of preterm labour in women with BEEC, and advises considering elective cesarean delivery.<sup>[[48]](#ref48)</sup>
- **Psychosocial.** 35% have not become sexually active (versus 11% of controls); high education levels but delayed social milestones.<sup>[[21]](#ref21)</sup>

### Hypospadias

> See the dedicated [Hypospadias & Epispadias](/docs/special-populations/05f-lifelong-care/hypospadias-epispadias) page for the full adult and reoperative decision framework. The summary below is the transition-clinic orientation.

Long-term follow-up reveals significant adult implications of childhood hypospadias repair:<sup>[[28]](#ref28)[[29]](#ref29)[[30]](#ref30)</sup>

- **Reintervention rate.** 39.2% of a 193-patient cohort had at least one reintervention; proximal anatomy and repair before 12 months were associated with more interventions.<sup>[[28]](#ref28)</sup> This observational age association does not establish optimal timing or supersede the current EAU usual primary-repair window of 6–18 months.<sup>[[28]](#ref28)[[48]](#ref48)</sup>
- **Suboptimal urinary or sexual outcomes** in 53% of adolescents and young adults at long-term follow-up.<sup>[[28]](#ref28)</sup>
- **Urethral stricture** was found in 47% of adults presenting with complications after childhood repair, versus 11% of those never operated on, and the strictures were longer (5.5 versus 3.0 cm) in a retrospective series of 93 adults.<sup>[[30]](#ref30)</sup>
- **Adult reoperation.** In a retrospective 32-patient series, 90% of the 30 men undergoing urethroplasty had two-stage repair; initial success was 83%, with four recurrent strictures and one fistula at a median 9.5 years.<sup>[[31]](#ref31)</sup> In a separate series of 48 two-stage buccal mucosa graft repairs, eight needed first-stage graft revision and nine developed a fistula requiring closure.<sup>[[32]](#ref32)</sup>
- **LUTS** (weak stream, dribbling, spraying) are twice as common as in controls; paternity rates are lower (24% versus 29%).<sup>[[29]](#ref29)</sup>
- **Adult presenting problems.** The EAU 2026 guideline, citing a systematic review, lists strictures (45–72%), LUTS (50–82%), urethrocutaneous fistula (16–30%), uncorrected hypospadias (14–43%), spraying (24%), UTI (15–25%), ventral curvature (14–24%) and lichen sclerosus (8–43%) among men with urological issues, and advises referral to a urethral reconstructive urologist.<sup>[[48]](#ref48)</sup>

### Undescended Testis / Cryptorchidism

A history of childhood cryptorchidism carries an age-of-orchiopexy-dependent testicular cancer risk that should be part of transition counseling. In a large population-based cohort, the standardized incidence ratio for testicular cancer versus the general population was 2.23 (95% CI 1.58-3.06) after orchiopexy before age 13 and 5.40 (95% CI 3.20-8.53) after orchiopexy at or after age 13; no statistically significant difference was detected among surgery ages below 13.<sup>[[59]](#ref59)</sup> This supports routine testicular self-examination counseling (not oncologic surveillance imaging) for any transitioning patient with a history of orchiopexy, with counseling emphasis proportional to age at surgery.

A separate scenario is the adult or postpubertal adolescent who presents with a still-undescended testis that was never surgically corrected. This patient is at increased testicular cancer risk and should be counseled about the risks and benefits of late orchiopexy; orchiectomy is a reasonable option when the contralateral testis is normal.

In a retrospective six-center cohort of children undergoing scrotal ultrasound, concurrent malignant germ cell tumor was found in 31/1,097 (2.8%) with microlithiasis and 45/36,766 (0.12%) without; these figures do not measure future cancer risk after an incidental finding.<sup>[[60]](#ref60)</sup> The AUA early-stage testicular cancer guideline (2019, amended 2023; Statement 5, Moderate Recommendation, Grade C) advises no further evaluation for isolated microlithiasis without a solid mass or other risk factor. With cryptorchidism or another germ-cell-tumor risk factor, counsel on periodic self-examination and clinical follow-up; the guideline does not mandate routine ultrasound surveillance.<sup>[[63]](#ref63)</sup>

### Disorders / Differences of Sex Development (DSD)

> See the dedicated [Differences of Sex Development](/docs/special-populations/05f-lifelong-care/differences-of-sex-development) page for the adult urological domains: gonadal tumor surveillance and gonadectomy timing, feminizing-genitoplasty revision, testicular adrenal rest tumors, and androgen insensitivity. The summary below is the transition-clinic orientation.

Transition in DSD is particularly complex due to the intersection of medical, psychological, and identity-related needs:<sup>[[33]](#ref33)[[34]](#ref34)[[35]](#ref35)</sup>

- **Hormone replacement.** Lifelong testosterone or estradiol replacement for gonadal deficiency; glucocorticoid / mineralocorticoid replacement in CAH.
- **Gonadal tumor risk.** Assess diagnosis, gonadal differentiation, location and patient preferences. Imaging and tumor markers cannot reliably exclude precursor lesions in retained gonads; specialist discussion of surveillance limitations and gonadectomy is needed.
- **Fertility preservation** should be addressed in adolescence, with close collaboration between endocrinology, urology / gynecology, and reproductive medicine.<sup>[[34]](#ref34)[[36]](#ref36)</sup>
- **Psychosocial support.** A cornerstone of DSD management; loss to follow-up after pediatric care is a major problem, with patients frequently unable to find specialized adult providers.<sup>[[35]](#ref35)</sup>
- **Multidisciplinary teams** are recommended at all ages, including pediatric endocrinology, genetics, psychology, urology, and gynecology.<sup>[[36]](#ref36)[[35]](#ref35)</sup>

### Prune Belly Syndrome

> See the dedicated [Prune Belly Syndrome](/docs/special-populations/05f-lifelong-care/prune-belly-syndrome) page for the full adult workup: renal / transplant outcomes, dynamic bladder-function surveillance, fertility, malignancy, and abdominoplasty. The summary below is the transition-clinic orientation.

The Eagle-Barrett triad comprises abdominal-wall deficiency, urinary-tract dilation and bilateral cryptorchidism. Bladder emptying may change over time: the 56% CIC figure is from one historical 34-patient study, not a universal adult prognosis.<sup>[[65]](#ref65)</sup> Assess residuals, renal status, infections and indicated UDS; PBS alone is not neurogenic LUTD. Small studies suggest possible functional benefit from abdominoplasty and document fertility impairment, but neither benefit nor infertility is inevitable. Congenital renal vulnerability persists alongside potentially modifiable obstruction and infection.<sup>[[2]](#ref2)[[7]](#ref7)</sup>

### Anorectal Malformations (Cloacal Anomalies)

> See the dedicated [Anorectal Malformations](/docs/special-populations/05f-lifelong-care/anorectal-malformations) page for the associated urologic anomalies, the intertwined bowel–bladder reconstruction (ACE plus catheterizable channel), the bowel-management ladder, and the psychiatric burden. The summary below is the transition-clinic orientation.

Urologic anomalies accompany 50–65% of ARMs and rise with malformation complexity; recto-bladder-neck fistulae and cloacal malformations carry the highest renal and bladder morbidity, and associated spinal anomalies (up to 44%) add neurogenic bladder. Adult issues center on renal preservation, neurogenic-bladder management (CIC, augmentation), the deeply intertwined bowel and bladder programs, sexual / fertility counseling, and a high and often underrecognized psychiatric burden. Transition is poorly systematized (most centers lack a protocol), so a structured multidisciplinary handoff with colorectal surgery, gynecology, and nephrology is needed.<sup>[[2]](#ref2)[[7]](#ref7)</sup>

### Survivors of Pediatric Genitourinary Malignancy

Primary management of pediatric genitourinary cancer is outside this atlas's scope, but its reconstructive consequences reach adult care. "Bladder preservation" therapy for pelvic rhabdomyosarcoma (multimodal chemotherapy and radiation with organ-sparing surgery) is associated with significant urinary dysfunction and decreased bladder capacity in the treated survivor. In a single-institution cohort of 26 female five-year survivors, the 23 survivors at analysis had median follow-up of 20.3 years; 23/26 (88%) had at least one grade 3/4 late effect (median 3 grade 3/4 effects per patient), and patients who received radiotherapy had a substantially greater burden of late effects than those who did not (median 9.5 versus 1 late effect per patient).<sup>[[61]](#ref61)</sup> A childhood pelvic-rhabdomyosarcoma survivor presenting in adulthood with an acquired small-capacity, poorly compliant bladder is a plausible candidate for the same augmentation-cystoplasty and bladder-reconstruction ladder used for other congenital and acquired causes of a hostile bladder; sexual dysfunction is also a recognized long-term complication in this population.

## Augmentation Cystoplasty: Lifelong Surveillance

Patients with augmentation cystoplasty (most commonly for neurogenic bladder or exstrophy) require lifelong follow-up for multiple complications:<sup>[[37]](#ref37)[[38]](#ref38)[[39]](#ref39)</sup>

- **Metabolic complications.** Hyperchloremic metabolic acidosis, vitamin B12 deficiency (ileal segments), electrolyte disturbances; annual basic metabolic panel recommended.<sup>[[39]](#ref39)</sup>
- **Urinary tract infections.** Bacteriuria is common; distinguish symptomatic infection from asymptomatic bacteriuria to avoid unnecessary antibiotics.
- **Stone formation.** Related to mucus production, stasis, and metabolic changes.
- **Perforation.** Life-threatening; patients must know warning signs (abdominal pain, fever, peritonitis).
- **Malignancy risk.** The most debated long-term concern:
  - Reported follow-up probabilities of malignancy after augmentation ranged from 0–5.5% across studies; mean latency was 19 years in the systematic review, while a nationwide 16-patient tumor series reported median latency of 20 years.<sup>[[37]](#ref37)[[38]](#ref38)</sup>
  - **Adenocarcinoma** is the most common histologic type (52%), and about half of tumors arise at the entero-urinary anastomosis.<sup>[[37]](#ref37)[[40]](#ref40)</sup>
  - Tumors are often diagnosed at an advanced stage with a poor prognosis (1-year survival 56% in a nationwide series of 16 patients).<sup>[[38]](#ref38)</sup>
  - Whether augmentation itself is an independent risk factor (versus the underlying congenital bladder abnormality) remains debated; one matched cohort found no significant difference in cancer incidence between augmented and non-augmented patients (4.6% versus 2.6%, P = 0.54).<sup>[[41]](#ref41)</sup>
  - **Renal transplant immunosuppression** was associated with bladder malignancy across the augmented and nonaugmented patients with congenital bladder dysfunction (3/20 versus 8/286 without immunosuppression); this is observational evidence, not a causal risk estimate for every patient.<sup>[[41]](#ref41)</sup>
  - The AUA / SUFU guideline recommends (Statement 58; Expert Opinion) annual assessment with focused history, physical exam, basic metabolic panel, and urinary-tract imaging for adult NLUTD patients with bowel-incorporating reconstructions. Applying that framework to non-neurogenic congenital conditions requires individualized judgment.<sup>[[39]](#ref39)</sup>
  - AUA/SUFU recommends against screening or surveillance cystoscopy in NLUTD patients, including chronic indwelling-catheter users (Statements 19 and 20; Strong Recommendation, Evidence Level B).<sup>[[50]](#ref50)</sup> New hematuria, recurrent symptomatic UTI or other concerning changes warrant investigation. In the Higuchi cohort, annual endoscopy/cytology was stopped after five surveillance years, beginning a median 15 years after augmentation; patients were not undergoing annual endoscopy throughout the entire 15-year observation interval<sup>[[42]](#ref42)</sup>. An older three-cancer series advocated starting at postoperative year 10, but this does not establish screening benefit or supersede current NLUTD guidance.<sup>[[39]](#ref39)[[42]](#ref42)[[43]](#ref43)</sup>
  - Urine FISH remains unvalidated for routine augmented-bladder screening. A 36-patient study obtained FISH in 24 and biopsy in 32, with only two cancers. Both were FISH-positive, producing the reported 100% sensitivity; that tiny event count cannot establish dependable sensitivity, survival benefit or a replacement for clinical evaluation.<sup>[[44]](#ref44)</sup>
  - Molecular profiling reveals mutations similar to gastrointestinal adenocarcinomas (TP53, KRAS, MYC), with potentially targetable alterations (ATM, BRCA1, EGFR, ERBB2) in a small selected tumor series. These are exploratory treatment leads for established cancer, not evidence for population screening.<sup>[[45]](#ref45)</sup>

## Models of Care

The AUA Working Group on Genitourinary Congenitalism (2015) emphasized that management of complex congenital urologic disease in adults is difficult due to the lack of long-term data, and recommended consensus-based approaches with providers spanning the life course.<sup>[[27]](#ref27)</sup> Models include:

- **Dedicated transitional urology clinics.** Joint pediatric-adult clinics where patients are seen by both teams simultaneously during the transition period; associated with improved adherence in observational studies.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[13]](#ref13)</sup>
- **Specialist adult congenital urology centers.** Concentrated expertise for rare conditions; European consensus supports lifelong specialist care; comparative superiority has not been established.<sup>[[3]](#ref3)</sup>
- **Multidisciplinary teams.** Urology, nephrology, neurosurgery, physiatry, psychology, social work, and transition coordinators.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[9]](#ref9)</sup>
- **Digital health solutions.** Emerging tools for remote monitoring, patient education, and telehealth follow-up to address geographic disparities.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup>

In one 24-patient referral series, management changed in 17/24, including procedures in 14/24 (seven augmentation/diversion, five Botox and two stone operations). This selected cohort illustrates unmet needs but does not predict the intervention rate for all transition patients.<sup>[[46]](#ref46)</sup>

## Barriers to Successful Transition

| Category | Examples |
|---|---|
| **Patient / family** | Low health literacy, desire for normalcy, reluctance to leave familiar pediatric team, cognitive impairment |
| **Provider** | Limited adult-urologist training in congenital conditions, unfamiliarity with reconstructed anatomy |
| **System** | Lack of transition coordinators, insurance gaps (aging out of pediatric coverage), geographic disparities, no standardized protocols |
| **Psychosocial** | Mental-health comorbidities, social isolation, delayed autonomy, inadequate vocational support |

## Key Recommendations Summary

- **Begin transition planning at age 12–14** using validated readiness-assessment tools; conduct part of each visit without parents present.<sup>[[8]](#ref8)[[9]](#ref9)</sup> The EAU 2026 guideline gives the window as from age 12 and by 16 at the latest.<sup>[[48]](#ref48)</sup>
- **Establish a multidisciplinary transition team** including a dedicated transition coordinator.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[9]](#ref9)</sup>
- **Prepare a comprehensive hand-off packet** with medical / surgical history, current management plans, action plans for common complications, and supply / equipment needs.<sup>[[9]](#ref9)</sup>
- **Transfer during medical and social stability.** Avoid transfer during acute illness, major surgery or psychosocial crisis. For young people with CKD, KDIGO 2024 also advises more frequent assessment than for older adults at the same CKD stage and, with the patient's agreement, caregiver involvement during the first 1–3 years after transfer (Practice Points 5.3.1.1.3 and 5.3.1.2.3).<sup>[[10]](#ref10)</sup>
- **Ensure lifelong surveillance.** Renal function, bladder dynamics, metabolic parameters, and malignancy screening as indicated by the specific condition and surgical history.<sup>[[2]](#ref2)[[3]](#ref3)[[39]](#ref39)</sup>
- **Address sexual health and fertility proactively.** These are consistently identified as unmet needs across all congenital urologic conditions.<sup>[[2]](#ref2)[[47]](#ref47)[[24]](#ref24)</sup>
- **Recognize that adult management frequently differs from childhood.** Bladder dynamics change with growth and aging, new complications emerge, and surgical needs evolve.<sup>[[4]](#ref4)[[46]](#ref46)</sup>
