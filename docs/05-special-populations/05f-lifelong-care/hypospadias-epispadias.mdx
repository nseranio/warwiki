# Hypospadias & Epispadias

**Hypospadias** is a congenital anomaly in which the urethral meatus opens proximally on the ventral surface of the penis, often with ventral curvature, deficient ventral penile skin, and a dorsally hooded foreskin.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[2]](#ref2)</sup> **Epispadias** is a much rarer congenital malformation in which the urethral plate fails to tubularize dorsally, leaving the meatus on the dorsal penis and often placing the patient within the exstrophy-epispadias complex.<sup>[[3]](#ref3)[[4]](#ref4)</sup>

Hypospadias and epispadias persist into adult reconstructive practice. Adults present with uncorrected distal anomalies, failed childhood repair, urethral stricture, urethrocutaneous fistula, chordee, sexual dysfunction, infertility concerns, urinary incontinence, catheterizable channels, bladder augmentation, and renal surveillance needs. The operation rarely amounts to moving the meatus. It involves scarred genital skin, urethral substitution, continence reconstruction, and transition from pediatric to adult lifelong care. This article is filed with [Transitional Urology](/docs/special-populations/05f-lifelong-care/transitional-urology) instead of under voiding & outlet disorders.

---

## Definition and Terminology

| Condition | Core defect | Typical curvature | Reconstructive implication |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Hypospadias** | Ventral ectopic meatus with incomplete ventral urethral development | Ventral chordee | Adult redo surgery often means penile urethral stricture repair, fistula closure, chordee correction, and genital skin substitution |
| **Epispadias** | Dorsal urethral plate defect with dorsal ectopic meatus | Dorsal chordee | Adult care often centers on continence, bladder neck reconstruction, sexual function, and exstrophy-related renal surveillance |
| **Bladder exstrophy-epispadias complex (BEEC / EEC)** | Spectrum of failed lower abdominal wall, bladder, pelvic-ring, and urethral closure | Variable | Requires lifelong surveillance after staged reconstruction, augmentation, bladder neck surgery, or catheterizable channel creation |

Hypospadias and epispadias are usually diagnosed and repaired in childhood, but adult presentations are common in reconstructive practice because childhood repairs can fail late and uncorrected variants may remain functionally acceptable until urinary or sexual expectations change.

## Hypospadias in Adults

### Classification

Hypospadias is usually classified by the final meatal location after release of chordee. This matters because a meatus that appears distal before degloving may become more proximal once tethering is released.

| Type | Meatal location | Approximate frequency | Adult surgical relevance |
|---|---|---:|---|
| **Distal / mild** | Glanular, coronal, subcoronal, distal penile | ~70% in preoperative meatal-location classifications<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup> | May be asymptomatic if uncorrected; late problems include spraying, meatal stenosis, cosmetic concern, or failed childhood repair |
| **Middle** | Mid-penile | ~10% in preoperative meatal-location classifications<sup>[[66]](#ref66)</sup> | More likely to require urethral substitution if scarred from prior surgery |
| **Proximal / severe** | Proximal penile, penoscrotal, scrotal, perineal | ~20% in preoperative meatal-location classifications<sup>[[66]](#ref66)</sup> | Highest risk of chordee, multiple operations, psychosexual concerns, fertility impairment, and complex adult revision |

Pediatric operative notes may also record a **GMS (glans-urethral meatus-shaft) score**, which combines glans and urethral-plate quality, meatal location, and penile curvature assessed intraoperatively after degloving; it was developed for the operating room because office examination, particularly of curvature, is less reliable. Validation studies in children found higher GMS scores associated with more postoperative complications.<sup>[[58]](#ref58)[[59]](#ref59)</sup> The score has not been validated for adult redo hypospadias, but a high childhood GMS score on an old chart is a useful flag for anticipated tissue-quality and complexity on reoperation.

A normal-appearing meatus at birth does not exclude hypospadias: **megameatus with intact prepuce (MIP)**, a distal hypospadias hidden by a complete, unretracted foreskin, has been reported in boys thought to have normal preputial development and is classically found when the foreskin is retracted at circumcision.<sup>[[60]](#ref60)</sup> Discovering MIP does not itself require abandoning circumcision; comparative series found that prior circumcision did not complicate later repair of hypospadias with an intact prepuce, and completing a newborn circumcision after discovering distal hypospadias was not associated with added complications.<sup>[[61]](#ref61)[[62]](#ref62)</sup> Ventral skin deficiency or chordee, by contrast, is a reason to defer circumcision, since preputial skin may be needed for the repair.<sup>[[60]](#ref60)</sup>

The Cleveland Clinic adult presentation framework is useful for reconstructive triage:<sup>[[5]](#ref5)</sup>

| Category | Adult presentation | Frequency in the Cleveland Clinic series |
|---|---|---:|
| **Category I** | Continuous multiple surgeries with significant scarring and tissue loss | 58.2% |
| **Category II** | Delayed complications after initially successful childhood repair | 29.1% |
| **Category III** | No previous repair | 12.7% |

### Epidemiology

Hypospadias is one of the most common congenital anomalies in males, with an estimated incidence of 1 in 200-300 live male births.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup> Reported incidence has increased in some populations over recent decades, although ascertainment, registry methods, environmental exposures, and diagnostic threshold all complicate interpretation.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup> The EAU 2026 paediatric guideline cites a European registry prevalence of 18.6 per 10,000 male births (range 5.1–36.8), with wide regional variation worldwide.<sup>[[48]](#ref48)</sup>

Adults presenting to reconstructive practices are typically in early to middle adulthood. Contemporary adult series report a median age of 37 years in an operative cohort and a mean presentation age of 34.6 years in a complication cohort, often decades after childhood reconstruction.<sup>[[5]](#ref5)[[6]](#ref6)</sup>

### Etiology and Developmental Biology

Hypospadias is multifactorial. Normal urethral development requires coordinated androgen signaling, urethral-fold fusion, glanular urethral canalization, corporal development, and ventral penile skin formation.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[2]](#ref2)</sup>

Important contributors include:

- **Genetic factors.** Familial clustering is recognized; variants in androgen metabolism and genital tubercle development pathways are implicated.
- **Androgen signaling disruption.** Inadequate androgen production, conversion, receptor signaling, or local tissue response can impair urethral-fold fusion.
- **SRD5A2 variants.** One selected 33-patient Chinese series found variants in 15/25 patients with posterior hypospadias; this is not the prevalence across all proximal hypospadias.<sup>[[7]](#ref7)</sup>
- **Environmental factors.** Endocrine disruptors have been proposed, but their contribution to human hypospadias remains debated.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup>
- **Associated anomalies.** Proximal hypospadias with cryptorchidism, bifid scrotum, micropenis, or atypical genitalia should prompt consideration of a disorder/difference of sex development pathway.

### Adult Presentation

Adults fall into two practical groups: uncorrected hypospadias and complications of prior repair.

#### Uncorrected Hypospadias

Many adults with uncorrected distal hypospadias are asymptomatic or well adapted. In a survey of 56 adults with hypospadias, including nine with failed or incomplete childhood repairs, 32% were unaware they had a congenital anomaly, only 5% expressed dissatisfaction with appearance, and 36% reported angulation or spraying, although only 5% sat to void.<sup>[[8]](#ref8)</sup> The EAU 2026 guideline notes conflicting natural-history data and that more than half of the men in the more recent untreated series opted for surgical correction.<sup>[[48]](#ref48)</sup>

That older reassurance should be balanced against newer adult-centered observations: men with uncorrected hypospadias can be more bothered by genital difference, urinary spraying, sexual function, or penile appearance than early surgical series suggested.<sup>[[9]](#ref9)</sup> Adult repair should therefore be driven by symptoms, anatomy, expectations, and tissue quality rather than by meatal position alone.

#### Complications of Childhood Repair

Adults after childhood repair most often present with voiding dysfunction, urethral stricture, fistula, recurrent infection, chordee, or dissatisfaction with genital appearance.<sup>[[5]](#ref5)[[6]](#ref6)</sup>

| Presenting problem | Reported frequency / pattern | Reconstructive concern |
|---|---:|---|
| **Lower urinary tract symptoms** | 49% as a presenting complaint among 93 adults in Hoy; 45/55 (81.8%) with any voiding symptom in Ching<sup>[[5]](#ref5)[[6]](#ref6)</sup> | Often reflects stricture, diverticulum, hair-bearing neourethra, or bladder dysfunction |
| **Spraying / split stream** | Spraying in 24% of 93 adults in Hoy; split stream not separately quantified<sup>[[6]](#ref6)</sup> | Meatal stenosis, glans dehiscence, irregular neomeatus, or distal stricture |
| **Urethrocutaneous fistula** | ~18% in Hoy<sup>[[6]](#ref6)</sup> | Requires stricture exclusion before closure |
| **Recurrent UTI** | 15% of 93 adults overall and 19% of 74 repaired in childhood in Hoy; 20/55 (36.4%) overall in Ching<sup>[[5]](#ref5)[[6]](#ref6)</sup> | Look for obstruction, diverticulum, stones, hair, post-void residual, or catheterizable-channel issues |
| **Chordee** | ~14% overall in Hoy<sup>[[6]](#ref6)</sup> | Requires erection assessment; may need staged straightening before urethral reconstruction |
| **Cosmetic dissatisfaction** | 16% of 19 unrepaired versus 4% of 74 repaired in childhood in Hoy<sup>[[6]](#ref6)</sup> | Must be separated from functional goals and realistic tissue constraints |
| **Urethral stricture** | 11% of 19 unrepaired versus 47% of 74 repaired in childhood in Hoy<sup>[[6]](#ref6)</sup> | Most common adult indication for hypospadias-related urethroplasty |

The EAU 2026 paediatric guideline, citing a systematic review of adults with hypospadias-related urological issues, gives wider ranges: stricture 45–72%, LUTS 50–82%, fistula 16–30%, recurrent UTI 15–25%, ventral curvature 14–24% and lichen sclerosus 8–43%.<sup>[[48]](#ref48)</sup> Case mix and definitions differ between series, so the frequencies above are not interchangeable.

Childhood surgery changes the adult disease phenotype. Compared with unrepaired adults, previously operated patients have higher rates of urethral stricture, recurrent UTI, and LUTS, and their strictures are significantly longer in some series (5.5 cm versus 3.0 cm).<sup>[[6]](#ref6)</sup> Lichen sclerosus / balanitis xerotica obliterans is also over-represented in adults with hypospadias who were not previously repaired in the Cleveland Clinic cohort.<sup>[[5]](#ref5)</sup>

An enlarged prostatic utricle or other Müllerian remnant may present with post-void dribbling, recurrent UTI, or difficult catheterization; in a retrospective series of 30 children aged 9–34 months with posterior hypospadias, 12/30 (40%) had enlarged utricles, with a descriptive increase across more proximal subtypes. All were initially asymptomatic, and 3 of 8 conservatively managed patients with an enlarged utricle became symptomatic after repair.<sup>[[49]](#ref49)</sup>

### Adult Evaluation

Adult evaluation documents the current anatomy and the childhood operative history, which is often unknown. Ask specifically about number of operations, stents/catheters, fistula repairs, chordee repair, skin grafts/flaps, infections, urinary retention, and prior dilation or urethrotomy.

Core assessment:

- **Penile examination.** Meatal location, glans configuration, skin quality, lichen sclerosus, hair-bearing skin, fistulae, scars, prior flap territories, ventral skin shortage, and penile length.
- **Erection-time curvature.** Patient photographs, induced erection, or intracavernosal injection when operative correction is considered.
- **Uroflowmetry and PVR.** Low plateau flow or elevated residual suggests obstruction.
- **RUG / VCUG.** Defines stricture length, diverticulum, fistula, and proximal urethra.
- **Cystourethroscopy.** Evaluates distal meatus, hair, stones, diverticulum, stricture caliber, and proximal urethra.
- **Sonourethrography.** Useful when the extent of spongiofibrosis may exceed the fluoroscopic lumen, especially in post-hypospadias penile strictures.
- **Pelvic / transabdominal ultrasound.** Can identify an enlarged prostatic utricle, seen as a midline teardrop-shaped cystic structure communicating with the urethra, when recurrent UTI or difficult catheterization raises suspicion.<sup>[[49]](#ref49)</sup>
- **Urine culture.** Treat infection before reconstruction.
- **Semen and endocrine assessment.** Appropriate in severe/proximal hypospadias, infertility concern, cryptorchidism history, or pubertal endocrine concerns.<sup>[[10]](#ref10)[[11]](#ref11)</sup>

### Urethral Stricture Management

Urethral stricture is the most common adult surgical indication after hypospadias repair. The 2023 AUA urethral stricture guideline recommends that surgeons offer urethroplasty for penile urethral strictures given the expected high recurrence rate with endoscopic treatment (Moderate Recommendation). The AUA recommendation for simple dilation/meatotomy of an uncomplicated first meatal/fossa stricture excludes previous hypospadias repair, LS and prior failed interventions; these require an individualized reconstructive strategy.<sup>[[12]](#ref12)</sup>

| Approach | Typical role | Reported outcome pattern |
|---|---|---|
| **Two-stage urethroplasty** | Complex redo hypospadias, long penile strictures, poor urethral plate, dense fibrosis, LS, hair-bearing neourethra, multiple prior operations | Commonly preferred; retrospective mixed-approach series report 83% initial success across one- and two-stage urethroplasty (Morrison), failure-free survival of 82% at 1 year and 57% at 10 years across techniques (Verla), and 26/29 (89.7%) free of additional procedures after staged repair, one-stage repair or PU (Horiguchi)<sup>[[13]](#ref13)[[14]](#ref14)[[15]](#ref15)</sup> |
| **One-stage substitution urethroplasty** | Shorter, healthier strictures with usable plate and adequate vascularized coverage | Can be successful in selected patients, but patient selection is decisive<sup>[[14]](#ref14)[[15]](#ref15)</sup> |
| **Buccal mucosa graft** | Preferred graft for staged or substitution repair | In one 32-patient adult reoperative series, skin grafts were associated with more recurrence than buccal mucosa grafts; buccal mucosa avoids a hair-bearing urethral lining<sup>[[13]](#ref13)[[16]](#ref16)</sup> |
| **Perineal urethrostomy** | Elderly patients, severe scarring, multiple failures, patient preference, or desire to avoid multistage penile reconstruction | Durable and rational salvage option, particularly when sexual/cosmetic goals do not justify repeated penile reconstruction<sup>[[16]](#ref16)</sup> |

Operative principles:

- Do not close a fistula until distal obstruction and stricture are excluded.
- Avoid anastomotic excision-and-primary-anastomosis for most penile post-hypospadias strictures; penile shortening, chordee, and recurrence risk are high, with poor outcomes reported in failed-hypospadias cohorts.<sup>[[14]](#ref14)</sup>
- Remove hair-bearing urethra, stones, diverticula, and diseased plate when they drive infection or obstruction. For isolated hairs, endoscopic laser epilation has been described; for more extensive growth, urethrotomy followed by either replacement with nonhair-bearing tissue or attempted depilation (for example, dilute thioglycolate instilled into the neourethra) with close follow-up for regrowth has been reported in a case of recurrent hairball and stone after multiple urethroplasties. Depilation is a described option in select cases, not a substitute for excising diseased hair-bearing segments that are driving infection or obstruction.<sup>[[54]](#ref54)</sup>
- Correct chordee before committing to urethral tubularization.
- Use well-vascularized coverage over suture lines: dartos, tunica vaginalis, local fascia, or staged open graft bed as anatomy allows.
- Counsel that "success" may require several operations and long follow-up.

Some expert-center series find no detected adult–pediatric difference, but this is not proof of equivalent risk. One comparison included only eight adult primary repairs, while the EAU 2026 guideline cites a prospective controlled study in which the complication rate after primary TIP was 2.5 times higher in adults than in children.<sup>[[48]](#ref48)</sup> Reoperation, proximal anatomy and scarring materially affect risk.<sup>[[17]](#ref17)[[18]](#ref18)</sup> The apparent conflict between series reporting adult-childhood equivalence and those reporting a large adult penalty tracks case mix rather than age alone: a 620-patient adult series restricted to distal repair reported a 12.7% complication rate, in the same range commonly reported for pediatric distal TIP,<sup>[[40]](#ref40)</sup> while a 111-patient series of patients older than 14 years, including 22 with proximal hypospadias and 30 with chordee of at least 30°, reported 74.77% one-stage success during follow-up through 12 months.<sup>[[50]](#ref50)</sup> This success endpoint is distinct from the separately reported postoperative complications.<sup>[[50]](#ref50)</sup> Restricting the comparison by meatal location and chordee severity, rather than treating "adult" as a single risk category, reconciles most of the apparent disagreement.

### Master Decision Framework for Adult Repair

Technique selection for adult hypospadias is organized around four anatomical and clinical variables: (1) meatal location (distal versus proximal), (2) presence and severity of chordee, (3) primary versus reoperative status, and (4) tissue quality (urethral plate, genital skin, prior grafts, lichen sclerosus). Adult repair uses related techniques, but pediatric results and pooled adult–child cohorts cannot be assumed to predict an individual adult outcome.<sup>[[17]](#ref17)[[39]](#ref39)[[48]](#ref48)</sup>

#### Distal hypospadias (glanular, coronal, subcoronal)

Primary distal hypospadias with a usable urethral plate and minimal curvature is usually suitable for one-stage repair. Prior repair, curvature, glans anatomy and tissue quality can change that plan.<sup>[[40]](#ref40)[[48]](#ref48)</sup>

*A midline plate incision allows a selected urethral plate to widen before tubularization, with vascularized coverage shown over the neourethra. Incision depth, plate suitability, glans closure and coverage are patient-specific. This is not a universal repair for every meatal position or curvature. (Original WARWIKI schematic; see the figure source record and review limits.)*

| Technique | Role | Outcome pattern |
|---|---|---|
| **Tubularized Incised Plate (TIP / Snodgrass)** | Default single-stage approach | A retrospective adult/adolescent distal-TIP series (age >16; n = 620) reported 12.7% complications; it did not compare TIP with Mathieu<sup>[[40]](#ref40)[[39]](#ref39)</sup> |
| **Mathieu (meatal-based flap)** | Alternative single-stage when urethral plate is thin or narrow | Selection depends on plate, glans and flap anatomy; the cited adult TIP cohort does not establish a comparative stenosis ranking |
| **MAGPI (meatal advancement and glanuloplasty)** | Most distal (glanular) variants only, minimal chordee | Limited adult applicability |
| **Glans approximation procedure (GAP)** | Historically described Thiersch-Duplay plate-tubularization option for a glanular/coronal meatus with a deep glandular groove | Original series was 24 children with no reported meatal or urethral stenosis at 3-18 months; no adult outcome series exists, so adult use rests on extrapolation rather than demonstrated adult efficacy<sup>[[55]](#ref55)</sup> |

#### Proximal hypospadias (penile shaft, penoscrotal, scrotal, perineal)

The most challenging category. In 167 boys with a meatus at or proximal to the penoscrotal junction after degloving, 93/167 (56%) developed a complication over follow-up (median 31.7 months for the cohort); this does not represent all proximal penile repairs or predict adult outcomes.<sup>[[39]](#ref39)[[41]](#ref41)</sup> The decision pivots on chordee severity and urethral plate quality:

- **No or mild chordee, intact urethral plate:** single-stage extended TIP (with or without dorsal inlay graft) or onlay island flap.<sup>[[39]](#ref39)[[42]](#ref42)</sup> The EAU 2026 paediatric guideline calls the use of TIP for proximal hypospadias a matter of debate, advises TIP only when curvature is absent or below 30 degrees, and describes the preputial island onlay as a good option for proximal hypospadias or an unsuitable plate for tubularization.<sup>[[48]](#ref48)</sup>
- **Moderate-to-severe chordee (&gt; 30°) requiring urethral plate transection:** staged repair preferred, with the Bracka two-stage technique as the dominant paradigm:<sup>[[39]](#ref39)[[13]](#ref13)</sup> The EAU 2026 paediatric guideline reports lower pooled complication rates for staged than for one-stage proximal repair (21% versus 42%), and states that two-stage repair is preferred over the past few years because of lower recurrence of ventral curvature.<sup>[[48]](#ref48)</sup>
  - **Stage 1.** Chordee correction, urethral plate transection if needed, placement of a free graft (buccal mucosa or preputial skin) as a neourethral plate on the ventral surface.
  - **Stage 2** (about 6 months later). Tubularization of the grafted plate to create the neourethra.

A tunneled buccal mucosa tube graft has been described after penile-shaft optimization at a prior stage. The 37-patient report is not evidence for avoiding staged repair: 11/34 with more than one year of follow-up had complications, and functional testing was available in only 13/37.<sup>[[43]](#ref43)</sup>

#### Reoperative / failed hypospadias

Adults presenting with stricture, fistula, persistent hypospadias, hair-bearing urethra, or residual chordee after childhood repair represent the most complex subgroup. In a review of a prospective urethroplasty database of 50 adults referred for complications of childhood hypospadias repair, urethral stricture was the most common presenting complication (36 of 50, 72%), followed by urethrocutaneous fistula (12), persistent hypospadias (7), hair in the urethra (6) and severe chordee (7); the series is also cited above for its 76% eventual success rate after additional procedures.<sup>[[44]](#ref44)</sup> Long, complex stricture in this population is associated with worse tissue quality and increased fibrosis from the prior surgery.<sup>[[6]](#ref6)[[44]](#ref44)</sup> A multicenter series of 1,176 patients evaluated for failed hypospadias repair (mean age 31) found that most required either repeat one-stage repair (64.6%) or a staged approach (35.4%), and 88.1% eventually reached a successful outcome only after completion of all secondary procedures; adults reaching a reconstructive urologist for failed hypospadias have typically already had more than one prior operative attempt.<sup>[[52]](#ref52)</sup>

- **Two-stage repair with buccal mucosa graft** is dominant. In the Morrison series, staged repair was used in 90% of men undergoing urethroplasty; 83% initial success pooled one- and two-stage repairs, with median follow-up 9.5 years.<sup>[[13]](#ref13)</sup> Not every staged repair used BMG.<sup>[[13]](#ref13)</sup> A separate 79-patient series included 48 staged and 31 one-stage repairs. Nine staged patients developed fistulae, and two required repeat urethroplasty; six one-stage patients required fistula closure. The abstract labels 9/48 as 16%, an internal inconsistency, so use counts rather than that percentage.<sup>[[18]](#ref18)</sup> One institutional practice places the onlay graft laterally, at either the 3 or 9 o'clock position with fixation sutures to the corpus cavernosum, specifically to avoid a curved neourethra in salvage reconstruction; this is an institution-specific configuration rather than a demonstrated advantage over the more commonly described dorsal onlay placement.<sup>[[57]](#ref57)</sup>
- **Penile skin coverage.** When local penile skin is inadequate, a scrotal fasciocutaneous rotation flap has been described for resurfacing. In one center's experience across 193 complex hypospadias repairs, it was used in 47 cases; the distal flap was judged reliable in 45 of 47, with two distal-flap-end necroses accompanying dehiscence of the distal urethroplasty. Among 43 of the 51 scrotal-skin recipients with longer-term follow-up, 10 (23%) later required flap revision (four for late scarring, four for a dog-ear deformity, and two using tissue expansion for iatrogenic penoscrotal webbing or aesthetic concerns).<sup>[[67]](#ref67)</sup> This is a single-center experience, not comparative effectiveness data for skin coverage choice.<sup>[[56]](#ref56)</sup>
- **One-stage distal urethroplasty** is an option for selected isolated distal complications (single fistula or short stricture). For distal strictures after failed hypospadias repair, a 2026 meta-analysis of 20 heterogeneous studies reported pooled success of 78.1% with one-stage and 76.2% with staged repair, without a statistically significant difference; tissue quality remains central to selection.<sup>[[18]](#ref18)[[44]](#ref44)[[65]](#ref65)</sup>
- **Perineal urethrostomy** is a legitimate definitive option for elderly patients, multiply failed repairs, or those who decline complex reconstruction. The 2023 AUA Urethral Stricture Guideline states that surgeons may offer PU as a long-term alternative to urethroplasty (Conditional Recommendation) and should offer it in populations at high risk of failure of urethral reconstruction (Expert Opinion).<sup>[[12]](#ref12)</sup> In one series, 4/29 (13.8%) adults with hypospadias-related strictures underwent PU; across all reconstructive approaches, 23/25 patient-reported-outcome respondents (92%) were satisfied or very satisfied.<sup>[[15]](#ref15)</sup>
- **Risk rises with each prior operation.** In a pediatric cohort, complications occurred after primary TIP in 135/1085 (12%) and after reoperative TIP in 61/191 (32%), inlay in 16/46 (35%), and two-stage repair in 49/124 (40%). The authors also reported that a single reoperation roughly doubled complication risk versus primary repair, with about 40% risk after three or more reoperations; each prior surgery was associated with 1.5-fold higher odds of another complication (OR 1.51). They proposed reduced vascularity of previously operated tissue as an explanation.<sup>[[51]](#ref51)</sup> This pediatric-cohort escalation curve is the basis for counseling an adult considering yet another reoperation after multiple prior failed repairs.<sup>[[51]](#ref51)</sup>

#### Graft material selection

Buccal mucosa is commonly selected for adult salvage reconstruction. For failed repair with lichen sclerosus or scarred penile skin, the EAU 2026 urethral stricture guideline recommends against penile skin grafts or flaps for urethral substitution (strong recommendation).<sup>[[48]](#ref48)</sup> In a survey of 134 GURS respondents about urethral-stricture repair, 99% preferred buccal mucosa as their primary graft harvest site, and respondents favored BMG over fasciocutaneous flaps for penile urethroplasty regardless of circumcision status; this was not a hypospadias-specific outcome comparison.<sup>[[45]](#ref45)</sup> In the Morrison series, skin grafts were significantly associated with stricture recurrence compared with BMG.<sup>[[13]](#ref13)</sup> A retrospective pediatric comparison used different eras (108 preputial grafts in 2001–2013 versus 112 buccal grafts in 2013–2016), with complications in 31% versus 20%; selection, era and follow-up confounding preclude an adult causal comparison.<sup>[[46]](#ref46)</sup> A randomized trial enrolled 98 adults with extensive anterior strictures and analyzed 92; at its 12-month primary endpoint, success (no voiding LUTS, patent urethra on retrograde urethrography and no further intervention) was 93.2% with penile skin versus 97.9% with BMG (p = 0.346), with median follow-up of 20 months, but excluded LS, fistula, diverticulum and unsalvageable scarred plates.<sup>[[47]](#ref47)</sup> This is not an equivalence trial or justification for penile skin in complex failed hypospadias with diseased skin.<sup>[[47]](#ref47)</sup>

#### Prognostic factors

| Factor | Impact on outcome |
|---|---|
| **Meatal location (proximal)** | Major risk factor. Complications reached 56% in one 167-boy proximal series<sup>[[41]](#ref41)</sup>; the EAU 2026 paediatric guideline gives a range of 14–68% for proximal repairs<sup>[[48]](#ref48)</sup> |
| **Reoperation status** | 33% vs 14% in a mixed adult–pediatric single-surgeon cohort; not adult-only estimates<sup>[[17]](#ref17)</sup>. The EAU 2026 paediatric guideline gives about 23.3% for redo versus 12.2% for primary repairs<sup>[[48]](#ref48)</sup> |
| **Adult vs pediatric age** | Conflicting comparative evidence; procedure, case mix and follow-up matter |
| **Graft type (skin vs BMG)** | Skin grafts associated with higher recurrence in complex cohorts |
| **Surgeon experience** | Complications decrease substantially with case volume |
| **Stricture length** | Not independently associated with recurrence in some series |

#### Long-term outcomes and surveillance

Failure-free survival after urethroplasty for failed-hypospadias-related strictures was 82% at 1 year, declining to 57% at 10 years in the Verla series, underscoring the need for prolonged surveillance.<sup>[[14]](#ref14)</sup> Lower urinary tract symptoms (weak stream, dribbling, spraying) remain twice as common in men who underwent hypospadias repair compared with controls; paternity rates are modestly lower (about 24% versus about 29% in age-matched controls), although semen quality does not differ in isolated hypospadias; and psychosexual outcomes are particularly affected in proximal hypospadias.<sup>[[39]](#ref39)</sup> The case for long-term rather than early-discharge follow-up after the original childhood repair is reinforced by a large pediatric cohort (1,280 patients) with severity-stratified complication rates of 10.7% (distal), 18.8% (mid-shaft) and 53.8% (proximal); only 47% of complications were detected within the first postoperative year, and median time to complication was 69.2 months overall (83.1 months after distal repair, 29.4 months after proximal repair).<sup>[[53]](#ref53)</sup>

#### Summary algorithm

- **Distal, primary, no chordee:** single-stage TIP (or Mathieu)
- **Proximal, no / mild chordee, good plate:** single-stage extended TIP with or without dorsal inlay graft, or onlay island flap
- **Proximal, moderate-to-severe chordee:** correct curvature first and divide the plate if needed; consider staged repair with healthy preputial skin or buccal mucosa according to tissue quality and prior surgery.<sup>[[46]](#ref46)[[48]](#ref48)</sup>
- **Reoperative / complex:** refer failed repair to a specialist center (EAU weak recommendation). Use staged buccal mucosa graft urethroplasty when the plate or surrounding tissues are inadequate; selected isolated distal complications may be repaired in one stage.<sup>[[48]](#ref48)</sup>
- **Multiply failed / elderly / patient preference:** perineal urethrostomy as definitive management<sup>[[12]](#ref12)</sup>

### Sexual Function and Psychosexual Outcomes

Adult outcomes are heterogeneous because severity, number of operations, chordee, penile size, cosmesis, and partner experience all vary. Reported erectile dysfunction rates range widely, and sexual dissatisfaction is more common in proximal or multiply operated patients.<sup>[[19]](#ref19)[[20]](#ref20)[[21]](#ref21)[[22]](#ref22)</sup>

| Domain | Pattern |
|---|---|
| **Erections** | Most men can have penetrative sex, but ED rates vary by series and severity |
| **Ejaculation** | Spraying, dribbling, or altered force can be bothersome, and ejaculatory problems are not always a minority concern: a low-response-rate cohort of 119 respondents from a 1978-1993 childhood-repair program found premature ejaculation in 83.2% overall and mild erectile dysfunction in 8.9% of the glanular, 50% of the distal and 72.2% of the proximal subgroup, so ask about these directly rather than assuming they are uncommon<sup>[[22]](#ref22)</sup> |
| **Penile length / appearance** | Proximal hypospadias is associated with greater concern about penile length and appearance |
| **Physical contact** | Some adolescents and adults with proximal hypospadias report uncertainty or avoidance around genital contact |
| **Satisfaction with repair** | Many men are satisfied with childhood repair, especially after uncomplicated surgery; multiple surgeries predict poorer psychosexual outcomes |

The practical adult consultation should address urinary goals, sexual goals, appearance goals, and the patient's own language for distress. Reconstructive options can improve obstruction and fistula, but they may not normalize penile size, glans shape, sensation, or lifelong body image.

### Fertility and Endocrine Outcomes

Hypospadias can be part of a broader testicular dysgenesis phenotype. In a young adult cohort of nonsyndromic hypospadias, oligozoospermia or azoospermia occurred in 32/172 men who provided semen (18.6%), with higher rates in complex hypospadias and in men born small for gestational age.<sup>[[10]](#ref10)</sup> Population-level linkage studies also show reduced paternity among men born with genital anomalies, including hypospadias.<sup>[[23]](#ref23)</sup>

Mechanisms may include impaired spermatogenesis, cryptorchidism, altered Leydig cell function, ejaculatory dysfunction, genital self-image, or reduced sexual opportunity. Young adults with severe hypospadias, cryptorchidism, or infertility concern should be offered semen analysis and endocrine evaluation rather than reassurance based only on successful childhood voiding reconstruction.<sup>[[10]](#ref10)[[11]](#ref11)</sup>

## Epispadias in Adults

> See the dedicated [Bladder Exstrophy-Epispadias Complex](/docs/special-populations/05f-lifelong-care/bladder-exstrophy-epispadias) page for continence reconstruction, diversion, malignancy surveillance, pregnancy, and lifelong follow-up.

### Classification and Spectrum

Epispadias exists on a spectrum within the exstrophy-epispadias complex.<sup>[[3]](#ref3)[[4]](#ref4)[[24]](#ref24)</sup>

| Condition | Severity | Approximate prevalence | Adult reconstructive burden |
|---|---|---:|---|
| **Isolated epispadias** | Mildest | ~1 in 117,000 | Dorsal urethral reconstruction, chordee correction, and continence assessment |
| **Classic bladder exstrophy** | Intermediate | ~1 in 30,000 | Bladder closure, epispadias repair, bladder neck reconstruction, augmentation, catheterizable channel, renal surveillance |
| **Cloacal exstrophy** | Most severe | ~1 in 200,000 | Multisystem reconstruction involving GU, GI, abdominal wall, pelvic ring, and continence pathways |

EAU 2026 estimates 1 in 30,000–50,000 live births for epispadias plus classic bladder exstrophy, with male predominance.<sup>[[70]](#ref70)</sup> The isolated-epispadias estimate above is male-specific; the subtype frequencies are approximate historical estimates that vary by source and ascertainment.<sup>[[4]](#ref4)[[72]](#ref72)</sup>

Male epispadias is classified by the dorsal meatal location:<sup>[[3]](#ref3)[[25]](#ref25)</sup>

- **Glanular epispadias.** Meatus on the dorsal glans; mildest.
- **Penile epispadias.** Meatus on the dorsal penile shaft.
- **Penopubic / complete epispadias.** Entire dorsal urethra open to the pubic symphysis; highest continence burden.

Female epispadias is characterized by a bifid clitoris and patulous urethra; incontinence is common when the bladder neck and sphincteric mechanism are involved.<sup>[[4]](#ref4)</sup>

Concealed epispadias is a rare variant with intact prepuce, later diagnosis, and generally milder anatomy than complete penopubic epispadias.<sup>[[26]](#ref26)</sup>

### Clinical Features

| Feature | Epispadias pattern | Adult implication |
|---|---|---|
| **Meatus** | Dorsal ectopic opening | Determines reconstruction type and continence risk |
| **Curvature** | Dorsal chordee | Can impair penetrative intercourse and require straightening |
| **Penile morphology** | Short, broad penis with dorsal urethral plate defect | Cosmetic and sexual goals should be discussed explicitly |
| **Pelvis** | Pubic diastasis in exstrophy spectrum | Affects continence mechanics and pelvic floor support |
| **Bladder neck** | Underdeveloped or surgically reconstructed | Drives continence, catheterization, and outlet obstruction risk |
| **Female genitalia** | Bifid clitoris and patulous urethra | Continence and sexual pain/function are central adult concerns |

Incontinence severity correlates with the degree of dorsal meatal displacement. Glanular and some penile epispadias patients may be continent or minimally incontinent, whereas penopubic epispadias has the highest risk because the sphincteric outlet is underdeveloped.<sup>[[25]](#ref25)[[26]](#ref26)[[27]](#ref27)</sup>

### Adult Evaluation

Adult epispadias and exstrophy evaluation should not stop at the genital exam. The reconstructive surgeon should establish the patient's complete urinary drainage anatomy, renal status, continence pathway, sexual function, and prior operations.

Core assessment:

- **Operative history.** Bladder closure, pelvic osteotomy, epispadias repair, bladder neck reconstruction, augmentation cystoplasty, bladder neck closure, continent catheterizable channel, ureteral reimplant, stones, revisions.
- **Voiding / catheterization pattern.** Urethral voiding, clean intermittent catheterization, Mitrofanoff/Monti use, stomal leakage, channel stenosis, recurrent UTI, mucus, stones.
- **Continence phenotype.** Daytime, nocturnal, stress-related, urgency-related, overflow, or stomal leakage.
- **Renal surveillance.** Serum creatinine/eGFR, renal ultrasound, hydronephrosis, recurrent pyelonephritis, stones.
- **Malignancy risk.** Document retained bladder and diversion anatomy, and assess new urinary symptoms in adults with bladder exstrophy; the EAU 2026 guideline notes increased, earlier-onset cancer risk in BEEC and no standardized surveillance protocol.<sup>[[71]](#ref71)</sup>
- **Urodynamics.** Capacity, compliance, detrusor overactivity, outlet resistance, leak point, and safety of storage pressures.
- **Sexual and fertility history.** Erections, curvature, ejaculation, vaginal/introitus pain, pregnancy goals, assisted reproduction history.

### Long-Term Urological Outcomes

Adult EEC outcomes are diverse because childhood surgical strategies vary. Continence may be achieved through urethral voiding, bladder neck reconstruction, augmentation, or bladder neck closure with a catheterizable channel.

| Reconstruction pathway | Continence pattern |
|---|---|
| **Bladder neck reconstruction alone** | In a mixed-age classic-exstrophy cohort, 91/142 (64%) assessed after isolated BNR were continent at last follow-up (dry for at least 3 hours without nighttime leakage); median follow-up from the first continence procedure was 7.2 years for the full cohort.<sup>[[28]](#ref28)</sup> Catheterization and later augmentation needs vary.<sup>[[27]](#ref27)[[28]](#ref28)</sup> |
| **BNR + augmentation cystoplasty** | Used when capacity/compliance is inadequate; increases need for mucus/stone/channel surveillance<sup>[[28]](#ref28)</sup> |
| **Bladder neck closure + continent catheterizable stoma** | In a mixed-age classic-exstrophy cohort, 124/133 (93%) assessed after this pathway were continent at last follow-up (dry for at least 3 hours without nighttime leakage; whole-cohort median follow-up 7.2 years).<sup>[[28]](#ref28)</sup> The pathway entails lifelong catheterization and reservoir management.<sup>[[29]](#ref29)</sup> |
| **Primary isolated glanular/penile epispadias** | In a pediatric cohort, 13/13 glanular/penile cases were daytime-continent at follow-up (median age 8 years for the whole cohort); 38% of boys across all epispadias types received pelvic-floor biofeedback.<sup>[[30]](#ref30)</sup> |

In contemporary adult classic bladder exstrophy cohorts, only a minority void spontaneously per urethra without catheterization, and many adults perform clean intermittent self-catheterization.<sup>[[29]](#ref29)[[31]](#ref31)</sup> Continence can continue to improve during puberty; in one series, 16 of 35 patients with volitional voiding who were incontinent at age 10 (46%) later became fully continent without major surgery or CIC, although three received Deflux injections.<sup>[[32]](#ref32)</sup>

Lower urinary tract symptoms are common and often underrecognized without questionnaires. In an adult EEC survey, moderate or severe LUTS was reported by 12/15 men (80%) and 6/9 women (67%) with evaluable symptom scores; both storage and voiding symptoms were common.<sup>[[33]](#ref33)</sup>

Renal surveillance is essential. In a 23-patient tertiary adult referral series, 10/23 had CKD stage II or higher and 7/23 had hydronephrosis; 8/14 with urodynamic data had poor or intermediate compliance.<sup>[[34]](#ref34)</sup>

### Sexual Function and Fertility

Adults with complete male epispadias can often achieve satisfactory sexual intercourse, but abnormal ejaculation, diminished sensation, erectile-maintenance difficulty, penile curvature, and body-image concerns are common enough to require direct questioning.<sup>[[35]](#ref35)[[36]](#ref36)</sup>

| Domain | Reported pattern |
|---|---|
| **Satisfactory intercourse** | 12/15 (80%) in one survey of complete male epispadias; only 15 of 74 eligible men responded<sup>[[35]](#ref35)</sup> |
| **Sexual activity** | High among pubertal/post-pubertal men; lower but substantial among women in EEC cohorts |
| **Ejaculation** | Abnormal ejaculation reported by 8/15 (53%) responders in the same survey<sup>[[35]](#ref35)</sup> |
| **Erectile function** | Some men report diminished sensation or difficulty maintaining erections |
| **Fertility** | A meaningful proportion of men father children; some require assisted reproduction |
| **Women with EEC** | Fertility is achievable; pregnancy requires coordinated adult reconstructive, obstetric, and pelvic floor care |

Adult sexual and fertility outcomes require individualized assessment; the cited review addresses male congenital-urology populations and offers no primary evidence for female sexual-pain or general-population QoL equivalence.<sup>[[37]](#ref37)</sup>

### Surgical Management in Adults

Epispadias repair aims to place the meatus in an anatomic position, correct dorsal curvature, reconstruct functional genitalia, and preserve or improve continence. The two classic male epispadias repair families are the modified Cantwell-Ransley and Mitchell-Bagli approaches.<sup>[[3]](#ref3)</sup>

Adult operations are usually individualized salvage or completion procedures:

- **Residual dorsal chordee correction.** Straightening before urethral or sexual reconstruction.
- **Redo urethral reconstruction.** Repair of dehiscence, stenosis, fistula, or unsatisfactory meatal position.
- **Bladder neck reconstruction.** When continence can reasonably be achieved through the urethra.
- **Augmentation cystoplasty.** When capacity or compliance is unsafe or inadequate.
- **Bladder neck closure with continent catheterizable channel.** When outlet continence cannot be salvaged or urethral voiding is not realistic.
- **Channel revision.** Stenosis, false passage, leakage, stones, or catheterization difficulty.
- **Introitoplasty / genital reconstruction.** Selected women with obstructive introitus, sexual pain, or genital functional concerns.

A combined procedure can reduce the number of planned operations in selected children; in a retrospective series of 19 boys (mean age 5.2 years), 13/19 (69%) were completely continent and three later required augmentation plus continent diversion for inadequate bladder capacity.<sup>[[38]](#ref38)</sup>

## Hypospadias vs Epispadias

| Feature | Hypospadias | Epispadias |
|---|---|---|
| **Urethral opening** | Ventral / undersurface | Dorsal / upper surface |
| **Penile curvature** | Ventral chordee | Dorsal chordee |
| **Foreskin / skin pattern** | Dorsally hooded foreskin, ventral skin deficiency | Dorsal urethral/skin defect, often with ventral preputial hooding; concealed variants exist |
| **Incidence** | Common: ~1 in 200-300 live male births | Rare: isolated epispadias ~1 in 117,000 |
| **Incontinence** | Rare unless obstruction/repair complication | Common in penopubic epispadias and exstrophy spectrum |
| **Associated anomalies** | Cryptorchidism, DSD concern in severe proximal cases | Bladder exstrophy, pubic diastasis, pelvic-floor and bladder-neck abnormalities |
| **Adult reconstructive problem** | Stricture, fistula, chordee, hair-bearing neourethra, sexual/fertility concerns | Incontinence, catheterizable-channel issues, renal dysfunction, sexual/fertility concerns |

## Related Congenital Urethral Anomalies in the Adult

Two other congenital urethral anomalies can present in adulthood alongside, or instead of, hypospadias and epispadias. Primary repair is pediatric and is not reproduced here; the points below are what the adult reconstructive surgeon needs to recognize and act on.

**Urethral duplication.** A duplicated urethra can be sagittal (a dorsal and a ventral channel, as in Y-type duplication with a perineal or anal-adjacent opening) or, less often, side-by-side. Classification separates a blind-ending channel, a patent channel with its own bladder origin or branching off the primary urethra, and duplication with one external meatus.<sup>[[63]](#ref63)</sup> An adult can present with stream splitting, recurrent UTI, or an accessory perineal or para-anal opening traced to this anomaly rather than to a fistula. The key management point carried from childhood practice is that in typical Y-type duplication the ventral channel may be the only functional urethra, although variants exist; both channels and the functional route should be identified (VCUG, with endoscopy as needed) before either channel is closed or revised to preserve voiding and continence.<sup>[[63]](#ref63)[[68]](#ref68)[[69]](#ref69)</sup>

**Megalourethra.** This dilated-urethra anomaly is scaphoid (ventral corpus spongiosum deficient or absent) or fusiform (more extensive loss of erectile and spongy tissue, often with prune belly syndrome). An adult with a history of scaphoid megalourethra repair has typically had the redundant ventral urethral wall excised and the urethra retubularized over the dorsal wall, which can leave a residual caliber or cosmetic difference to assess if LUTS or sexual concerns arise; fusiform disease carries a greater chance of significant corporal tissue deficiency, which can require corporal reconstruction or a penile prosthesis later in life rather than a simple urethral revision.<sup>[[64]](#ref64)</sup> As with other prune-belly-associated anomalies, renal and urinary-tract surveillance from childhood should be continued into adult follow-up.

## Key Recommendations for Adult Management

### Hypospadias

- Follow patients with significant childhood hypospadias repair into adulthood; late stricture, fistula, chordee, LUTS, and sexual concerns may present decades later.<sup>[[6]](#ref6)</sup>
- Manage post-hypospadias penile strictures with reconstruction; dilation and urethrotomy have a high recurrence rate.<sup>[[12]](#ref12)</sup>
- Prefer buccal mucosa grafting and staged reconstruction when tissue quality, stricture length, lichen sclerosus, hair, diverticulum, or multiple prior operations make one-stage repair unsafe.<sup>[[13]](#ref13)[[14]](#ref14)[[16]](#ref16)</sup>
- Discuss perineal urethrostomy early in patients with severe scarring, multiple failures, older age, or preference to avoid prolonged multistage penile reconstruction.
- Offer semen analysis and endocrine evaluation for severe/proximal hypospadias, cryptorchidism history, infertility concern, or pubertal endocrine abnormalities.<sup>[[10]](#ref10)[[11]](#ref11)</sup>

### Epispadias / Exstrophy

- Transition to adult urology should include urinary, sexual, fertility, renal, catheterization, and psychosocial review, not just a genital examination.
- Monitor renal function and upper tracts long term; CKD, hydronephrosis, stones, and poor compliance are common adult issues.<sup>[[34]](#ref34)</sup>
- Define the continence pathway anatomically: urethral voiding, BNR, augmentation, bladder neck closure, catheterizable channel, or urinary diversion.
- Use urodynamics liberally when continence, upper-tract dilation, recurrent UTI, catheterization difficulty, or augmentation safety is unclear.
- Ask directly about sexual function and fertility; many adults have functional sexual lives, but ejaculation, sensation, curvature, pain, and reproductive assistance may matter more than spontaneous reporting suggests.<sup>[[35]](#ref35)[[36]](#ref36)</sup>
