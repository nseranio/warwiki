# Differences of Sex Development

**Differences (formerly "disorders") of sex development (DSD)** are congenital conditions in which chromosomal sex, gonadal differentiation, or genital anatomy develops atypically.<sup>[[2]](#ref2)</sup> Many patients are identified in infancy, but a substantial proportion first present in adolescence, with delayed puberty, primary amenorrhea, virilization, or questions about gender identity and fertility, precisely as care must move from pediatric to adult services.<sup>[[4]](#ref4)</sup> DSD transitional care is now counted among urology's highest-priority research topics, even as its evidence base remains thin.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup>

For the general transition framework (readiness tools, hand-off, barriers) see [Transitional Urology](/docs/special-populations/05f-lifelong-care/transitional-urology); for reoperative hypospadias technique see [Hypospadias & Epispadias](/docs/special-populations/05f-lifelong-care/hypospadias-epispadias); for the lower-urinary-tract management ladder see [Neurogenic Bladder](/docs/clinical-conditions/03d-nlutd/neurogenic-bladder). DSD is distinct from gender-affirming care, though there is conceptual overlap with [GAS](/docs/special-populations/05c-gender-affirming/overview).

## Classification (Orientation)

The 2006 Chicago Consensus introduced the "DSD" umbrella term, as a medical classification; patient preferences for condition-specific or intersex terminology vary. It organized the field by karyotype:<sup>[[2]](#ref2)[[3]](#ref3)</sup>

- **46,XX DSD**, e.g., congenital adrenal hyperplasia (CAH), ovotesticular DSD
- **46,XY DSD**, e.g., androgen insensitivity syndrome (AIS), 5α-reductase deficiency, gonadal dysgenesis, persistent Müllerian duct syndrome (PMDS). PMDS is usually discovered incidentally at inguinal hernia repair or orchiopexy in an otherwise normally virilized male, when a uterus and fallopian tubes are found to have persisted because of failed anti-Müllerian hormone signaling; adult management centers on completing orchiopexy and, when reoperative surgery is needed, preserving rather than routinely excising the retained uterus (dividing it if necessary to protect the vas deferens and gonadal vessels), since the older concern about cyclic hematuria or uterine malignancy from a retained uterus is not well supported. Long-term testicular malignancy, attributed to the associated cryptorchidism, was reported in 33% of a personal series of 157 cases.<sup>[[35]](#ref35)</sup>
- **Sex-chromosome DSD**, e.g., Turner (45,X), Klinefelter (47,XXY), mixed gonadal dysgenesis (45,X/46,XY). Klinefelter syndrome is often diagnosed only in adulthood, when small testes or hypogonadism are evaluated. The European Academy of Andrology guideline recommends testosterone replacement when serum testosterone is low-normal, for bone health and hypogonadal symptoms, and highlights an increased adult burden of cardiovascular and metabolic disease (venous thromboembolism, peripheral vascular disease, diastolic dysfunction, dyslipidemia, diabetes) and of certain malignancies (extragonadal mediastinal germ cell tumors, breast cancer, non-Hodgkin lymphoma), which shape lifelong surveillance separately from the gonadal-tumor surveillance discussed below for other DSD conditions.<sup>[[34]](#ref34)</sup>

Pediatric DSD care is well-organized around multidisciplinary teams (endocrinology, urology/surgery, genetics, psychology); adult services are far less developed, so DSD patients are at high risk of loss to follow-up, compromising gonadal surveillance, hormonal management, and psychosocial well-being.<sup>[[4]](#ref4)[[5]](#ref5)[[6]](#ref6)[[7]](#ref7)</sup> The urologist's adult role concentrates in a few DSD-specific domains below.

## Gonadal Tumor Surveillance and Gonadectomy Timing

Several conditions, particularly those with Y-chromosome material and dysgenetic gonads, carry an elevated risk of gonadal germ-cell tumors (gonadoblastoma, dysgerminoma/seminoma), while risk is low in others (e.g., complete AIS in situ before adulthood).<sup>[[8]](#ref8)[[9]](#ref9)[[10]](#ref10)</sup>

- The choice between prophylactic gonadectomy and gonadal retention with surveillance is risk-stratified by diagnosis, gonadal location, and histology, and, where risk permits, is increasingly deferred so the patient can participate in shared decision-making.<sup>[[8]](#ref8)[[9]](#ref9)</sup>
- **Retained gonads** require an individualized specialist plan. Self-examination and imaging may help assess accessible gonads, but neither imaging nor conventional tumor markers reliably excludes precursor lesions or establishes safe surveillance. High-risk dysgenetic gonads with Y material warrant explicit discussion of gonadectomy; retention is not made risk-free by normal tests.<sup>[[8]](#ref8)[[9]](#ref9)[[10]](#ref10)</sup>
- Mixed gonadal dysgenesis (45,X/46,XY) is the prototype of the surveillance-versus-removal dilemma and benefits from explicit, individualized counseling.<sup>[[10]](#ref10)</sup>
- **Turner syndrome with occult Y-chromosome material.** A minority of patients with Turner syndrome carry a small 46,XY mosaic cell line that a standard karyotype can miss; FISH or PCR for Y-chromosome material is used when this is a concern. Bilateral gonadectomy at diagnosis was the historical default once Y material was confirmed, but the 2024 international Turner syndrome clinical practice guideline moved toward individualized, shared decision-making about gonadectomy versus surveillance rather than routine removal.<sup>[[32]](#ref32)</sup> A multicenter cohort of girls with Turner syndrome and Y-chromosome material, in whom gonads were assumed nonfunctional, found spontaneous thelarche in 42% and spontaneous menarche in 11%, alongside a gonadoblastoma rate (about 19% of gonadectomy specimens) similar to earlier reports — evidence that some of these gonads retain function, which is now part of the shared decision-making conversation rather than an automatic indication to remove them.<sup>[[33]](#ref33)</sup>
- The EAU 2026 paediatric guideline reports neoplastic lesions in 12% of participants in the DSD-LIFE study, and 36% in 46,XY gonadal dysgenesis. Its adapted risk table (after Looijenga) places gonadal dysgenesis with Y material and an abdominal gonad, PAIS with a nonscrotal gonad, Frasier syndrome and Denys-Drash syndrome with Y in the high-risk group (15% to 60%), against about 2% for complete AIS, 3% for ovotesticular DSD and 0% for 5α-reductase deficiency. It notes that malignancy in complete AIS appeared only after the second decade, which allows gonadectomy to be deferred until after puberty. Intra-abdominal gonads should be brought down or fixed to the abdominal wall so they can be monitored, and high-risk gonads that cannot be brought down, or streak gonads, should be removed after interdisciplinary risk-benefit review. It recommends specialized immunohistochemistry (PLAP, OCT3/4) on biopsies and states that serum miRNA markers are not yet in clinical use. It gives a strong recommendation to tell patients and caregivers that a Y chromosome in dysgenetic gonads increases malignancy risk.<sup>[[31]](#ref31)</sup>

## Timing of Genital Surgery

The timing of DSD genital surgery is among the most debated questions in the field. Current consensus emphasizes shared decision-making and, where feasible, deferring irreversible procedures until the individual can participate, which is directly relevant at transition, when adolescents may be making autonomous decisions about genitoplasty, gonadectomy, or fertility preservation for the first time.<sup>[[2]](#ref2)[[4]](#ref4)</sup> The EAU 2026 paediatric guideline states that surgery that alters appearance is not urgent and that the evidence on outcomes is sparse, with no randomized trials. It gives a strong recommendation for a multidisciplinary shared-decision model covering gender assignment, genital surgery (in accordance with national regulations) and gonadectomy, and records that the European Society for Paediatric Urology disagreed with a Council of Europe proposal to restrict surgery to emergencies.<sup>[[31]](#ref31)</sup> This is a marked shift from older published positions that favored early infant or childhood surgery on grounds such as parental concern, presumed lack of later memory, and easier tissue handling; those positions were not supported by comparative timing outcomes and should not be read as current practice.<sup>[[41]](#ref41)</sup>

A systematic review and meta-analysis of 29 observational studies (1,178 females with CAH who underwent genital reconstruction; mean age at surgery 2.7 years, mean follow-up 10.3 years) gives outcome context for counseling, though it did not compare early with later surgery: 88.7% of patients identified as female, mean Female Sexual Function Index score was 25.13/36, 48% reported comfortable intercourse, and vaginal stenosis was reported in 27%. Outcomes were not reported by every included study, and the review's authors rated the overall certainty of evidence as very low.<sup>[[36]](#ref36)</sup>

## Revision After Feminizing Genitoplasty

**Vaginal stenosis** is a recognized indication for revision in adults who underwent childhood feminizing genitoplasty (most often for a urogenital sinus, as in CAH). In a single-center series, 13 patients presenting with vaginal stenosis (median age 19) had prior vaginoplasty at a median age of 15 months (range birth–233 months) and presented with inability to have intercourse from stenotic tissue.<sup>[[11]](#ref11)</sup> Which primary repair a given adult patient had depends less on the length of the common urogenital-sinus channel than on where it joins relative to the bladder neck: a low or mid-level confluence is typically managed with a posterior sinus or vaginal opening and a perineal flap, while a high confluence requires separating the vagina from the urinary tract with a pull-through and reconstructing the sinus itself as urethra.<sup>[[41]](#ref41)</sup> Approach by severity:<sup>[[11]](#ref11)[[12]](#ref12)</sup>

- **Distal stenosis.** Partial urogenital mobilization with perineal or lateral flaps was the commonest approach in the small series (10/13 patients). The whole cohort had physiological vaginal length and width in 12/13 at median follow-up of 11 months, which is not a generalizable long-term success rate.
- **Complete cicatrization / high stenosis.** Bowel vaginoplasty is reserved for these severe cases. Buccal-mucosa vulvovaginoplasty or neovaginal construction has also been reported as a substitution option when scarring leaves insufficient local tissue, with reports of short-term success; denominators and follow-up are not well established for this option.<sup>[[40]](#ref40)</sup>
- **High vaginal-urogenital-sinus confluence (more than 3 cm from the perineum).** Pull-through vaginoplasty has been described, distinguishing total urogenital mobilization (TUM), which frees the sinus circumferentially and anteriorly off the pubourethral ligament, from a partial mobilization that preserves that most anterior fixation to avoid a possible continence cost from dissecting the future urethra off the ligament. TUM itself has been described as reducing operative time by roughly 70% versus separate vaginal and urethral dissection in a small cloacal-repair series, without a proven continence advantage for either approach in this context.<sup>[[39]](#ref39)</sup> A posterior sagittal approach and the anterior sagittal trans-ano-rectal approach (ASTRA) have both been described as exposures for complex primary or revision high-urogenital-sinus repair; preliminary ASTRA reports describe no fistula, continence problems, or stricture, but without a stated denominator and with the authors cautioning that longer follow-up is needed to assess stenosis.<sup>[[40]](#ref40)</sup>
- **Primary vaginoplasty deferred to adulthood.** Total urogenital mobilization with perineal flap or modified McIndoe has been reported to give good outcomes.

The Endocrine Society CAH guideline notes that urinary incontinence is rare long-term, a minority need additional vaginal surgery after puberty, and premenarchal dilation should be avoided; persistent complications include introital scarring, urethrovaginal fistula, and clitoral pain or loss of sensation.<sup>[[13]](#ref13)</sup> Dissatisfaction with surgical function and clitoral arousal is high (about 47% each) in partially androgenized 46,XY women after feminizing surgery.<sup>[[14]](#ref14)</sup>

## Masculinizing Genitoplasty and Hypospadias Sequelae

Adults raised male after masculinizing DSD surgery (or PAIS, proximal hypospadias) present with the familiar reconstructive sequelae: urethral stricture, fistula, hair-bearing or persistent hypospadias, and chordee. The reoperative technique (staged buccal-mucosa urethroplasty, outcomes, predictors) is covered on the [Hypospadias & Epispadias](/docs/special-populations/05f-lifelong-care/hypospadias-epispadias) page and the [urethroplasty atlas](/docs/surgical-techniques/urethral-reconstruction) and is not reproduced here. The DSD-specific point is the patient-reported burden: physician-rated anatomical appearance is poor in about 11%, and about 38% report dissatisfaction with anatomical appearance after masculinizing/hypospadias surgery; appearance and function should be reassessed at transition.<sup>[[15]](#ref15)</sup> Reoperation risk is a related concern. A single-center review of proximal hypospadias repair found unplanned reoperation in 24/30 patients (80%) with an associated DSD diagnosis versus 17/37 (45.9%) without one (p = 0.024), with the difference concentrated in the first 24 postoperative months; older age at initial repair and a staged (versus single-stage) repair were independently associated with reoperation. A separate NIH-sponsored multicenter prospective cohort of young children with DSD-associated moderate-to-severe genital atypia who underwent masculinizing genitoplasty reported complications, mainly glans dehiscence and urethrocutaneous fistula, in 12 of 31 patients (41%) at 12 months. These studies differ in design and population; neither should be read as proof that a DSD diagnosis is an independent risk modifier distinct from the anatomic severity that often accompanies it.<sup>[[37]](#ref37)[[38]](#ref38)</sup>

## Testicular Adrenal Rest Tumors (TART) in CAH

TART is the defining benign testicular pathology in adult males with classic CAH, with prevalence of 30–50%, and is benign, but can compress seminiferous tubules and causes pain, hypogonadism, and infertility.<sup>[[16]](#ref16)[[18]](#ref18)</sup>

- **Screening.** Periodic testicular ultrasound from adolescence, repeated every 1–2 years; high-frequency ultrasound is as sensitive as MRI.<sup>[[13]](#ref13)[[17]](#ref17)</sup>
- **Medical management.** Intensifying or optimizing glucocorticoid therapy to suppress ACTH may shrink TART and improve spermatogenesis; long-standing fibrotic lesions become unresponsive.<sup>[[16]](#ref16)[[18]](#ref18)</sup>
- **Surgery is generally avoided.** Testis-sparing surgery has not improved gonadal function and is reserved for size-related pain.<sup>[[16]](#ref16)</sup>
- **Crinecerfont** (CRF1 antagonist, FDA-approved December 2024) is adjunctive to glucocorticoid replacement for androgen control in classic CAH. The adult phase III trial demonstrated glucocorticoid dose reduction while maintaining androgen control; it does not establish TART regression or fertility benefit. Replacement and stress-dose glucocorticoids remain necessary to prevent adrenal crisis.<sup>[[19]](#ref19)[[30]](#ref30)</sup>
- **Fertility.** Offer semen analysis and cryopreservation early, before TART-related tubular damage becomes irreversible.<sup>[[18]](#ref18)</sup>

## Androgen Insensitivity Syndrome — Adult Benign Issues

For adults with complete AIS (CAIS):<sup>[[20]](#ref20)[[21]](#ref21)</sup>

- **Vaginal hypoplasia.** Vaginal dilation is first-line to achieve functional depth and avoid dyspareunia; surgical vaginoplasty is reserved for dilation failure.
- **Bone health.** Bone mineral density is reduced (androgens contribute directly, independent of estrogen); assess sex-steroid exposure and fracture risks, obtain DXA when indicated, and individualize repeat testing. Ensure exercise and adequate calcium/vitamin D; low density in a young adult does not automatically justify bisphosphonate treatment.
- **Hormone replacement after gonadectomy.** Continuous unopposed estrogen is used (no uterus). Alternative hormone strategies for persistent symptoms require specialist discussion, and testosterone is not an automatic add-on.

In partial AIS raised male, ongoing issues include hypospadias-complication management (cross-linked above), gynecomastia, and hypogonadism monitoring.<sup>[[20]](#ref20)[[22]](#ref22)</sup>

## Lower Urinary Tract, Sexual, and Fertility Health

- **Lower urinary tract.** After urogenital reconstruction, post-void dribbling and stricture-related symptoms are common (e.g., 39% dribbling, 28% needing dilation in one long-term series); bladder behavior can change at puberty. Investigate the actual anatomical and functional cause; DSD or prior surgery alone is not a neurogenic diagnosis. Safe storage and emptying principles may overlap with NLUTD management; see [Neurogenic Bladder](/docs/clinical-conditions/03d-nlutd/neurogenic-bladder).<sup>[[23]](#ref23)[[24]](#ref24)[[25]](#ref25)</sup>
- **Sexual health.** Dysfunction is pervasive across subtypes; the European DSD-LIFE study (1,040 participants) reported reduced activity and dissatisfaction. The often-quoted dyspareunia estimates of about 56% in partially androgenized 46,XY women and 70% in CAIS come from small subgroups of a separate German study of 57 people, not the entire DSD-LIFE cohort. Sexuality should be explicitly addressed, with referral to pelvic-floor physiotherapy, sexual-health counseling, and psychology.<sup>[[14]](#ref14)[[26]](#ref26)</sup>
- **Fertility.** Address in adolescence with reproductive-medicine collaboration; potential varies widely by diagnosis (often preserved in CAH and 46,XX, limited in gonadal dysgenesis).<sup>[[4]](#ref4)[[18]](#ref18)</sup>

## Transition and Models of Care

The general transition machinery, namely readiness assessment, joint pediatric-adult clinics, transition coordinators, and the barriers (scarce adult DSD expertise, geographic and financial disparity), is shared across congenital urology and is detailed on the [Transitional Urology](/docs/special-populations/05f-lifelong-care/transitional-urology) hub.<sup>[[27]](#ref27)[[28]](#ref28)</sup> DSD adds two emphases: psychology as a continuous cornerstone (disclosure, body image, gender identity, relationships) and the value of standardized longitudinal DSD registries for an evidence base that remains thin.<sup>[[6]](#ref6)[[29]](#ref29)</sup>

## Key Principles

- **Gonadectomy versus surveillance is risk-stratified.** The decision depends on karyotype, gonadal location, and histology. Shared decision-making is deferred to where risk permits, and the limitations of surveillance for retained gonads are explained.<sup>[[8]](#ref8)[[9]](#ref9)</sup>
- **Defer irreversible genital surgery** to patient participation wherever feasible, a live issue at transition.<sup>[[2]](#ref2)[[4]](#ref4)</sup>
- **Vaginal stenosis is a principal reason for feminizing-genitoplasty revision.** Urogenital mobilization with flaps is used for distal disease and bowel vaginoplasty for complete cicatrization or high stenosis; premenarchal dilation is avoided.<sup>[[11]](#ref11)[[13]](#ref13)</sup>
- **TART affects 30–50% of classic-CAH men.** Screen with ultrasound, treat medically, bank sperm early, avoid surgery.<sup>[[16]](#ref16)[[18]](#ref18)</sup>
- **CAIS adults need vaginal dilation, bone-density surveillance, and estrogen replacement after gonadectomy.**<sup>[[20]](#ref20)[[21]](#ref21)</sup>
- **Related topics have dedicated pages.** Reconstruction after prior hypospadias surgery, the NLUTD ladder, and the transition process are covered there.<sup>[[15]](#ref15)[[25]](#ref25)[[27]](#ref27)</sup>
