# Bladder Exstrophy-Epispadias Complex

The **bladder exstrophy-epispadias complex (BEEC)** is a rare congenital spectrum of genitourinary malformations (prevalence ≈ 1 in 10,000 births) that demands **lifelong multidisciplinary care**. As childhood survivors reach adulthood in growing numbers, adult and transitional urology now carries an increasingly central role in their management.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[2]](#ref2)</sup>

For the adult reconstructive urologist, BEEC is a problem of continence reconstruction that frequently outlasts the native bladder, of bowel incorporated into the urinary tract with its lifelong metabolic and oncologic consequences, of genital and abdominal-wall reconstruction, and of fertility, pregnancy, and psychosocial burden. Because the European consensus holds that these patients require **lifelong follow-up at specialist centers**, this article sits within [Transitional Urology](/docs/special-populations/05f-lifelong-care/transitional-urology) rather than under prolapse or incontinence.<sup>[[2]](#ref2)</sup>

---

## Spectrum and Overview

BEEC spans a range of severity: isolated **epispadias** (mildest), **classic bladder exstrophy (CBE)**, and **cloacal exstrophy / OEIS complex** (most severe). The defect involves the urinary tract, abdominal wall, musculoskeletal pelvis, pelvic floor, and genitalia — and sometimes the spine and anus.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[3]](#ref3)</sup> Management is primarily surgical, beginning in the neonatal period, with the staged goals of abdominal-wall closure, urinary continence, renal preservation, and genital reconstruction.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup>

---

## Urinary Continence and Lower Urinary Tract Function in Adults

Continence is the dominant issue in transitional care, and most patients require **multiple procedures** over a lifetime.

- **Few void normally per urethra.** Only ~25% of classic bladder exstrophy patients are expected to void per urethra without catheterization or diversion. A French long-term cohort (median follow-up 22 years) found only **21.4%** voided spontaneously per urethra while **76.2%** performed clean intermittent self-catheterization (CISC).<sup>[[4]](#ref4)[[5]](#ref5)</sup>
- **The need for augmentation or diversion rises with age** — roughly 50% by age 10 and **~70% by age 18** in a 36-year multi-institutional cohort; among adults without diversion, 85.5% performed CIC.<sup>[[6]](#ref6)</sup>
- **LUTS are common but under-recognized** — moderate-to-severe symptoms in 80% of males and 67% of females, many apparent only on detailed validated questionnaires.<sup>[[7]](#ref7)</sup>
- **Continence depends heavily on the procedure.** After **bladder neck closure with a continent catheterizable stoma**, continence reaches **93%**, versus **64%** after isolated bladder neck reconstruction.<sup>[[4]](#ref4)</sup>

### Continence Surgery — Approach and Outcomes

The approach is a stepwise escalation:

| Procedure | Continence | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| **Bladder neck reconstruction (BNR)** — modified Young-Dees-Leadbetter | ≈ **64%** (≥3-hour dry interval) | Most common technique; failure usually reflects inadequate capacity / compliance. No patient needing redo BNR voided per urethra without CIC<sup>[[4]](#ref4)[[8]](#ref8)</sup> |
| **BNR + augmentation cystoplasty ± continent catheterizable stoma** | Higher than isolated BNR | Added when capacity / compliance is insufficient; redo BNR is most effective combined with augmentation<sup>[[5]](#ref5)[[6]](#ref6)</sup> |
| **Bladder neck closure (BNC) + continent catheterizable stoma** | **93%** (95% CI 87–97%) | Most reliable continence option; reserved for BNR failure or non-candidates for volitional voiding<sup>[[4]](#ref4)</sup> |
| **Combined BNR + continent stoma** | **71%** (preliminary) | Hybrid for borderline BNR candidates who strongly desire volitional voiding; 60% of failures later needed bladder-neck transection<sup>[[9]](#ref9)</sup> |
| **Endoscopic bulking (dextranomer / hyaluronic acid)** | 5-yr failure-free **70%** (male epispadias), 45% (female) | Better after prior BNR; a lower-morbidity adjunct<sup>[[10]](#ref10)</sup> |
| **Artificial urinary sphincter (AS792)** | 91% (historical) | Largely supplanted by reconstructive techniques<sup>[[11]](#ref11)</sup> |

## Continent Urinary Diversion — Options and Long-Term Complications

When the native bladder cannot be preserved or is inadequate, continent diversion is performed. These reservoirs commit the patient to **lifelong surveillance**.

- **Continent catheterizable pouches** (Penn, Indiana, Mainz) — in untreated adults, 72% opted for a continent pouch with **100% continence** at mean 6.5 years; the **Indiana pouch** shows excellent durability (mean revision-free survival ≈ **198 months / 16.5 years**).<sup>[[12]](#ref12)[[13]](#ref13)</sup>
- **Continent anal diversion** — a 45-year experience reported **97% daytime continence**, but with secondary rectal-reservoir malignancies, prophylactic alkaline substitution in 41%, and adverse sexual-function impact.<sup>[[14]](#ref14)</sup>
- **Ileal conduit** — the simplest option for patients with limited healthcare access, low motivation for self-catheterization, or significant comorbidity; malignancy can still arise in the conduit decades later.<sup>[[12]](#ref12)[[15]](#ref15)</sup>
- **Stomal stenosis** is the most common long-term stomal complication (**25%** at median 1.9 years). Monofilament suture for umbilicoplasty significantly reduces it (p = 0.009); scar contractures respond to stomal incision, keloids to excision and local tissue rearrangement.<sup>[[16]](#ref16)</sup>
- **Bladder / pouch calculi** form in **26%** of continent diversions — co-leading long-term complication alongside stomal stenosis.<sup>[[17]](#ref17)</sup>
- Continent catheterizable channels themselves are not permanent: roughly **half require ≥1 surgical revision** over 12+ years.<sup>[[9]](#ref9)</sup>

## Augmentation Cystoplasty — Metabolic and Renal Monitoring

Any bowel segment incorporated into the urinary tract obliges **lifelong metabolic surveillance**:<sup>[[18]](#ref18)</sup>

- **Hyperchloremic metabolic acidosis** — the most common metabolic complication, particularly with ileal or colonic segments; prophylactic alkalinization is often required.
- **Vitamin B12 deficiency** — risk rises when &gt;60 cm of terminal ileum is used; check annually.
- **Bone demineralization** — chronic acidosis drives bone loss; consider DEXA.
- **Renal function** — in a cross-sectional study, **74%** of BEEC patients had at least one sign of kidney injury (elevated BP, reduced eGFR, or proteinuria); recurrent UTI/bacteriuria in 44% and nephrolithiasis in 29%.<sup>[[19]](#ref19)</sup>
- **EAU/ESPU monitoring** — physical exam, renal ultrasound, blood-gas (pH and base excess), renal function, and vitamin B12 (if ileum used).<sup>[[18]](#ref18)</sup>

See also the broader [augmentation surveillance discussion](/docs/special-populations/05f-lifelong-care/transitional-urology#augmentation-cystoplasty-lifelong-surveillance) on the transitional-urology hub.

## Malignancy Risk and Surveillance

Adults with BEEC carry a **substantially elevated bladder-malignancy risk** — estimated at **~694-fold** over the age-matched population by age 40.<sup>[[20]](#ref20)[[21]](#ref21)</sup>

- **Adenocarcinoma** is the most common histology, though squamous cell and urothelial carcinoma also occur.<sup>[[20]](#ref20)[[22]](#ref22)</sup>
- Risk is highest with **ureterosigmoidostomy** or other diversions mixing urine and feces (38% neoplasia), but even "low-risk" patients without such exposure carry a **3.3%** rate.<sup>[[20]](#ref20)</sup>
- Persistent histological changes (cystitis glandularis, intestinal metaplasia, polyps) are found in up to **62%** of exstrophic bladders after closure and may be premalignant.<sup>[[23]](#ref23)</sup>
- Augmentation itself does **not** clearly add cancer risk beyond the underlying congenital baseline, but **immunosuppression** (e.g., post-transplant) is an independent risk factor.<sup>[[24]](#ref24)</sup>
- **Lifelong surveillance** with cystoscopy and urine cytology is recommended — some authors begin before age 30 — plus **annual colonoscopy** for ureterosigmoidostomy.<sup>[[20]](#ref20)</sup>

## Abdominal Wall Reconstruction

Closing the anterior abdominal-wall defect in adults is a substantial challenge:

- **Osteotomy is generally not required in adults** — multiple series achieve closure without pelvic osteotomy using the vesical-plate muscular coat and/or multilayered flap reconstruction.<sup>[[25]](#ref25)[[26]](#ref26)</sup>
- Multilayered reconstruction with superficial fascial and myofascial flaps (rectus anterior sheath, external oblique aponeurosis) gave reliable closure with no recurrent dehiscence in a 12-patient series.<sup>[[26]](#ref26)</sup>
- Primary abdominal-wall closure was achieved in **87%** of untreated adults in one series.<sup>[[12]](#ref12)</sup>

---

## Genital Reconstruction in Males

Penile inadequacy is a major adult concern:

- **Penoplasty** (corporeal mobilization, chordee correction, dermal grafting) — required in most; **92% esthetic satisfaction** in one series of 65, though 29% needed revision.<sup>[[27]](#ref27)</sup>
- **Substitution phalloplasty** — for severe inadequacy when local tissue is insufficient. The **radial forearm free flap** is the dominant technique (89% of cases in a systematic review), overall complication rate ~15%; a **penile prosthesis** was implanted in 68%, with a 25% prosthesis complication rate (mainly erosion).<sup>[[28]](#ref28)</sup>
- A 6-year institutional experience (11 phalloplasties; 8 CBE, 3 cloacal exstrophy) reported **100% flap survival**, with inflatable penile prostheses placed in five; one fatal pulmonary embolism occurred in the pedicled anterolateral-thigh-flap group.<sup>[[29]](#ref29)</sup>

## Sexual Function and Fertility

- **Sexual function scores** (erectile and general) are often comparable to controls on validated questionnaires, yet more BEEC patients have **not become sexually active** (35% vs 11%) and fewer have children (22% vs 45%).<sup>[[30]](#ref30)[[31]](#ref31)</sup>
- **Male fertility is frequently impaired** — oligoasthenoteratozoospermia in ~71%, weak / dribbling ejaculation common, and ~50% of men who father children require **assisted reproductive technology**. **Early sperm banking should be discussed.**<sup>[[32]](#ref32)[[33]](#ref33)</sup>
- **Female fertility is better preserved** (up to 100% in one series), though obstetric management must account for pelvic anatomy and prior surgery.<sup>[[34]](#ref34)</sup>
- Genital reconstruction is frequently needed to optimize sexual outcomes — **96% of men and 25% of women** in one series required such procedures.<sup>[[34]](#ref34)</sup>

---

## Pregnancy and Obstetric Management

Pregnancy in women with BEEC is **high-risk** and requires multidisciplinary planning:

- Fertility is impaired — only **50%** of women attempting conception succeeded (19/38) in one series, though others report higher rates.<sup>[[35]](#ref35)[[34]](#ref34)</sup>
- The **miscarriage rate is high (~35%)**, with stillbirth / neonatal death in ~7%.<sup>[[35]](#ref35)</sup>
- **Cesarean delivery** is recommended in most cases — particularly after continent diversion — to protect reconstructed anatomy, though vaginal delivery has been reported in select cases.<sup>[[35]](#ref35)[[36]](#ref36)[[37]](#ref37)</sup>
- Pregnancy complications include recurrent UTI / pyelonephritis (~33%), upper-tract dilation requiring stenting / nephrostomy (~11%), and uterine prolapse.<sup>[[35]](#ref35)[[38]](#ref38)</sup>
- Delivery should occur at a **tertiary center with urology coverage**, with incision type and awareness of reconstructed reservoirs planned with the urological team in advance.<sup>[[37]](#ref37)[[38]](#ref38)</sup>

## Psychosocial and Quality of Life

- Generic QoL (SF-36 / RAND-36) is often **comparable to the general population** — though this may reflect adaptation rather than absence of burden.<sup>[[31]](#ref31)[[39]](#ref39)[[40]](#ref40)</sup>
- **Mental-health concerns** affect ~20%; depressive symptoms, anxiety, and low self-esteem around sexual relationships are common. **Dissatisfaction with genital appearance and urinary incontinence are the strongest predictors of reduced QoL.**<sup>[[39]](#ref39)[[41]](#ref41)[[42]](#ref42)</sup>
- **Caregiver burden** is significant (mean Zarit score 36.4), underscoring family-centered support.<sup>[[41]](#ref41)</sup>
- Multidisciplinary psychosocial support — behavioral health, peer support, educational consultation, and formal transition planning — is considered essential.<sup>[[43]](#ref43)</sup>

## Management of Previously Untreated Adults

In resource-limited settings, some patients present with untreated BEEC in adulthood:

- **Continent catheterizable pouch** (e.g., Penn pouch) — preferred by most patients, with excellent continence.<sup>[[12]](#ref12)</sup>
- **Cystectomy with ileal conduit** — simpler, for patients with limited healthcare access or low motivation for self-catheterization.<sup>[[12]](#ref12)[[15]](#ref15)</sup>
- **Bladder preservation** with ileocystoplasty and bladder-neck reconstruction — feasible if random biopsies show no significant dysplasia, with mandatory lifelong cancer surveillance.<sup>[[44]](#ref44)</sup>

## Lifelong Surveillance

| Domain | Frequency | Method |
|---|---|---|
| Renal function + upper-tract imaging | Annually | Serum creatinine / eGFR, renal ultrasound<sup>[[18]](#ref18)[[19]](#ref19)</sup> |
| Metabolic panel (if bowel incorporated) | Every 6–12 months | Blood gas, electrolytes, vitamin B12 (if ileum used)<sup>[[18]](#ref18)</sup> |
| Malignancy surveillance | Per risk | Cystoscopy + cytology (augmented / diverted); annual colonoscopy for ureterosigmoidostomy<sup>[[20]](#ref20)</sup> |
| Stomal assessment | Each visit | Inspect stoma, troubleshoot catheterization<sup>[[16]](#ref16)</sup> |
| Sexual / reproductive health | Periodically | Counseling; **early sperm banking** for males; semen analysis if fertility desired<sup>[[32]](#ref32)[[33]](#ref33)</sup> |
| Obstetric planning | Childbearing age | Pre-pregnancy multidisciplinary planning; tertiary-center delivery<sup>[[37]](#ref37)</sup> |
| Psychosocial / mental health | Periodically | Screening + validated QoL instruments<sup>[[40]](#ref40)[[43]](#ref43)</sup> |

## Key Principles

- **Continence usually outlasts the native bladder.** Only ~21–25% void per urethra long-term; most end up on CIC, and ~70% need augmentation or diversion by adulthood.<sup>[[4]](#ref4)[[5]](#ref5)[[6]](#ref6)</sup>
- **Bladder neck closure + continent catheterizable stoma is the most reliable continence construct (93%)**; isolated BNR achieves ~64%.<sup>[[4]](#ref4)</sup>
- **Bowel in the urinary tract = lifelong metabolic + oncologic surveillance** — acidosis, B12, stones, and a ~694-fold malignancy risk by age 40.<sup>[[18]](#ref18)[[20]](#ref20)</sup>
- **Adult abdominal-wall closure rarely needs osteotomy** — multilayered flap reconstruction is reliable.<sup>[[25]](#ref25)[[26]](#ref26)</sup>
- **Discuss fertility early** — male fertility is often impaired (ART in ~50% of fathers; offer sperm banking); female fertility is better preserved but pregnancy is high-risk and usually delivered by cesarean at a tertiary center.<sup>[[32]](#ref32)[[35]](#ref35)[[37]](#ref37)</sup>
- **Genital appearance and incontinence drive QoL** — address them proactively alongside mental-health screening and caregiver support.<sup>[[41]](#ref41)[[43]](#ref43)</sup>
