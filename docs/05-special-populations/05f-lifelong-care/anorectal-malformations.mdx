# Anorectal Malformations

**Anorectal malformations (ARMs)** occur in roughly 1 in 4,000–5,000 live births and span a spectrum from a simple perineal fistula to a complex cloacal malformation.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup> They matter in transitional urology because the **urologic burden is lifelong and rises with malformation complexity** — yet these patients are among the least systematically transitioned in all of congenital urology.

This page covers the **ARM-specific** adult and transitional urological problems and the bowel–bladder intersection that defines them. For the general transition framework see [Transitional Urology](/docs/special-populations/05f-lifelong-care/transitional-urology); for the neurogenic-bladder management ladder see [Neurogenic Bladder](/docs/clinical-conditions/03d-nlutd/neurogenic-bladder); for the operative detail of catheterizable channels and augmentation see [Catheterizable Channels](/docs/surgical-techniques/04b-bladder-reconstruction/catheterizable-channels) and [Bladder Augmentation](/docs/surgical-techniques/04b-bladder-reconstruction/bladder-augmentation).

## Associated Urologic Anomalies

Urologic anomalies are present in **50–65% of ARM patients**, with prevalence rising in proportion to ARM complexity.<sup>[[2]](#ref2)</sup> Common findings include hydronephrosis, vesicoureteral reflux (VUR), solitary kidney, renal ectopia/fusion, hypospadias, and undescended testes.<sup>[[2]](#ref2)</sup> **Males with recto-bladder-neck fistulae and females with cloacal malformations** (especially long common channels) carry the highest urologic co-morbidity, and **spinal cord anomalies — found in up to 44%** — may contribute to neurogenic bladder.<sup>[[2]](#ref2)[[3]](#ref3)</sup> Screening of the urinary tract and spine is therefore mandatory at diagnosis and informs lifelong risk.<sup>[[3]](#ref3)</sup>

---

## Long-Term Urologic Sequelae

| Domain | Adult picture |
|---|---|
| **Lower urinary tract dysfunction / incontinence** | Reported in 1.7–30.5% of adults, far higher in cloacal malformations; neurogenic bladder from spinal anomalies may require CIC or augmentation<sup>[[4]](#ref4)[[5]](#ref5)</sup> |
| **Renal impairment** | Renal disease was concentrated in complex malformations in selected adult cohorts; one female-born ARM/Hirschsprung cohort reported CKD/ESRD in 7/64 and transplantation in 3/64, mostly among patients with cloaca<sup>[[4]](#ref4)[[5]](#ref5)</sup> |
| **Recurrent UTI** | Significantly elevated with low sacral ratios (0.4–0.69)<sup>[[4]](#ref4)</sup> |
| **Sexual / fertility** | Erectile dysfunction in ~35% of adult males; absent ejaculation/azoospermia in ~15% with rectourethral fistula; delayed coital debut across subtypes; cloacal women report lower QoL and incontinence affecting sexual well-being<sup>[[6]](#ref6)[[7]](#ref7)[[8]](#ref8)</sup> |
| **Occult unsafe bladder** | 12/41 asymptomatic male **children** after high-ARM repair had unsafe urodynamic findings in one prospective study; symptoms alone may miss risk, but this does not establish a universal adult testing schedule<sup>[[9]](#ref9)</sup> |

The reconstructive/functional urologist's lifelong goals are **renal preservation** (upper-tract surveillance, especially with VUR, solitary kidney, or prior augmentation), **safe low-pressure storage** (urodynamics, CIC, neurogenic-bladder management), **lifelong follow-up after augmentation with investigation of concerning symptoms**, and **proactive sexual-health and fertility counseling**.<sup>[[4]](#ref4)[[9]](#ref9)</sup>

## The Bowel–Bladder Intersection

What distinguishes ARM from the other transitional conditions is how tightly **bowel and bladder management are coupled** — and how often the urologist co-owns both. Patients with concurrent **sacral agenesis** are the prototype: in a 2026 pediatric cohort, **18/41** used CIC and **22/33 children aged at least 4 years** used transanal irrigation. These are different denominators, not rates from an unselected adult ARM population.<sup>[[10]](#ref10)</sup>

This coupling shapes reconstruction:

- **Combined antegrade continence enema (ACE/Malone) + catheterizable bladder channel** can be created in a single operation, letting the patient manage both systems through abdominal stomas.<sup>[[11]](#ref11)</sup>
- For neurogenic bladder refractory to antimuscarinics or botulinum toxin, **augmentation cystoplasty** is an option for selected patients with refractory unsafe storage. It requires lifelong renal/metabolic and complication follow-up. The AUA/SUFU guidance applies to **adult NLUTD**: annual clinical review, a basic metabolic panel, and urinary tract imaging after bowel reconstruction; cystoscopy for concerning symptoms such as gross hematuria or symptomatic recurrent UTI, rather than routine screening of asymptomatic patients.<sup>[[12]](#ref12)</sup>
- **Urodynamic evaluation** should address suspected unsafe storage or emptying and help establish risk in complex ARM, particularly with spinal/upper-tract abnormalities or absent prior bladder assessment. The pediatric high-ARM study supports looking beyond symptoms; it does not define the frequency of repeat studies in every asymptomatic adult.<sup>[[9]](#ref9)</sup>

## Bowel Management (Urology-Relevant Overview)

Bowel management is a lifelong, individualized, stepwise endeavor; the reconstructive urologist should understand its logic because it is co-managed with colorectal surgery and shares the catheterizable-channel platform. For **persistent postoperative defecatory dysfunction**, first review the anatomy and prior repair, using contrast imaging and examination under anesthesia when needed to assess a stricture, mislocated anus, prolapse, or fistula. This is not a requirement for anesthesia before treating every uncomplicated episode of constipation.<sup>[[13]](#ref13)[[18]](#ref18)</sup> The clinical history, examination, and selected contrast imaging help distinguish phenotypes that guide treatment:<sup>[[18]](#ref18)</sup>

- **Hypomotile / constipation-predominant** (dilated colon, paradoxically commoner in "simpler" malformations) → stimulant laxatives ± large-volume enemas.
- **Hypermotile / diarrhea-predominant** (non-dilated colon, often after resection) → small-volume saline enemas, anti-motility agents, constipating diet.

Escalation ladder:

- **Laxatives for constipation with continence potential** — senna is commonly used within specialist programs, with dosing titrated to emptying, stool consistency, and adverse effects. A small 2017 randomized crossover trial favored senna over polyethylene glycol, but a **2025 crossover trial in 15 children**, with 21 days per treatment, found no significant efficacy or preference difference among sennosides, magnesium hydroxide, and polyethylene glycol. These small trials do not establish universal senna superiority or treatment equivalence.<sup>[[18]](#ref18)[[19]](#ref19)[[20]](#ref20)</sup>
- **Retrograde enemas/irrigation** within a structured bowel-management program when laxatives do not achieve satisfactory emptying and cleanliness; volumes and additives depend on anatomy and phenotype. A review reports approximately 70% continence at one year in a structured program, which is not a universal success rate.<sup>[[18]](#ref18)</sup>
- **Antegrade continence enema (ACE/Malone)** for independence or when a retrograde program is unsuccessful or impractical. A **2026 systematic review of 19 studies/696 children with ARM** reported continence in **404/479** with available outcome data and complications in **128/474** with available complication data; heterogeneous definitions and follow-up limit a single durable success estimate. A separate 163-patient series reported 96% successful incontinence management and complications in 41 patients, illustrating how cohort definitions affect estimates. Counsel about stomal stenosis, leakage, revisions, and ongoing dependence on an irrigation program.<sup>[[18]](#ref18)[[21]](#ref21)[[22]](#ref22)</sup>
- **Permanent colostomy** as last resort — carried by ~12% of one adult ARM cohort.<sup>[[23]](#ref23)</sup>

## Psychosocial Burden and Quality of Life

**Continence is an important correlate of quality of life** in ARM. Adult and pediatric patient-reported studies associate soiling with poorer bowel-related quality of life; they do not establish one strongest modifiable determinant across all patients or prove that a particular intervention improves mental health.<sup>[[26]](#ref26)[[27]](#ref27)</sup> A pediatric bowel-management cohort reported improved quality-of-life and voiding-dysfunction scores at one year; the observational design does not isolate a causal treatment effect.<sup>[[28]](#ref28)</sup>

The psychiatric burden is high and under-recognized:

- In a **selected tertiary-care cohort of 81 adults with mixed congenital colorectal/pelvic diagnoses**, **51.9%** had a recorded psychiatric diagnosis and **30.9%** a substance-use disorder. These are not population prevalence estimates for ARM alone.<sup>[[29]](#ref29)</sup>
- A cross-sectional study of **98 adults** associated fecal incontinence, self-efficacy, and health-related quality of life. Its statistical model does not prove that changing self-efficacy mediates improvement or that continence treatment alone resolves psychological distress.<sup>[[30]](#ref30)</sup>
- Stressors are developmentally staged; in adulthood the dominant psychological challenge is **psychosexual anxiety** and the dominant practical one is **navigating complex health systems** ; these findings support attention to transition barriers without attributing all distress to one cause.<sup>[[31]](#ref31)[[32]](#ref32)</sup>

The implication for transitional care is concrete: optimize bowel management **alongside** assessment and treatment of mental-health needs. Screening for depression, anxiety, and substance use and access to social work/psychology are appropriate components of comprehensive transition care; continence care does not replace them.<sup>[[29]](#ref29)[[33]](#ref33)</sup>

## Transition of Care

ARM transition is poorly systematized: in an international survey **72% of practitioners had no transition protocol**, 82% held no multidisciplinary meeting with adult providers, 33% of centers stopped follow-up before age 10, and 42% felt their patients were not ready to transition.<sup>[[14]](#ref14)</sup> The **ARM-Net Consortium** recommends beginning transition around **age 13–16** with a structured multidisciplinary approach spanning urology, colorectal surgery, gynecology, nephrology, and psychosocial support.<sup>[[15]](#ref15)</sup>

The general transition machinery (readiness tools, joint clinics, coordinators, barriers) is shared across congenital urology — see the [Transitional Urology](/docs/special-populations/05f-lifelong-care/transitional-urology) hub.<sup>[[16]](#ref16)[[17]](#ref17)</sup> Effective ARM-specific solutions are joint pediatric-adult clinics, fostering patient self-advocacy, educating adult providers, and **collaborative urology–colorectal–gynecology co-management** through the transition window.<sup>[[24]](#ref24)[[25]](#ref25)</sup>

## Key Principles

- **Urologic anomalies accompany 50–65% of ARMs** and scale with complexity; recto-bladder-neck fistula and cloaca carry the highest renal/bladder morbidity, and spinal anomalies (up to 44%) may contribute to neurogenic dysfunction.<sup>[[2]](#ref2)[[3]](#ref3)</sup>
- **Symptoms alone do not establish bladder safety** — 12/41 asymptomatic boys in a high-ARM cohort had unsafe urodynamic findings; establish adult testing needs from anatomy, prior assessments, and current risk.<sup>[[9]](#ref9)</sup>
- **Bowel and bladder are coupled** — combined ACE + catheterizable channel through abdominal stomas is the reconstructive expression of that coupling.<sup>[[10]](#ref10)[[11]](#ref11)</sup>
- **Match bowel treatment to phenotype and patient goals** — laxatives, retrograde irrigation, ACE, and selected diversion have different roles; the small laxative trials do not establish one universally superior agent.<sup>[[18]](#ref18)[[19]](#ref19)[[20]](#ref20)</sup>
- **Address continence and mental health together** — high psychiatric burden in selected referral cohorts supports integrated care, not extrapolation of those percentages to all adults with ARM.<sup>[[27]](#ref27)[[29]](#ref29)</sup>
- **Transition is poorly systematized** — most centers lack a protocol; a structured multidisciplinary handoff is the fix.<sup>[[14]](#ref14)[[15]](#ref15)</sup>
