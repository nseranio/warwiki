# Testicular Torsion

**Testicular torsion** is a urological emergency in which twisting of the spermatic cord compromises testicular blood flow. Ischemia progresses rapidly. Salvage is best when detorsion occurs within the first 4 to 6 hours, but a longer symptom duration does not justify withholding urgent surgery. Surgical exploration with detorsion and bilateral orchiopexy is the definitive treatment. The clinical diagnosis takes priority over imaging: a patient with a high pretest probability goes to the operating room, not the ultrasound suite.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[2]](#ref2)[[3]](#ref3)</sup>

---

## Epidemiology

- **Incidence:** approximately 1 in 4,000 males &lt;25 years annually
- Accounts for 10–15% of acute scrotal disease in children
- **Bimodal age distribution:**
  - **Neonatal period** (first 28 days of life). Often extravaginal torsion, frequently missed on initial evaluation
  - **12–18 years.** The peak adolescent incidence; typically intravaginal torsion on bell-clapper anatomy
- Can occur at any age, including adult life<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[4]](#ref4)</sup>

---

## Risk Factors

| Risk factor | Mechanism |
|---|---|
| **Bell-clapper deformity** | Abnormal high investment of the tunica vaginalis → testis hangs freely like a clapper in a bell → excessive mobility enables intravaginal torsion. Autopsy studies put the rate in the scrotal testis at 4.9–16%<sup>[[23]](#ref23)</sup> |
| **Prior history of torsion** | ~50% report antecedent similar self-resolving episodes |
| **Family history** | Genetic predisposition to bell-clapper anatomy |
| **Cold weather** | Hyperactive cremasteric reflex |
| **Rapid pubertal growth** | Testicular enlargement in an already loose investment |
| **Antecedent trauma** | Minor scrotal trauma can precipitate torsion in predisposed anatomy |
| **History of testicular surgery or undescended testis (cryptorchidism)** | Predisposes to torsion; ask about it when taking the history. An undescended testis accounted for 8.9% (8/90) and 9.7% (11/107) of all pediatric torsion cases in two single/two-center retrospective series. Presentation is often atypical (groin mass, abdominal pain, restlessness in infants) and can mimic an incarcerated inguinal hernia; four Kargl patients were initially suspected of having a hernia; final salvage rates were 3/8 (37.5%) and 2/11 (18%) respectively, lower than typical intravaginal torsion in a normally descended testis<sup>[[32]](#ref32)[[33]](#ref33)</sup> |

---

## Pathophysiology

The spermatic cord twists around its longitudinal axis within (or outside of) the tunica vaginalis:

- Venous outflow is obstructed first (the low-pressure venous system yields to the twist before arterial inflow)
- Progressive venous congestion leading to edema and increasing intratesticular pressure
- Eventually arterial inflow is compromised, leading to global ischemia
- Damage depends on both duration and degree of twisting; there is no universal time threshold at which a testis is certainly nonviable

**Ischemia-reperfusion injury** is a second pathophysiologic hit: detorsion restores flow to injured tissue, driving oxidative stress, neutrophil activation, cytokine release, endothelial dysfunction, and further germ cell loss. This explains why even "salvaged" testes frequently undergo partial atrophy and exhibit abnormal semen parameters long-term.<sup>[[5]](#ref5)[[6]](#ref6)</sup>

## Clinical Presentation

### Classic symptoms

- **Severe, acute, unilateral scrotal pain.** The defining presentation
- **Onset typically &lt;24 hours**
- **Nausea and vomiting.** Present in most cases (partly autonomic, partly referred pain)
- Scrotal swelling, erythema
- Can be associated with lower abdominal or inguinal pain, which may mislead toward non-testicular etiologies in adolescent males

### Physical examination

| Sign | Significance |
|---|---|
| **High-riding testis with transverse lie** | Classic; caused by cord shortening from twist |
| **Absent cremasteric reflex** | The EAU cites 100% sensitivity and 66% specificity for absence of the reflex in torsion, so an absent reflex is a warning sign that does not confirm torsion. Diagnosis rests on history and examination, and a preserved reflex should not delay exploration when the presentation is otherwise concerning<sup>[[20]](#ref20)</sup> |
| **Tender, swollen, firm testicle** | Common; neonatal and atypical presentations may differ |
| **Abnormal testicular lie** | Transverse or elevated position |

### Prior episodes

Up to approximately 50% of patients report previous similar episodes of self-resolving pain (intermittent torsion–detorsion), an important historical clue that should not be dismissed.<sup>[[7]](#ref7)</sup> For a patient with a history suggesting intermittent torsion who is pain-free at the time of evaluation, urgent exploration is not required, but bilateral scrotal exploration and orchiopexy should still be performed as soon as reasonably possible rather than deferred indefinitely.

---

## Diagnosis

### Clinical diagnosis first

Testicular torsion is primarily a clinical diagnosis. When history and exam are consistent with torsion, immediate surgical exploration is indicated and should not be delayed for imaging. Imaging is reserved for cases with intermediate clinical probability.<sup>[[3]](#ref3)</sup>

### TWIST Score

The **TWIST score (Testicular Workup for Ischemia and Suspected Torsion)** is a validated 7-point clinical decision tool.<sup>[[4]](#ref4)[[8]](#ref8)</sup>

| Finding | Points |
|---|---|
| Testicular swelling | 2 |
| Hard testis | 2 |
| Absent cremasteric reflex | 1 |
| Nausea / vomiting | 1 |
| High-riding testis | 1 |

### Risk stratification (Barbosa):

| Risk | TWIST | Pooled performance in the meta-analysis | Action |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Low** | 0–2 | Sensitivity 0.984; missed torsion is possible (65.2% of patients) | Reassess clinical probability; persistent or concerning symptoms still require urgent assessment ± ultrasound |
| **Intermediate** | 3–4 | Sensitivity 0.922, specificity 0.682 (19.9% of patients) | Color Doppler US |
| **High** | 5–7 | Specificity 0.975 (14.9% of patients) | Urgent surgical exploration |

- The meta-analysis reported AUC 0.924 for the Barbosa cut-offs in five pediatric studies (1,060 patients; 199 torsions).<sup>[[8]](#ref8)</sup>
- TWIST supports triage; it cannot independently prove or exclude torsion. Prevalence and thresholds vary by setting.<sup>[[20]](#ref20)</sup> A 2026 meta-analysis of patients younger than 25 reported sensitivity 94.2% (95% CI 72.8–99%) and specificity 98% (95% CI 91.5–99.5%) when comparing high-risk scores 5–7 with low-risk scores 0–2.<sup>[[34]](#ref34)</sup>

### Imaging — color Doppler ultrasound

When imaging is chosen for intermediate-risk patients:

- **Color Doppler ultrasonography** is the imaging test when imaging will not delay exploration, as EAU recommends.<sup>[[20]](#ref20)</sup> A 2026 diagnostic meta-analysis reported sensitivity 95.3% and specificity 98.3% across 42 studies (4,422 participants), with patient selection the main risk-of-bias and applicability concern.<sup>[[35]](#ref35)</sup>
- Performance varies with operator, age and timing. EAU cites sensitivity 63.6–100% and specificity 97–100%; preserved or increased arterial flow can occur in torsion (24% of 208 boys with torsion in one multicenter study), and high-resolution ultrasound visualization of the twisted cord had 97.3% sensitivity and 99% specificity. EAU gives a strong recommendation that Doppler use should not delay intervention<sup>[[20]](#ref20)</sup>
- **Findings:**
  - **Absent or decreased intratesticular blood flow** on the affected side
  - **Whirlpool sign.** Twisted spermatic cord visible on gray-scale or color Doppler (most specific finding); a systematic review and meta-analysis pooling studies across pediatric and adult patients reported sensitivity 73% and specificity 99%, with sensitivity rising to 92% once neonates (in whom the sign performs poorly) were excluded<sup>[[31]](#ref31)</sup>
  - **Hypoechoic / heterogeneous parenchyma** in late/progressed torsion

A normal ultrasound cannot exclude the diagnosis. Clinical suspicion drives management.<sup>[[2]](#ref2)</sup>

### Laboratory findings

If history and physical examination are highly suspicious for torsion, laboratory work is not always necessary and should not delay surgical intervention. On urinalysis, pyuria or bacteriuria suggests epididymo-orchitis, but pyuria can also occur with torsion and is not definitive; leukocytosis can occur with other infectious causes and does not rule out torsion. Obtain a CBC and coagulation profile when a bleeding risk is present, in anticipation of urgent surgery.<sup>[[24]](#ref24)</sup>

## Management

### Emergent surgical exploration

Any high-risk or clinically convincing case goes to the operating room without delay.

### Manual detorsion (bridge therapy)

Can be attempted in the ED while awaiting surgical transfer:<sup>[[2]](#ref2)[[9]](#ref9)</sup>

- **Outward (medial-to-lateral) rotation** ("opening a book"); do not delay surgical exploration<sup>[[20]](#ref20)</sup>
- If pain worsens with that direction, rotate the opposite way (atypical torsion direction). In a retrospective review of 104 scrotal explorations for torsion (rotation direction recorded in 81), the testis was rotated laterally, the atypical direction, in 46% (38/81); attempting the standard medial-to-lateral maneuver on these can worsen the torsion<sup>[[22]](#ref22)</sup>
- **Ultrasound guidance** may improve success
- Pain relief does not prove complete detorsion; residual torsion can remain. Urgent exploration and fixation are still required after apparent manual success

### Timing and salvage rates

| Time from symptom onset | Salvage (approximate, from surgical series; not EAU figures) |
|---|---|
| **&lt;6 hours** | ~90% |
| **6–8 hours** | Testicle usually salvageable |
| **8–12 hours** | Variable; salvage becomes less reliable |
| **&gt;12 hours** | Risk of permanent damage / orchiectomy increases significantly |
| **&gt;24 hours** | Necrosis is likely, especially with a twist above 360 degrees; EAU still advises semi-elective exploration |

A systematic review of 30 studies included 2,116 torsion patients overall. Among the 1,283 patients reported in six-hour time intervals, testicular survival was 97.2% at 0–6 hours, 79.3% at 7–12 hours, 61.3% at 13–18 hours, 42.5% at 19–24 hours, 24.4% at 25–48 hours, and 7.4% beyond 48 hours. Survival meant retention of the testis, irrespective of later atrophy or impaired function; these data support exploration even after prolonged symptoms.<sup>[[26]](#ref26)</sup>

In a national database analysis of 2,876 pediatric torsion operations, 31.9% ended in orchiectomy, and the rate was highest at ages 1 to 9 years (49.9%).<sup>[[10]](#ref10)</sup> The EAU 2026 guideline gives a critical window of about 4 to 6 hours and notes that the degree of twist also determines salvage: severe atrophy occurred after as little as 4 hours with a twist above 360 degrees, no atrophy was seen with 180 to 360 degrees and up to 12 hours of symptoms, and an absent or severely atrophic testis was found in every case above 360 degrees with symptoms beyond 24 hours. It recommends immediate treatment for torsion (strong) and urgent exploration within 24 hours of onset, with semi-elective exploration beyond that, and reports that up to half of patients develop testicular atrophy despite timely detorsion.<sup>[[20]](#ref20)</sup>

## Operative Technique — Scrotal Exploration and Orchiopexy

### Incision

- **Midline raphe incision.** Allows bilateral access through a single incision. FIX-IT consensus also accepts a transverse incision over the affected testis and does not identify a preferred approach.<sup>[[21]](#ref21)</sup>
- **Bilateral hemiscrotal incisions.** An alternative that provides separate access to each testis.
- Carry through dartos into each hemiscrotum

### Delivery and detorsion

1. Deliver the affected testis through the dartos pouch
2. **Detorse** the testis by rotating opposite to the direction of twist; palpate the spermatic cord to appreciate the direction
3. **Wrap in warm moist saline gauze** and observe for 10–20 minutes for reperfusion or color return. A handheld Doppler probe can be used intraoperatively to confirm restored blood flow, as an adjunct or prior step before deciding whether a tunica albuginea incision (bleeding test) is needed
4. Deliver and inspect the contralateral testis (usually normal but confirm absence of bell-clapper and synchronous torsion)

### Viability assessment

After observation:

- **Pink, viable testis:** orchiopexy
- **Dusky testis with uncertain viability:** retain and fix the testis after detorsion and reassessment. FIX-IT expert consensus reserves tunica albuginea incision and flap coverage for selected cases in the hands of surgeons familiar with the technique.<sup>[[21]](#ref21)</sup>
- **Black, clearly necrotic testis:** orchiectomy
- **Exception — solitary testis.** If the affected testicle is the patient's solitary testicle, pexy it even if viability is questionable, rather than remove it

### Orchiopexy — fixation techniques

For confirmed torsion, fix the viable affected testis and the contralateral testis; if the affected testis requires orchiectomy, fix the contralateral side. If exploration finds neither torsion nor bell-clapper anatomy, FIX-IT consensus advises against routine fixation.<sup>[[11]](#ref11)[[21]](#ref21)</sup>

Comparative evidence for fixation techniques is limited and largely observational. FIX-IT is expert consensus: a dartos pouch or sutured fixation is acceptable in children, while sutured fixation is recommended in adults.<sup>[[12]](#ref12)[[13]](#ref13)[[14]](#ref14)[[21]](#ref21)</sup>

#### Sutureless dartos pouch (scrotal pouch) orchiopexy

- Create a subdartos pouch below the dartos layer
- **Obliterate the partial tunica vaginalis sac**
- Place testis in the pouch in an extravaginal position
- Tissue adherence secures the testis, with no transparenchymal sutures
- **Preserves tunica albuginea and seminiferous tubule integrity**
- Kozminski series: 1,104 procedures, including 949 for undescended testes and 155 for torsion; the 98.7% overall success (1,090/1,104 with normal postoperative testicular position and size) is not a torsion-specific salvage rate<sup>[[14]](#ref14)</sup>

![Drawing of sutureless fixation of the right testis within a dartos pouch](/img/figures/testicular-torsion-clement-2022-4.jpg)

*Sutureless fixation of the right testis within a dartos pouch. From Clement KD, et al. BJU Int. 2022;130(5):662-670, Fig. 4 (CC BY-NC).<sup>[[21]](#ref21)</sup>*

#### Sutured point-fixation (traditional)

- FIX-IT accepts three- or four-point fixation, preferring three points
- **Nonabsorbable suture preferred**; prolonged-absorption polydioxanone is a consensus alternative
- Avoid fixing the testes to each other across the scrotal septum.<sup>[[21]](#ref21)</sup>

![Drawing of three-point suture fixation of the right testis at the medial, lateral and inferior aspects](/img/figures/testicular-torsion-clement-2022-5.jpg)

*Three-point suture fixation of the right testis; dashed lines mark the lateral, medial and inferior fixation points. From Clement KD, et al. BJU Int. 2022;130(5):662-670, Fig. 5 (CC BY-NC).<sup>[[21]](#ref21)</sup>*

#### Jaboulay fixation (tunica plication)

- **Plication of the tunica vaginalis** around the testis without transparenchymal sutures
- A retrospective pediatric comparison found no significant difference in reported outcomes; this does not establish equivalence<sup>[[12]](#ref12)</sup>

#### Eversion of tunica vaginalis

- **Evert the tunica vaginalis** to create extensive serosal-scrotal adherence
- Lent 1993 series (46 procedures in 35 patients): reported 87.5% salvage and no recurrences in this small series<sup>[[15]](#ref15)</sup>

## Advanced Salvage — Tunica Albuginea Decompression + Tunica Vaginalis Flap (TVF)

For testes that remain ischemic after detorsion (particularly with prolonged ischemia, 12–24+ hours), tunica albuginea incision with tunica vaginalis flap (TVF) coverage may salvage a testis that would otherwise be lost. The technique addresses **testicular compartment syndrome**, which is elevated intratesticular pressure from ischemia-induced edema that impairs reperfusion even after untwisting the cord.<sup>[[16]](#ref16)[[17]](#ref17)[[18]](#ref18)</sup>

### Rationale

- In a three-boy report, pressure was measured in one 11-year-old: 34 mm Hg after detorsion, falling to 5 mm Hg after incision. These are case measurements, not validated diagnostic thresholds<sup>[[18]](#ref18)</sup>
- The tunica albuginea behaves like a non-compliant compartment; decompression allows the swollen parenchyma room to expand and reperfuse

### Technique

1. **Detorse** the testis; observe 10–20 min
2. If still dusky with questionable viability, make a longitudinal incision in the tunica albuginea on the anterior or lateral surface
3. The testis often blushes pink dramatically within minutes as the compartment decompresses
4. **Harvest a tunica vaginalis flap** raised from the hemiscrotum
5. **Cover the exposed seminiferous tubules** with the TVF, sewing its edges to the remaining tunica albuginea with fine absorbable suture (5-0 or 6-0)
6. Proceed with orchiopexy

### Outcomes

- Figueroa 2012 retrospective before/after series (59 included patients): orchiectomy 35.9% to 15% after introduction of TVF; temporal selection and small groups limit causal interpretation<sup>[[16]](#ref16)</sup>
- Kim 2025 systematic review (eight studies, 293 patients): most studies had high risk of bias. Three comparative studies did not establish a significant salvage advantage; selected salvage use remains supported by limited evidence<sup>[[17]](#ref17)</sup>
- Chu 2018 matched-cohort analysis (32 TVF cases matched to 32 orchiectomy cases by age and ischemia time) stratified TVF outcome by ischemia duration rather than by era: postoperative viability was 95% with ischemia ≤24 hours versus 67% with &gt;24 hours, but atrophy remained common in both groups, 67% versus 83% respectively. TVF traded orchiectomy for a still-substantial chance of atrophy even in the more favorable, shorter-ischemia group<sup>[[28]](#ref28)</sup>
- Animal studies show mixed histopathologic results. Pressure reduction is real but does not always translate to better tubular preservation.

TVF is not a routine step; it is a salvage maneuver when detorsion alone leaves a non-viable-appearing testis and the alternative is orchiectomy.

## Outcomes and Complications

### Testicular atrophy

- In a single-center retrospective cohort of 145 children, 56.6% of salvaged testes developed atrophy, with median volume loss 57.4%; these are study-specific estimates<sup>[[19]](#ref19)</sup>
- A systematic review and meta-analysis of 12 pediatric studies found a mean early (intraoperative) testicular loss rate of 39%, while long-term (&gt;12 months) loss approached 50% in the studies that followed patients that far, meaning a testis called "salvaged" at the index operation still carries a substantial chance of later loss<sup>[[27]](#ref27)</sup>
- Risk factors: age &lt;6, delayed surgery, intraoperative evidence of poor perfusion
- Most atrophy manifests at 3–6 months; first scheduled follow-up US is at this interval

### Compensatory contralateral hypertrophy

The unaffected testis undergoes compensatory enlargement in both orchiopexy and orchiectomy groups, a normal physiologic response.

### Fertility

- **Abnormal semen parameters are common** after unilateral torsion, even with successful salvage
- Mechanism: ischemia-reperfusion injury, germ cell apoptosis, antisperm antibody formation, hypothalamic-pituitary-gonadal axis disruption<sup>[[5]](#ref5)</sup>
- Discuss possible subfertility during follow-up and offer individualized fertility assessment when appropriate. Do not delay emergency detorsion for sperm banking
- **Paternity with a single testis.** Among 122 men screened after unilateral torsion, 72 met fertility-analysis criteria, including a stable female partner and unprotected intercourse aimed at conception for at least 2 years (49 orchiectomy, 23 orchiopexy); first pregnancies culminating in live birth occurred in 83.67% (41/49) after orchiectomy and 91.30% (21/23) after orchiopexy, a reassuring counterpoint particularly for patients left with a single normal testis after orchiectomy, distinct from the semen-parameter concern above, which concerns ischemia-reperfusion effects on a salvaged testis<sup>[[25]](#ref25)</sup>

### Recurrent torsion — even after fixation

Mor et al. identified recurrent torsion after fixation in 8 of 179 patients (4.5%) operated on between 1991 and 2003 with mixed fixation methods. This is not a universal modern recurrence estimate:<sup>[[13]](#ref13)</sup>

- Can occur 0.5 to 23 years after initial fixation (mean approximately 7 years)
- **Both ipsilateral and contralateral testes** are at risk of recurrent torsion
- Occurs even with nonabsorbable polypropylene sutures
- Patients should be counseled that orchiopexy reduces but does not eliminate risk; any new acute scrotal pain warrants immediate evaluation

### Neonatal torsion — a special case

Neonatal torsion occurs in the first 28 days and is usually extravaginal. Distinguish established prenatal torsion from new acute postnatal torsion, which requires urgent exploration. EAU 2026 gives a weak recommendation for contralateral orchiopexy in neonatal torsion. For prenatal torsion, operative timing depends on clinical findings and requires early pediatric-urology assessment.<sup>[[20]](#ref20)</sup> Because metachronous contralateral torsion can follow perinatal torsion, the guideline advises early but not acute exploration of both testes with fixation.<sup>[[20]](#ref20)</sup> Salvage rates in this age group are far lower than in older children: a systematic review of 18 case series (268 operated neonates) found an overall salvage rate of 8.96%, rising to about 21.7% when surgery was performed emergently, and a separate meta-analysis of 196 neonates from 9 studies found bilateral exploration salvaged about 7% of ipsilateral testes while preventing asynchronous (contralateral) torsion in about 4%.<sup>[[29]](#ref29)[[30]](#ref30)</sup>

## Counseling

Preoperative and discharge elements:

- **Contralateral fixation** is recommended when torsion is confirmed; affected-side fixation depends on viability
- **Salvage is time-dependent.** Emphasize that hours matter and revisit the timeline with the patient and family
- **Possibility of orchiectomy** if the testis is non-viable; consent for this preoperatively
- **Atrophy is common** even after successful salvage; 3–6 month follow-up ultrasound
- **Fertility implications.** Discuss risk and subsequent assessment without delaying emergency surgery
- **Recurrence remains possible.** Any future acute scrotal pain prompts immediate re-evaluation

### Post-operative management

Watch for scrotal hematoma or wound infection. Most patients are discharged the same day. Recommend scrotal support for the first few weeks and avoidance of heavy lifting or vigorous activity, and counsel on an athletic cup for future contact sports. Obtain scrotal ultrasound follow-up when long-term non-viability is a concern. After orchiectomy, delayed placement of a [testicular prosthesis](/docs/surgical-techniques/04e-genital-reconstruction/testicular-prosthesis) can be discussed. FIX-IT expert consensus favors placement after puberty in children to permit sizing against the adult testis.<sup>[[21]](#ref21)</sup>

## Differential Diagnosis — Acute Scrotum

| Diagnosis | Key distinguishing features |
|---|---|
| **Testicular torsion** | Severe acute pain, absent cremasteric reflex, high-riding testis, TWIST ≥5 |
| **Torsion of the appendix testis** | Blue dot sign at superior testis pole (present in only about 10–23% of confirmed cases — an unreliable finding whose absence does not exclude appendage torsion<sup>[[20]](#ref20)</sup>); normal cremasteric reflex; localized tenderness at upper pole |
| **Epididymitis / epididymo-orchitis** | Often gradual pain, dysuria or fever; pain relief with elevation and a preserved reflex cannot exclude torsion |
| **Incarcerated inguinal hernia** | Groin mass extending into scrotum; GI symptoms |
| **Hydrocele with acute rupture/bleed** | Transillumination + ultrasound differentiates |
| **Fournier's gangrene** | Systemic toxicity, crepitus, necrotizing skin signs |
| **Henoch-Schönlein purpura** | Purpuric skin lesions; may have scrotal involvement mimicking torsion |
