# Necrotizing Fasciitis / Fournier's Gangrene

Fournier's gangrene (FG) is a rapidly progressive, polymicrobial necrotizing fasciitis of the perineum, genitalia, and perianal region that constitutes a true surgical emergency. Despite its recognition as a distinct entity since Jean-Alfred Fournier's 1883 description, FG shares all the pathophysiologic features of type I necrotizing soft tissue infection (NSTI) and should be conceptualized and managed under the same framework. Overall mortality ranges from 20%–40% in contemporary series and may exceed 70% in the presence of septic shock.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup> Survival depends entirely on early recognition, aggressive and repeated surgical debridement, and broad-spectrum antimicrobial therapy.

## Definition and Pathophysiology

Fournier's gangrene is type I NSTI of the perineum and external genitalia, characterized by synergistic infection of multiple aerobic and anaerobic organisms that exploit fascial planes to spread with devastating speed. The infection originates most commonly in the:

- Anorectal region (anorectal abscess, anal fistula, rectal perforation)
- Urogenital tract (urethral stricture, periurethral abscess, catheter trauma, scrotal trauma, urinary extravasation)
- Skin and subcutaneous tissue (trauma, pressure ulcer, insect bite)

The hallmark is **obliterative endarteritis** of subcutaneous vessels, causing ischemic necrosis of skin and fascia with bacterial gas production. The infection spreads along fascial planes — Colles' fascia, Scarpa's fascia, Dartos fascia — often with deceptively minimal overlying skin findings initially. The Dartos fascia is relatively resistant to infection spread to the testicular blood supply (which arises directly from the aorta via the testicular arteries), explaining why testicular salvage is usually possible despite extensive scrotal involvement.

### Microbiology

FG is almost invariably polymicrobial (mixed aerobic-anaerobic synergism):<sup>[[2]](#ref2)</sup>

| Category | Common Organisms |
|---|---|
| Aerobic gram-positive | *Staphylococcus aureus*, *Streptococcus* spp. (including Group A *Streptococcus*), *Enterococcus* spp. |
| Aerobic gram-negative | *Escherichia coli*, *Klebsiella pneumoniae*, *Pseudomonas aeruginosa*, *Proteus* spp. |
| Anaerobic | *Bacteroides fragilis*, *Fusobacterium* spp., *Clostridium* spp., *Peptostreptococcus* spp. |

Monomicrobial infections (e.g., Group A *Streptococcus*, *Clostridium perfringens*) do occur but are less common.

## Risk Factors

| Category | Risk Factors |
|---|---|
| **Metabolic** | Diabetes mellitus (most common, present in 40%–70% of cases), obesity, malnutrition |
| **Immunosuppression** | HIV/AIDS, malignancy, corticosteroid use, chemotherapy, organ transplant |
| **Vascular** | Peripheral vascular disease, chronic venous insufficiency |
| **Local predisposing** | Urethral stricture, perianal abscess, anorectal fistula, urinary tract instrumentation, inguinal hernia repair, circumcision, trauma |
| **Behavioral** | Alcohol use disorder, intravenous drug use |
| **Renal** | Chronic kidney disease; notably, SGLT-2 inhibitors (empagliflozin, canagliflozin, dapagliflozin) carry an FDA black-box warning for increased FG risk<sup>[[3]](#ref3)</sup> |

## Clinical Presentation and Diagnosis

### Clinical Features

Early FG may be easily mistaken for cellulitis or simple scrotal/perineal abscess. Progression to frank necrosis can occur within hours.

**Early signs:**
- Perineal, scrotal, or perianal erythema, pain, and edema out of proportion to visible skin changes
- Induration and tenderness of the scrotum or perineum
- Systemic signs: fever, tachycardia, malaise

**Later signs:**
- Skin discoloration: gray, bronze, or violaceous discoloration with blistering
- Crepitus on palpation (subcutaneous gas)
- Foul-smelling, purulent or necrotic discharge
- Skin necrosis with cutaneous gangrene
- Septic shock: hemodynamic instability, altered mental status, organ dysfunction

:::note Key Diagnostic Principle
**The clinical diagnosis of FG is made at the bedside** — do not delay operative debridement to obtain confirmatory imaging in a patient with systemic sepsis and perineal crepitus or necrosis. CT can be valuable for delineating extent of infection and identifying the source in equivocal cases, but time to surgery is the primary determinant of survival.
:::

### Imaging

**CT of the abdomen and pelvis with IV contrast** is the most useful imaging modality when the diagnosis is not obvious:<sup>[[4]](#ref4)</sup>

- Subcutaneous gas is the pathognomonic finding — highly specific for NSTI
- Fascial thickening with fat stranding
- Fluid collections along fascial planes
- May identify the source of infection (anorectal abscess, perirectal fistula, urethral abscess)
- Can guide determination of proximal extent of disease for operative planning

**Plain radiographs** may demonstrate subcutaneous gas but lack sensitivity and anatomic detail.

**Ultrasound** has limited utility for deep fascial plane assessment but may identify superficial fluid collections or testicular perfusion.

### Fournier's Gangrene Severity Index (FGSI)

The FGSI is a validated scoring tool that uses physiological derangements to predict mortality:<sup>[[5]](#ref5)</sup>

| Variable | Deviation from Normal | Points |
|---|---|---|
| Temperature (°C) | ≥41 or &lt;30 | 4; &lt;30 = 4 |
| Temperature | 39–40.9 | 3 |
| Temperature | 38.5–38.9 | 1 |
| Temperature | 36–38.4 (normal) | 0 |
| Temperature | 34–35.9 | 1 |
| Temperature | 32–33.9 | 2 |
| Temperature | 30–31.9 | 3 |
| Heart rate (bpm) | ≥180 | 4 |
| Heart rate | 140–179 | 3 |
| Heart rate | 110–139 | 2 |
| Heart rate | 70–109 (normal) | 0 |
| Heart rate | 55–69 | 2 |
| Heart rate | 40–54 | 3 |
| Heart rate | &lt;40 | 4 |
| Respiratory rate | ≥50 or &lt;6 | 4 |
| Serum sodium (mEq/L) | ≥160 or &lt;111 | 4 |
| Serum creatinine (mg/dL) | >3.5 | 4 |
| Serum potassium (mEq/L) | ≥7.0 or &lt;2.5 | 4 |
| Hematocrit (%) | ≥60 or &lt;20 | 4 |
| WBC (×1,000/mm³) | ≥40 or &lt;1 | 4 |
| Serum bicarbonate (mEq/L) | &lt;15 | 4 |

**Interpretation:** FGSI ≤9 is associated with ~75% survival; FGSI >9 is associated with ~12% survival in the original series. The FGSI provides useful prognostic information but should not delay or defer operative intervention.

**Laboratory Indicators (LRINEC score):** The Laboratory Risk Indicator for Necrotizing Fasciitis (LRINEC score) incorporates WBC, CRP, hemoglobin, sodium, creatinine, and glucose — a score ≥6 has sensitivity ~68% and specificity ~85% for NSTI. A negative LRINEC score does not exclude NSTI.

:::note
Both scoring systems are **prognostic and supportive tools**. The decision to operate must be based on the overall clinical picture and should not be delayed for score calculation in a clinically obvious or rapidly deteriorating patient.
:::

## Surgical Management

### Timing

**Emergency surgical debridement is the cornerstone of treatment and the single most important determinant of survival.** Time from diagnosis to operating room must be minimized. Each additional hour of delay worsens outcomes.<sup>[[6]](#ref6)</sup>

Simultaneous resuscitation (fluid resuscitation, vasopressors if required, blood transfusion) is initiated in parallel with operative preparation — resuscitation does not precede or replace surgery.

### Extent of Debridement

The goal of the initial operation is **complete excision of all infected and necrotic tissue** to healthy, viable, bleeding margins:<sup>[[7]](#ref7)</sup>

- Wide excision of all visibly necrotic skin, subcutaneous tissue, and fascia
- Extend excision along fascial planes until viable tissue with bleeding edges is encountered — gross inspection underestimates the extent of infection
- Scrotum: orchiectomy is rarely required because the testicular blood supply (from the testicular arteries, not the scrotal vessels) is typically preserved even with extensive scrotal destruction
- Penis: penile involvement requires debridement of necrotic Dartos fascia and Buck's fascia; penectomy is very rarely required
- Perineum/anus: debridement may extend to the ischiorectal fossa and perirectal space; fecal diversion (loop colostomy) should be considered when:
  - Anorectal origin is identified
  - Perirectal involvement is extensive
  - Wound contamination from stool is expected to compromise healing
- Urinary diversion: suprapubic catheter (SPT) placement is recommended when extensive perineal involvement is present or urethral injury has occurred

### Intraoperative Assessment

- Healthy tissue bleeds briskly when cut; necrotic tissue does not
- The "finger test": fascial planes in NSTI allow finger dissection without resistance through a normally adherent plane — a positive test indicates necrotizing infection
- Leave the wound **open** after debridement; do not attempt primary closure at the initial operation

### Planned Re-Look Operations

**Systematic planned re-look debridement every 24–48 hours** is standard practice until the wound is demonstrably clean with no residual necrosis:<sup>[[7]](#ref7)</sup>

- First re-look at 24 hours after initial debridement
- Subsequent re-looks every 24–48 hours until wound appears clean, granulating, and free of necrosis
- The average patient requires 3–5 operative debridements; complex cases may require more
- Clinical improvement between operations: decreasing fever, improving hemodynamics, stable wound margins

:::note Re-Look Is Not Optional
Inadequate initial debridement with single-operation approach is strongly associated with increased mortality. Planned re-look is not a sign of incomplete initial surgery — it reflects the biology of the disease and the reality that necrosis evolves over the first 48 hours.
:::

## Antimicrobial Therapy

Broad-spectrum empiric antibiotics must be initiated **immediately** upon diagnosis and continued through the perioperative course:<sup>[[2]](#ref2)</sup>

**Standard empiric regimen (polymicrobial coverage):**
- Beta-lactam/beta-lactamase inhibitor (e.g., piperacillin-tazobactam) **plus** an agent with activity against Group A *Streptococcus* (e.g., vancomycin or clindamycin) **plus** metronidazole for anaerobic coverage
- Alternative: carbapenem (e.g., meropenem or imipenem-cilastatin) for broad coverage in critically ill patients

**Adjuncts:**
- Clindamycin provides anti-toxin effect (inhibits bacterial protein synthesis — especially relevant for streptococcal toxic shock)
- De-escalate antibiotics based on intraoperative wound culture and sensitivity results (cultures should be obtained at time of initial debridement)

Duration: Continue until all signs of systemic infection have resolved, debridement wounds are clearly clean, and the patient is hemodynamically stable. Typical duration is 7–14 days after source control is achieved.

**Hyperbaric oxygen (HBO):** Evidence remains insufficient to recommend HBO as standard adjunct therapy. It may be considered at centers with HBO capability as an adjunct to surgical and antibiotic management, but should never delay operative debridement.

## Wound Management

After each debridement, manage open wounds with:<sup>[[8]](#ref8)</sup>

- Moist dressings (saline-moistened gauze or negative pressure wound therapy [NPWT])
- NPWT (wound VAC) can accelerate granulation tissue formation and reduce need for dressing changes once wound is clean
- Testicles, if exteriorized, can be placed in subcutaneous thigh pouches as a temporary measure
- Daily or twice-daily wound care with antiseptic rinse once wound is stable

## Reconstruction Timing and Options

Reconstruction is deferred until all infection has resolved, the wound is clean with healthy granulation tissue, and systemic illness has resolved — typically **2–4 weeks** after the final debridement:<sup>[[9]](#ref9)</sup>

| Wound Extent | Reconstruction Option |
|---|---|
| Small scrotal defects | Secondary intention healing or primary delayed closure |
| Moderate scrotal defects | Split-thickness skin graft (STSG) |
| Large scrotal defects | STSG; if testicles exteriorized in thigh pouches — reconstruction with local or regional flaps when conditions allow |
| Perineal defects | STSG; gracilis myocutaneous flap for complex perineal floor defects |
| Penile shaft skin loss | STSG over corporal bodies |

Colostomy reversal is typically performed at 3–6 months after the index operation, once the patient has recovered.
