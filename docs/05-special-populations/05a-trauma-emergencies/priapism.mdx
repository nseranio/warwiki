# Priapism

**Priapism** is a persistent penile erection lasting more than 4 hours that continues beyond, or is unrelated to, sexual stimulation.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[2]](#ref2)</sup> It is classified by pathophysiology into three subtypes: **ischemic** (low-flow, veno-occlusive), **non-ischemic** (high-flow, arterial), and **stuttering** (recurrent ischemic), each with different management pathways. Ischemic priapism accounts for roughly 95% of cases and is a urologic emergency: beyond 4 hours, progressive ischemia, acidosis, smooth-muscle injury, and fibrosis destroy corporal tissue, and longer duration reduces the chance of erectile recovery. Medical detumescence can still succeed after 24–36 hours; duration alone does not mandate a particular operation.<sup>[[3]](#ref3)[[4]](#ref4)</sup>

The combined 2022 AUA/SMSNA guideline includes acute ischemic, recurrent ischemic, sickle-cell-associated and non-ischemic priapism, and prolonged erections after intracavernosal medication. Its acute ischemic recommendations were first published in 2021.<sup>[[2]](#ref2)[[3]](#ref3)[[25]](#ref25)</sup>

## Classification and Pathophysiology

### Ischemic (low-flow, veno-occlusive) priapism

Venous outflow obstruction causes blood stasis in the corpora cavernosa, followed by progressive hypoxia, hypercapnia, acidosis, and eventually smooth-muscle necrosis and fibrosis. The corpora are rigid and painful; the glans is soft (spongiosum is not involved).<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup>

**Corporal blood gas:**

| Parameter | Ischemic | Normal arterial |
|---|---|---|
| pO₂ | &lt;30 mmHg | &gt;90 mmHg |
| pCO₂ | &gt;60 mmHg | &lt;40 mmHg |
| pH | &lt;7.25 | ~7.40 |
| Color | Dark, deoxygenated | Bright red |

These are the typical values in AUA/SMSNA Table 5; non-ischemic corporal blood resembles arterial blood.<sup>[[25]](#ref25)</sup>

### Non-ischemic (high-flow, arterial) priapism

Uncontrolled arterial inflow via an arteriocavernosal fistula, usually after blunt perineal or penile trauma (straddle injury, saddle trauma). The corpora remain oxygenated; the erection is partial and non-painful. AUA/SMSNA recommends an initial period of observation for diagnosed non-ischemic priapism (statement 23; Expert Opinion).<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[4]](#ref4)[[25]](#ref25)</sup>

### Stuttering (recurrent ischemic) priapism

Recurrent confirmed ischemic episodes with intervening detumescence; episodes may last less than or more than four hours. Short duration does not exclude recurrent ischemic priapism.<sup>[[3]](#ref3)[[5]](#ref5)[[25]](#ref25)</sup>

### Molecular mechanisms (ischemic)

- Dysregulation of the **NO–cGMP–PDE5 pathway**; chronic hypoxia reduces endothelial NO and alters PDE5 set-point
- **Rho-Rho kinase pathway** dysregulation
- **Adenosine accumulation** signals through the A2B receptor to drive fibrosis<sup>[[6]](#ref6)</sup>
- **Oxidative stress** and reactive-oxygen-species injury
- In sickle-cell disease, intravascular hemolysis leads to impaired NO bioavailability, exaggerated corpus cavernosum relaxation and sickling in the corpora during nocturnal tumescence<sup>[[7]](#ref7)[[8]](#ref8)</sup>

## Epidemiology and Etiology

| Cause | Comment |
|---|---|
| **Sickle cell disease** | Important cause, especially in children; lifetime prevalence estimates vary and may approach half of men with SCD. Do not interpret age at first event among affected men as cumulative risk for all men with SCD<sup>[[5]](#ref5)[[9]](#ref9)</sup> |
| **Intracavernosal injection therapy** | Major cause: papaverine, phentolamine, PGE1 (alprostadil). Can account for &gt;50% of cases in some series<sup>[[10]](#ref10)</sup> |
| **Medications** | Antidepressants (e.g., trazodone) and antipsychotics (e.g., chlorpromazine, risperidone), α-blockers, anticoagulants, recreational cocaine |
| **Hematologic disorders** | Thalassemia, polycythemia vera, multiple myeloma, CML/CLL |
| **Malignancy** | Metastatic infiltration of corpora (rare but reported; bladder, prostate and renal primaries) |
| **Neurologic** | Spinal cord injury (especially cervical), autonomic dysreflexia |
| **Idiopathic** | Significant proportion of adult cases |
| **Trauma** | Perineal or straddle injury; causes non-ischemic (high-flow) priapism specifically. Other reported non-ischemic causes include cavernosal artery laceration from intracavernosal injection therapy, iatrogenic injury from a prior surgical shunt for ischemic priapism, penile revascularization surgery, Peyronie's surgery, and penile tattooing; priapism after spinal cord injury is also more often non-ischemic than ischemic<sup>[[28]](#ref28)[[29]](#ref29)[[30]](#ref30)[[31]](#ref31)[[32]](#ref32)</sup> |

In a US emergency-department sample (2006–2009), the national incidence of priapism was 5.34 per 100,000 males per year, with a 31.4% higher rate of visits in summer than winter months; 13.3% of visits led to hospital admission, and the estimated mean total annual charge for priapism care was about $123.9 million, 86.8% of it from inpatient admissions.<sup>[[33]](#ref33)[[34]](#ref34)</sup>

## Initial Evaluation

### History

AUA/SMSNA calls for medical, sexual and surgical history and examination of the genitalia and perineum (statement 1; Clinical Principle).<sup>[[25]](#ref25)</sup>

- Baseline erectile function
- **Duration of erection** (drives the urgency of intervention)
- Preceding sexual activity, perineal trauma, recreational drugs, new medications
- Prior priapism episodes (stuttering pattern)
- Known sickle-cell, thalassemia, leukemia
- Current ICI use and agent
- **Prior urological surgery, specifically an indwelling penile prosthesis.** An inflated or malfunctioning prosthesis can be mistaken for priapism on history and exam; confirming implant status before corporal aspiration or phenylephrine avoids a wasted or harmful procedure on a prosthetic cylinder

### Physical examination

- **Ischemic**: rigid corpora, soft glans, painful
- **Non-ischemic**: partially tumescent, non-painful, may have perineal ecchymosis
- Examine perineum and genitalia for evidence of recent trauma

### Corporal blood gas

Corporal blood gas is the reference test for distinguishing subtypes. A 19–21 gauge needle is placed laterally into the corpus cavernosum (avoiding the dorsal neurovascular bundle at 12 o'clock and the urethra at 6 o'clock). Aspirate; color is the first clue. Send for gas analysis. AUA/SMSNA recommends corporal blood gas at initial presentation (statement 2; Clinical Principle). Its discussion permits omission at clinician discretion when the diagnosis is clear, including ICI-induced or known recurrent ischemic episodes. Aspiration is also therapeutic; etiologic testing must not delay definitive treatment (statement 4; Expert Opinion).<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[4]](#ref4)[[25]](#ref25)</sup> Reported needle/catheter gauges for this same lateral corporal access vary across sources (16–21 gauge); when higher-volume therapeutic aspiration and irrigation are anticipated, a larger-bore angiocatheter (16–18 gauge) drains viscous, deoxygenated blood more efficiently than a narrow-gauge needle.<sup>[[27]](#ref27)</sup> AUA Appendix B uses a 16–18-gauge butterfly needle near the base at 3 or 9 o'clock in its sample aspiration protocol.<sup>[[25]](#ref25)</sup> In an anesthetized patient, a large-bore needle (14–16 gauge) can instead be placed transglanularly, through the tip of the glans into both corpora; the needle is left in situ rather than repeatedly placed and withdrawn, and connecting tubing between the two corpora can allow aspirating old blood from the contralateral side through a single access point.<sup>[[36]](#ref36)</sup> A transglanular rather than lateral-shaft needle entry has also been reported to reduce the chance of significant shaft ecchymosis and hematoma formation, an author preference without a comparative outcome study.<sup>[[42]](#ref42)</sup>

### Imaging

- **Color Doppler ultrasound (CDUS)** may be used when the ischemic versus non-ischemic diagnosis is indeterminate (AUA/SMSNA statement 3; Expert Opinion).<sup>[[25]](#ref25)</sup> EAU 2026 recommends penile and perineal duplex before corporal aspiration (strong), because aspiration can produce aberrant flow that mimics a non-ischemic or reperfusion picture, and recommends blood gas analysis of aspirated corporal blood in the emergency setting to distinguish the subtype (strong).<sup>[[24]](#ref24)</sup> Ischemic: absent or minimal cavernosal arterial flow. Non-ischemic: high arterial flow, often with visible arteriocavernosal fistula.<sup>[[11]](#ref11)[[12]](#ref12)</sup> In a 52-patient cohort, all non-ischemic cases had a peak systolic velocity above 50 cm/sec, while pre-shunt ischemic cases had peak systolic velocity below 50 cm/sec (some with a high-velocity, high-resistance pattern and diastolic reversal after aspiration); post-shunt ischemic cases had overlapping, variable findings that did not predict outcome.<sup>[[35]](#ref35)</sup>
- **MRI** may help assess cavernosal viability in prolonged or refractory cases. AUA/SMSNA states that MRI is unlikely to have a role in the initial diagnostic and treatment phase because of the urgency of ischemic priapism.<sup>[[25]](#ref25)</sup> EAU 2026 gives MRI a weak recommendation for prolonged or refractory ischemic priapism as an adjunct to predict smooth-muscle viability (sensitivity for non-viable muscle 100% against biopsy in a 38-patient study).<sup>[[24]](#ref24)</sup>
- **CT/MR angiography** defines pudendal arterial anatomy for selective embolization in non-ischemic priapism. AUA/SMSNA instead specifies penile duplex assessment of fistula location and size (statement 24; Expert Opinion), with scanning of the shaft and perineum before embolization; it does not call for routine CT or MR angiography.<sup>[[25]](#ref25)</sup>

## Management — Ischemic Priapism (the Acute Emergency)

AUA/SMSNA recommends prompt first-line treatment with intracavernosal phenylephrine and corporal aspiration, with or without irrigation (statement 8; Moderate Recommendation, Grade C). Observation, oral medication, cold compresses and exercise must not delay definitive therapy for diagnosed acute ischemic priapism (statement 5; Expert Opinion). The pathway allows earlier surgical escalation according to ischemia duration, response and patient goals.<sup>[[2]](#ref2)[[25]](#ref25)</sup>

### First-line treatment — aspiration plus phenylephrine

Laterally into the corpus cavernosum; aspirate 20–30 mL of dark blood. Combine aspiration with intracavernosal phenylephrine, with or without saline irrigation, for acute ischemic priapism.<sup>[[25]](#ref25)</sup> Aspiration-only steps should not create unnecessary treatment delays. After treatment, edema, ecchymosis or partial tumescence may mimic ongoing ischemia. Persistent erection after shunting requires corporal blood gas or Doppler assessment before further surgery (statement 13; Moderate Recommendation, Grade C).<sup>[[25]](#ref25)</sup>

### Intracavernosal phenylephrine within first-line treatment

**Phenylephrine** is the α-agonist of choice because of α1 selectivity and avoidance of β-mediated cardiac stimulation; hypertension and other serious cardiovascular effects remain possible.<sup>[[13]](#ref13)</sup>

- **Use a verified dilute preparation**, ideally pharmacy-prepared, with independent concentration/dose checks and BP/heart-rate monitoring.
- **AUA Appendix A.** Give 100–500 micrograms in 1 mL normal saline, optimally premixed by pharmacy, with doses at least five minutes apart. Aspirate before injection when combining treatments. Monitor blood pressure and heart rate (statement 9; Clinical Principle) and stop injections for blood-pressure changes. Appendix B separately describes a sample aspiration/irrigation cycle with a 3–5-minute wait after injection and a firm-wrap option for rapid refilling; it is not evidence of a superior protocol.<sup>[[25]](#ref25)</sup>
- **EAU adult regimen:** 200 micrograms intracavernosally every 3–5 minutes, maximum 1 mg within one hour (level of evidence 2b; EAU's strong recommendation covers intracavernous sympathomimetic injection when aspiration fails, without a stated drug or dose). At 200 micrograms/mL, each dose is 1 mL. Different institutional/AUA-based regimens exist; avoid mixing a concentration from one protocol with a volume from another.<sup>[[24]](#ref24)</sup>
- **Children and significant cardiovascular disease:** reduce dose and individualize under specialist supervision; a universal pediatric 100-microgram dose/500-microgram ceiling is not established by these guidelines.
- EAU advises avoiding sympathomimetics in malignant/poorly controlled hypertension and with MAO inhibitors; its algorithm footnote also lists a history of cerebrovascular disease as a contraindication and advises pulse and blood-pressure checks during injection and for one hour afterward. Monitor during administration and for adverse effects afterward; stop/escalate for cardiovascular intolerance.<sup>[[24]](#ref24)</sup> AUA/SMSNA instead discusses cautious intracavernosal use with MAO inhibitors, using low starting doses, gradual escalation and cardiovascular monitoring (Appendix A); this differs from the EAU contraindication.<sup>[[25]](#ref25)</sup>

### Repeat aspiration and irrigation within first-line treatment

Corporal saline irrigation and re-aspiration may accompany phenylephrine to clear stagnant blood and coagulum. These are components of combined first-line treatment. AUA advises definitive treatment when repeated attempts over about one hour fail, with earlier escalation appropriate for prolonged ischemia or poor response. Do not restart an additional hour of irrigation after an unsuccessful injection protocol.<sup>[[25]](#ref25)</sup>

### Surgical shunting — for persistent ischemia despite first-line treatment

The shunt creates a communication between the rigid corpus cavernosum and either the glans or the corpus spongiosum, allowing stasis blood to drain.

For detailed operative technique comparisons, see [Priapism Shunts & Decompression](/docs/surgical-techniques/04j-sexual-dysfunction/priapism-shunts-decompression).

**Distal shunts (attempted first):**

| Shunt | Technique |
|---|---|
| **[Winter](/docs/surgical-techniques/04j-sexual-dysfunction/priapism-shunts-decompression#winter-shunt)** | Percutaneous large-bore biopsy needle (Tru-Cut) through the glans into the distal corpus cavernosum, creating a fistula |
| **[Ebbehoj](/docs/surgical-techniques/04j-sexual-dysfunction/priapism-shunts-decompression#ebbehoj-shunt)** | Stab incision with a scalpel through the glans into the distal corpus cavernosum |
| **[T-shunt](/docs/surgical-techniques/04j-sexual-dysfunction/priapism-shunts-decompression#t-shunt-lue)** | T-shaped scalpel incision distally through the glans into corpus cavernosum (variant of Ebbehoj) |
| **[Al-Ghorab](/docs/surgical-techniques/04j-sexual-dysfunction/priapism-shunts-decompression#al-ghorab-shunt)** | Open excision of distal tunica albuginea through a glanular incision; no shunt has established universal comparative superiority |

**Historical proximal/venous shunts:** counsel that evidence is inadequate to quantify their benefit after distal failure (AUA/SMSNA statement 12; Moderate Recommendation, Grade C); they are not an automatic next step.<sup>[[25]](#ref25)</sup>

- **[Quackels (cavernosospongiosum)](/docs/surgical-techniques/04j-sexual-dysfunction/priapism-shunts-decompression#quackels-shunt).** Perineal approach connecting corpus cavernosum to bulbar spongiosum
- **[Grayhack (caverno-saphenous)](/docs/surgical-techniques/04j-sexual-dysfunction/priapism-shunts-decompression#grayhack-shunt).** Saphenous vein mobilized and anastomosed to the corpus cavernosum (largely of historical interest)
- **[Barry (caverno-dorsal vein)](/docs/surgical-techniques/04j-sexual-dysfunction/priapism-shunts-decompression#barry--caverno-dorsal-vein-shunt).** Corpus cavernosum to dorsal penile vein; closure timing described in individual reports is not a standard protocol

### Tunneling and alternative decompression

**Tunneling** extends the distal shunt by passing a dilator (Hegar or Mecholl) proximally through the cavernosum from the shunt site to disrupt ischemic coagulum along the entire length of the corpus. AUA/SMSNA recommends a distal shunt with or without tunneling after first-line treatment fails (statement 10) and considering tunneling for persistent ischemia after a distal shunt (statement 11; both Moderate Recommendations, Grade C). Comparative evidence is limited.<sup>[[2]](#ref2)[[25]](#ref25)</sup>

- **[Burnett snake technique](/docs/surgical-techniques/04j-sexual-dysfunction/priapism-shunts-decompression#burnett-snake-maneuver).** Al-Ghorab shunt with retrograde corporal tunneling with a 7/8 Hegar dilator
- **[Penoscrotal decompression (PSD)](/docs/surgical-techniques/04j-sexual-dysfunction/priapism-shunts-decompression#penoscrotal-decompression).** Glans-sparing proximal corporal decompression through penoscrotal or proximal shaft corporotomies; increasingly used as salvage after failed distal shunts and as an alternative primary surgical strategy for prolonged ischemic priapism<sup>[[14]](#ref14)[[18]](#ref18)[[19]](#ref19)[[20]](#ref20)</sup>

### Step 5b — Penoscrotal decompression (PSD)

PSD treats prolonged ischemic priapism as a **corporal compartment syndrome**. Instead of creating a corporoglanular fistula through the glans, PSD opens the proximal corpora, evacuates stagnant blood and clot directly, irrigates the corporal bodies, and closes the corporotomies. This can spare the glans and avoid some distal-shunt complications while preserving the option for later elective penile prosthesis placement if erectile function does not recover.<sup>[[18]](#ref18)[[19]](#ref19)</sup>

**When to consider PSD:**

| Scenario | Role of PSD |
|---|---|
| **Failed aspiration / irrigation / phenylephrine** | Surgical decompression option when medical detumescence fails |
| **Failed distal shunt or failed corporoglanular tunneling** | Salvage strategy that bypasses the glans and directly clears proximal corporal clot |
| **Prolonged ischemic priapism, especially &gt;36–48 h** | Alternative to glans-based shunting in centers familiar with the technique; counsel that erectile recovery remains time-dependent |
| **PSD failure or very late presentation with nonviable corpora** | Confirm persistent ischemia and discuss prosthesis timing or symptom-focused management through shared decision-making |

**Technique options:**

| Approach | Exposure | Technical notes |
|---|---|---|
| **Original penoscrotal approach** | Longitudinal or transverse incision at the penoscrotal junction; familiar to implant surgeons | Expose ventrolateral tunica, protect urethra with a Foley catheter, perform 2–3 cm longitudinal corporotomy, evacuate dark blood and clot, irrigate to clear return, repeat contralaterally, close tunica with absorbable suture<sup>[[18]](#ref18)[[19]](#ref19)</sup> |
| **Modified proximal shaft approach** | Proximal penile shaft incision or degloving exposure, analogous to penile fracture repair | May make bilateral corporal exposure faster and more familiar for general urologists comfortable with fracture exploration; evacuation, irrigation, and tunical closure are otherwise the same<sup>[[20]](#ref20)</sup> |

**Operative pearls:**

- Place a Foley catheter before dissection so the urethra / corpus spongiosum can be palpated and protected.
- Put corporotomies on the ventrolateral corporal surface, away from the dorsal neurovascular bundle and away from the urethra.
- Evacuate aggressively but deliberately: prolonged cases often contain viscous, gelatinous, or organized clot that will not drain through a small distal shunt.
- **Laterality evidence is observational.** In a 25-patient series, recurrence occurred after 2/10 unilateral versus 0/15 primary bilateral procedures. A 26-patient later series also favored bilateral decompression; these small, selected comparisons do not establish a universal mandate.<sup>[[19]](#ref19)[[20]](#ref20)</sup>
- Apply a light compressive dressing after closure; monitor for recurrent rigidity, hematoma, skin issues, and early pain relief.

### Step 6 — Early penile prosthesis implantation

AUA/SMSNA permits consideration of a prosthesis for untreated acute ischemic priapism &gt;36 hours or refractory disease after shunting, with or without tunneling (statement 14; Expert Opinion). It recommends counseling that erectile recovery is unlikely after &gt;36 hours (statement 7; Moderate Recommendation, Grade B) and discussing early versus delayed placement (statement 15; Moderate Recommendation, Grade C). Its discussion defines early placement as within two weeks. EAU recommends discussing implantation after delayed presentation (&gt;48 hours) or failed injection/distal-shunt therapy (weak).<sup>[[2]](#ref2)[[3]](#ref3)[[24]](#ref24)[[25]](#ref25)</sup>

- Erectile recovery becomes unlikely with prolonged confirmed ischemia, but “zero after 36 hours” is too absolute. Verify the duration, including any intervening detumescence.
- Early implantation can avoid later dense fibrosis and length loss, but carries infection/erosion risks. EAU advises delaying implantation after a shunt to reduce those risks (strong), and notes that interventions beyond 48–72 hours can relieve the erection and pain but do little to prevent later ED.
- Discuss early versus delayed placement, device choice and the patient's goals. A malleable device can later be exchanged for an inflatable implant; neither implantation nor one device type is mandatory.<sup>[[15]](#ref15)[[24]](#ref24)</sup>

See [penile implants — revision scenarios](/docs/surgical-techniques/04j-sexual-dysfunction/penile-implants/revision-scenarios) for the cavernotome toolkit and other techniques used in the fibrosed corpus.

## Special Considerations — Sickle Cell Disease

AUA/SMSNA advises against delaying standard acute treatment for disease-specific systemic interventions (statement 18; Expert Opinion).<sup>[[3]](#ref3)[[25]](#ref25)</sup>

- **Exchange transfusion.** AUA/SMSNA advises against its use as primary treatment (statement 19; Expert Opinion) and that no data show it ends episodes sooner than standard procedures or the natural history. Waiting to arrange it delays effective intervention while ischemic injury progresses; the guideline notes that preparation and exchange with extended-antigen-matched red cells usually takes six hours or more.<sup>[[25]](#ref25)</sup>
- **Simple transfusion to Hb 9–10 g/dL** is reasonable before general anesthesia if surgical shunting is needed, to reduce sickling risk intraoperatively.
- **ASPEN syndrome** (association of sickle cell, priapism, exchange, and neurologic events) has been described after rapid exchange transfusion; this is another argument against reflexive exchange.
- **Sequencing.** Treat acute SCD-associated ischemic priapism with phenylephrine and corporal aspiration, with or without irrigation, before escalating to shunting. Cold compresses and cold irrigation are avoided because they can precipitate intravascular sickling.<sup>[[2]](#ref2)[[3]](#ref3)[[25]](#ref25)</sup>

### Chronic prevention in SCD

After an acute episode resolves, prevention of recurrence is the main goal:

- **Hydroxyurea.** Established SCD therapy; benefit specifically preventing recurrent priapism remains incompletely established
- **Scheduled monthly transfusion** programs in selected patients
- **Pseudoephedrine** at bedtime (limited evidence)
- **PDE5 inhibitors.** Low-dose daily sildenafil has been used with the paradoxical rationale of restoring PDE5 regulatory tone; evidence is limited but mechanism is biologically plausible<sup>[[5]](#ref5)</sup>
- **GnRH analogues / antiandrogens.** Specialist options with limited evidence; counsel about libido, erectile function, fertility and effects on sexual maturation

## Management — Non-Ischemic (High-Flow) Priapism

**Not an emergency.** Management pathway:<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[3]](#ref3)</sup>

1. **Observation and conservative measures.** Observation and site-specific compression; cold measures are avoided in patients with sickle cell disease.<sup>[[24]](#ref24)[[25]](#ref25)</sup>
2. About 60% of high-flow priapism has been reported to resolve spontaneously over weeks (series-based figure). The AUA/SMSNA guideline advises counseling that non-ischemic priapism is not an emergency and offering an initial period of observation (Expert Opinion). EAU 2026 likewise treats it as non-emergent, with definitive management at the treating physician's discretion (weak), site-specific perineal compression as the first step (weak), and androgen deprivation considered only in adults (level 3)
3. **Selective arterial embolization.** AUA/SMSNA recommends offering embolization when non-ischemic priapism persists after observation and the patient wishes to be treated (statement 25; Moderate Recommendation, Grade C).<sup>[[25]](#ref25)</sup>
   - **Temporary agents** include autologous clot and absorbable gelatin sponge (Gelfoam); material choice depends on anatomy, prior treatment and interventional expertise
   - **Permanent agents** include coils and some particles/glues. AUA/SMSNA found no direct comparison establishing an optimal material or a firm difference in erectile outcomes between resorbable and non-resorbable agents; EAU prefers temporary material for a first embolization (weak).<sup>[[24]](#ref24)[[25]](#ref25)</sup> Across reported series, angioembolization resolves non-ischemic priapism in about 73–89% of cases, though repeat treatment is sometimes needed, and post-procedure ED is reported in an estimated 20–25%. Rare complications include perineal abscess, gluteal ischemia and penile gangrene. Counsel about recurrence and ED; comparative estimates are limited by selection and technique.<sup>[[37]](#ref37)[[38]](#ref38)[[39]](#ref39)[[40]](#ref40)[[41]](#ref41)</sup> Separately, AUA/SMSNA's heterogeneous 42-study summary reported successful correction in 85% and post-treatment ED in 15%; these are pooled series estimates, with inconsistent outcome reporting.<sup>[[25]](#ref25)</sup>
4. **Surgical ligation.** AUA/SMSNA recommends repeat embolization over surgical ligation after failed embolization (statement 27; Moderate Recommendation, Grade C), and counseling about ED, recurrence and treatment failure (statement 26; Moderate Recommendation, Grade C).<sup>[[25]](#ref25)</sup> EAU 2026 recommends embolization when conservative management fails (strong), a first embolization with temporary material (weak), repeat embolization with temporary or permanent material for recurrence (weak), and fistula ligation only as a final option after repeated embolization fails (weak), given a high risk of ED with ligation.<sup>[[24]](#ref24)</sup>

## Management — Stuttering / Recurrent Ischemic Priapism

Stuttering (recurrent) ischemic priapism should be distinguished from sleep-related painful erections, a separate entity causing frequent nighttime awakenings from penile pain due to prolonged but non-ischemic nocturnal erections. Management of true stuttering priapism is further complicated by the broad spectrum of presentations in number, frequency and length of the prolonged erections.

**Acute episode:** treat exactly as acute ischemic priapism (aspiration plus phenylephrine).

**Preventive strategies.** The 2022 AUA/SMSNA guideline advises informing patients that optimal prevention strategies are unknown (Conditional Recommendation, Grade C) and that hormonal regulators may impair fertility and sexual function (Strong Recommendation, Grade B). It describes at-home phenylephrine as reasonable for aborting episodes but not as a preventive strategy.<sup>[[25]](#ref25)</sup> EAU 2026 rates its stuttering-priapism recommendations weak (level 3), apart from treating each acute episode as ischemic priapism (strong); its list adds hormonal therapy for frequent relapses (not before sexual maturity), PDE5 inhibitors started only when flaccid, and digoxin, alpha-adrenergic agonists, baclofen, gabapentin or terbutaline only for frequent, uncontrolled relapses.<sup>[[24]](#ref24)</sup> Options reported in the literature:<sup>[[3]](#ref3)</sup>

- **At-home phenylephrine self-injection.** For episodes not yet meeting 4-hour threshold; patient must be thoroughly trained and aware of hypertensive-crisis risk
- **Daily low-dose PDE5 inhibitor.** An off-label preventive option with limited evidence; begin only while flaccid, not to treat an acute episode
- **α-agonist agents** (pseudoephedrine, etilefrine where available)
- **Ketoconazole.** Off-label specialist option; requires attention to hepatotoxicity, adrenal suppression and drug interactions
- **GnRH analogues / antiandrogens.** Limited-evidence options with fertility and sexual-function consequences; avoid before sexual maturation
- **Hydroxyurea** / chronic transfusion for SCD-associated stuttering

**PIN trial (2025):** In 64 men with sickle-cell anemia, this phase 2 feasibility trial did not establish added preventive benefit from tadalafil over placebo when both groups received hydroxyurea (between-group p = 0.654; median follow-up 10 months). It was designed around recruitment, retention and adherence; improvement from each group’s baseline does not establish hydroxyurea efficacy or treatment equivalence. Discuss prevention individually with hematology and sexual-medicine specialists.<sup>[[26]](#ref26)</sup>

## Outcomes

### Duration is the dominant predictor of ED

| Duration | Risk of ED |
|---|---|
| &lt;12 hours | Low |
| 12–24 hours | Moderate |
| 24–36 hours | High; episodes over 20 hours were associated with de novo ED (OR 25.2) in the same single-center series<sup>[[10]](#ref10)</sup> |
| &gt;36 hours | High risk; OR 61.3 was reported in a single-center retrospective series of 186 cases, not a universal risk estimate<sup>[[10]](#ref10)</sup> |
| &gt;48 hours | Recovery is less likely; the cited 43% figure applies to all 35 followed patients in a mixed-duration cohort, not the >48-hour subgroup<sup>[[16]](#ref16)</sup> |

### Other predictors

- In the 35-patient follow-up cohort, complete detumescence was the only significant predictor in multivariable analysis; this is not a universally validated prediction model<sup>[[16]](#ref16)</sup>
- **Degree of fibrosis on MRI.** Heterogeneous low-signal areas from hemosiderin and fibrosis correlate with ED severity
- In a single-county-hospital study of 334 ischemic-priapism encounters, phenylephrine and/or aspiration resolved 78%; some episodes >36 hours still responded<sup>[[17]](#ref17)</sup>
- In that same cohort, shunting occurred in 10%, with lower rates for SCD (2.2%) than other etiologies (15.2%)<sup>[[17]](#ref17)</sup>

### Long-term complications

- **Erectile dysfunction.** The dominant long-term morbidity
- **Penile fibrosis.** Progressive, adenosine-A2B-driven; detected in 20/35 (57%) of a followed mixed-duration cohort, more often after episodes >48 hours<sup>[[6]](#ref6)[[16]](#ref16)</sup>
- **Penile shortening.** Especially with delayed prosthesis implantation
- **Acquired penile curvature**
- **Psychological distress** and relationship-impacting sexual dysfunction<sup>[[5]](#ref5)</sup>

## Prolonged Priapism (≥ 24 hours) — the Operative Window

Prolonged ischemia increases the likelihood of failed medical treatment and later ED, but 24 hours is not an automatic surgical cutoff. Treat promptly and escalate according to response, confirmed perfusion and the patient's goals.<sup>[[2]](#ref2)[[17]](#ref17)[[24]](#ref24)</sup>

**Distal shunts alone have limited durability** in prolonged cases because the ischemic coagulum spans the entire corporal length. Success of traditional distal shunting falls as duration passes 36–48 hours, and glans-based shunts can be limited by recurrent occlusion, urethral injury, cavernosospongiosal fistula, and the inability to clear proximal clot.<sup>[[14]](#ref14)[[18]](#ref18)[[23]](#ref23)</sup>

### Where PSD fits

PSD is a glans-sparing proximal decompression option after failed medical treatment, including selected cases before or after distal shunting. Its place depends on presentation and expertise; it is not a mandatory step before prosthesis discussion.<sup>[[24]](#ref24)</sup>

| Evidence anchor | Findings |
|---|---|
| **Fuchs 2018** | Initial PSD series: 6/6 achieved detumescence and immediate pain relief; acute malleable prosthesis comparators had high revision / extrusion burden in ischemic tissue<sup>[[18]](#ref18)</sup> |
| **Baumgarten 2020** | 25 patients/27 PSD procedures; recurrence 2/10 unilateral versus 0/15 primary bilateral. Spontaneous penetrative erections were reported by **9/15 with documented sexual follow-up**, not 60% of all treated patients<sup>[[19]](#ref19)</sup> |
| **Basile 2025** | Two-center retrospective series, **26 patients**: immediate detumescence 96%, pain relief 92%, composite success 18/26 (69%), complications 2/26. Sexual outcomes sometimes included subsequent prosthesis use<sup>[[20]](#ref20)</sup> |
| **VanDyke 2023/2024 surgeon survey** | Among high-volume male-genital surgeons who had performed both procedures, PSD was perceived as more effective than corporoglanular tunneling (47.3% vs 18.7% rating "very or extremely effective"), although tunneling remains more widely performed<sup>[[21]](#ref21)</sup> |

**Practical approach:**

1. Prompt aspiration/irrigation plus phenylephrine, with cardiovascular monitoring.
2. If ischemia persists, distal shunting ± tunneling; EAU also permits PSD as an alternative first surgical option with a weak recommendation.<sup>[[24]](#ref24)</sup>
3. After a shunt, persistent tumescence may reflect reperfusion rather than ongoing ischemia: reassess with corporal blood gas or Doppler before repeat surgery.<sup>[[2]](#ref2)</sup> Ischemic priapism can evolve into a non-ischemic, high-flow state after treatment, especially after a cavernoglanular or proximal shunt: the shunt itself can create the arteriovenous communication that produces a high-flow picture on repeat exam, which is why equivocal post-shunt tumescence is worked up with color Doppler or blood gas rather than assumed to be treatment failure.
4. Discuss prosthesis options for prolonged confirmed ischemia or refractory disease, including the effect of recent shunting on timing and complication risk.

### PSD vs acute prosthesis

PSD avoids implanting hardware into swollen, ischemically injured tissue and keeps later inflatable penile prosthesis placement available under more controlled conditions. That is its major appeal. Its limitation is equally important: decompression may relieve pain and rigidity without restoring erectile function if smooth-muscle necrosis has already occurred.

Early penile prosthesis remains appropriate when the presentation is very late, corporal viability is doubtful, the patient prioritizes length preservation over native erectile recovery, or PSD / shunting fails. The EAU systematic review found prosthesis reliably resolves priapism but carries nontrivial complication risk, while post-shunting reviews emphasize that evidence after failed shunting remains retrospective and center-dependent.<sup>[[22]](#ref22)[[23]](#ref23)</sup>

The choice between decompression, early implant and later implant is individualized. PSD evidence remains based on small retrospective cohorts, and a surgeon-preference survey cannot establish superiority or predict recovery for an individual patient.<sup>[[18]](#ref18)[[19]](#ref19)[[20]](#ref20)[[21]](#ref21)</sup>
