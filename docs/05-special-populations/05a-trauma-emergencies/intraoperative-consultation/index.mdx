# Intraoperative Urologic Consultation

**Intraoperative urologic consultation** is the urologist's response to suspected or confirmed iatrogenic urinary tract injury (IUTI) or trauma recognized during another specialist's operation. The call typically comes from a colorectal, gynecologic, general, vascular, or obstetric surgeon asking for immediate assessment of the ureter, bladder, urethra, or kidney, with the patient under anesthesia, the field open, and a decision window measured in minutes. The goals are the same every time: immediate recognition, appropriate repair or temporization, preservation of renal function, and a durable plan that prevents the patient from returning to the clinic with a delayed diagnosis.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[2]](#ref2)</sup>

The role has shifted over the last three decades. The old paradigm in which the primary surgeon managed urinary complications has largely given way to consultant-led repair: in one colorectal series, 7 of 9 ureteral injuries (78%) were diagnosed intraoperatively and repaired by a consulting urologist. In a follow-up series of 65 patients with iatrogenic bladder perforation, 63 were treated intraoperatively by a consultant urologist, and long-term success of the vesicorrhaphy was 98.4%.<sup>[[2]](#ref2)</sup> The 2023 WSES guidelines describe the urologist's role in the operating room as fundamental when injury is suspected.<sup>[[2]](#ref2)</sup>

For the specific non-urologic operations that generate these injuries, see the nested [Procedures Causing GU Injury](/docs/special-populations/05a-trauma-emergencies/intraoperative-consultation/procedures-causing-gu-injury) reference. For organ-specific trauma management outside the iatrogenic context, see [Ureteral Trauma](/docs/special-populations/05a-trauma-emergencies/ureteral-trauma), [Bladder Trauma](/docs/special-populations/05a-trauma-emergencies/bladder-trauma), [Renal Trauma](/docs/special-populations/05a-trauma-emergencies/renal-trauma), [PFUI](/docs/special-populations/05a-trauma-emergencies/pfui), and [GU Injury Overview](/docs/special-populations/05a-trauma-emergencies/gu-injury-overview).

## When to Get the Call

Urologic consultation should be obtained in the following situations:<sup>[[2]](#ref2)</sup>

- Suspected or confirmed ureteral injury during pelvic or abdominal surgery
- **Bladder injury** requiring complex repair or involving the trigone / ureteral orifices
- **Urethral injury** during pelvic surgery (rectal resection, taTME)
- **Renal vascular injury** or significant renal trauma
- Uncertainty about urinary tract anatomy or extent of injury
- Need for intraoperative diagnostic maneuvers: cystoscopy, retrograde pyelography, indigo-carmine assessment of ureteral jets

A low threshold is appropriate. Delayed-diagnosis outcomes are worse than intraoperative recognition across every category of IUTI (see the Timing section).

---

## What to Bring

When the call comes from a field outside the urologist's routine practice, arrive prepared:

- **Cystoscope** (flexible and rigid) with light cord and camera
- **Guidewires.** Atraumatic access wires and appropriate support wires; confirm intraluminal position before advancing a stiff support wire
- **Ureteral catheters.** 5-Fr open-ended for retrograde pyelogram; 6-Fr cone tip
- **Double-J ureteral stents** in a range of sizes (4.7-Fr × 22, 24, 26; 6-Fr for edematous ureters)
- **Indigo carmine** (40 mg slow IV) or an appropriate alternative dye for urinary efflux assessment, with agent-specific contraindications checked
- **Absorbable suture**: 4-0 / 5-0 Vicryl or PDS for ureter; 2-0 / 3-0 for bladder
- **Closed-suction drain** (10-Fr round or 15-Fr flat)

Before scrubbing, establish where the injury is, how stable the patient is, what the primary team has already done, and whether anything has been transected, clipped or tied.

## General Repair Principles

Regardless of organ injured, five principles apply:<sup>[[4]](#ref4)[[2]](#ref2)</sup>

1. **Choose repair by injury location.** Ureteral injury above the pelvic brim favors ureteroureterostomy; below, ureteroneocystostomy. Bladder dome injury is managed differently than trigonal injury.
2. **Debride devitalized tissue.** Thermal, crush, and delayed-recognition injuries extend beyond the visible margin; trim back to viable, well-perfused tissue.
3. **Preserve the adventitia.** Over-mobilization strips periureteral blood supply and converts a repairable injury into a necrotic segment.
4. **Tension-free, watertight, mucosa-to-mucosa anastomosis** with spatulated ends and fine absorbable suture.
5. **Stent, drain, and image.** Double-J ureteral stent for every ureteral repair, retroperitoneal drain near every anastomosis, with the follow-up imaging and device-removal plan tailored to the organ and repair.

### Damage Control — "Drain Now, Fix Later"

In the hemodynamically unstable patient, when urologic expertise is limited, or when contamination and tissue condition make durable repair unsafe, use damage-control drainage. Options include:<sup>[[4]](#ref4)[[2]](#ref2)</sup>

- **External urinary drainage.** Percutaneous nephrostomy for upper tract, large-bore Foley ± SPT for lower tract
- For a transected ureter, proximal-stump ligation is an option only with a prompt nephrostomy plan; alternatively externalize/intubate the proximal ureter to drain it
- **Wide retroperitoneal or pelvic drain**
- Reassess definitive repair after resuscitation and source control; timing depends on tissue condition, associated injuries and available expertise, not a universal 5–7-day deadline

Delayed staged repair in this setting can preserve the kidney and avoid converting a reconstructable injury into a nephrectomy indication.

## Ureteral Injury

### Epidemiology and Detection Failure

Large database studies report ureteral injury in about 0.28–0.69% of colorectal operations, and rates are higher in gynecologic procedures.<sup>[[2]](#ref2)</sup> A systematic review of stent- and fluorescence-based prevention techniques reported injury rates of 0–1.9% across its included studies.<sup>[[3]](#ref3)</sup> The dominant clinical problem is detection failure: 50–70% of ureteral injuries are missed intraoperatively, and as few as 17.6% were identified during the primary surgery in one retrospective series of 294 patients.<sup>[[2]](#ref2)</sup> Intraoperative detection, compared with postoperative detection, was associated with lower odds of complication-management failure: pooled OR 0.22 (95% CI 0.12–0.41).<sup>[[6]](#ref6)</sup>

### Intraoperative Recognition

When ureteral injury is suspected, the following maneuvers are high-yield:<sup>[[4]](#ref4)[[2]](#ref2)</sup>

- **Direct inspection** of the ureter in its entire course; mandatory during emergency laparotomy if injury is suspected.
- **Cystoscopy with ureteral efflux assessment.** Useful, but normal jets do not exclude partial, ischemic or thermal injury; absent or bloody efflux warrants further evaluation.
- **Retrograde pyelogram.** Via cystoscopy or through an open cystotomy when cystoscopic access is not feasible. A described technique obtains and saves a fluoroscopic scout image of the distal and mid ureters first, then uses a 30-degree cystoscope with a cone- or whistle-tip catheter through a sheath of at least 21 Fr; dilute (50/50) contrast is injected slowly under active fluoroscopy, following the ureter to the renal pelvis and stopping once both are opacified (usual collecting-system volume 3–10 mL, higher with hydronephrosis), with images reviewed for filling defects, kinking or extravasation.<sup>[[16]](#ref16)</sup>
- **Intravenous indigo carmine** (40 mg slow IV infusion): watch for blue dye leaking into the surgical field or for a visibly absent ureteral jet.
- **Direct injection of methylene blue or fluorescein** into the renal pelvis demonstrates leakage at the injury site.
- **IV sodium fluorescein.** In a prospective observational study after laparoscopic hysterectomy, 25 mg of 10% IV sodium fluorescein produced visible green ureteral-jet coloration on cystoscopy in 28 of 30 patients; a visible jet does not exclude injury, and retrograde pyelography follows if suspicion persists.<sup>[[13]](#ref13)</sup>
- **Retrograde passage of a ureteral catheter.** Helps assess patency; do not force it across an obstruction. A recognized ligation injury requires assessment for suture release and stenting or reconstruction.
- **Limited longitudinal ureterotomy.** In selected cases of substantial fibrosis or anatomic distortion where a stent cannot be safely passed through the existing lumen, a short longitudinal ureterotomy gives direct access: minimize ureteral and periureteral dissection, place lateral stay sutures for traction, pass the stent antegrade into the bladder or retrograde into the kidney, and close around the stent with interrupted absorbable sutures that include adventitia and only superficial ureteral muscle, leaving a drain until output subsides. This is an access maneuver for a reconstructive surgeon, not routine catheterization.<sup>[[12]](#ref12)</sup>

The 2023 WSES guidelines call intraoperative cystoscopy with retrograde pyelogram the gold standard for detecting ureteral injury (weak recommendation, GRADE 2C). It requires a hemodynamically stable patient, urologic expertise and appropriate positioning. When positioning is not feasible, cystotomy for retrograde cannulation is an acceptable alternative.<sup>[[2]](#ref2)</sup>

### Injury Types

- **Complete transection.** The classic recognized injury; straightforward to repair if recognized.
- **Partial transection.** May heal with stenting alone or may require primary repair.
- **Complete occlusion** (ligation or clipping): a common iatrogenic injury pattern (neither the ACS nor the EAU guideline ranks injury types) and the one most likely to be recognized only after postoperative hydronephrosis and flank pain.
- **Thermal / crush injury.** The most subtle and most dangerous. Damage can extend beyond the visible lesion and present later as a leak or stricture; the extent and timing are variable. Thermal injuries require aggressive debridement back to healthy tissue.<sup>[[2]](#ref2)</sup>

### Repair by Location

| Injury level | First-line repair | Adjunct / alternative |
|---|---|---|
| **Upper ureter** (renal pelvis to iliacs) | Ureteroureterostomy | Boari flap + psoas hitch if long defect |
| **Mid ureter** (over iliac vessels) | Ureteroureterostomy | TUU if available contralateral ureter |
| **Distal ureter** (caudal to iliacs) | Ureteroneocystostomy | Psoas hitch, Boari flap |
| **Extensive loss** (&gt;5–8 cm) | Boari flap, ileal substitution | Renal auto-transplantation |
| **Severe peritonitis or physiological instability** | Drainage/source control and staged reconstruction | Major reconstruction only after recovery |

<sup>[[2]](#ref2)[[5]](#ref5)</sup>

**Core technique (applies to all repairs):**<sup>[[4]](#ref4)</sup>

- Debride devitalized ends back to viable, spatulated mucosa
- Spatulate both ends on opposite sides to maximize lumen
- Tension-free, watertight, single-layer interrupted 4-0 or 5-0 absorbable anastomosis
- **Double-J ureteral stent** across the anastomosis, leave 4–6 weeks
- **Closed-suction retroperitoneal drain** near (not on) the anastomosis
- Avoid skeletonization — preserve adventitia and longitudinal collaterals

**Minimally invasive repair.** Laparoscopic and robotic ureteroureterostomy is feasible in experienced hands. Ureteroureterostomy and reimplantation treat different defect locations; uncontrolled operative-time comparisons should not determine procedure selection.<sup>[[2]](#ref2)</sup> In one series, 55.2% of MIS cases were converted to open for urologic reconstruction.<sup>[[2]](#ref2)</sup>

### The Skokan Algorithm

A useful teaching algorithm (widely disseminated through Kato et al.'s *A Clinical Guide to Urologic Emergencies*, Chapter 7, 2021, and attributed to Alexander Skokan, MD) structures the decision flow for iatrogenic ureteral injury around time of recognition:<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup>

- **Intraoperative detection.** Direct evaluation of injury, then spatulated primary repair over DJ stent for short defects; reimplantation (± psoas hitch / Boari) for distal injuries; damage-control drainage if unstable.
- **Postoperative detection.** Delayed-phase CT and, where needed, retrograde pyelography, then retrograde or antegrade stent and/or nephrostomy for drainage, then reconstruction of persistent injury after inflammation and infection are controlled. Timing is individualized.

Drainage is usually the first step in delayed-recognition injury. A well-selected early repair remains possible; persistent obstruction, tissue viability, sepsis and specialist expertise guide the decision.

## Bladder Injury

### Epidemiology

One emergency-laparoscopy case series reported 0.36% bladder injury and recognition of only 1 of 6 injuries intraoperatively. These small-cohort figures are not general detection rates.<sup>[[2]](#ref2)</sup> In gynecologic and obstetric surgery, more than 90% of bladder injuries are recognized intraoperatively.<sup>[[7]](#ref7)</sup> The proximity of the bladder to the operative field and routine intraoperative bladder filling and dye instillation are likely contributors.

### Intraoperative Recognition

Signs of bladder injury:
- Clear or urine-colored fluid in the field
- Visible Foley balloon or mucosa
- Gross hematuria in the Foley drainage
- Gas in the Foley tubing (during laparoscopy: pneumoperitoneum entering the bladder)

**Confirmation maneuvers:**
- Retrograde instillation of methylene blue or saline (300 mL) through the Foley, with direct observation for extravasation
- **Intraoperative cystoscopy** to define the location of injury relative to the trigone and ureteric orifices
- Indigo carmine IV with cystoscopic confirmation of bilateral ureteral jets, which is needed when the injury is near the trigone

### Repair

Direct repair is feasible with good outcomes in most cases:<sup>[[2]](#ref2)[[4]](#ref4)</sup>

- **Watertight absorbable closure.** ACS favors two layers; EAU finds no evidence that two layers are superior to a watertight single layer. Choose by defect and tissue condition
- Urethral catheter drainage for 7–14 days (standard)
- **Suprapubic tube** considered for: spinal cord injury, complex repair, significant hematuria, associated urethral injury, prolonged catheterization needs
- **Closed-suction drain** in the pelvis
- **Post-repair cystogram.** ACS recommends imaging for most repairs, with longer drainage for complex injuries; EAU permits omission after selected simple repairs. Use the [cystography follow-up framework](/docs/evaluation/imaging/cystography) and document the chosen plan

### Special Considerations

- **Injury near the trigone or ureteral orifices.** Place bilateral ureteral stents or retrograde catheters before closure to protect the orifices; if the ureteral orifice is directly involved, perform ureteroneocystostomy into a healthy bladder wall rather than attempting to close over the orifice.<sup>[[2]](#ref2)</sup>
- **Intraperitoneal injury.** Traumatic ruptures and major iatrogenic injuries require repair. Selected small, uncomplicated iatrogenic perforations without peritonitis or ileus may be managed with catheter drainage under close specialist observation.<sup>[[4]](#ref4)[[2]](#ref2)</sup>
- **Extraperitoneal injury.** Most can be managed non-operatively with catheter drainage alone; surgical repair is indicated for injuries involving the bladder neck, associated vaginal / rectal injuries, or pelvic fracture with bony fragment in the bladder.<sup>[[4]](#ref4)</sup>
- **Laparoscopic bladder injury.** Often requires conversion to open for definitive repair, although laparoscopic repair is feasible with expertise.<sup>[[2]](#ref2)</sup>
- **Dense bladder–uterine adhesions.** Advancing a sponge stick or finger aggressively through dense adhesions between the bladder and lower uterine segment or cervix (for example after prior cesarean section) can thin or perforate the bladder; sharp dissection is used instead to mobilize the adherent bladder and obtain a tension-free closure if cystotomy occurs. This inter-organ dissection plane is distinct from using an intravaginal sponge stick as an external landmark during retropubic exposure (see below).<sup>[[11]](#ref11)</sup>

### Transvesical Approach for Complex Extraperitoneal Injury

For extraperitoneal injuries involving the bladder neck or when ureteral orifice proximity is uncertain, a transvesical approach via wide anterior cystotomy provides the best exposure. This allows direct inspection of the entire bladder lumen, including the bladder neck and ureteral orifices, and permits direct ureteral catheterization to rule out concurrent ureteral injury.<sup>[[4]](#ref4)</sup>

When a transvaginal approach is chosen instead, deep vaginal retractors (Heaney, Nichols, Jackson) and a headlamp aid visualization; if a retropubic approach is used, a silicone or metal stent, or a sponge stick, placed in the vagina can help delineate the vaginal wall from the bladder during mobilization and repair. As a general starting point, some sources test the anastomosis with a minimum of 180–240 mL instilled in the bladder and use a larger-caliber urethral catheter (at least 20 Fr) after repair to allow irrigation of clot or debris; select volume and catheter size for the individual repair rather than treating these as fixed rules (see [Methylene Blue](/docs/foundations/pharmacology/intraoperative-adjuncts/visualization-agents/methylene-blue) on leak-test volume). This is established teaching; neither the ACS nor the WSES guideline cited above gives these specifics.

### Bladder Within an Inguinal Hernia

A general surgeon repairing an inguinal hernia who finds the bladder in the sac (present in an estimated 1–4% of inguinal hernias) should page urology intraoperatively.<sup>[[10]](#ref10)</sup> Decompress first with a large-bore urethral catheter; if the herniated portion does not drain, cystoscopic cannulation of the herniated segment and aspiration can decompress it. Once decompressed, the hernia can usually be reduced without resection. Reserve partial bladder resection for bladder wall necrosis, herniation of a true bladder diverticulum, a tight hernia neck, or tumor within the herniated segment; resection can be completed through the inguinal approach or by conversion to midline laparotomy, depending on surgeon comfort and the need for pelvic exposure.<sup>[[10]](#ref10)</sup>

### Outcomes

- **98.4% long-term success** for intraoperative vesicorrhaphy performed by a consultant urologist.<sup>[[2]](#ref2)</sup>
- Approximately 1% complication rate after repair of iatrogenic bladder injury, in 26 studies/595 injuries; meta-regression did not identify an association with catheter duration. This is not proof that any two catheter-duration protocols are equivalent.<sup>[[7]](#ref7)</sup>

## Urethral Injury

### Setting

Iatrogenic urethral injury is rare and is concentrated in operations that involve deep mesorectal dissection: **abdominoperineal resection (APR)** and **transanal total mesorectal excision (taTME)**.<sup>[[2]](#ref2)[[8]](#ref8)</sup> Other settings include radical prostatectomy (intraoperative rectal injury with secondary urethral involvement), complex pelvic reconstruction, and prolonged urethral catheterization leading to pressure necrosis.

### The taTME Experience

A multicenter international study identified 34 urethral injuries during taTME over 7 years.<sup>[[8]](#ref8)</sup> The injuries clustered on the learning curve: 20 of 34 occurred during teams' first 8 cases ("early experience"), with the remainder distributed between cases 12 and 101. Outcomes:<sup>[[8]](#ref8)</sup>

- **22%** conversion to open
- **8%** rate of unplanned APR or Hartmann procedure
- **26%** urethral repair complication rate
- 9% rate of failed repair requiring permanent urinary diversion
- 18% of patients with successful repair reported persistent urinary dysfunction

### Prevention Strategies<sup>[[8]](#ref8)</sup>

- **Structured taTME training and proctoring** for early-experience injuries
- **Stringent case selection** for later-experience injuries related to case complexity
- **Preoperative urethral catheterization** with a large-caliber catheter (e.g., 20-Fr) to aid identification
- Consideration of cystoscopic light-wand illumination or NIR-imaging of the catheter balloon in difficult cases

### Repair

Repair is organ-specific — see [PFUI](/docs/special-populations/05a-trauma-emergencies/pfui) for the reconstructive framework. Principles include tension-free mucosa-to-mucosa anastomosis, prolonged catheter drainage (minimum 21 days, often 4–6 weeks), suprapubic diversion for complex repairs, and delayed posterior urethroplasty when primary repair fails.

**Posterior prostate capsule involvement.** When a perineal colorectal dissection (for example APR) directly involves the posterior prostate capsule rather than the membranous urethra itself, this is a distinct injury pattern from membranous urethral disruption: the prostate can be partially resected with the capsule reapproximated, or the capsular defect simply cauterized, with careful attention to hemostasis and to the proximity of the external sphincter. This is established teaching; the taTME data above do not address this specific injury pattern.

## Renal Injury

Most renal trauma is managed non-operatively. Iatrogenic renal injury during other operations is typically a hilar vascular injury, for example during laparoscopic adrenalectomy, donor nephrectomy, retroperitoneal lymphadenectomy or emergency laparotomy for trauma. See [Renal Trauma](/docs/special-populations/05a-trauma-emergencies/renal-trauma) for the full framework.

### Indications for Intraoperative Operative Management<sup>[[4]](#ref4)[[5]](#ref5)</sup>

- **Hemodynamic instability** not responding to resuscitation
- **Grade V injuries with complete hilar avulsion** (high likelihood of nephrectomy)
- **Severe renal vascular injury** without self-limiting bleeding
- **Main renal vein injury** without self-limiting bleeding (angioembolization is not indicated for venous injury)

### Operative Technique for Grade IV Salvage

For Grade IV injuries in hemodynamically stable patients, renal salvage may be attempted:<sup>[[4]](#ref4)</sup>

- Control bleeding with cautery, topical hemostatic agents (oxidized cellulose, fibrin sealant), and suture ligation of individual vessels
- Repair collecting system injuries with running absorbable sutures (watertight closure)
- Reapproximate parenchymal defects with bolstered capsular sutures (pledget or Gelfoam bolster)
- Consider partial nephrectomy for superior- or inferior-pole injuries involving the collecting system
- **Omental pedicle flap** buttress when local tissue is unavailable
- **Closed-suction drain** after any collecting system repair

### Vascular Injury Management<sup>[[4]](#ref4)[[5]](#ref5)</sup>

- **Segmental vessels.** Ligation can control bleeding, accepting loss of the supplied parenchymal territory when necessary
- **Main renal vein injury.** Primary repair feasible if the lumen is not narrowed &gt;50%
- **Main renal artery injury.** Options include primary repair, end-to-end anastomosis, saphenous vein interposition, or selected endovascular stenting; results depend on injury, ischemia time and expertise, and a single success percentage is misleading
- **Nephrectomy** is the typical outcome for major hilar injuries, particularly in the unstable patient

### Unstable Patient

For an explored high-grade renal injury with ongoing life-threatening hemorrhage, ACS prioritizes rapid nephrectomy when the patient remains unstable or needs ongoing blood/pressor resuscitation. Instability from another source is not, by itself, an indication to remove an otherwise salvageable kidney; do not open a stable blunt-trauma perirenal hematoma merely because another organ is bleeding.<sup>[[4]](#ref4)</sup>

## Prevention — Preoperative Risk Stratification

### High-Risk Situations<sup>[[2]](#ref2)</sup>

Risk factors for IUTI include the following. The odds ratios are from a colectomy database analysis (Coakley) summarized in the WSES guidelines:

- Diverticular disease (OR 2.12)
- T4 malignancy (OR 1.80)
- Open surgery (OR 1.32)
- Previous radiotherapy
- Locally advanced colorectal cancers
- Visceral obesity
- Previous colorectal or gynecologic surgery
- Inflammation and large tumors

### Prophylactic Ureteral Stenting

- **Gynecologic surgery:** a 2023 mixed-design meta-analysis reported an association with fewer injuries (OR 0.61, 95% CI 0.39–0.96; estimated NNT 224).<sup>[[6]](#ref6)</sup>
- A 2024 review of complex gynecologic surgery found no demonstrated reduction in its three RCTs (3,277 participants; RR 0.90, 95% CI 0.49–1.65). Select stents for anatomic identification in high-risk cases, not as proven universal prevention.<sup>[[9]](#ref9)</sup>
- An earlier (2020) systematic review and meta-analysis of five RCTs found a lower ureteral-injury risk with prophylactic catheterization before laparoscopic gynecologic surgery (RR 0.44, 95% CI 0.20–0.97). This earlier, smaller pooled result has not been confirmed by the larger 2024 review above; secondary summaries of this 2020 review disagree on its operative-time and blood-loss findings, so those outcomes are not reported here.<sup>[[15]](#ref15)</sup>
- **Colorectal surgery:** benefit remains uncertain; lighted/fluorescent catheters can aid identification. The 2023 WSES guideline found no randomized comparison of prophylactic stenting versus no stent and recommends selective use in high-risk cases.<sup>[[2]](#ref2)[[6]](#ref6)</sup> In the same NSQIP colectomy database used above for risk factors, prophylactic ureteral catheter placement was associated on multivariate analysis with a lower rate of ureteral injury (OR 0.45, 95% CI 0.25–0.81), but also with higher rates of ileus, wound infection, urinary tract infection and 30-day readmission; this is an observational association from a retrospective database, not proof that stenting prevents injury or a basis for routine placement.<sup>[[14]](#ref14)</sup>
- **Preoperative imaging.** CT with excretory phase defines urinary tract anatomy and variations in high-risk patients.<sup>[[2]](#ref2)</sup>
- **Trocar sites.** The WSES guidelines advise visual inspection of trocar insertion and extraction sites during laparoscopy to avoid unrecognized bladder injury.<sup>[[2]](#ref2)</sup>

## Timing and Outcome

**Timing of recognition** is an important, potentially modifiable determinant of outcome. Intraoperative detection is associated with a markedly lower rate of complication management failure: pooled OR 0.22 (95% CI 0.12–0.41) compared with postoperative detection.<sup>[[6]](#ref6)</sup> Delayed-diagnosis ureteral injuries are more likely to require nephrectomy; delayed bladder injuries are more likely to present as urinary peritonitis, sepsis, or fistula.

In a case series of non-endoscopic bladder injuries summarized in the WSES guidelines, delayed diagnosis was more likely after laparoscopic surgery than after open surgery.<sup>[[2]](#ref2)</sup> Early urologic consultation and a low threshold for intraoperative cystoscopy during high-risk minimally invasive pelvic procedures follow from these data.

## Ethical Decision-Making for Complex or Irreversible Repair

When time and patient stability permit, gather the primary surgeon's account, relevant records and imaging, anesthesiologist input, and the designated surrogate's knowledge of the patient's wishes before committing to a potentially irreversible complex reconstruction or other unplanned procedure. A low-morbidity, reversible intervention such as stenting a low-grade ureteral injury generally does not need this discussion. For a major, higher-morbidity or irreversible reconstruction, weigh patient condition, procedure morbidity, reversibility, long-term quality of life, and the harm of a second anesthetic or delayed repair. Most such decisions are best deferred with temporizing measures until the patient can participate in shared decision-making; selected exceptions are reasonable when deferral itself causes greater harm. A surrogate may not always be present or reachable; when one has not been designated or cannot be reached, the consulting urologist acts in the patient's best interest and remains the ultimate decision maker for the urologic question, drawing on input from the primary surgeon, anesthesiologist and any available surrogate.<sup>[[17]](#ref17)</sup>

## Documentation and Handoff

Every intraoperative consultation should produce:

- **A urology operative note** distinct from the primary team's note: injury type, location, mechanism, repair technique, suture material, stent placement, drain location
- **A postoperative catheter and stent management plan.** Catheter duration, cystogram timing, stent removal date, clinic follow-up
- **Imaging follow-up plan.** An individualized assessment for leak/obstruction and device removal; cystography where indicated after bladder repair
- **Direct communication with the primary team and the patient** (postoperatively) about the injury and the recovery timeline

## Key Guideline Recommendations

### WSES Guidelines (2023)<sup>[[2]](#ref2)</sup>

- Immediate repair of IUTI can be achieved with good results when detected intraoperatively.
- Three factors influence outcome: (1) injury characteristics, (2) patient condition, (3) available urologic expertise.
- The urologist's role in the operating room is fundamental when IUTI is suspected.
- Repair success depends on dynamic management and urologic expertise.

### ACS Best Practices Guidelines (2025)<sup>[[4]](#ref4)</sup>

- Direct inspection of the ureter at laparotomy in patients with suspected ureteral injury.
- Damage-control approach with external urinary drainage for unstable patients.
- **Double-J ureteral stent** for repair of all ureteral injuries, adult and pediatric.
