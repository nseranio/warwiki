# Bladder & Ureteral Trauma

Bladder injuries complicate roughly 1.6% of all blunt trauma admissions and up to 29% of pelvic fractures with gross hematuria.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup> Ureteral injuries are far rarer, accounting for less than 1% of all urologic trauma, with the majority caused by penetrating mechanisms in adults.<sup>[[2]](#ref2)</sup> Delayed or missed diagnosis of ureteral injury carries serious morbidity including urinoma, abscess, stricture, and potential loss of the affected renal unit. This page covers cystography protocols, bladder injury classification and management, ureteral grading, imaging, and operative repair based on the ACS Best Practices Guidelines for Management of Genitourinary Injuries (August 2025).<sup>[[3]](#ref3)</sup>

---

## Bladder Injury

### Mechanism and Classification

Bladder injuries are classified by the location of urinary extravasation relative to the peritoneum:

| Type | Frequency (Blunt) | Mechanism | Key Features |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Extraperitoneal (EPB)** | ~63% | Pelvic ring distortion, bony fragment laceration, or contrecoup force | 60%–90% associated with pelvic fracture; urine confined to extraperitoneal space |
| **Intraperitoneal (IPB)** | ~32% | Hydraulic rupture at bladder dome when the bladder is full | Urinary ascites; peritonitis and ileus risk |
| **Combined** | &lt;5% | High-energy polytrauma | Both IPB and EPB components; requires operative repair |

The highest risk of bladder injury accompanies displaced pelvic ring fractures — particularly pubic symphysis diastasis >1 cm and disruption of the obturator ring with >1 cm displacement.<sup>[[4]](#ref4)</sup>

**Complex extraperitoneal bladder injuries** are a subset that mandate operative repair despite their extraperitoneal location. Features that confer complexity include:<sup>[[3]](#ref3)</sup>

- Concomitant urethral or bladder neck injury
- Concomitant vaginal or rectal injury
- Significant intravesical clot burden compromising catheter function
- Intraluminal foreign bodies (bone fragments, bullets)
- Need for anterior pelvic internal fixation

### AAST Bladder Injury Scale

| Grade | Description |
|---|---|
| **I** | Contusion; intramural hematoma; partial-thickness laceration |
| **II** | Extraperitoneal laceration &lt;2 cm |
| **III** | Extraperitoneal laceration ≥2 cm, or intraperitoneal laceration &lt;2 cm |
| **IV** | Intraperitoneal laceration ≥2 cm |
| **V** | Intraperitoneal or extraperitoneal laceration extending into the bladder neck or ureteral orifice (trigone) |

### Indications for Cystography

Perform CT cystography (CTC) or conventional cystography for patients with any of the following:<sup>[[3]](#ref3)</sup>

- Gross hematuria with pelvic fracture **or** mechanism raising concern for bladder injury
- Penetrating injury with trajectory toward the bladder and **any** degree of hematuria
- Clinical indicators: inability to void, low urine output, abdominal distention, elevated creatinine from peritoneal absorption of urine
- Radiologic signs: low-density perivesical or peritoneal free fluid on CT

For patients with pelvic fracture and **microhematuria alone without high-risk features**, cystography is generally not indicated due to the low incidence of bladder rupture.

:::note Do Not Passively Fill
**Do not use passive antegrade bladder filling** (i.e., clamping the urinary catheter during IV contrast CT) as a substitute for cystography. This method provides inadequate bladder distension and is insufficient for ruling out bladder injury.<sup>[[3]](#ref3)</sup>
:::

### Cystography Protocol

**CT Cystography (CTC) — preferred modality:**
- Add 50 mL of iohexol to 500 mL normal saline (~2–5 gm iodine/100 mL)
- Fill bladder retrograde to a **minimum of 300 mL** (or until patient discomfort or contrast stops flowing)
- Image after full distension; post-drainage images are not routinely needed
- Advantages: no patient transport to fluoroscopy; no rotation required for pelvic fracture patients; CT technologists can perform with Foley in place

**Conventional Cystography:**
- Use higher-density contrast: diatrizoate meglumine (Cystografin) or iothalamate meglumine (Cysto-Conray), ~8–25 gm iodine/100 mL
- Minimum 300 mL retrograde fill
- Images: anteroposterior, bilateral oblique, and post-drainage; add lateral view if suspicion persists
- Sensitivity and specificity equivalent to CTC (~90%–95% sensitive, ~100% specific)<sup>[[5]](#ref5)</sup>

### Nonoperative Management of Bladder Injury

NOM is appropriate for **uncomplicated extraperitoneal bladder injuries** from blunt trauma:<sup>[[3]](#ref3)</sup>

- Continuous large-bore urethral catheter drainage (≥18 Fr)
- Duration: **minimum 7 days**; some centers extend to 2–3 weeks
- Confirm healing with cystography prior to catheter removal
- Antibiotic prophylaxis for catheterization duration is not strongly evidence-based; may be considered with anterior pelvic hardware

### Operative Management of Bladder Injury

**Indications for operative repair:**
- All intraperitoneal bladder injuries
- Bladder injuries found incidentally at exploratory laparotomy (regardless of type)
- All complex extraperitoneal injuries (see features above)
- Penetrating bladder injuries (generally)
- Patients undergoing laparotomy for other indications with concurrent EPB

**Technique:**
- Lower abdominal midline incision
- **Intraperitoneal injuries:** widen the dome rupture to confirm no concomitant extraperitoneal component
- **Extraperitoneal injuries:** transvesical approach via wide anterior cystotomy — provides full bladder lumen exposure including bladder neck; allows ureteral orifice inspection and confirmation of ureteral patency by direct catheterization or observation of clear urine efflux
- **Two-layer closure** with absorbable 2-0 or 3-0 suture (mucosal layer + seromuscular layer)
- Leave a closed-suction pelvic drain
- Urethral catheter drainage is standard; SPT may be added for prolonged drainage needs (spinal cord injury, complicated repairs, associated urethral injury, significant hematuria)
- **Post-repair cystography** at minimum 7 days; extend to 3–4 weeks for complex repairs<sup>[[3]](#ref3)</sup>

:::note Orthopaedic Coordination
When bladder repair and anterior pelvic internal fixation are both indicated, performing them in the same surgical setting is preferred. In symphyseal diastasis, repair the bladder first — pelvic reduction can make subsequent bladder repair technically impossible. No high-quality evidence confirms that SPT placement increases infection rates with anterior pelvic hardware, although most orthopaedic trauma surgeons consider a definitive SPT a relative contraindication to anterior internal fixation.<sup>[[3]](#ref3)</sup>
:::

## Ureteral Injury

### Epidemiology and Mechanism

Ureteral injuries account for &lt;1% of all urologic trauma. The ureter's retroperitoneal location, small size, and mobility protect it from most external forces. When traumatic ureteral injury does occur:<sup>[[3]](#ref3)</sup>

- Penetrating trauma (especially ballistic) is the predominant adult mechanism
- Blunt deceleration accounts for 5%–40%; children are disproportionately affected given hyperextensible vertebral column and less protective perirenal fat
- UPJ avulsion is the classic blunt injury pattern in pediatric patients
- **Hematuria is absent in up to 55% of ureteral injuries** — its absence cannot be used to exclude the diagnosis
- Concomitant injuries with penetrating ureteral trauma: bowel (46%), vascular structures (14%)
- Up to 12.5% of intraoperative ureteral injuries are missed at initial laparotomy; delayed diagnosis leads to urinomas, abscesses, strictures, renal failure, and sepsis<sup>[[6]](#ref6)</sup>

### Imaging and Staging

**CT with delayed phase (excretory phase)** is the recommended imaging modality for stable patients with suspected ureteral injury:<sup>[[3]](#ref3)</sup>

- Indirect signs: periureteral fluid, ipsilateral delayed nephrogram, hydronephrosis
- Diagnostic findings: contrast extravasation; lack of opacification of the ureter distal to the injury

**Retrograde pyelography (RPG)**, performed cystoscopically by a urologist, is the most accurate test for equivocal cases — confirms exact location and extent of injury.

**Intraoperative detection adjuncts** (use when preoperative imaging not obtained or injury suspected at laparotomy):<sup>[[3]](#ref3)</sup>

- Direct visual inspection of ureter
- IV injection of methylene blue or fluorescein
- Direct injection of dye into the renal pelvis
- Retrograde ureteral catheter passage via cystoscopy or cystotomy

:::note Intraoperative Pyelography Limitation
Single-shot IV pyelogram at laparotomy **cannot reliably exclude ureteral injuries** — do not use it as the sole diagnostic method for ureteral evaluation.
:::

### AAST Organ Injury Scale — Ureter

| Grade* | Type | Description |
|---|---|---|
| **I** | Hematoma | Contusion or hematoma without devascularization |
| **II** | Laceration | &lt;50% transection |
| **III** | Laceration | ≥50% transection |
| **IV** | Laceration | Complete transection with &lt;2 cm devascularization |
| **V** | Laceration | Avulsion with >2 cm devascularization |

\*Advance one grade (up to grade III) for bilateral injuries.<sup>[[7]](#ref7)</sup>

### Endoscopic Management

Grade I ureteral contusions can be managed with ureteral stent placement alone.<sup>[[3]](#ref3)</sup>

:::note Ballistic Injury Caution
High-velocity ballistic mechanisms cause microvascular injury that may not be apparent acutely. Delayed ureteral necrosis with extravasation can occur in the first 5–7 days, with subsequent stricture formation over weeks to months. Avoid endoscopic stenting alone for severe or long-segment contusions from ballistic mechanisms.<sup>[[8]](#ref8)</sup>
:::

For **delayed-presentation** incomplete transections: obtain RPG and attempt retrograde ureteral stenting. Place percutaneous nephrostomy tube when stenting fails or leakage persists.

### Surgical Management

#### Principles of Ureteral Repair

All successful repairs require:<sup>[[3]](#ref3)</sup>

1. Complete debridement of devitalized tissue
2. Preservation of periureteral adventitia and vasculature — balance mobilization against vascularity
3. Spatulation of both ureteral ends
4. Tension-free, watertight mucosa-to-mucosa anastomosis with absorbable sutures
5. **Double-J ureteral stent** across the repair (recommended for both adult and pediatric patients)
6. Retroperitoneal closed-suction drain

#### Repair Strategy by Injury Location

**Distal ureter (distal to iliac vessels):**

| Option | Indication |
|---|---|
| Ureteroneocystostomy (ureteral reimplantation) | Preferred repair when distal blood supply is compromised; both intravesical and extravesical approaches acceptable (success 91%–98%)<sup>[[9]](#ref9)</sup> |
| Psoas hitch | When tension-free direct reimplantation is not possible; bladder mobilized and sutured to ipsilateral psoas tendon (success ~97%)<sup>[[10]](#ref10)</sup> |
| Boari flap | Extensive mid-to-lower ureteral defects beyond psoas hitch range; technically demanding — rarely performed acutely; defer to damage control if expertise unavailable |

**Proximal ureter (proximal to iliac vessels):**

| Option | Indication |
|---|---|
| Ureteroureterostomy | Standard repair; spatulated primary anastomosis over double-J stent |
| Heineke-Mikulicz conversion | Incomplete transection found at laparotomy — convert longitudinal laceration to transverse repair over stent |
| Resection + primary repair | High-velocity GSW with blast effect: resect devitalized tissue, then primary repair |
| Ureteropyelostomy | UPJ avulsion: anastomose proximal ureter directly into renal pelvis |

#### Damage Control Strategy

In hemodynamically unstable polytrauma patients, definitive repair must be deferred. Temporizing options include:<sup>[[3]](#ref3)</sup>

- **Cutaneous ureterostomy** (exteriorize ureter through abdominal wall)
- **Ureteral ligation + percutaneous nephrostomy** tube
- **Intubated ureterostomy**: ureteral stent, feeding tube, or small-caliber catheter secured to the proximal ureteral end and externalized to drainage
- In extremis: allow temporary intraperitoneal urinary drainage, suctioned via temporary abdominal closure

Definitive reconstruction is performed at re-exploration after resuscitation, or at subsequent referral to a center with reconstructive urology expertise.

#### Delayed Diagnosis Management

For patients diagnosed in a delayed fashion with no planned subsequent laparotomy:<sup>[[3]](#ref3)</sup>

- Prioritize retrograde ureteral stenting
- Use percutaneous nephrostomy when stenting is not possible or to augment drainage for persistent leakage
