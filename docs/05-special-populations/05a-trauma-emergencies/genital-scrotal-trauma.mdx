# Genital & Scrotal Trauma

**Genital and scrotal trauma** in men involves the penis, anterior urethra, scrotum and testicles. Blunt mechanisms cause up to 75% of testicular injuries,<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[3]](#ref3)</sup> and penetrating penile injuries are usually gunshot injuries.<sup>[[3]](#ref3)</sup> Female genital trauma ranges from superficial labial contusions to complex vaginal lacerations and internal pelvic organ injuries, with straddle injury from bicycle-related trauma being the most common unintentional mechanism.<sup>[[2]](#ref2)</sup> Management below follows the ACS Best Practices Guidelines for Management of Genitourinary Injuries (August 2025).<sup>[[3]](#ref3)</sup>

---

## Testicular Trauma

### Mechanism and Classification

Blunt testicular trauma is the predominant mechanism; typically only one testis is involved, and rupture is found in about half of direct blunt scrotal injuries.<sup>[[4]](#ref4)</sup> Blunt force may cause testicular rupture (parenchymal laceration with tunica albuginea disruption), testicular torsion, hematocele, dislocation, or intratesticular hematoma.<sup>[[3]](#ref3)</sup> Penetrating scrotal trauma is associated with unilateral testicular injury in approximately 50% of cases and bilateral injury in approximately 30%, with spermatic cord vascular or vas deferens injury in up to 9%.<sup>[[3]](#ref3)</sup>

### AAST Testicular Injury Scale

| Grade | Description |
|---|---|
| **I** | Contusion or hematoma |
| **II** | Subclinical laceration of tunica albuginea |
| **III** | Laceration of tunica albuginea with &lt;50% parenchymal loss |
| **IV** | Major laceration of tunica albuginea with ≥50% parenchymal loss |
| **V** | Total testicular destruction or avulsion |

### Evaluation and Imaging

The degree of scrotal pain and swelling does not reliably correlate with the severity of testicular injury.<sup>[[3]](#ref3)</sup> Obtain urinalysis during genital-trauma evaluation; visible hematuria in a male patient warrants retrograde urethrography under the EAU 2026 guideline.<sup>[[4]](#ref4)</sup>

ACS advises scrotal ultrasound with duplex Doppler for all patients with blunt scrotal trauma, pain, ecchymosis and significant swelling.<sup>[[3]](#ref3)</sup> AUA recommends ultrasound for most blunt injuries with findings suggestive of testicular rupture (Statement 30a; Recommendation, Grade C).<sup>[[8]](#ref8)</sup> The EAU strongly recommends ultrasound for suspected testicular trauma; heterogeneous parenchyma with loss of contour is a highly sensitive and specific sign of rupture, but studies disagree on how much ultrasound adds to examination alone. CT and MRI have not been shown to detect rupture more often.<sup>[[4]](#ref4)</sup>

Ultrasound findings suggestive of testicular rupture:
- Parenchymal heterogeneity
- Loss of normal testicular contour
- Disruption or defect in the tunica albuginea

**Ultrasound Limitation.** ACS describes interruption of the tunica albuginea as the most specific sign of rupture, although it is not always visualized.<sup>[[3]](#ref3)</sup> AUA identifies contour loss and heterogeneous parenchymal echotexture as the most specific findings; suspected rupture, equivocal imaging or a large hematoma warrants exploration.<sup>[[8]](#ref8)</sup> If ultrasound is inconclusive and rupture is strongly suspected clinically, do not delay surgical exploration for additional imaging. MRI can be used as a second-line modality for inconclusive cases when exploration is not immediately planned.<sup>[[3]](#ref3)</sup>

Up to 10% of testicular tumors are discovered incidentally at the time of scrotal trauma evaluation; all scrotal ultrasounds should be reviewed for incidental lesions.<sup>[[3]](#ref3)</sup>

Penetrating scrotal trauma does not require imaging before surgical exploration when clinical suspicion of injury is high.

### Management: Blunt Testicular Trauma

| Injury | Management |
|---|---|
| Minor blunt trauma, contained hematoma or hematocele **without** rupture | Conservative: scrotal ice, elevation, analgesics |
| Isolated expanding scrotal hematoma **without** rupture | Surgical exploration and drainage to prevent pressure necrosis and impaired testicular perfusion |
| Hematocele smaller than three times the size of the contralateral testis | Conservative management; the EAU advises repeat ultrasound if needed<sup>[[4]](#ref4)</sup> |
| Hematocele larger than three times the contralateral testis | Early surgery, even without rupture; at minimum evacuate the clot from the tunica vaginalis sac<sup>[[4]](#ref4)</sup> |
| **Testicular dislocation** | Manual replacement with secondary orchidopexy; immediate orchidopexy if manual reduction fails<sup>[[4]](#ref4)</sup> |
| **Testicular rupture**, or inconclusive ultrasound | Urgent surgical exploration, debridement, and repair (see below); the EAU gives a strong recommendation to explore the injured testis in every rupture and every inconclusive study<sup>[[4]](#ref4)</sup> |

AUA recommends exploration, debridement and tunical closure for suspected testicular rupture, with orchiectomy only when nonsalvageable (Statement 30c; Standard, Grade B).<sup>[[8]](#ref8)</sup>

**Testicular repair technique:**<sup>[[3]](#ref3)</sup>

1. Inguinal or scrotal approach (scrotal approach commonly used for acute trauma)
2. Debridement of nonviable, extruded seminiferous tubules
3. Closure of tunica albuginea with absorbable sutures
4. When the albuginea cannot close primarily, a tunica vaginalis flap or graft can cover the defect (AUA Statement 30c discussion). This can preserve tissue, including in a solitary testicle with complex injury<sup>[[3]](#ref3)[[8]](#ref8)</sup>
5. Orchiectomy for completely nonviable or destroyed testicles

**Timing of Repair.** Explore suspected rupture urgently. Reports of better salvage with repair within 3 days describe outcomes, not permission to wait 72 hours. Every effort should be made to achieve testicular salvage.<sup>[[3]](#ref3)</sup> The EAU cites testis preservation in more than 90% with early surgery for large hematoceles, against orchiectomy in 45 to 55% when surgery is delayed beyond three days.<sup>[[4]](#ref4)</sup>

**Intratesticular hematoma** contained within an intact capsule: follow with serial scrotal ultrasound with Doppler until resolution to evaluate for testicular ischemia or an underlying testicular tumor necessitating surgical intervention.<sup>[[3]](#ref3)</sup>

**Bilateral testicular loss.** Complete testicular loss from bilateral orchiectomy for nonviable or destroyed testes leaves the patient hypogonadal. Arrange endocrine follow-up with serum testosterone and counsel on testosterone replacement. This is established teaching; neither the ACS nor the EAU guideline addresses it.

### Management: Penetrating Scrotal Trauma

ACS advises urgent exploration, debridement and repair for all penetrating scrotal trauma.<sup>[[3]](#ref3)</sup> AUA recommends prompt exploration for most penetrating scrotal injuries, with repair or orchiectomy when nonsalvageable (Statement 30b; Recommendation, Grade C), citing frequent testicular injury and limited ultrasound sensitivity.<sup>[[8]](#ref8)</sup>

- Bilateral testicular exploration is warranted based on injury trajectory and symptoms
- When a testicle is injured: attempt repair and salvage to the maximum extent possible; technique mirrors blunt injury repair
- Augmented repair with tunica vaginalis flap when insufficient viable tunica albuginea remains for primary closure
- Injured vas deferens: ligate and debride acutely; delayed repair may be considered
- Complete spermatic cord disruption: the EAU notes that realignment without vasovasostomy may be considered when feasible, with staged microsurgical vasovasostomy later (few cases reported)<sup>[[4]](#ref4)</sup>
- Most scrotal skin defects close primarily because of scrotal elasticity, even when the skin is only minimally attached; extensive tissue loss (for example, blast injury) needs staged reconstruction<sup>[[4]](#ref4)</sup>
- Orchiectomy for nonviable or destroyed testicles

**Venous Thromboembolism Prophylaxis.** Neither the ACS nor the EAU guideline gives a recommendation specific to scrotal exploration; scrotal exploration for hematocele evacuation or testicular repair is a short operation without prolonged immobility in most patients. The EAU trauma guideline's general chapter notes trauma patients overall are at elevated DVT risk and favors compression stockings and LMWH where bleeding risk allows, with early mobilization once able.<sup>[[4]](#ref4)</sup> Apply the individual risk-based approach on the [VTE Prophylaxis](/docs/foundations/pharmacology/perioperative-eras/vte-prophylaxis) hub rather than a fixed regimen.

## Scrotal Degloving and Soft Tissue Injuries

Degloving injuries of the scrotum may result from industrial machinery, motor vehicle trauma, or assault. The dartos fascia and scrotal skin have significant mobility and regenerative capacity. Management principles:

- Irrigate and debride nonviable tissue while preserving potentially viable genital skin (AUA Statement 31; Standard, Grade B). Repeated procedures may precede definitive reconstruction.<sup>[[8]](#ref8)</sup> The EAU notes that scrotal elasticity allows primary closure of most defects, even when the skin is only minimally attached; extensive tissue loss (for example, blast injury) usually needs complex staged reconstruction.<sup>[[4]](#ref4)</sup> As a practical estimate, up to approximately 50% scrotal skin loss can still be closed primarily; this is an author-attributed figure rather than a validated cutoff, and residual skin viability, perfusion, geometry and closure tension still govern the choice.<sup>[[7]](#ref7)</sup>
- Testicles can be temporarily placed in thigh pouches if complete scrotal skin loss prevents primary closure. This is established teaching; neither the ACS nor the EAU guideline addresses it.
- Delayed scrotal reconstruction with split-thickness skin grafting or local flaps once wound is clean
- Avoid tension on testicular blood supply when using temporary thigh pouches

## Female Genital Trauma

### Epidemiology and Mechanism

Female genital trauma involves the vulva, labia majora and minora, clitoris, perineum, vagina, and potentially internal pelvic organs. The most common unintentional mechanism is straddle injury from bicycle-related trauma. Penetrating injuries occur from falls onto sharp objects, pelvic fracture bone fragments, or assault. Sexual assault is an important etiology requiring forensic evaluation and multidisciplinary response.<sup>[[3]](#ref3)</sup>

### Evaluation

- Ensure a chaperone is present for all female genital examinations
- Tailor examination to age, symptoms and suspected depth of injury; a speculum is not routine in prepubertal children
- When abuse or sexual assault is suspected: involve a Sexual Assault Nurse Examiner (SANE) or Sexual Assault Response Team (SART) early. Obtain consent and minimize repeat examinations; use anesthesia when necessary to assess or repair suspected internal injury<sup>[[5]](#ref5)</sup>
- Consider cross-sectional CT imaging when stable enough for scanning and pelvic or internal-organ injury is suspected; do not delay hemorrhage control
- Perform urethral and bladder evaluation (cystography or cystourethroscopy) when gross hematuria or lower urinary tract injury is suspected; the EAU recommends flexible or rigid cystoscopy to exclude urethral and bladder injury, and gynecologic assessment when blood is present at the introitus<sup>[[4]](#ref4)</sup>

### Management by Injury Type

**Labial and vaginal lacerations:**
- Superficial without significant bleeding: local wound care
- Deep or actively bleeding lacerations: surgical debridement and repair with absorbable sutures under local or general anesthesia; thorough irrigation; multi-layer closure when contamination is absent; ligate active bleeding sources

**Large labial or perineal hematomas (>5 cm):** Require incision and drainage to prevent expansion, pressure necrosis, and infection.

**Proximal vaginal canal and cervical injuries:**
- Obtain subspecialty gynecologic care following resuscitation and vaginal packing for hemorrhage control
- Injuries extending beyond vagina and cervix: exploratory laparoscopy or laparotomy to exclude intraperitoneal organ or anorectal involvement
- Perform urologic evaluation when vaginal trauma is associated with gross hematuria or concern for urethral or bladder involvement

**Sexual assault workup** (when applicable):<sup>[[3]](#ref3)</sup>
- Forensic specimen collection
- Testing for sexually transmitted infections
- Emergency contraception consideration
- HIV post-exposure prophylaxis consideration

### Transfer Criteria

Patients with complex vaginal or cervical injuries presenting to lower-level trauma centers should be transferred to a facility with gynecologic and urologic expertise once hemodynamically stabilized.

## Penetrating Penile Trauma

Inspect the wound trajectory and assess for urethral injury when there is blood at the meatus, gross hematuria or inability to void; use retrograde urethrography or cystoscopy when indicated.<sup>[[3]](#ref3)</sup> Small superficial wounds with intact Buck’s fascia may be managed without exploration under the EAU 2026 guideline.<sup>[[4]](#ref4)</sup> For significant penetrating injuries, the ICSM recommends exploration, conservative debridement of nonviable tissue, hemostasis, foreign-body removal and repair of injured corpora or urethra, with urinary diversion in selected cases (Strong; moderate-quality evidence).<sup>[[12]](#ref12)</sup> See [Penile Fracture](/docs/special-populations/05a-trauma-emergencies/penile-fracture) for blunt tunical injury.

## Penile Suspensory-Ligament Rupture

Penile suspensory-ligament injury after intercourse may cause penile-base instability, altered erection angle or a palpable gap below the pubic symphysis. The ligament is also divided in some penile augmentation procedures.<sup>[[6]](#ref6)</sup> A described repair uses a transverse infrapubic incision and nonabsorbable sutures between the tunica albuginea and pubic symphysis; protect the dorsal neurovascular bundle.<sup>[[9]](#ref9)[[10]](#ref10)[[11]](#ref11)</sup> In a retrospective series of 118 repairs for mixed indications, 66 followed sexual trauma; 85% had a composite good result (stability, straightening and return to normal sexual function) at mean follow-up of 8 months, and four patients had long-term erectile dysfunction. These are not trauma-specific outcomes.<sup>[[9]](#ref9)</sup>

## Penile / Glans Amputation

Traumatic penile or glans amputation is rare but represents a true surgical emergency. AUA advises prompt penile replantation (Statement 32; Clinical Principle), without a numerical ischemia cutoff.<sup>[[8]](#ref8)</sup> The EAU advises considering replantation in every patient, within 24 hours of amputation, and prefers a microsurgical repair (dorsal arteries, dorsal vein and nerves, corpora and urethra; the cavernosal arteries are usually too small to repair) with both urethral and suprapubic catheters.<sup>[[4]](#ref4)</sup> Tissue viability and functional recovery vary; skin necrosis, urethral complications and impaired sensation or erections remain possible.<sup>[[4]](#ref4)</sup>

**Field / transfer essentials:**

- Transport the amputated segment in a two-bag system: saline-soaked gauze in the inner bag, placed in a second bag on ice (AUA Statement 32 discussion)<sup>[[3]](#ref3)[[8]](#ref8)</sup>
- Minimize warm and cold ischemia; contact the replant team immediately. Treat the 24-hour window as a target rather than a reason to discard a potentially salvageable segment. The EAU describes washing the part with saline, wrapping it in saline-soaked gauze, placing it in a sterile bag and immersing that bag in iced water, without direct contact with ice<sup>[[4]](#ref4)</sup>
- Hemostasis of the stump with a pressure dressing (the EAU also allows a tourniquet around the stump); do not clamp<sup>[[4]](#ref4)</sup>
- Transfer urgently to a microsurgical-capable center if local expertise is unavailable (ACS 2025 Best Practices)<sup>[[3]](#ref3)</sup>
- AUA advises psychological, interpersonal or reproductive counseling when loss of sexual, urinary or reproductive function is anticipated (Statement 33; Expert Opinion).<sup>[[8]](#ref8)</sup> Provide psychological support; obtain urgent psychiatric assessment and protection from further harm when injury was self-inflicted. An individual recurrence percentage cannot be inferred from selected case reports<sup>[[3]](#ref3)[[4]](#ref4)</sup>

For the complete operative protocol (preoperative preparation, dorsal-artery / dorsal-nerve / deep-dorsal-vein anastomosis steps, optional IEPA repair to prevent shaft-skin necrosis, glans-specific protocol, postoperative leech / HBO / antithrombotic adjuncts, allotransplantation alternative, and pediatric considerations), see the dedicated atlas page: [Microsurgical Penile / Glans Replantation](/docs/surgical-techniques/04e-genital-reconstruction/penile-replantation).

When the amputated segment is non-viable or lost, alternatives include the [Total Anterior Scrotal Flap (Zhao)](/docs/surgical-techniques/04e-genital-reconstruction/total-anterior-scrotal-flap) for partial-loss reconstruction with length deficit, or phalloplasty options under [GAS Masculinizing Surgery](/docs/surgical-techniques/04k-gender-affirming-surgery/masculinizing-surgery) / penile allotransplantation for total loss.

## Pediatric Genital Trauma

### General Considerations

The most common cause of pediatric genital injury is unintentional trauma (straddle injury, zipper entrapment, toilet seat crush injuries). All pediatric genital trauma evaluations must include an assessment for child abuse.<sup>[[3]](#ref3)</sup>

The ACS 2025 best-practices document strongly encourages examination under anesthesia in children, particularly when abuse or intentional injury is suspected, and strongly considers it for female genital trauma when abuse is suspected.<sup>[[3]](#ref3)</sup> The AAP report says most suspected-abuse examinations need no instruments; prepubertal office speculum examination is contraindicated, and suspected intravaginal trauma calls for vaginoscopy under anesthesia.<sup>[[5]](#ref5)</sup> Involve pediatric forensic and multidisciplinary abuse-assessment expertise early to plan the examination.

### Specific Pediatric Injuries

**Penile tourniquet/strangulation:** Penile swelling and erythema with or without urinary complaints in a child requires evaluation for hair, thread, or rubber band tourniquet injuries. Expedited removal prevents ischemia and necrosis.

**Penile zipper injuries:**
- Administer local or general anesthesia based on severity and patient tolerance
- Options: mineral oil lubrication, incising cloth between zipper teeth, metal cutting tool to release the median bar, screwdriver to release the zipper shield

**Penile toilet seat crush injuries:** Usually minor; manage with supportive care. More severe injuries with testicular rupture or urethral injury concerns require standard adult evaluation and management principles.

## Genital Bite Injuries

| Type | Management |
|---|---|
| **Animal bite** | Copious irrigation; tissue debridement; **absorbable suture closure**; prophylactic antibiotics (amoxicillin–clavulanic acid); assess tetanus vaccination and exposure-specific rabies prophylaxis; notify local health department as indicated |
| **Human bite** | Copious irrigation; tissue debridement; prophylactic antibiotics (amoxicillin–clavulanic acid or moxifloxacin); **do not perform primary skin closure** due to high contamination and infection risk |

Human bite wounds carry a high polymicrobial infection risk and must not be closed primarily. Animal bites, conversely, can be closed with absorbable suture after thorough irrigation and debridement.<sup>[[3]](#ref3)</sup> The EAU adds that antibiotics follow local resistance patterns, rabies risk depends on region, animal and whether the attack was provoked, and human bites call for hepatitis B vaccination or immunoglobulin and an offer of HIV post-exposure prophylaxis.<sup>[[4]](#ref4)</sup>
