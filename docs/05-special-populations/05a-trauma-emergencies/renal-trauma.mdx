# Renal Trauma

Renal injuries are the most common genitourinary (GU) injuries, occurring in up to 3% of all civilian trauma patients, with more than 90% resulting from blunt mechanisms.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup> Most are low-grade injuries managed nonoperatively. High-grade renal injuries (AAST grades IV–V) carry the greatest risk of life-threatening hemorrhage and of long-term complications including renovascular hypertension and chronic kidney disease. This page covers imaging indications, AAST grading, nonoperative management (NOM), angioembolization, operative techniques and follow-up, following the ACS Best Practices Guidelines for Management of Genitourinary Injuries (August 2025).<sup>[[2]](#ref2)</sup>

## Epidemiology and Mechanism

Renal trauma occurs most often after motor vehicle collisions, falls from height, and direct blows to the flank. Penetrating injuries (particularly gunshot wounds) carry a higher risk of associated injuries to adjacent structures (liver and duodenum on the right; diaphragm, spleen, stomach, pancreas, and colon on the left).<sup>[[3]](#ref3)</sup> Pediatric patients are disproportionately affected due to underdeveloped cartilaginous ribs and relatively larger kidney-to-body ratio, with renal injuries commonly following pedestrian–motor vehicle crashes and recreational vehicle accidents.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup>

## Indications for Imaging

Plain radiography and ultrasound are not recommended as primary diagnostic tools for suspected renal injury. The ACS guideline calls contrast-enhanced abdominal/pelvic CT the gold standard for hemodynamically stable or stabilized patients.<sup>[[2]](#ref2)</sup><sup>[[4]](#ref4)</sup>

Obtain CT imaging for suspected renal injury when any of the following are present:<sup>[[2]](#ref2)[[4]](#ref4)</sup>

- Gross hematuria (any mechanism)
- Microhematuria with hypotension (AUA threshold: systolic blood pressure &lt;90 mmHg), in the absence of indications for emergent laparotomy
- Penetrating injury to the abdomen, flank or lower chest
- Significant mechanism: fall from height, rapid deceleration, direct flank blow
- Clinical findings of renal trauma: flank ecchymosis, lower rib fractures on chest radiograph

Flank bulging with ecchymosis (Grey-Turner's sign) is described as a classic finding of retroperitoneal hemorrhage. Rib fractures at ribs 11–12 raise concern for renal laceration; groin ecchymosis is a late sign of pelvic hematoma.

**Key point.** Microhematuria without hypotension in a patient with no other high-risk features does not mandate renal imaging. This selective approach reduces unnecessary CT radiation exposure.

## CT Imaging Protocol

| Phase | Timing After Contrast | Clinical Role |
|---|---|---|
| Portal venous phase | ~70 seconds | Essential for parenchymal injury and active bleeding detection<sup>[[5]](#ref5)</sup> |
| Arterial phase | ~20–40 seconds | Optional in the ACR criteria (recommended if vascular injury is suspected, and by AAST and EAU for all trauma imaging); improves detection of pseudoaneurysm (PSA) and arteriovenous fistula (AVF)<sup>[[5]](#ref5)</sup> |
| Delayed (excretory) phase | 3–20 min (optimal ~10 min) | Required to detect collecting system injury and urinary extravasation |

ACS selects delayed-phase imaging when renal injury is demonstrated on portal venous images, the mechanism raises concern for renal or ureteral injury, or unexpected perirenal or periureteral fluid is present.<sup>[[2]](#ref2)</sup> AUA calls for immediate and delayed contrast-enhanced CT images whenever renal injury is suspected (Statement 3; Clinical Principle).<sup>[[4]](#ref4)</sup> When the patient must leave the scanner before review, delayed images may be acquired up to approximately 60 minutes after the initial contrast injection, and the ACS guideline notes that CT without further contrast can show extravasation from an earlier injection.<sup>[[2]](#ref2)</sup>

## AAST Organ Injury Scale — 2025 Update and Historical 2018 Table

AAST published a new renal scale in 2025. Use the [official current scale](https://www.aast.org/resources/trauma-tools/organ-injury-scale.html) for contemporary grading. The table and diagram below describe 2018 criteria, retained to interpret older studies and guideline recommendations; do not silently mix grading versions.<sup>[[6]](#ref6)[[13]](#ref13)</sup>

| Grade | Description |
|---|---|
| **I** | Subcapsular hematoma without parenchymal laceration; parenchymal contusion |
| **II** | Perirenal hematoma confined to Gerota's fascia; parenchymal laceration ≤1 cm depth, no urinary extravasation |
| **III** | Parenchymal laceration >1 cm depth without urinary extravasation; active bleeding contained by Gerota's fascia; PSA or AVF arising from the kidney and contained by Gerota's fascia |
| **IV** | Parenchymal laceration into the collecting system with urinary extravasation; renal pelvis laceration or ureteropelvic junction (UPJ) disruption; segmental renal artery or vein injury; active bleeding extending beyond Gerota's fascia; segmental or complete kidney infarction |
| **V** | Shattered kidney with loss of identifiable anatomy; main renal artery or vein laceration or hilar avulsion; devascularized kidney with active bleeding |

**Grade IV Substratification.** AAST grade IV injuries with segmental renal infarction (without active bleeding) carry a significantly lower hemorrhage risk than other grade IV injuries and have been proposed for downgrade to grade III. Revascularization is not warranted for segmental infarction.<sup>[[7]](#ref7)</sup>

## Nonoperative Management (NOM)

### Eligibility Criteria

AUA recommends noninvasive management for hemodynamically stable renal injury (Statement 4; Standard, Grade B). Patients with blunt or penetrating injuries across grades I–V are candidates when:<sup>[[2]](#ref2)[[4]](#ref4)</sup>

- Hemodynamics are normal on presentation or after initial resuscitation
- No independent indication for operative exploration exists (peritonitis, bowel injury, etc.)

Persistent renal hemorrhagic shock requires intervention. AUA requires immediate intervention after no or only transient response to resuscitation (Statement 5a; Standard, Grade B). For an unstable patient with a deep or complex renal laceration (AAST grades III–V), a perirenal hematoma &gt;4 cm or vascular contrast extravasation warrants immediate embolization or surgery (Statement 5b; Recommendation, Grade C). Hemodynamic stability alone is insufficient for observation when an associated injury requires exploration or renal pelvis/UPJ disruption requires drainage or repair.<sup>[[2]](#ref2)[[4]](#ref4)</sup>

Liberal opening of Gerota's fascia during exploratory laparotomy results in a high rate of nephrectomy, and the ACS guideline favors NOM, with angioembolization when available, even for high-grade injuries.<sup>[[2]](#ref2)</sup> In the MiGUTS grade V series, 15 of 25 image-confirmed patients (60%) were managed without nephrectomy.<sup>[[8]](#ref8)</sup>

### NOM by Injury Grade

| Injury pattern | Management considerations |
|---|---|
| Low-grade, uncomplicated | Observation is usually sufficient when stable |
| High-grade with viable parenchyma | Close monitoring; embolization or drainage when indicated |
| Renal pelvis / UPJ avulsion | Prompt urologic intervention rather than simple observation |
| Active hilar bleeding or failed hemorrhage control | Operative control, potentially nephrectomy |

Success varies with selection and the definition of NOM; angioembolization outcomes must not be presented as success rates for all patients within a grade.<sup>[[2]](#ref2)[[4]](#ref4)</sup>

### NOM Principles

NOM requires a clinical environment capable of serial hemodynamic and laboratory monitoring. Elements include:<sup>[[2]](#ref2)</sup>

- Bed rest and close hemodynamic monitoring (ICU for higher-grade injuries)
- Serial hematocrit measurement
- Availability of urology and interventional radiology consultation
- Observation period: no standardized duration is recommended; 48–72 hours or more may be appropriate for higher-grade injuries, especially when repeat imaging shows no need for intervention
- **Injury to the renal pelvis or proximal ureter** initially managed with NOM but may require expeditious drainage via ureteral stenting or percutaneous nephrostomy within the same admission

## Angioembolization (Renal Artery Angiography with Embolization, RAE)

### Indications

RAE is appropriate for hemodynamically stable patients or those who respond to resuscitation with any of the following:<sup>[[2]](#ref2)</sup>

- Active arterial contrast extravasation on CT (arterial or portal venous phase)
- Renal PSA
- Renal AVF
- Persistent gross hematuria
- Large or expanding perirenal hematoma (hematoma rim distance >3.5 cm independently predicts need for bleeding intervention; each additional centimeter from 1.5 to ≥4 cm increases odds of intervention by approximately 66%)<sup>[[9]](#ref9)</sup>

### Technical Considerations

- **Sub-selective embolization** is preferred over main renal artery embolization to preserve renal parenchyma and function
- In a National Trauma Data Bank cohort (266 AE and 215 surgical repairs, 2013–2018), AE was successful in 94.2% of grade IV and 85.3% of grade V injuries, versus 82.1% and 56% for surgical repair; success meant no subsequent nephrectomy, partial nephrectomy, repair or AE, and the comparison is observational. Reassess ongoing bleeding for repeat AE or surgery. The ACS guideline reports angioembolization failure in 27–29% of patients with active renal hemorrhage, many needing laparotomy, and a 97% success rate for repeat angioembolization<sup>[[10]](#ref10)</sup><sup>[[2]](#ref2)</sup>
- Grade V injuries with complete hilar avulsion most likely require emergent surgical exploration regardless of RAE availability
- Grade V shattered kidney without hilar avulsion can be managed conservatively with RAE as needed
- Hybrid OR suites (combined IR/surgical capability) allow concurrent angioembolization and open exploration in refractory shock

**Clinical Pearl.** A validated nomogram incorporating mechanism, shock, pararenal hematoma presence, hematoma rim distance, and active extravasation predicts need for bleeding control interventions after high-grade renal trauma and can guide RAE versus observation decisions.<sup>[[9]](#ref9)</sup>

## Operative Management

> For the unstable patient (perirenal packing, temporary vascular control, endovascular bridges and nephrectomy without a salvage attempt), see [Damage Control in Genitourinary Trauma](/docs/special-populations/05a-trauma-emergencies/damage-control-gu-trauma).

### Retroperitoneal Zonal Anatomy

A hematoma found on CT or at laparotomy is triaged by its retroperitoneal zone.<sup>[[15]](#ref15)</sup>

| Zone | Location | Usual source | Exploration |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Zone 1** | Central-medial | Great vessels, duodenum, pancreas | Generally explored regardless of expansion |
| **Zone 2** | Lateral (perinephric) | Kidney, then colon | Per the renal-injury criteria below |
| **Zone 3** | Pelvic | Usually pelvic fracture | Often managed with pelvic stabilization and resuscitation; angioembolization for persistent bleeding or instability |

Zone 1 and Zone 3 hematomas typically fall to vascular surgery or orthopedics rather than urology; Zone 2 is the renal surgeon's usual concern.

### Indications for Exploration

**Absolute:**
- Hemodynamic instability unresponsive to resuscitation with active renal hemorrhage
- Grade V hilar avulsion
- Expanding, pulsatile, or ruptured Zone II retroperitoneal hematoma

**Blunt trauma:** A non-expanding Zone II retroperitoneal hematoma found at laparotomy for other injuries should not be explored; it is associated with significantly higher nephrectomy rates. Obtain postoperative CT for characterization.<sup>[[2]](#ref2)</sup>

**Penetrating trauma:** Zone II hematomas after penetrating injury may be explored due to increased likelihood of clinically significant renal pelvis, ureteral, or vascular injury.

### Preoperative Preparation

- Midline incision from xyphoid to pubis
- Balanced resuscitation (permissive hypotension, limited crystalloid, blood product transfusion)
- Activate massive transfusion protocol when appropriate
- Before exploring the injured kidney: palpate the contralateral kidney. If absent or abnormal (polycystic, atrophic), a single-shot IV pyelogram (AUA: 2 mL/kg contrast with an image at 10–15 minutes) may demonstrate contralateral excretion; poor opacification in shock is inconclusive and must not delay lifesaving control<sup>[[4]](#ref4)</sup>

### Renal Exposure Approaches

**Medial approach:** Opens retroperitoneum in the midline to isolate renal artery and vein before entering Gerota's fascia. Provides definitive vascular control but may be difficult with large central hematomas distorting hilar anatomy. When a large retroperitoneal hematoma obscures direct visualization or palpation of the aorta, the inferior mesenteric vein in the left-colon mesentery is a useful landmark: incise just medial to it, cephalad to the inferior mesenteric artery, toward the ligament of Treitz. The left renal vein crossing anterior to the aorta is the first major vessel encountered and the key hilar landmark, with the left renal artery usually just posterior and slightly superior to it; right hilar access is through the same incision, elevating the left renal vein and dissecting in the intra-aortocaval space to find the right renal artery, with the right renal vein lying lateral to the vena cava at a similar level. Leave the renal vessels unclamped unless major bleeding requires control, to limit warm ischemia.<sup>[[17]](#ref17)</sup>

**Lateral approach:** Mobilizes the colon and opens the hematoma directly; kidney is rapidly mobilized with manual hilar compression available. Allows rapid inspection of parenchyma, collecting system, and vasculature.

### Operative Repair by Grade

| Grade | Operative Approach |
|---|---|
| **I–III** | Cautery, topical hemostatic agents, absorbable suture ligation of individual parenchymal vessels; partial nephrectomy if parenchyma cannot be approximated |
| **IV (stable)** | Parenchymal bleeding controlled as above; collecting system closed with running absorbable suture (watertight); Gerota's fascia or omental flap for buttress; closed-suction drain left in place. When the remaining capsule cannot cover a large polar defect, absorbable polyglactin mesh fitted to the defect and secured with interrupted sutures is a described alternative, with omentum mobilized over the repair when available.<sup>[[18]](#ref18)</sup> Two atlas sources instead favor a passive (Penrose) drain through a separate incision for a collecting-system repair, citing concern that suction could promote a urinary fistula; this is author teaching without comparative outcome data, and does not establish that the closed-suction approach above is unsafe.<sup>[[17]](#ref17)[[18]](#ref18)</sup> |
| **Hemodynamically unstable, or grade V found at exploration** | ACS advises nephrectomy without a salvage attempt in a patient who is unstable or needs ongoing transfusion and pressors, and for a shattered or avulsed kidney found at exploration. Nonoperative management of stable grade V injury is described above; reconstruction is attempted only when physiology and viable tissue permit<sup>[[2]](#ref2)[[14]](#ref14)</sup> |
| **Segmental vessels** | Ligation may control bleeding but sacrifices the supplied segment; assess remaining renal function |
| **Main renal vein injury** | Primary repair if lumen not narrowed >50%; left renal vein can tolerate greater narrowing due to collateral drainage; right-side compromise → nephrectomy |
| **Main renal artery injury** | Primary repair, end-to-end anastomosis, saphenous vein interposition, or hybrid endovascular stenting; outcomes are poor and nephrectomy is common |

Earlier uncontrolled analyses suggested nephrectomy was independently associated with higher mortality after trauma, but could not adjust for transfusion volume as a marker of hemorrhage severity. A propensity-score analysis of 12,780 TQIP patients with high-grade renal trauma (1,014 nephrectomies) found unadjusted mortality was higher after nephrectomy (10.6% vs 4.2%), but after adjusting for blood products transfused and other factors, nephrectomy was not independently associated with mortality or acute kidney injury. The excess mortality historically attributed to nephrectomy more likely reflects overall injury and transfusion burden than a direct effect of the operation; trauma nephrectomy should still be avoided when the physiology and tissue permit an alternative.<sup>[[16]](#ref16)</sup>

## Follow-Up Imaging

### Early Follow-Up (48–72 Hours)

Obtain contrast-enhanced CT with delayed phase within 48–72 hours for:<sup>[[2]](#ref2)</sup>

- Grades III–V injuries with ongoing blood loss or urine extravasation on initial imaging
- Penetrating destructive firearm injury (grade III or above)
- Symptoms suggesting complications (fever, worsening flank pain, ongoing blood loss)

Routine repeat CT is usually unnecessary for uncomplicated low-grade injury. AUA recommends follow-up CT for deep grade IV–V injury or clinical complication signs (Statement 7; Recommendation, Grade C). Its discussion places routine deep-injury imaging after 48 hours; fever, worsening flank pain, ongoing blood loss or abdominal distension prompts imaging without waiting for that interval. EAU strongly recommends repeat imaging, two to four days after injury, in high-grade and penetrating injuries and for fever, worsening flank pain or a falling hematocrit, and states that it can be omitted in grade 1–3 injuries that stay clinically well. Retrospective studies questioning routine imaging do not establish universal safe omission.<sup>[[4]](#ref4)[[11]](#ref11)[[14]](#ref14)</sup>

### Late Follow-Up (Weeks to Months)

Symptoms prompt imaging regardless of the planned follow-up:<sup>[[2]](#ref2)</sup>

- New-onset gross hematuria
- Clinical signs or symptoms of renal trauma complications

EAU gives a weak recommendation for review approximately 3 months after major injury, with urinalysis, renal function tests, blood pressure and individualized imaging (scintigraphy, CT or ultrasound); annual blood-pressure follow-up continues long term. This does not require routine CT after every minor injury.<sup>[[14]](#ref14)</sup>

## Complications

| Complication | Notes |
|---|---|
| **Delayed hemorrhage** | May present days to weeks post-injury; secondary PSA or AVF; managed with repeat RAE |
| **Urine leak / urinoma** | Stable parenchymal collecting-system leaks may be observed when renal pelvis or proximal ureteral avulsion is not suspected (AUA Statement 6; Clinical Principle). An enlarging urinoma, fever, increasing pain, ileus, fistula or infection warrants drainage (Statement 8; Recommendation, Grade C), usually by ureteral stent with percutaneous urinoma drainage or nephrostomy added when needed (Expert Opinion)<sup>[[4]](#ref4)</sup> |
| **Renovascular hypertension** | Rare but serious; associated with renal artery injury, large devitalized fragments, or Page kidney phenomenon (circumferential hematoma compressing parenchyma); activates renin-angiotensin system; may respond to antihypertensive treatment, drainage or selected surgery; the cited ten-patient referral series does not establish that most patients need nephrectomy;<sup>[[12]](#ref12)</sup> ACS advises primary care follow-up for new hypertension after nonoperative management of a high-grade injury, with referral to urology or nephrology as needed<sup>[[2]](#ref2)</sup> |
| **Abscess / perinephric infection** | Requires percutaneous drainage |
| **Renal insufficiency** | Follow renal function in high-grade injuries, especially with contralateral renal disease |

**Urine leak after partial nephrectomy.** A urine leak after an elective partial nephrectomy, when the collecting system was entered and closed intraoperatively, is managed on similar drainage principles to a traumatic collecting-system injury. The chapter authors describe most leaks appearing within 72 hours; when drain output is high (typically more than 50 mL per 24 hours), they compare drain-fluid and serum creatinine, and consider testing unnecessary when output is low. Low output in the first 48 hours makes a leak unlikely enough that the drain can usually be removed at about 48 hours, and high output with high drain-fluid creatinine does not by itself require imaging if the patient has no significant symptoms. Persistent abdominal pain, fever, ileus, urine from the incision, or a disproportionate creatinine rise prompts cross-sectional imaging. Initial management is adequate drainage of the urinoma, with an additional percutaneous drain if the existing drain is insufficient; if the collection has not resolved within 48 hours or symptoms persist beyond 24 hours, the authors advise retrograde double-J stenting (kept low-pressure to avoid worsening the defect) and Foley drainage. Most fistulas managed this way resolve over 4-6 weeks, and reoperation is uncommon. These are the chapter authors' reported intervals and practice, not a validated comparative algorithm.<sup>[[20]](#ref20)</sup>

**Pediatric Considerations.** In children, hypotension is a late sign of shock and should not be used as the primary trigger for CT imaging; instead, base decisions on hematuria and associated injury patterns. Ultrasound is insufficient for initial renal injury evaluation. NOM is recommended for all grades based on hemodynamic stability. Individualize follow-up to injury severity and the pediatric trauma pathway; avoid unnecessary repeat CT.<sup>[[2]](#ref2)</sup> A preexisting, often unrecognized UPJ obstruction or a duplicated collecting system changes how a urine leak behaves in a child with renal trauma: an obstructed system can drain more slowly than routine observation expects, so delayed-phase CT or retrograde pyelography should specifically assess for a renal pelvis laceration or UPJ disruption. When obstruction is confirmed, prompt ureteral stenting (with temporary urinoma drainage if needed) followed by pyeloplasty once the leak has healed is a described sequence; one center's reported practice removed the stent before a robotic pyeloplasty 8–12 weeks later, with minimal periureteral scarring. In a duplex kidney, confirm which moiety is leaking before stenting, since a stent in the wrong ureter will not divert the leak.<sup>[[19]](#ref19)</sup>

**Geriatric Considerations.** Anticoagulation status must be assessed on presentation; reversal should be considered for hemodynamically unstable patients or those with significant blood loss. Do not withhold contrast-enhanced CT based on age alone; evidence linking age alone to contrast-induced nephropathy is inconclusive. Early thromboembolism chemoprophylaxis should be considered for low-grade injuries in stable geriatric patients.<sup>[[2]](#ref2)</sup>
