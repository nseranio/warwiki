# Bladder Trauma

**Bladder trauma** is rupture or laceration of the bladder wall, most often after blunt injury. Risk estimates depend on the population: a single-center series of 5,518 pelvic fractures found bladder injury in 3.2% of men and 3.3% of women; the AUA guideline reports bladder injury in approximately 29% of patients with both pelvic fracture and gross hematuria, and a prospective single-center study of blunt trauma found bladder injury in 27% (21/78) and 16% (15/91) of patients imaged for gross hematuria across its two study periods — figures from a different selected population than the guideline's 29% estimate.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[5]](#ref5)[[8]](#ref8)</sup> The most common mechanism is pelvic ring distortion transmitting force to the perivesical structures. Hydraulic rupture at a distended dome is the hallmark of intraperitoneal injury. Diagnosis uses dedicated retrograde cystography rather than passive filling, and repair strategy follows injury type.

**Cross-Reference — Imaging.** Detailed cystography technique and CT protocol interpretation is covered in [Imaging](/docs/evaluation/imaging) (Evaluation & Workup). A protocol summary is included below.

## Classification

Bladder injuries are classified by the location of urinary extravasation relative to the peritoneum:

| Type | Frequency (Blunt) | Mechanism | Key Features |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Extraperitoneal (EPB)** | ~63%<sup>[[3]](#ref3)</sup> | Pelvic ring distortion, bony fragment, contrecoup | 60–90% associated with pelvic fracture; urine confined to extraperitoneal space |
| **Intraperitoneal (IPB)** | ~32%<sup>[[3]](#ref3)</sup> | Hydraulic rupture of distended dome | Urinary ascites; ileus; risk of peritonitis |
| **Combined** | &lt;5% (ACS, blunt trauma)<sup>[[3]](#ref3)</sup>; 5–8% (EAU, mechanism unspecified)<sup>[[6]](#ref6)</sup> | High-energy polytrauma | Both components; operative repair required |

The highest-risk fracture patterns are pubic symphysis diastasis >1 cm and obturator ring disruption with >1 cm displacement.<sup>[[2]](#ref2)</sup>

### AAST Bladder Injury Scale

| Grade | Description |
|---|---|
| **I** | Contusion; intramural hematoma; partial-thickness laceration |
| **II** | Extraperitoneal laceration &lt;2 cm |
| **III** | Extraperitoneal laceration ≥2 cm, or intraperitoneal laceration &lt;2 cm |
| **IV** | Intraperitoneal laceration ≥2 cm |
| **V** | Extension into the bladder neck or ureteral orifice (trigone) |

Advance one grade for multiple bladder lesions, up to Grade III, following the AAST organ injury scale convention.<sup>[[7]](#ref7)</sup>

### Complex Extraperitoneal Injury

Features favoring repair include the following; the pelvic-fixation indication requires coordination with the trauma and orthopaedic teams.<sup>[[3]](#ref3)[[5]](#ref5)[[6]](#ref6)</sup>

- Concomitant urethral or bladder neck injury
- Concomitant vaginal or rectal injury
- Intravesical clot burden impairing catheter drainage
- Intraluminal foreign body (bone fragment, projectile)
- Planned anterior pelvic internal fixation: ACS favors repair; EAU notes conflicting evidence on hardware infection

## Imaging — Cystography Protocol

**Indication:** In stable patients, gross hematuria with pelvic fracture is an AUA Standard (Statement 14a; Grade B). Gross hematuria with a concerning mechanism, or pelvic-ring fracture with clinical indicators of rupture (low urine output, urinary ascites, rising creatinine or abdominal distension), also warrants retrograde cystography (Statement 14b; Recommendation, Grade C). Penetrating pelvic injury with gross or microscopic hematuria requires bladder evaluation.<sup>[[3]](#ref3)[[5]](#ref5)</sup>

Microhematuria alone without high-risk features in stable blunt trauma does not require cystography. Gross hematuria is the principal sign: a prospective single-center comparison reported 100% sensitivity and 98.5% specificity for gross hematuria as its screening trigger, but patients with microscopic hematuria in the second study period did not undergo cystography.<sup>[[8]](#ref8)</sup> A separate 44-case series found gross hematuria in 95%.<sup>[[9]](#ref9)</sup> Microscopic hematuria with a high-risk pelvic fracture or posterior urethral injury can still warrant cystography under the EAU guideline.<sup>[[5]](#ref5)[[6]](#ref6)</sup>

Passive antegrade filling (clamping the Foley during CT) does not distend the bladder adequately. The ACS guideline states it is inadequate to evaluate for bladder injury and is not a substitute for retrograde cystography.<sup>[[3]](#ref3)[[5]](#ref5)</sup>

### CT Cystography (Preferred)

- Dilute contrast retrograde fill: 50 mL iohexol in 500 mL normal saline (approximately 2–5 g iodine/100 mL)<sup>[[3]](#ref3)</sup>
- Minimum fill volume: 300 mL (or until patient discomfort or contrast stops flowing); EAU strongly recommends active retrograde filling with 300–350 mL of dilute contrast<sup>[[6]](#ref6)</sup>
- Image after full distension; post-drainage images are not routinely required
- CT cystography can be performed by the radiology technologist with the Foley in place; no need for patient transport to fluoroscopy

### Conventional (Fluoroscopic) Cystography

- Higher-density contrast: diatrizoate meglumine (Cystografin) or iothalamate meglumine at approximately 8–25 g iodine/100 mL<sup>[[3]](#ref3)</sup>
- Retrograde gravity filling to at least 300 mL or patient tolerance; AUA requires a maximal-fill image and a post-drainage image, with additional views as needed<sup>[[5]](#ref5)</sup>
- Views: AP, bilateral obliques and post-drainage images; add a lateral view if concern persists without a visible leak
- Sensitivity and specificity are similar to CT cystography (approximately 90–95% sensitivity, approximately 100% specificity)<sup>[[3]](#ref3)[[4]](#ref4)</sup>

## Management

### Nonoperative Management (Uncomplicated EPB)

AUA recommends catheter drainage for uncomplicated extraperitoneal bladder injury (Statement 16; Recommendation, Grade C). The following ACS pathway concerns blunt trauma:<sup>[[3]](#ref3)[[5]](#ref5)</sup>

- Continuous large-bore urethral catheter drainage (≥18 Fr)
- Drainage duration differs by guideline: ACS specifies at least 7 days; AUA describes 2–3 weeks as standard, adjusted for healing and associated injuries<sup>[[3]](#ref3)[[5]](#ref5)</sup>
- Confirm healing with repeat cystography before catheter removal; EAU plans the first cystogram approximately 10 days after injury and gives a weak recommendation for conservative management of uncomplicated blunt extraperitoneal injury<sup>[[6]](#ref6)</sup>
- No high-level evidence supports antibiotic prophylaxis for the catheterization duration; ACS notes it may be considered with anterior pelvic hardware<sup>[[3]](#ref3)</sup>

### Operative Management

> For temporary drainage and deferred repair in the unstable patient, see [Damage Control in Genitourinary Trauma](/docs/special-populations/05a-trauma-emergencies/damage-control-gu-trauma).

**Indications for operative repair:**
- Intraperitoneal rupture from blunt or penetrating external trauma (AUA Statement 15; Standard, Grade B); selected small uncomplicated iatrogenic perforations follow a separate conservative-management pathway<sup>[[5]](#ref5)[[6]](#ref6)</sup>
- Complicated extraperitoneal injury (AUA Statement 17; Recommendation, Grade C; see above)<sup>[[5]](#ref5)</sup>
- Bladder injuries found incidentally at exploratory laparotomy: repair according to injury type
- Most penetrating bladder injuries require exploration; in gunshot injury, inspect for both entry and exit wounds and associated bowel or rectal injury.<sup>[[6]](#ref6)</sup>
- EPB during laparotomy for other injuries or pelvic ORIF: AUA advises considering concurrent bladder repair; this is not an automatic indication to repair every uncomplicated EPB<sup>[[5]](#ref5)</sup>
- Injury to a neobladder, continent reservoir or augmented bladder: healing is unpredictable and complication risk is high, so most need surgery, and ACS suggests considering tertiary referral<sup>[[3]](#ref3)</sup>

**Technique:**
- Open exposure, usually through a lower midline incision; laparoscopic repair is possible in selected isolated injuries
- **IPB:** With a pelvic fracture, open the injury widely enough to confirm that no concomitant extraperitoneal injury needs repair
- **EPB (transvesical approach):** Wide anterior cystotomy provides full intraluminal exposure including bladder neck and trigone; identify the ureteral orifices and confirm drainage, with catheterization when needed
- Use absorbable suture and a watertight closure. ACS favors two layers; EAU finds no evidence that two layers outperform a watertight single layer<sup>[[3]](#ref3)[[6]](#ref6)</sup>. EAU recommends surgical repair of blunt intraperitoneal rupture (strong) and of extraperitoneal rupture with bladder neck involvement or associated injuries needing surgery (strong)<sup>[[6]](#ref6)</sup>
- Leave a closed-suction pelvic drain
- AUA recommends urethral catheter drainage alone after repair (Statement 18; Standard, Grade B). Selected exceptions for an SPT include long-term catheterization, immobilization, a tenuous complex closure or significant hematuria<sup>[[5]](#ref5)</sup>
- Confirm healing after complex repair and catheter-only treatment. ACS recommends cystography for most repairs at ≥7 days, later for complex injuries; AUA/EAU allow omission after selected simple repairs; EAU states that after repair of a simple injury in a healthy patient the catheter can be removed at 5–10 days without cystography, and strongly recommends cystography after complex repair or with wound-healing risk factors such as steroids or malnutrition. See [cystography follow-up](/docs/evaluation/imaging/cystography) for the guideline differences<sup>[[3]](#ref3)[[5]](#ref5)[[6]](#ref6)</sup>

**Orthopaedic Coordination.** When bladder repair and anterior pelvic internal fixation are both indicated, performing them in the same surgical setting is preferred. With symphyseal diastasis, consider repairing the bladder first, because reduction can make the repair more difficult or impossible. Discuss catheter positioning with orthopaedics. AUA and EAU find no evidence that appropriate suprapubic drainage increases hardware infection, and ACS reports no adequate studies either way; ACS also notes that many orthopaedic surgeons still regard a definitive SPT as a contraindication to anterior internal fixation and use external fixation instead.<sup>[[5]](#ref5)[[6]](#ref6)[[3]](#ref3)</sup> In a 2024 two-center cohort, unrepaired extraperitoneal rupture and suprapubic drainage were associated with deep pelvic infection, but none of the 13 unrepaired patients underwent anterior ORIF.<sup>[[13]](#ref13)</sup> A separate 2024 series found deep infection in 7/106 within 90 days after anterior stabilization, with a higher rate in combined intraperitoneal and extraperitoneal injuries; these cohorts do not isolate effects of bladder repair or catheter route.<sup>[[14]](#ref14)</sup>

## Special Populations

**Pediatric:** Bladder is more intraabdominal in children; greater IPB risk for a given pelvic fracture. Smaller capacity requires more precise catheter sizing and lower expected fill volumes: about (7 × weight in kg) + 2 mL under 2 years, and (age in years + 2) × 30 mL above 2 years (ACS 2025). High index of suspicion warranted even with microhematuria, and after bladder augmentation or substitution for congenital anomalies.<sup>[[3]](#ref3)</sup>

**Older adults:** Chronic retention and a distended bladder make rupture more likely after low-force trauma such as a fall from standing or a low-speed collision. Establish baseline voiding status before catheter removal, and restart chronic alpha-blockers before the trial of void (ACS text says 24–72 hours before catheter removal; its key-points box says at least 3 days). Use anticholinergics for bladder spasm cautiously because of dementia risk, and discuss that risk before continuing them.<sup>[[3]](#ref3)</sup>

**Pregnant:** Bladder displaced superiorly and anteriorly in late pregnancy, increasing IPB risk. Early urologic consultation is advisable.

**Neurogenic bladder:** Chronically distended bladder is at higher risk for hydraulic rupture from lower-energy mechanisms.

**Iatrogenic (TURBT):** Perforation during transurethral resection of a bladder tumor is usually extraperitoneal and has been attributed to loop overshoot beyond the tumor, the obturator reflex during lateral-wall resection, anterior bladder-wall movement with respiration, or excessive wall tension during a deep resection.<sup>[[11]](#ref11)</sup> Reported incidence depends heavily on ascertainment: a small prospective cohort that performed routine postoperative cystography found extravasation in 21 of 36 patients (58%), far above the 0.5–1% range reported in general series, and a separate retrospective series found perforation more often in women than men (7.2% vs 2.6%, p &lt; 0.001), an association not established as causal.<sup>[[10]](#ref10)[[12]](#ref12)</sup> Intraoperative cystography can confirm a suspected perforation; once hemostasis is achieved, further resection and irrigation are typically stopped to limit extravasation. Bipolar resection with saline irrigation avoids the hyponatremia risk of a monopolar perforation but has not been shown to prevent perforation itself.

## Complications

- **Persistent urinary extravasation** from poor wound healing, technical error, or failed conservative management usually resolves with additional catheter drainage; occasional cases need urinary diversion.
- **Fistulization** to bowel, vagina or skin can follow an unrepaired or inadequately repaired leak.
- **Incontinence or neurogenic bladder dysfunction** can follow pelvic nerve injury sustained with the fracture or the bladder injury itself.
- **Delayed presentation**, sometimes years to decades later, with pelvic pain, hematuria or infection from erosion of retained pelvic hardware or bony callus into the bladder.

Mortality after bladder rupture is driven mainly by associated injuries (pelvic vascular injury, solid-organ injury, other polytrauma) rather than the bladder injury in isolation.<sup>[[3]](#ref3)[[6]](#ref6)</sup>
