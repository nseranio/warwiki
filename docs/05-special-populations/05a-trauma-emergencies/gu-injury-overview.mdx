# Genitourinary Injury Overview

**Traumatic genitourinary (GU) injuries** are identified in up to 10% of trauma patients presenting to emergency departments.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup> The spectrum ranges from clinically silent renal contusions managed with observation to life-threatening renal vascular injuries and devastating pelvic fracture urethral disruptions. Evaluation is systematic and coordinated across trauma, urology and orthopaedic teams.

## Incidence by Organ

| Organ | Relative Frequency | Predominant Mechanism |
|---|---|---|
| Kidney | Most common GU injury (up to 3% of civilian trauma patients)<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup> | Blunt (>90%) |
| Bladder | Prevalence varies by mechanism and degree of hematuria; about 29% with pelvic fracture and gross hematuria (AUA)<sup>[[3]](#ref3)</sup> | Blunt |
| Urethra (male) | Reported rates vary; evaluate high-risk fracture patterns | Blunt (pelvic fracture) |
| Ureter | Rare; 582 of 22,706 GU injuries in one registry<sup>[[2]](#ref2)</sup> | Penetrating (majority) |
| External genitalia | Variable; underreported | Blunt, penetrating, straddle |

## Key Principles

**Hematuria helps identify injury but its absence cannot exclude it.** Degree of hematuria correlates poorly with injury severity. Hematuria is absent in up to 55% of ureteral injuries.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup> Conversely, isolated microscopic hematuria without other risk factors rarely indicates significant injury.

**Mechanism guides workup.** Blunt deceleration injures the kidney and UPJ. Pelvic ring fractures risk bladder and urethral injury. Straddle mechanisms injure the anterior (bulbar) urethra. In stable patients, a penetrating trajectory near GU structures warrants evaluation even without hematuria; imaging must not delay hemorrhage control in unstable patients.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[3]](#ref3)</sup>

**Delayed diagnosis carries major morbidity.** Missed ureteral injuries can present as urinoma, abscess or renal loss. Missed urethral injuries can present as stricture or incontinence. Early systematic evaluation is intended to prevent these complications.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[3]](#ref3)</sup>

**Not all trauma centers have urologic coverage.** Stabilization, urinary diversion and transfer to a center with reconstructive urology expertise are often the initial steps.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup>

## AAST Organ Injury Scales — Quick Reference

AAST grades are organ-specific descriptions, not a shared treatment threshold. A stable patient with a high-grade renal injury may be managed nonoperatively, whereas a lower-grade ureteral laceration may need repair. The relevant scale is combined with physiology, mechanism, associated injury and available expertise; the organ-specific pages give grading and management.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[3]](#ref3)</sup>

## Transfer Criteria

Transfer to a higher-level trauma center or a center with reconstructive urology is considered for:<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[3]](#ref3)</sup>

- Grade IV–V renal injuries requiring angioembolization or operative repair
- Ureteral injuries requiring complex reconstruction (Boari flap, ileal ureter)
- Pelvic fracture urethral injuries for suprapubic tube placement and delayed urethroplasty planning
- Complex bladder injuries with associated orthopedic, urethral, or bowel injury
- Penile amputation or complex external genital injuries
- Fournier's gangrene requiring multiple debridements and eventual reconstruction
