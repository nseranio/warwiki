# Pelvic Fracture Urethral Injury (PFUI)

**Pelvic fracture urethral injury (PFUI)** is a disruption of the membranous (posterior) urethra caused by pelvic ring fractures from high-energy blunt trauma. The ACS guideline cites urethral injury in up to 5% of men with anterior pelvic ring fractures; the EAU reports that falls from height account for 6–25% of PFUIs in cited series.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[38]](#ref38)</sup> The injury results from distraction of the membranous urethra away from the bulbar urethra as the pelvic ring disrupts. For most men with complete posterior disruption, the standard initial management is suprapubic tube (SPT) placement, followed by delayed anastomotic urethroplasty (typically 3–6 months later). Reported outcomes vary by procedure and endpoint: in a 122-patient PFUI cohort, 111 (91%) needed no repeat intervention at a median 7-month follow-up; a separate series reported 95% success after perineal and 97% after transpubic anastomotic urethroplasty.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[2]](#ref2)[[3]](#ref3)[[4]](#ref4)[[5]](#ref5)</sup>

Complete obliteration generally requires reconstruction; blind cut-through procedures risk false passages and rectal injury (see the limitations of [Core-Through Urethrotomy](/docs/surgical-techniques/04a-urethral-reconstruction/core-through-urethrotomy)). For short bulbar EPA, see [Excision and Primary Anastomosis](/docs/surgical-techniques/04a-urethral-reconstruction/excision-primary-anastomosis).

## Epidemiology and Mechanism

PFUI predominantly affects males involved in motor vehicle collisions, falls from height, or crush injuries; mean age at urethroplasty was 41.6 years in the cited 122-patient multicenter PFUI cohort.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[2]](#ref2)[[4]](#ref4)</sup> The mechanism involves disruption of the pelvic ring (most commonly fractures of the sacroiliac joint, anterior arch with displacement, and/or diastasis of the pubic symphysis), producing shearing forces at the relatively fixed membranous urethra.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[6]](#ref6)</sup>

Each millimeter of symphysis pubis diastasis or inferomedial pubic bone fracture displacement is associated with an estimated 10% relative increased risk of urethral injury.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[6]](#ref6)</sup> Complete injuries (approximately 65%) result in a gap between the two urethral stumps filled with fibrotic tissue; incomplete injuries (approximately 35%) maintain some degree of urethral continuity.<sup>[[6]](#ref6)</sup>

## Classification

### Goldman classification (1997), expanding Colapinto and McCallum

The original 1977 scheme comprised types I–III; Goldman and colleagues added IV, IVa and V.<sup>[[39]](#ref39)</sup>

- **Type I.** Posterior urethra stretched but intact
- **Type II.** Partial or complete pure posterior injury of the membranous urethra above an intact urogenital diaphragm<sup>[[39]](#ref39)</sup>
- **Type III.** Disruption of the membranous urethra extending below the urogenital diaphragm, involving the anterior urethra
- **Type IV.** Bladder neck injury with extension into the proximal urethra
- **Type IVa.** Injury to the base of the bladder with periurethral extravasation simulating Type IV
- **Type V.** Isolated anterior urethral injury<sup>[[7]](#ref7)</sup>

### AAST Organ Injury Scale

| Grade | Description |
|---|---|
| **I** | Contusion (blood at meatus, normal urethrography) |
| **II** | Stretch injury (elongation without extravasation) |
| **III** | Partial disruption (extravasation with contrast reaching bladder) |
| **IV** | Complete disruption (&lt;2 cm separation) |
| **V** | Complete disruption &gt;2 cm separation, or extension into prostate / vagina |

## Clinical Presentation and Diagnosis

### Clinical signs

High suspicion is warranted with any of:<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup>

- **Blood at the urethral meatus.** Most classic sign
- Suprapubic fullness
- Perineal laceration or butterfly hematoma
- Scrotal hematoma
- Difficulty or inability to pass a Foley catheter
- Inability to void

A high-riding prostate on rectal examination has low sensitivity and specificity for urethral injury and cannot exclude injury. Rectal examination remains relevant to assess associated rectal injury.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup>

### Initial imaging — urethrography

- **Retrograde urethrography (RUG)** is the preferred initial modality. Extravasation without bladder filling suggests complete disruption, but RUG does not always distinguish complete from partial rupture; flexible cystourethroscopy can help.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[6]](#ref6)[[7]](#ref7)</sup> The EAU strongly recommends evaluating male urethral injury with flexible cystourethroscopy and/or RUG. It advises injecting 20 to 30 mL of contrast with the meatus occluded, tilting the X-ray beam to 30° rather than moving a patient with a pelvic fracture, and delaying RUG in an unstable patient until stabilised.<sup>[[38]](#ref38)</sup>
- **Combined antegrade and retrograde urethrography** (via SPT tract) is the fundamental preoperative evaluation before delayed urethroplasty, assessing gap length and alignment.<sup>[[8]](#ref8)</sup>
- **Sequencing:** if the patient has a pelvic fracture and CT will assess for arterial extravasation, perform CT first and RUG second; contrast extravasation from urethral injection can produce significant artifact on CT.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup>

## MRI in Preoperative Planning

Pelvic MRI is an adjunct for complex PFUI, particularly when urethrography does not define the proximal stump, displacement, scar or false passages adequately. Combined antegrade/retrograde urethrography remains the basic reconstruction study; MRI is not mandatory for every repair.<sup>[[8]](#ref8)[[9]](#ref9)[[22]](#ref22)[[38]](#ref38)</sup>

### Protocol

Standard MRI protocol for PFUI evaluation:<sup>[[21]](#ref21)</sup>

- **T2-weighted sagittal images.** A central sequence. Used to identify the proximal and distal urethral stumps, measure gap length, and calculate the pubourethral stump angle (PUA) and pubourethral stump length (PUL).
- **T2-weighted coronal images.** Lateral prostatic displacement and the relationship of the urethra to the corpora cavernosa.
- **T1- and T2-weighted axial images.** Corpora cavernosa integrity, dorsal venous complex, periurethral scar tissue, and false passages.
- The original PUA study obtained MRI at least 3 months after injury; this study protocol does not require postponing imaging in a patient selected for earlier reconstruction.<sup>[[10]](#ref10)</sup>
- No contrast is typically required; the high intrinsic soft-tissue contrast of T2-weighted sequences is sufficient.
- A described protocol variant (the "Joshi" protocol) fills the bladder with saline, uses a pre-study alpha-1 blocker to facilitate bladder-neck opening, introduces saline or jelly through the meatus, and acquires a T2 sagittal image with a full bladder while the patient attempts to void; in a 40-patient prospective comparison, this protocol measured urethral gaps 0.3–1.1 cm shorter, with better image-quality scores, than conventional MRI. This is a selected, study-specific protocol rather than routine practice.<sup>[[54]](#ref54)</sup>

### Additional MRI information

Accuracy estimates below come from small, selected cohorts (21–27 patients), not universal test performance. Neither imaging modality eliminates uncertainty at exploration.

| Parameter | Urethrography | MRI | Clinical Significance |
|---|---|---|---|
| Urethral gap length | Estimates; often inaccurate | Matched operative length within 0.5 cm in 23/27 (85%); correctly determined in 18/21 (86%) | Determines need for ancillary maneuvers<sup>[[8]](#ref8)[[22]](#ref22)[[23]](#ref23)</sup> |
| Prostatic displacement (direction + degree) | May incompletely depict displacement | Correctly determined in 16/18 (89%) and 19/21 (90%) patients with displacement | Lateral displacement predicts elaborate approach + ED<sup>[[22]](#ref22)[[21]](#ref21)[[23]](#ref23)</sup> |
| Scar tissue extent and density | Not visualized | Precisely delineated | Guides scar excision planning<sup>[[22]](#ref22)</sup> |
| Paraurethral false tracks | May be missed | Found in 3/21 patients (14%) | May inform operative planning<sup>[[22]](#ref22)</sup> |
| Cavernous body integrity | Not assessed | Avulsion and separation clearly shown | Predicts ED<sup>[[21]](#ref21)[[23]](#ref23)</sup> |
| Dorsal venous complex patency | Not assessed | Assessed on axial / sagittal | Anticipates bleeding during pubectomy |
| Pubourethral stump angle (PUA) | Not measurable | Measured on sagittal T2 | Independent predictor of elaborate approach<sup>[[10]](#ref10)[[24]](#ref24)</sup> |
| Relationship to rectum | Not assessed | Clearly delineated | Critical for avoiding rectal injury during dissection |

### The Pubourethral Stump Angle (PUA)

The **PUA** was first described by Horiguchi et al. (2018) and tested in an independent retrospective cohort of 184 men (online 2025; issue 2026).<sup>[[10]](#ref10)[[24]](#ref24)</sup>

- **Pubourethral stump length (PUL).** The distance between the distal end of the proximal urethral stump and the lower border of the inferior pubic ramus, measured on sagittal T2.
- **Pubourethral stump angle (PUA).** The angle between the long axis of the pubis and the line connecting the distal end of the proximal urethral stump to the lower border of the inferior pubic ramus, measured on sagittal T2.

A smaller (lower) PUA indicates the proximal urethral stump has been displaced superiorly and posteriorly behind the pubic symphysis, meaning the prostatic apex is hidden behind the bone, inaccessible from a simple perineal approach without pubectomy. A larger PUA indicates the proximal stump remains relatively accessible below the pubic ramus, amenable to simple perineal mobilization ± corporal splitting.

**Original study (Horiguchi 2018, n = 74):**<sup>[[10]](#ref10)</sup> 28 patients required a simple perineal approach (mobilization ± corporal splitting); 46 required an elaborate approach (inferior pubectomy or abdominoperineal with rerouting). On univariate analysis, disruption at the prostate apex, greater urethral gap length, longer PUL, and lower PUA were all significantly associated with an elaborate approach. On multivariate analysis, only low PUA was an independent predictor. Overall urethroscopic success (no recurrent stricture) was 94.6%.

**Validation study (Horiguchi 2026, n = 184):**<sup>[[24]](#ref24)</sup> An elaborate approach (step 3 or higher) was required in 70 patients (38.0%). On multivariate analysis, two independent predictors emerged:

- **Smaller PUA.** OR 0.95 per degree (95% CI 0.94–0.98, p &lt; 0.0001)<sup>[[24]](#ref24)</sup>
- **Prior transurethral treatment.** OR 2.77 (95% CI 1.16–6.65, p = 0.02).<sup>[[24]](#ref24)</sup> The combined model, incorporating both predictors, had AUC 0.782; prior treatment was associated with, but was not shown to cause, complexity

### MRI prediction of erectile dysfunction

**Narumi 1993 (n = 27)**, an early MRI–ED correlation study, identified three significant MRI variables predicting permanent impotence:<sup>[[23]](#ref23)</sup>

- **Avulsion of the corpus cavernosum**
- **Superior and/or lateral prostatic displacement**
- **Separation of the corporeal body**

The study estimated a 95% probability of persistent ED with cavernous avulsion plus superior/lateral displacement, and 83% probability of normal potency without those findings at 12 months. These are exploratory estimates from 27 selected patients, not validated individual prognoses.<sup>[[23]](#ref23)</sup>

**Koraitim 2013 (n = 90)** identified three independent multivariate predictors of post-PFUI ED:<sup>[[25]](#ref25)</sup>

- **Diastasis of pubic symphysis.** OR 15.9 (the strongest predictor)<sup>[[25]](#ref25)</sup>
- **Lateral displacement of prostate.** OR 6.9<sup>[[25]](#ref25)</sup>
- **Long urethral gap.** OR 2.0<sup>[[25]](#ref25)</sup>

These injury features can support counseling alongside the erectile history and examination; imaging alone cannot determine whether an individual patient’s ED will recover.<sup>[[25]](#ref25)</sup>

**Dixon 1992 (n = 18)** further demonstrated that MRI reveals injuries at multiple locations along the erectile pathway, including sacral and ischial fractures (which may damage the pudendal nerve) and cavernous body avulsions, which is additional information beyond conventional urethrography.<sup>[[21]](#ref21)</sup>

### MRI impact on surgical planning

**Narumi 1993** demonstrated that MRI findings prompted a change in the clinically planned surgical approach from perineal to combined perineal and transpubic in 7 of 27 patients (26%).<sup>[[23]](#ref23)</sup>

**Koraitim and Reda 2007 (n = 21)** confirmed that MRI:<sup>[[22]](#ref22)</sup>

- Correctly estimated the length of the urethral defect in 86%<sup>[[22]](#ref22)</sup>
- Correctly demonstrated the type and degree of prostatic displacement in 89%<sup>[[22]](#ref22)</sup>
- Precisely delineated the site and density of scar tissue
- Revealed paraurethral false tracks in 3 of 21 patients (14%)<sup>[[22]](#ref22)</sup>
- Demonstrated cavernous avulsion and lateral prostatic displacement in all 6 patients with posttraumatic impotence

### Radiographic predictors of surgical complexity

| Prediction System | Modality | Key Parameter | Cutoff | Predictive Value |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Horiguchi PUA<sup>[[10]](#ref10)[[24]](#ref24)</sup> | MRI (sagittal T2) | Pubourethral stump angle | Lower PUA → elaborate | AUC 0.782 for PUA + prior treatment (184 men) |
| Koraitim Gapometry / Urethrometry Index<sup>[[26]](#ref26)</sup> | Urethrography | Gap length / bulbar urethral length | &gt;0.35 → elaborate | AUC 0.979; 91% specificity, 95% PPV |
| Wang PUVD<sup>[[55]](#ref55)</sup> | MRI urethrography | Gap distance + pubourethral vertical distance (PUVD) | Longer gap, shorter PUVD → pubectomy | Independent predictors on multivariate analysis; ROC accuracy 83.7% (gap) and 67.4% (PUVD) in 43 men |
| Yepes PUS Position<sup>[[27]](#ref27)</sup> | Urethrography (RGU/VCUG) | Proximal stump above vs. below inferior pubic symphysis | Above → elaborate | OR 66 (p &lt; 0.001) |
| Scherñuk 2026<sup>[[28]](#ref28)</sup> | Urethrography | Any of the tested radiographic predictors | Any positive criterion | 100% sensitivity, 42.3% specificity (36 evaluable men) |

The 2026 external validation by Scherñuk et al. excluded 45/81 patients (often because the bladder neck did not open), leaving 36 evaluable cases. It found that radiological predictors from urethrography alone show limited reliability and accuracy, though the Yepes PUS position correlated with complexity. The absence of any radiological criteria may help identify straightforward cases (100% of patients without predictors underwent non-complex repairs).<sup>[[28]](#ref28)</sup> That study did not directly compare MRI with urethrography.

An earlier, separate 120-patient study by the same author used a related but distinct index — urethral gap length as a fraction of bulbar urethral length, rather than total urethral length — and found a simple perineal repair typical below a gap-to-bulbar-length ratio of about one-third (group means 0.21 for simple repairs vs 0.64 for elaborate repairs), with an elaborated perineal or transpubic approach more likely above that ratio. This bulbar-length index predates, and should not be conflated with, the refined gap/total-urethral-length index and formal 0.35 cutoff above.<sup>[[53]](#ref53)</sup>

## Acute Management

> For suprapubic drainage in the unstable polytrauma patient and the other organs' temporizing options at a damage-control laparotomy, see [Damage Control in Genitourinary Trauma](/docs/special-populations/05a-trauma-emergencies/damage-control-gu-trauma).

### Suprapubic Tube (SPT) — Preferred

AUA recommends SPT placement as preferred initial management for most PFUI (Statement 20b; Recommendation, Grade C); ACS 2025 also favors initial SPT for complete injury:<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[3]](#ref3)</sup>

- SPT is the usual initial drainage strategy for complete male PFUI; selected stable patients may undergo endoscopic realignment.
- Incomplete injuries may be managed with SPT or urethral catheterization (single gentle attempt by experienced personnel; stop immediately if resistance or new bleeding).
- SPT should be ≥14 Fr Foley (not pigtail) to ensure adequate drainage and facilitate exchange.
- Bladder may be displaced by pelvic hematoma; ultrasound, fluoroscopy, or needle aspiration may be needed to localize the bladder for percutaneous placement.
- When realignment is selected, an endoscopic approach is preferred over open realignment because of operative burden and blood loss.<sup>[[38]](#ref38)</sup>

### Primary Endoscopic Realignment (PER)

PER involves simultaneous antegrade and retrograde cystoscopy to place a catheter across the urethral injury. The evidence is conflicting: the EAU gives a weak recommendation to perform early endoscopic realignment in male PFUI when feasible,<sup>[[38]](#ref38)</sup> whereas ACS favors SPT for complete injury and AUA prefers SPT for most PFUI. AUA permits primary realignment in stable patients (Statement 22; Option, Grade C) and advises against prolonged endoscopic attempts (Clinical Principle).<sup>[[3]](#ref3)</sup> The EAU advises catheterization for 3 weeks after realignment of a partial injury and 6 weeks after a complete injury, with voiding urethrography at removal, and recommends against repeating endoscopic treatment after failed realignment (strong).<sup>[[38]](#ref38)</sup> For the full atlas treatment (dual endoscopic, fluoroscopic IR, and magnetic-catheter variants, technique steps, catheter protocols, and comparative outcomes), see [Primary Endoscopic Realignment (PER)](/docs/surgical-techniques/04a-urethral-reconstruction/primary-endoscopic-realignment).

The TURNS multicenter prospective cohort study (McCormick 2023, 26 centers, 69 patients with complete PFUI) found that PER was not associated with a lower rate of urethral obstruction versus SPT alone: 97% versus 94% developed obstruction (p = 0.471), and urethroplasty was required in 87% versus 91% (p = 0.784).<sup>[[11]](#ref11)</sup>

A 2025 meta-analysis (Owen et al., 11 nonrandomized comparative studies, 769 patients) found no statistically significant differences between primary realignment and SPT with delayed urethroplasty: stricture OR 1.23 (95% CI 0.24–6.37), incontinence OR 1.02 (0.59–1.79), and ED OR 0.97 (0.64–1.46). Wide confidence intervals and treatment-selection bias preclude a conclusion of equivalence.<sup>[[12]](#ref12)</sup>

The ACS Best Practices Guidelines (2025) state: "No strong evidence exists to indicate that PER significantly impacts stenosis rates or complexity of future urethral reconstruction following complete PFUI." However, for partial injuries, successful PER may lower the stenosis rate. The guideline advises that any PER attempt be made by a urologist within a few days of injury in a hemodynamically stable patient, and that open realignment has no role because of substantially increased complications. It reports urethral stenosis rates well over 90% after complete injury from pelvic fracture or straddle trauma, usually manifesting within the first year.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup>

## Delayed Definitive Reconstruction

### Timing

The AUA Urethral Stricture Disease Guideline (2023; Expert Opinion) recommends that definitive reconstruction be planned only after major injuries stabilize and the patient can be safely positioned for urethroplasty. This is usually 3–6 months after trauma; no optimal time has been established (range 6 weeks to 4 yr).<sup>[[13]](#ref13)</sup>

The EAU (2026) makes three timing recommendations for male PFUI. Do not perform immediate urethroplasty (under 48 hours; strong), because of poor visualization, bleeding of about 3 L on entering the pelvic hematoma, and higher rates of stricture, incontinence and ED. Early urethroplasty (2 days to 6 weeks) may be considered for complete disruption in selected patients: stable, short gap, soft perineum and able to lie in lithotomy (weak). For complete disruption, suprapubic diversion with urethroplasty at least 3 months after injury is the standard (strong); at that point the hematoma has resolved and the prostate has descended toward its normal position.<sup>[[38]](#ref38)</sup>

Scarberry and Gómez (2018), in a retrospective cohort of 39 patients, reported that early reconstruction at 3–6 weeks (when the perineum is soft, the fracture is stable, and associated injuries permit lithotomy positioning) was feasible; the study did not detect differences from a ≥12-week delay. Overall cohort outcomes were failure 5.1%, incontinence 7.7%, ED 56.4%, with no significant differences between early and delayed groups.<sup>[[14]](#ref14)</sup>

### Preoperative preparation

- **Imaging.** Combined antegrade / retrograde urethrography; add pelvic MRI for complex or inadequately defined anatomy (above)
- **Cystoscopy.** Flexible cystoscopy through the SPT to assess the proximal stump, bladder neck competence, and any periurethral pathology. In revision surgery after a chronically indwelling suprapubic catheter, intraoperative cystoscopy can also identify eggshell calculi that have formed on the catheter balloon and help confirm the prostatic apex as the correct proximal anastomotic site.<sup>[[47]](#ref47)</sup>
- **Bowel planning.** When a urethrorectal fistula or bowel procedure is anticipated, coordinate preparation and diversion decisions with the colorectal team; proximity to the rectum alone is not a universal indication for bowel preparation
- **Positioning.** Exaggerated dorsal lithotomy; positioning must be confirmed safe given orthopedic injuries

## The Webster / Ramon Progressive Perineal Approach — Detailed Technique

[The technique](/docs/roots/history/urethral-surgery#webster-and-goldwasser--elaborated-perineal-approach-19861991) described by [Webster](/docs/roots/surgeons/s-z/george-webster) and Ramon (1991) in 74 patients with defects ranging from 1.5 to 7 cm achieved a 96% success rate using sequential ancillary maneuvers as needed.<sup>[[15]](#ref15)</sup>

### Step 1 — Exposure and bulbar urethral mobilization

1. **Midline perineal incision** from the posterior scrotal raphe to a point just anterior to the anus.
2. The bulbospongiosus muscle is divided in the midline raphe, exposing the corpus spongiosum and bulbar urethra.
3. The bulbar urethra is mobilized circumferentially from the perineal body distally to the penoscrotal junction, which provides 2–3 cm of additional urethral length. A gloved finger in the rectum can guide posterior dissection of the urethra off the perineal body and reduce the risk of rectal injury during this step.<sup>[[52]](#ref52)</sup>
4. The urethra is transected at the level of the obliterative stricture; the distal end is spatulated ventrally.
5. The fibrotic scar tissue filling the distraction defect is excised, working toward the proximal (prostatic) stump.
6. The proximal stump is identified, often by palpating a metal sound passed through the SPT into the prostatic urethra.
7. The proximal stump is spatulated dorsally to create a wide anastomotic opening.
8. If a tension-free anastomosis can be achieved at this point, the spatulated ends are anastomosed over a catheter using interrupted absorbable sutures (typically 4-0 or 5-0 PDS / Vicryl), placed at the 12, 3, 6, and 9 o'clock positions. One described setup uses eight 4-0 PDS sutures (4 cardinal positions plus 4 in between), bending the needle into a J-shaped "ski" configuration for proximal-stump access and securing each suture on a serially numbered mosquito clamp so orientation is preserved for tying in order; a long-armed nasal speculum can aid exposure for this suture placement.<sup>[[46]](#ref46)</sup>
9. In Fu et al.'s 301-patient cohort, simple perineal anastomosis was performed in 103 (34.2%); in Webster and Ramon's separate 74-patient cohort, 66 required one or more sequential maneuvers.<sup>[[15]](#ref15)[[17]](#ref17)</sup>

### Step 2 — Corporal body separation (crural splitting)

If mobilization alone is insufficient:

1. The intercrural septum is divided sharply.
2. The corpora cavernosa are separated laterally.
3. The bulbar urethra is routed between the separated corpora, shortening the distance by straightening its course.
4. Provides an additional 1–2 cm of effective length.
5. Used in 17–36% of cases.<sup>[[4]](#ref4)[[16]](#ref16)[[17]](#ref17)</sup> The Fu/Xu series reported an 86.5% success rate with corporal splitting.<sup>[[17]](#ref17)</sup>

### Step 3 — Inferior pubectomy

If Steps 1–2 are insufficient. A described sub-classification splits this step into 3a (periosteal elevation only) and 3b (bone removal if 3a proves insufficient); reassessing after 3a alone can avoid an unnecessary bone wedge.<sup>[[56]](#ref56)</sup>

1. The inferior margin of the pubic symphysis is exposed by dissecting the periosteum. In the described Step 3a, a midline periosteal incision is made with diathermy and the periosteum is elevated bilaterally, for example with the electrocautery tip bent to 45° and slid beneath it; after this exposure the surgeon can reassess whether a bone wedge is still needed.<sup>[[56]](#ref56)</sup>
2. A wedge of bone is removed from the inferior border of the symphysis using an oscillating bone saw or rongeur. A described alternative (Step 3b) uses a gouge directed vertically for a standard pubectomy or more horizontally for a deeper resection, with loosened fragments removed by rongeur or forceps; a suprapubic bougie (or a gloved rectal finger to feel its tip at the prostatic apex when it is not palpable perineally) helps confirm the target before bone removal.<sup>[[56]](#ref56)</sup>
3. The periosteum on the pelvic (deep) surface is carefully preserved to protect the dorsal venous complex and avoid catastrophic hemorrhage.
4. Provides direct access to the prostatic apex, which may be displaced superiorly behind the pubic bone.
5. Effectively shortens the distance between the urethral stumps by removing the bony obstacle.
6. Used in 10–32% of cases.<sup>[[4]](#ref4)[[16]](#ref16)[[17]](#ref17)</sup> Success rate 84.2% in Fu/Xu.<sup>[[17]](#ref17)</sup>
7. Complications related to pubic resection (instability, herniation) are now rarely seen with modern technique.<sup>[[30]](#ref30)</sup>

### Step 4 — Supracrural urethral rerouting

Reserved for the most complex, long-gap defects:

1. The urethra is mobilized extensively and rerouted around one side of the corpora cavernosa (supracrurally) rather than between them.
2. Provides the maximum possible urethral length gain.
3. Used in only 2–5% of cases.<sup>[[4]](#ref4)[[16]](#ref16)[[17]](#ref17)</sup>
4. Kizer / Brandes reported recurrence in 3 of only 4 rerouted cases. This small, selected subgroup does not establish comparative inferiority, but supports caution and individualized salvage planning.<sup>[[16]](#ref16)</sup>
5. However, Li et al. 2025 (n = 37) reported 91.2% success (31/34 with follow-up) using one-sided rerouting via a perineal approach with inferior pubectomy, particularly advocating this technique for young patients and children to minimize secondary trauma to the corpora cavernosa.<sup>[[18]](#ref18)</sup>

### The Sa / Xu Progressive Transperineal Strategy — the largest series

**Sa et al. 2021 (n = 1,637)**, a large single-center PFUI series, reported a 92.4% success rate (1,363/1,475 with follow-up) using a progressive transperineal anastomotic strategy:<sup>[[29]](#ref29)</sup>

- After full mobilization of the distal bulbomembranous urethra, the stenotic urethra is transected directly at the proximal margin of the stenotic segment to expose the proximal disrupted end.
- If the stenosis location is too deep to fully mobilize (complex cases), urethral transection is selected at the distal margin of the stenotic urethra instead.
- Distal and proximal disrupted urethras are then trimmed and anastomosed without tension.
- Among 112 failed patients: 10 received endoscopic urethrotomy, 99 underwent secondary / third anastomotic urethroplasty, 3 perineal skin flap urethroplasty.
- **Incontinence.** 8.5% (125/1,475 patients with follow-up after final successful urethroplasty).<sup>[[29]](#ref29)</sup>
- **De novo ED.** Reported in 15 patients. The abstract reports 1.6% without the eligible erectile-function denominator; 15/1,637 cannot quantify de novo ED or exclude surgery-related ED.<sup>[[29]](#ref29)</sup>

## Abdominoperineal (Transpubic) Approach

### Indications<sup>[[30]](#ref30)[[31]](#ref31)[[32]](#ref32)</sup>

- Distraction defects &gt;5 cm (some authors use &gt;3 cm)
- Significant lateral or superior prostatic displacement not accessible perineally
- Failed prior perineal urethroplasty
- Associated urethrorectal fistula requiring tissue interposition
- Periurethral cavities, false passages, or open bladder neck
- Pediatric cases with complex anatomy

### Technique (Pratap 2006)<sup>[[30]](#ref30)[[31]](#ref31)</sup>

1. **Abdominal phase.** Lower midline or Pfannenstiel incision; bladder mobilized; retropubic space entered.
2. **Pubic resection.** Segment of the pubic symphysis is resected (partial or complete pubectomy), providing wide exposure to the prostatic urethra from above.
3. **Scar excision.** Fibrotic tissue between urethral stumps excised under direct vision from both above and below.
4. **Perineal phase.** Simultaneous perineal dissection mobilizes the bulbar urethra.
5. **Anastomosis.** Spatulated urethral ends anastomosed through the subpubic route or, if needed, via supracrural rerouting.
6. **Tissue interposition.** In cases with urethrorectal fistula, a gracilis muscle flap or rectus abdominis muscle flap is interposed between the repaired rectum and urethra.<sup>[[32]](#ref32)[[33]](#ref33)</sup>
7. **Bladder neck repair.** Performed when indicated by the actual injury. In this selected 25-patient cohort, 20 had bladder-neck laceration and underwent simultaneous repair; 80% is not a general prevalence among complex PFUI.<sup>[[31]](#ref31)</sup>

### Outcomes

- Pratap 2006 (11 adults, 10 prepubescent boys): 20/21 (95%) had a wide anastomosis on postoperative cystourethrography, and 2/21 (9.5%) developed postoperative incontinence; mean stricture length was 5.2 cm and mean follow-up was 28 months.<sup>[[30]](#ref30)</sup>
- Pratap 2006 (n = 25): 92% overall success; incontinence 4%; potency status unchanged by surgery.<sup>[[31]](#ref31)</sup>
- Koraitim 1995 (n = 32 transpubic): 97% success.<sup>[[5]](#ref5)</sup>

## Management of Associated Urethrorectal Fistula (URF)

Associated URF is an uncommon, complex reconstructive problem. The cited fistula-treatment series do not establish its incidence among all PFUI.<sup>[[32]](#ref32)[[33]](#ref33)</sup>

**Xu 2010 (n = 31)** reported outcomes by approach:<sup>[[32]](#ref32)</sup>

- **Simple perineal.** 4/4 (100%) success<sup>[[32]](#ref32)</sup>
- **Transperineal with inferior pubectomy.** 16/18 (88.9%); the authors concluded it may be appropriate as a first-line procedure<sup>[[32]](#ref32)</sup>
- **Combined transpubic-perineal.** 7/9 (77.8%) in Xu’s 31-patient series<sup>[[32]](#ref32)</sup>

**Guo 2017 (n = 32)** specifically evaluated **gracilis muscle interposition** for PFUI with URF:<sup>[[33]](#ref33)</sup>

- Overall success 91% (29/32)<sup>[[33]](#ref33)</sup>
- One-stage repair with perineal anastomosis and corporal splitting: 17/18 (94%)<sup>[[33]](#ref33)</sup>
- Perineal anastomosis, inferior pubectomy and corporal splitting: 12/14 (86%)<sup>[[33]](#ref33)</sup>
- All 22 patients without any previous repair were successfully treated (100%)<sup>[[33]](#ref33)</sup>
- Only 7/10 (70%) with prior failed urethroplasty and URF repair were cured<sup>[[33]](#ref33)</sup>
- In Guo’s 32-patient single-arm series, a vascularized gracilis flap was interposed as a tissue barrier between the repaired urethra and rectum; the observed fistula outcomes do not isolate the flap’s effect on recurrence.<sup>[[33]](#ref33)</sup>

For broader fistula context, see the [Rectourethral Fistula](/docs/clinical-conditions/03f-fistulas/in-males/rectourethral) article.

## Robotic-Assisted Posterior Urethroplasty (RPU)

Robotic approaches have emerged as a viable option, particularly for radiation-induced posterior urethral stenosis and complex cases requiring abdominal access.<sup>[[19]](#ref19)[[34]](#ref34)[[35]](#ref35)</sup>

**Zhang / Zhao 2023 (n = 105)**, the largest RPU cohort:<sup>[[34]](#ref34)</sup>

- Mean follow-up 18.7 mo
- 57.1% had prior pelvic radiation (predominantly post-prostate cancer treatment, not PFUI)<sup>[[34]](#ref34)</sup>
- Techniques included EPA, bladder-neck resitting, Y-V plasty and BMG urethroplasty (the abstract’s EPA count/percentage are inconsistent, so that percentage is not reproduced)
- 39% required a combined abdominoperineal approach<sup>[[34]](#ref34)</sup>
- ≥Clavien-Dindo grade 3 complications within 30 days: 6.7%<sup>[[34]](#ref34)</sup>
- Incontinence requiring AUS 28.6%<sup>[[34]](#ref34)</sup>
- Reintervention rate 24.8%<sup>[[34]](#ref34)</sup>

**Cavallo / Vanni 2021 (n = 12)**, combined robotic transabdominal and open transperineal:<sup>[[19]](#ref19)</sup> mean age 65.9 yr (predominantly post-radiation, not PFUI); 66.7% required prostatectomy as part of the procedure; stenosis recurrence 16.7%; 75% required subsequent AUS.

**Liu / Zhao 2022 (n = 9)** was a single-port BMG feasibility series: eight patients had prostate-cancer treatment and one had trauma; median follow-up was 11.7 months. It does not establish comparative durability for PFUI.<sup>[[35]](#ref35)</sup>

**Distinction:** most RPU series are dominated by radiation-induced posterior urethral stenosis rather than PFUI. Different etiologies, radiation exposure and selection prevent attributing outcome differences to either tissue quality or the operative approach alone. For traumatic PFUI, the open perineal approach remains the standard; robotic assistance is reserved for complex cases requiring abdominal access.<sup>[[19]](#ref19)[[34]](#ref34)</sup>

## Functional Outcomes and Complications

### Erectile dysfunction

ED is a major long-term morbidity. The original pelvic trauma often causes impairment before reconstruction; urethroplasty can still cause new or worsened dysfunction, so document baseline function:<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[2]](#ref2)[[5]](#ref5)[[20]](#ref20)[[29]](#ref29)</sup>

- **ED rates after PFUI.** The EAU cites approximately 34% erectile dysfunction following PFUI unrelated to urethroplasty<sup>[[38]](#ref38)</sup>
- **New ED after reconstruction.** Koraitim (1995) reported 2.5%; Sa 2021 reported 15 events, with an incompletely specified denominator in the abstract<sup>[[5]](#ref5)[[29]](#ref29)</sup>
- Plamadeala 2025 assessed erectile function in 49 of 70 men; 97.9% of that assessed subgroup had impairment after trauma. Its results section reports 16.3% improving after repair and its abstract reports 12.2%; the discrepancy limits inference beyond the assessed subgroup<sup>[[20]](#ref20)</sup>
- The ACS Best Practices Guidelines (2025) report ED rates of 20–35% after severe urethral injury<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup>

MRI may identify structural injury relevant to ED, but the 27-patient Narumi study is not a validated individual prognosis tool.<sup>[[23]](#ref23)[[25]](#ref25)</sup>

### Urinary incontinence

- Postoperative incontinence estimates have different definitions: ACS guidance cites 15–20% after severe urethral injury.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup> Scarberry reported incontinence in 3/39 patients (7.7%) after delayed repair.<sup>[[14]](#ref14)</sup> Plamadeala reported de novo incontinence in 6.6% of the 61 men assessed for urinary continence within a 70-man primary-repair cohort.<sup>[[20]](#ref20)</sup> Sa reported incontinence after final successful repair in 125/1,475 followed patients (8.5%), without an incident-incontinence denominator.<sup>[[29]](#ref29)</sup>
- The ACS Best Practices Guidelines report incontinence rates of 15–20% after severe urethral injury<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup>
- In Cavallo’s selected 12-patient robotic/open reconstruction series, 4/12 (33.3%) developed new SUI, while 9/12 (75%) ultimately received an AUS; these are different outcomes<sup>[[19]](#ref19)</sup>
- In a small retrospective series of 15 men with a cystoscopically or radiographically open bladder neck before posterior urethroplasty, 6 were continent and 8 incontinent afterward (1 received simultaneous AUS placement); mean bladder-neck/prostatic-urethral opening length was 0.9 cm in the continent group versus 1.68 cm in the incontinent group. Treat this only as a historical association from a small cohort, not a validated continence-prediction threshold.<sup>[[59]](#ref59)</sup>

### Stricture recurrence

- Outcomes after posterior urethroplasty differ by endpoint: Johnsen found 111/122 (91%) without repeat intervention<sup>[[4]](#ref4)</sup>; Koraitim reported 95% perineal and 97% transpubic anastomotic success<sup>[[5]](#ref5)</sup>; Scarberry found 2/39 (5.1%) stricture failures<sup>[[14]](#ref14)</sup>; Fu reported 38/301 (12.6%) failures defined by postoperative instrumentation<sup>[[17]](#ref17)</sup>; and Plamadeala found 11/70 (15.8%) symptomatic recurrences requiring intervention<sup>[[20]](#ref20)</sup>.
- Among the 11 men with recurrence in a 70-man primary-repair cohort, median time to recurrence was 3 months (IQR 2–5 mo).<sup>[[20]](#ref20)</sup>
- 10-year recurrence-free survival: 83.8%<sup>[[20]](#ref20)</sup>
- **Salvage of a recurrent post-anastomotic stricture.** In a pediatric perineal anastomotic series (n = 65), recurrence developed in 7 (10.8%), always at the anastomosis and within the first postoperative year; 5 of the 7 were salvaged endoscopically and the other 2 by repeat perineal anastomotic repair.<sup>[[42]](#ref42)</sup> In a related pediatric series, visual internal urethrotomy for a short (&lt;1 cm) recurrence after perineal anastomotic urethroplasty succeeded in 20 of 22 (90%), with a mean 98-month follow-up; strictures not responsive to urethrotomy require repeat urethroplasty, with or without buccal mucosal grafting.<sup>[[43]](#ref43)</sup>

### Ischemic Stenosis vs. Technical Restenosis

In experienced centers, most posterior urethroplasty failures are not anastomotic (technical) restenosis but ischemic stenosis of the mobilized corpus spongiosum: after extensive mobilization the spongiosum becomes, in effect, a flap whose vascular supply is retrograde flow from the glans dorsal-artery arborization. One center's experience describes a selective workup to identify patients at risk for this ischemic pattern — dorsal penile nerve injury, a prior failed reconstruction elsewhere, a lateral-impact pelvic fracture, or a "wind-swept" fracture pattern — with pudendal duplex ultrasonography, reserving pudendal angiography for abnormal duplex parameters. In that experience, reliable reconstruction was possible whenever at least one internal pudendal artery was patent or reconstituted; the group at risk for ischemic stenosis was bilateral complete internal-pudendal obstruction, for whom the center performed penile arterial revascularization before urethral reconstruction.<sup>[[44]](#ref44)</sup> This is institutional experience, not a validated screening protocol; see [Penile Arterial Revascularization](/docs/surgical-techniques/04j-sexual-dysfunction/penile-arterial-revascularization) for technique and a 2012 outcomes series in post-PFUI arteriogenic ED.<sup>[[45]](#ref45)</sup>

A related distinction is drawn between bulbar urethral ischemic stenosis (a long, near-obliterated ischemic stricture with the bulbar urethra's length preserved but narrowed) and **bulbar urethral ischemic necrosis (BUIN)**, loss of a long segment or the entire bulbar urethra from vascular insufficiency, most often after a failed prior PFUI repair. After anastomotic urethroplasty, the urethra depends on retrograde flow from the dorsal penile arteries once the lateral circumflex and bulbourethral vessels are sacrificed by mobilization and transection; over-mobilization, prior hypospadias repair, multiple prior operations, and injury to the dorsal penile arteries during crural separation or pubectomy are described contributors. One imaging heuristic holds that penile and bulbar urethral lengths should be roughly equal, with the penoscrotal junction as their midpoint; a bulbar segment that appears substantially shorter than the penile urethra on imaging may suggest necrosis rather than simple stenosis. This is an attributed clinical heuristic from a single center, not a validated diagnostic rule.<sup>[[58]](#ref58)</sup>

### Predictors of recurrence and operative complexity

- **Postoperative complications within 90 days** were associated with stenosis recurrence (HR 4.85, p = 0.007)<sup>[[20]](#ref20)</sup>
- **Persistent urinary extravasation at the second RUG** was associated with stenosis recurrence (HR 6.36, p = 0.006)<sup>[[20]](#ref20)</sup>
- **Pelvic artery angioembolization** (p = 0.03). Likely a marker of more severe pelvic vascular injury<sup>[[4]](#ref4)</sup>
- **Longer distraction defects** (p = 0.01)<sup>[[4]](#ref4)</sup>
- **Prior transurethral treatment.** OR 2.77 for elaborate approach<sup>[[24]](#ref24)</sup>
- **Prior failed endoscopic procedures** may complicate subsequent urethroplasty<sup>[[5]](#ref5)</sup>

## MRI Findings to Inform Surgical Planning

These associations guide preparation; they do not dictate an approach or replace intraoperative assessment of a tension-free repair.

| MRI Finding | Surgical Implication | Predicted Approach |
|---|---|---|
| Large PUA, short gap, no lateral displacement | Simple perineal repair may be feasible | Steps 1–2<sup>[[10]](#ref10)[[24]](#ref24)</sup> |
| Small PUA, moderate gap, proximal stump behind pubis | Inferior pubectomy likely required | Step 3<sup>[[10]](#ref10)[[24]](#ref24)</sup> |
| Very small PUA, long gap, significant lateral / superior prostatic displacement | Abdominoperineal approach may be needed | Step 4 or transpubic<sup>[[10]](#ref10)[[24]](#ref24)[[25]](#ref25)</sup> |
| Cavernous avulsion + lateral prostatic displacement | Structural injury associated with ED in a small historical cohort; assess function and counsel with uncertainty | Any approach; ED counseling<sup>[[23]](#ref23)</sup> |
| No cavernous avulsion, no lateral displacement | Absence of these findings does not guarantee normal or recovered erectile function | Any approach; favorable prognosis<sup>[[23]](#ref23)</sup> |
| Periurethral false passages or cavitation | Anticipate complex dissection; consider tissue interposition | Elaborate perineal or transpubic<sup>[[22]](#ref22)</sup> |
| Prior transurethral treatment | 2.77× increased odds of elaborate approach | Prepare for Steps 3–4<sup>[[24]](#ref24)</sup> |

## Special Considerations

### Pediatric PFUI

Children with PFUI present unique challenges due to ongoing growth. The same principles apply (SPT followed by delayed urethroplasty), with special attention to preserving growth potential and minimizing damage to the developing corpora cavernosa.<sup>[[18]](#ref18)[[36]](#ref36)[[37]](#ref37)</sup>

- **Zhang / Xu 2006 (n = 24 boys, age 7–14).** Transperineal-inferior pubic approach yielded the best results (90% success) compared to simple perineal (60%) or transpubic-perineal (77.8%). However, 3 of 9 patients treated with the transpubic-perineal approach walked with an abnormal gait postoperatively.<sup>[[36]](#ref36)</sup>
- **Wang / Guo 2020 (n = 22 boys, age 2–14).** 90.9% overall success (20/22). The transperineal inferior pubic approach or simpler procedures were used in 20/22 boys without completely removing or incising the pubis; the combined transpubic-perineal approach was used in 2/22 boys with very long defects, and both succeeded.<sup>[[37]](#ref37)</sup>
- **Li 2025** specifically advocated one-sided urethral rerouting for young patients to minimize secondary trauma to the developing corpora cavernosa, preserving erectile potential.<sup>[[18]](#ref18)</sup>
- **Anterior sagittal trans-anorectal approach (ASTRA).** Described as an alternative exposure for pelvic fracture with severe urethral dislocation and longer (2–3 cm) defects, as an alternative to pubectomy/symphysiotomy; outcome data are limited to a single small series.<sup>[[40]](#ref40)</sup>
- **Sreeranga 2022 (n = 192, the largest pediatric PFUI series)**, from the same center as the adult Sa/Xu series above: 181 boys and 11 girls, ages ≤18 (primary vs redo cases 47% vs 53% in boys). Transperineal anastomotic urethroplasty was used in 88.4% of boys and transpubic urethroplasty in 9.4%; overall TPAU success was 81.2% in boys (88.3% for primary cases) and TPU success 64.7%, while girls treated with TPU had 100% success. Follow-up was unavailable for 74 boys and four girls. Secondary procedures (endoscopic urethrotomy or redo surgery) were needed in 39 boys and 1 girl.<sup>[[60]](#ref60)</sup>
- General pediatric technique and management principles have been summarized as follows: as in adults, delayed repair after several months allows the scar to mature and the pelvic hematoma to resolve, letting the bladder descend and often converting an apparently large acute defect into a smaller one; reevaluating gap length with imaging and cystourethroscopy closer to the repair date is recommended. Excision with primary anastomosis is described as typically feasible even for severe defects without tension concerns; additional length can be obtained first by partial pubectomy, then by mobilizing the bladder through an abdominal incision if needed. A suprapubic tube is placed when urethral drainage cannot be achieved acutely, and the perineal-repair catheter is left about 2–3 weeks. Postoperative complications (urinary retention, incontinence, penile shortening, ED) occur at lower rates than in adults with the same injury pattern; in one pediatric cohort, stricture recurred in about 10%, incontinence occurred in up to 20%, and ED occurred in up to half, with figures varying by series.<sup>[[61]](#ref61)</sup>
- **Erectile dysfunction risk.** Among 60 responding participants assessed a median of 13 years after trauma, 28 (47%) had erectile dysfunction at puberty (1 in 2 children, by the authors’ framing); urethral gap length 2.5 cm or greater and lateral prostatic displacement were the independent predictors on multivariate analysis, and the cause was arteriogenic in most affected boys on duplex ultrasound. These findings support counseling about ED risk in relation to gap length and lateral prostatic displacement, while keeping the 47% estimate specific to this follow-up cohort.<sup>[[41]](#ref41)</sup>

### Female PFUI

Female injuries follow a different pathway: evaluate with cystourethroscopy and vaginoscopy. The EAU strongly recommends early repair within seven days, rather than the delayed male-PFUI pathway. Bladder-neck/prostatic lacerations in males also require prompt injury-specific repair, rather than routine deferral.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[6]](#ref6)[[7]](#ref7)[[38]](#ref38)</sup>

A high index of suspicion is needed: female urethral injury is easily missed because catheterization of the short, straight female urethra is often possible despite the injury. Suspect urethral injury with unstable or multiple pelvic fractures, pelvic-ring or sacral injury, vaginal laceration, blood at the introitus, urethrorrhagia, labial swelling, retention, or difficulty passing a catheter. A 25-patient series found the injury could be missed on initial assessment, and vaginal laceration has been reported in most cases.<sup>[[48]](#ref48)</sup> In a systematic review of 158 women with pelvic-fracture urethral injury, radiologic studies (RUG/VCUG) diagnosed only 29% of reported cases, and physical examination with or without attempted catheterization diagnosed 21%; these are proportions of cases diagnosed by each method across the pooled literature, not formal test-sensitivity estimates, and they support examination under anesthesia with cystourethroscopy and vaginoscopy as the more reliable evaluation.<sup>[[49]](#ref49)</sup>

When vaginal cicatrization from the original injury restricts surgical access, a deep posterolateral episiotomy extending across the bulbospongiosus and transverse perineal muscles (and, if needed, the levator muscle) has been described to improve working space, with layered repair of the divided muscles and vaginoplasty when needed to restore usable vaginal caliber. Reported adjuncts for severe introital contracture include a thigh flap similar to the Singapore fasciocutaneous flap and, in another case, leaving a deep vaginal incision open under a glass vaginal mold for four weeks followed by frequent self-dilation; these are individual case examples rather than a standard protocol.<sup>[[50]](#ref50)[[51]](#ref51)</sup>

For selected female PFUI, an abdominal route has been described: enter the retropubic space extraperitoneally, use posterior/superior pubectomy for exposure, open the bladder neck under suprapubic-nephroscope guidance, and identify the distal urethral end over a dilator passed from the meatus, with a tension-free bladder-neck-to-urethral-mucosa anastomosis over a 14 Fr silicone catheter using six 5-0 polydioxanone sutures (posterior sutures tied before the catheter is passed across the join). For an associated urethrovaginal fistula, a transvaginal guidewire localizes the tract, the fistula is closed, and an omental flap is interposed between the urethra and anterior vaginal wall. These are the source authors' operative steps, without a comparative female outcome series.<sup>[[57]](#ref57)</sup>

### Associated injuries

PFUI rarely occurs in isolation:<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[6]](#ref6)</sup>

- **Bladder injury.** Concomitant bladder injury is reported in 15% of posterior urethral injuries<sup>[[3]](#ref3)</sup>
- **Rectal injury.** Up to 5% of cases in the EAU text (requires rectal and vaginal examination)<sup>[[38]](#ref38)</sup>
- **Vascular injury.** 13% required pelvic artery embolization in the TURNS series<sup>[[4]](#ref4)</sup>
- **Orthopedic injuries.** Multiple fractures, acetabular fractures and lower-extremity injuries may delay positioning for urethroplasty. AUA permits SPT placement during pelvic ORIF (Statement 21; Expert Opinion); its discussion finds no demonstrated increase in hardware infection, with gross fecal contamination or open fractures as possible exceptions.<sup>[[3]](#ref3)</sup> Pelvic-fixation hardware placed near the urethra or bladder can also complicate later reconstructive surgery and, rarely, erode into the urinary tract years afterward (see [Bladder Trauma — Complications](/docs/special-populations/05a-trauma-emergencies/bladder-trauma#complications))

### Impact of prior endoscopic interventions

The AUA Urethral Stricture Disease Guideline (2023) explicitly cautions against repeated endoscopic maneuvers: "Repeated endoscopic maneuvers including intermittent catheterization and blind 'cut to the light' procedures should be avoided since they are rarely successful." Koraitim warned that repeated urethrotomy of a long fibrous segment "would not only have a poor result but, by jeopardizing the elasticity of the anterior urethra, it also may undermine the chance for subsequent anastomotic urethroplasty."<sup>[[5]](#ref5)[[13]](#ref13)</sup>

The Horiguchi 2026 PUA validation study found an association: prior transurethral treatment was associated with an elaborate approach (OR 2.77, p = 0.02).<sup>[[24]](#ref24)</sup>

However, Kizer et al. found that early urethral realignment was associated with successful subsequent reconstruction in 100% (17/17) of patients in whom it was achieved, and the subsequent repair tended to be more straightforward.<sup>[[16]](#ref16)</sup>

## Management Algorithm — Male Posterior PFUI

Resuscitation takes priority; the female and bladder-neck injury exceptions above require separate planning.

| Phase | Action | Key Points |
|---|---|---|
| Acute (minutes–hours) | Establish urinary drainage | SPT preferred for complete PFUI; gentle single catheter attempt for incomplete; RUG before instrumentation when clinically feasible; do not delay life-saving care<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[2]](#ref2)[[3]](#ref3)</sup> |
| Acute (days) | Consider PER | Only if patient hemodynamically stable; no proven benefit over SPT for complete injuries; may benefit partial injuries<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[11]](#ref11)[[12]](#ref12)</sup> |
| Subacute (weeks–months) | Stabilize associated injuries | Orthopedic fixation, soft-tissue healing; urology follow-up for ED / incontinence assessment<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup> |
| Preoperative (3–6 mo)<sup>[[13]](#ref13)</sup> | Imaging and planning | Combined antegrade / retrograde urethrography; selective MRI for complex anatomy<sup>[[8]](#ref8)[[10]](#ref10)[[22]](#ref22)[[24]](#ref24)</sup> |
| Definitive repair | Delayed perineal anastomotic urethroplasty | Sequential ancillary maneuvers as needed; abdominoperineal approach for complex cases<sup>[[15]](#ref15)[[16]](#ref16)[[19]](#ref19)[[29]](#ref29)[[30]](#ref30)</sup> |
| Postoperative | Check the anastomosis before catheter removal, then follow long term | In one 70-man primary-repair cohort, pericatheter RUG was obtained at about 14 days and repeated weekly if extravasation persisted.<sup>[[20]](#ref20)</sup> Monitor for recurrence (median time to recurrence 3 months among the 11 recurrent cases), erectile dysfunction and incontinence; consider AUS after incontinence is stable.<sup>[[14]](#ref14)[[20]](#ref20)</sup> |

AUA recommends monitoring for urethral stricture, erectile dysfunction and incontinence for at least one year after urethral injury (Statement 23; Recommendation, Grade C). Uroflowmetry, RUG and cystoscopy are used as indicated.<sup>[[3]](#ref3)</sup>

## Key Takeaways

- Most complete male PFUI injuries are initially diverted with SPT and reconstructed after stabilization; selected patients may have earlier repair or endoscopic realignment. Female PFUI and bladder-neck lacerations require different timing.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[38]](#ref38)</sup>
- MRI supplements urethrography in complex cases. The PUA/prior-treatment model had AUC 0.782 in a retrospective validation cohort; imaging does not guarantee operative simplicity or predict an individual’s erectile recovery.<sup>[[24]](#ref24)</sup>
- Sequential perineal maneuvers aim for a tension-free anastomosis; selected complex cases need abdominal access. Success definitions, follow-up and injury severity vary across series.<sup>[[4]](#ref4)[[15]](#ref15)[[29]](#ref29)</sup>
- Contemporary realignment evidence conflicts: Owen’s 2025 meta-analysis found no stricture difference, whereas Wen and Jiang’s 2025 meta-analysis favored primary realignment. Both pooled heterogeneous nonrandomized cohorts; the prospective TURNS complete-PFUI cohort found no lower obstruction rate with realignment.<sup>[[12]](#ref12)[[64]](#ref64)[[11]](#ref11)</sup>
- Record baseline erectile and continence function and discuss both trauma-related morbidity and new dysfunction after reconstruction.<sup>[[20]](#ref20)</sup>
