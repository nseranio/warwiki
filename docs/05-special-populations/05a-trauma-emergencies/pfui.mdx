# Pelvic Fracture Urethral Injury (PFUI)

Pelvic fracture urethral injury (PFUI) is a devastating complication of high-energy pelvic ring disruption, occurring in up to 5% of patients with anterior pelvic ring fractures.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup> PFUI is predominantly a male injury caused by distraction of the membranous urethra away from the bulbar urethra at the time of pelvic ring displacement. Management requires immediate urinary diversion, multidisciplinary coordination with orthopaedic surgery, and planned delayed urological follow-up for the near-universal sequelae of urethral stenosis, erectile dysfunction, and urinary incontinence. This page summarizes the initial evaluation, classification, acute management, and long-term outcomes based on the ACS Best Practices Guidelines for Management of Genitourinary Injuries (August 2025).<sup>[[2]](#ref2)</sup>

## Epidemiology and Mechanism

PFUI results from disruption of the pelvic ring causing distraction of the membranous urethra from the bulbar urethra at the level of the urogenital diaphragm.<sup>[[3]](#ref3)</sup> Injury patterns most commonly associated with PFUI include:<sup>[[2]](#ref2)</sup>

- Fractures of the sacroiliac joint
- Anterior arch fractures with displacement
- Diastasis of the pubic symphysis

Each millimeter of symphysis pubis diastasis or inferomedial pubic bone fracture displacement is associated with an estimated **10% relative increased risk** of urethral injury.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup> Female urethral injury from PFUI is exceedingly rare but does occur; the short, mobile female urethra is less susceptible to distraction injury.

:::note Associated Injuries
Concomitant urethral injury and pelvic fracture require close multidisciplinary care and communication between urology, orthopaedics, trauma surgery, and intensive care. Each millimeter of symphysis diastasis or pubic bone displacement dramatically increases injury probability.
:::

## Clinical Presentation

Maintain a high index of suspicion for urethral injury in any patient with a pelvic fracture and any of the following signs:<sup>[[2]](#ref2)</sup>

- **Blood at the urethral meatus** — most specific clinical finding
- Suprapubic fullness (distended bladder unable to drain)
- Perineal laceration
- Scrotal or butterfly perineal hematoma (blood tracking along Colles' fascial planes)
- Inability to void or difficulty passing a Foley catheter

:::note High-Riding Prostate
A high-riding prostate on digital rectal exam has **both low sensitivity and low specificity** for posterior urethral disruption. Digital rectal exam to detect a high-riding prostate is **not recommended** as part of the diagnostic workup for suspected urethral injury.<sup>[[2]](#ref2)</sup>
:::

The absence of these signs does not exclude a urethral injury. When urethral injury is suspected, a digital rectal examination **and vaginal examination** (in females) should be performed to identify concomitant rectal or vaginal injury (~5% of urethral injuries involve a rectal or vaginal component).<sup>[[4]](#ref4)</sup>

## AAST Urethral Injury Scale

| Grade | Description |
|---|---|
| **I** | Contusion; blood at meatus; normal urethrogram |
| **II** | Stretch injury; elongation of urethra without extravasation on urethrogram |
| **III** | Partial disruption; extravasation of contrast at injury site with contrast visualized in the bladder |
| **IV** | Complete disruption; &lt;2 cm urethral separation; no contrast reaching the bladder |
| **V** | Complete disruption; posterior urethral separation >2 cm, or extension into prostate or vagina |

## Initial Evaluation: Retrograde Urethrogram (RUG)

RUG is the gold standard for diagnosis and classification of male urethral injury.<sup>[[5]](#ref5)</sup> Cystourethroscopy is an acceptable alternative when RUG is not readily available.

**Important sequencing consideration:** If the patient has a pelvic fracture and CT scanning will assess for arterial extravasation (e.g., pelvic angioembolization planning), perform the **CT first and the RUG second**. Contrast extravasation from urethral injection can cause significant artifact on CT that compromises assessment for arterial pelvic bleeding.<sup>[[2]](#ref2)</sup>

### RUG Technique

1. Position patient supine at 30°–45° oblique (or rotate C-arm if patient cannot move)
2. Insert a 16 Fr Foley catheter into the urethral meatus approximately 2 cm; inflate balloon with 2–3 cc air or saline to occlude contrast backflow. Alternatively: gauze lasso technique or cone-tip adapter at meatus
3. Apply gentle traction on penis to elongate and visualize entire urethral course
4. Obtain scout view before contrast injection (avoids false positives from prior IV contrast extravasation)
5. Inject 10–30 mL of undiluted non-ionic iodinated contrast under fluoroscopy (take images at 10 mL intervals); stop when extravasation is visualized or bladder fills
6. Fluoroscopy is preferred over static plain film for real-time assessment

**Interpretation:**
- Contrast reaching the bladder = **incomplete (partial) injury** — some urethral continuity preserved
- Contrast extravasation without bladder opacification = **complete injury**

:::note Female Urethral Evaluation
RUG is **not recommended** in patients with female anatomy due to poor technical feasibility. Evaluation of suspected female urethral injury requires cystourethroscopy and vaginoscopy, with gynecologic consultation. A complete speculum examination is essential to identify associated vaginal or rectal injury.<sup>[[2]](#ref2)</sup>
:::

## Acute Management: Urinary Diversion

### Suprapubic Tube (SPT)

**Complete PFUI** should be managed initially with **SPT placement** rather than urethral catheterization.<sup>[[2]](#ref2)</sup> SPT is also the preferred bladder drainage method in hemodynamically unstable patients.

**SPT insertion technique:**
- Insert by ultrasound-guided percutaneous placement or open cystotomy (bladder may be displaced by pelvic hematoma or poorly filled with concomitant bladder injury)
- Use a **14 Fr or larger true Foley catheter** (not pigtail catheters — these require exchange due to drainage difficulties and clogging)
- Coordinate with orthopaedics if anterior pelvic hardware is planned

**For incomplete injury:** SPT or urethral catheterization are both acceptable options. If a RUG demonstrates contrast reaching the bladder (partial injury), a **single, gentle attempt at Foley catheter placement** by experienced personnel is justified. Alternatively, flexible cystourethroscopy or fluoroscopic guidance can assist catheter placement across an incomplete injury.

:::note Catheter Placement After Failed RUG
When a urethral injury is suspected but imaging is not immediately available, a **single attempt** at urethral catheter placement by an experienced team member is justified. If any resistance is encountered or blood is noted, **stop immediately** and obtain urological consultation. Do not force the catheter.
:::

### Primary Endoscopic Realignment (PER)

PER involves endoscopic manipulation of the urethra under fluoroscopy to pass a catheter across the area of disruption in the acute setting (within a few days of injury, once the patient is hemodynamically stable).<sup>[[2]](#ref2)</sup>

Key evidence-based considerations:<sup>[[2]](#ref2),[[6]](#ref6)</sup>

- **No strong evidence** that PER significantly reduces stenosis rates or reconstructive complexity after **complete** PFUI compared to SPT alone
- For **partial** urethral injury, successful PER may lower stenosis rates in this subgroup
- PER does not eliminate the need for subsequent urological follow-up and potential urethroplasty
- **Open urethral realignment has no role in PFUI management** — associated with substantially increased complications (incontinence, erectile dysfunction, stricture formation)
- If a urologist elects PER, place SPT first while awaiting clinical stabilization; PER timing is within the first several days

## Long-Term Sequelae and Follow-Up

PFUI carries a high likelihood of long-term urological morbidity:<sup>[[2]](#ref2)</sup>

| Complication | Incidence | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| **Urethral stenosis/obliterative distraction defect** | >90% after complete injury | Typically manifests within 12 months of injury; nearly universal after complete PFUI |
| **Erectile dysfunction** | 20%–35% | Varies by injury location, severity, and management; injury to cavernous nerves and vessels at time of pelvic ring disruption |
| **Urinary incontinence** | 15%–20% | Bladder neck or sphincter involvement; worse with higher injury grades |

All patients with PFUI or straddle injury should have a **urology follow-up appointment scheduled at the time of hospital discharge** to coordinate evaluation for these sequelae and plan urethral reconstruction.<sup>[[2]](#ref2)</sup>

## Delayed Urethroplasty

Delayed posterior urethroplasty via a perineal approach (anastomotic urethroplasty) is the definitive treatment for post-PFUI obliterative urethral distraction defects. Key principles:<sup>[[7]](#ref7)</sup>

- Optimal timing: typically 3–6 months post-injury, after pelvic hematoma resolution and scar maturation
- Perioperative imaging: voiding cystourethrogram and/or flexible cystoscopy to characterize the defect
- Approach: perineal anastomotic urethroplasty (bulboprostatic or bulbomembranous anastomosis depending on defect location and length)
- Success rates: >90% at experienced centers
- Early primary realignment (when successful) does not eliminate the need for subsequent urethroplasty in most complete injury patients

## Special Populations

**Pediatric:** The basic principles of diagnosis and management mirror the adult algorithm; however, children may have limited tolerance for awake RUG, catheter placement, and cystourethroscopy. Procedural sedation or general anesthesia may be required for adequate evaluation.<sup>[[2]](#ref2)</sup>

**Geriatric:** Older adults are more likely to have preexisting lower urinary tract pathology (urethral stricture, prostatic enlargement) and may have urologic implants such as artificial urinary sphincters. A focused history to identify prior urethral conditions, surgeries, and prosthetics should be obtained before any urethral instrumentation.<sup>[[2]](#ref2)</sup>
