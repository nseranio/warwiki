# Penile Fracture

**Penile fracture** is a urological emergency: a traumatic tear of the tunica albuginea, the dense fibrous sheath encasing the corpora cavernosa, caused by excessive bending force applied to an erect penis. The diagnosis is primarily clinical. Prompt surgical repair is advised, and a retrospective comparison found worse erectile and curvature outcomes with conservative treatment.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup> The ACS estimates that about 15% of penile fractures involve a urethral injury, which is identified and repaired at the same operation.<sup>[[2]](#ref2)</sup> This page follows the ACS Best Practices Guidelines for Management of Genitourinary Injuries (August 2025).<sup>[[2]](#ref2)</sup>

## Mechanism and Anatomy

Penile fracture occurs when the erect penis bends excessively during sexual activity or is forcibly deflected against the perineum, partner's body, or bedding. The classic injury mechanism involves aggressive manual manipulation or atypical coital positioning. Intracavernosal pressure during the injury has been reported to exceed 1,500 mmHg, enough to rupture the tunica albuginea when the force is applied perpendicular to its fibers.

The tunica albuginea is thinnest on the ventral and lateral surfaces of the penile shaft, distal to the suspensory ligament; these are the most frequent sites of rupture.<sup>[[2]](#ref2)</sup> Fractures may be unilateral or bilateral, involving one or both corpora cavernosa. The penile and bulbar urethra belong to the anterior urethra. They run within the corpus spongiosum, ventral to the corpora cavernosa; the bulbospongiosus muscle overlies the urethral bulb. Associated spongiosal and urethral injury must be assessed.

## Clinical Presentation and Diagnosis

The diagnosis of penile fracture is made by history and physical examination in the majority of cases; imaging is not required when the clinical picture is unambiguous.<sup>[[2]](#ref2)</sup>

**Classic triad:**
1. Audible popping or cracking sound at the time of injury
2. Immediate detumescence with rapid deflation of the erection
3. Severe penile pain, ecchymosis, and swelling

**Physical examination findings:**
- Penile ecchymosis and hematoma (may involve the scrotum and perineum if Buck's fascia is disrupted)
- Penile deviation or deformity ("eggplant" or "rolled sausage" deformity depending on hematoma extent)
- Palpable defect in the tunica albuginea (present in some cases)

**When Imaging Is Indicated.** For patients with indeterminate history or physical examination (atypical mechanism, delayed presentation, obesity preventing palpation, or complex anatomy), penile ultrasound or MRI may be used to evaluate for fracture. Ultrasound is operator-dependent; MRI can clarify an inconclusive examination or ultrasound when promptly available. Imaging must not delay repair of a clinically clear fracture.<sup>[[2]](#ref2)</sup>

## Associated Urethral Injury

The ACS and EAU guidelines both cite urethral injury in about 15% of penile fractures (EAU range 1 to 38% depending on aetiology).<sup>[[2]](#ref2)</sup><sup>[[4]](#ref4)</sup> A 2023 systematic review of 15 studies (65 of 1,665 patients) found a lower point prevalence of 3.9%.<sup>[[5]](#ref5)</sup> Evaluate for urethral involvement when any of the following is present:<sup>[[2]](#ref2)</sup>

- Blood at the urethral meatus
- Gross hematuria
- Difficulty urinating, weak urinary stream, or acute urinary retention
- Inability to pass a urethral catheter

When urethral injury is suspected, perform RUG or cystourethroscopy before or at the time of surgical exploration. Avoid forceful or repeated blind catheter attempts. The urethra may be involved in up to 50% of penile penetrating (ballistic) injuries as well.<sup>[[2]](#ref2)</sup>

## Operative Management

### Timing

Arrange prompt surgical exploration and repair. The ACS guideline advises exploration within 7 days of injury and notes that earlier repair may improve long-term outcomes, including less pain, faster convalescence and a lower risk of curvature.<sup>[[2]](#ref2)</sup> The EAU strongly recommends immediate urethral repair when the urethra is injured.<sup>[[4]](#ref4)</sup> Seven days is an upper limit, not a target waiting period.

The el-Assmy retrospective study included 180 operated patients: 149 presented within 24 hours and 31 presented 30 hours to seven days after injury. It found no significant difference in complications between groups. This supports offering repair to late presenters; it does not establish equivalence or a benefit from delaying surgery.<sup>[[3]](#ref3)</sup>

Conservative (non-operative) management is associated with higher complication rates, including abscess, penile fibrosis, Peyronie's disease-like plaques and chronic erectile dysfunction. In the retrospective Gamal series (77 patients, median follow-up 20.8 months), 49 of 51 followed surgical patients (96%) and 9 of 18 followed conservative patients (50%) reported erections adequate for intercourse without curvature.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup> Surgical repair is the standard of care.

For patients presenting to a lower-level trauma center without urology coverage, consider early transfer to a center with urology expertise once the patient is stabilized.

### Surgical Approach

The incision approach is based on the suspected location of injury:<sup>[[2]](#ref2)</sup>

| Injury Location | Preferred Incision |
|---|---|
| Distal or unknown | Circumcising degloving incision (allows complete bilateral corpus exposure) |
| Proximal or ventral penoscrotal | Ventral midline penoscrotal incision |

**Technique:**

1. **Degloving:** Mobilize penile skin to allow full exposure of the bilateral corpora cavernosa and the region of suspected tunica albuginea disruption
2. **Artificial erection (optional):** Inject saline (with or without dilute methylene blue) directly into the corporal body to identify the exact location and extent of the tunica tear when the fracture site is not immediately apparent
3. **Tunica repair:** Close the tunica albuginea disruption with absorbable sutures (typically 2-0 polyglycolic acid or PDS); horizontal mattress or interrupted sutures depending on tear configuration; ensure a watertight closure
4. **Concomitant urethral repair:** When urethral injury is identified at the time of tunica repair, perform urethroplasty over a urethral catheter using a tension-free, watertight closure with absorbable suture; a Foley catheter is left in place regardless of whether SPT is also placed
5. **Drain:** Closed-suction drain placement is at surgeon discretion; typically not required for isolated fracture repair

### Post-Operative Care

- Urethral catheter for 7–14 days if urethral repair was performed
- Sexual abstinence for 4–6 weeks minimum
- Follow-up for assessment of penile curvature, erectile function, and voiding (if urethral repair performed)

## Outcomes and Complications

| Outcome | Notes |
|---|---|
| **Erectile dysfunction** | Rare after prompt repair; higher with delayed repair or conservative management |
| **Penile curvature** | More common with delayed repair; may require Nesbit procedure or grafting if symptomatic |
| **Urethral stricture** | Risk increases with concurrent urethral injury; follow-up voiding cystourethrogram or uroflowmetry is advised at 3 months if urethroplasty was performed. The EAU cites a 2% stricture rate after immediate urethral reconstruction,<sup>[[4]](#ref4)</sup> and the systematic review of penile fracture with urethral injury found 8.5% (5 of 59)<sup>[[5]](#ref5)</sup> |
| **Wound complications** | Uncommon; hematoma and wound dehiscence are the most frequent early complications |

## Penetrating Penile Injury

Penile penetrating injuries are most commonly caused by gunshot wounds and are frequently associated with abdominal, pelvic, and lower extremity injuries.<sup>[[2]](#ref2)</sup> The urethra is involved in up to 50% of penile ballistic injuries.<sup>[[2]](#ref2)</sup>

**Management:**
- Explore significant penetrating injuries promptly, with irrigation, conservative debridement, removal of foreign bodies and repair.<sup>[[2]](#ref2)</sup> Selected small superficial injuries with intact Buck’s fascia have been managed without exploration after assessment.
- Corporal body involvement: repair as for penile fracture (absorbable suture closure of corporotomy or tunica defect)
- Urethral involvement identified at exploration: debridement and primary urethroplasty over a urethral catheter with tension-free, watertight closure
- For patients at lower-level trauma centers: transfer to a center with urology expertise for definitive penile injury management once stabilized
