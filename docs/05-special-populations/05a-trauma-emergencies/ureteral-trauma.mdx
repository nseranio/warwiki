# Ureteral Trauma

Ureteral injuries are rare: the cited national trauma registry identified 582 among 22,706 GU injuries (about 2.6%), or approximately 3 per 10,000 trauma admissions. The ureter's retroperitoneal location, small caliber, and mobility protect it from most external forces, but when injury does occur, delayed or missed diagnosis is common and leads to serious morbidity including urinoma, abscess, stricture, and renal loss.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup> Hematuria is absent in up to 55% of ureteral injuries, so imaging is driven by mechanism and trajectory rather than by urinalysis.<sup>[[4]](#ref4)</sup> EAU gives a range of 50–75% for its presence and a strong recommendation to keep ureteral injury in mind in all abdominal penetrating trauma and in deceleration-type blunt trauma.<sup>[[9]](#ref9)</sup>

**Cross-Reference.** Ureter-specific imaging protocols (CT delayed phase, RPG technique) are covered in detail in [Imaging](/docs/evaluation/imaging) (Evaluation & Workup). A summary is included below.

## Epidemiology and Mechanism

- Penetrating trauma (ballistic > stab) is the predominant mechanism in adults<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup>
- Blunt deceleration accounts for 5–40% of ureteral injuries.<sup>[[4]](#ref4)</sup> **UPJ avulsion** is the characteristic blunt pediatric pattern, attributed to the more mobile pediatric spine and less protective perirenal fat
- Associated injuries are common: in the national registry, penetrating ureteral trauma involved small bowel in 46%, large bowel in 44%, and vascular structures in 38%; categories overlap.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup>
- In one traumatic-injury series, 5/40 injuries (12.5%) were missed at initial laparotomy; this is not a universal rate for iatrogenic injury. Delayed diagnosis increases morbidity<sup>[[3]](#ref3)</sup>
- **Iatrogenic** injury during pelvic surgery is a distinct population; one 103-patient series found delayed recognition in 20%, with more subsequent procedures and health-care use.<sup>[[6]](#ref6)</sup> Across a mixed iatrogenic-injury series, laparoscopic gynecologic surgery accounted for 64% of injuries, versus 26% general-surgical and 11% urologic procedures; intraoperative detection was lowest for laparoscopic procedures (12.5%) compared with open (43.5%) and endourologic (62.5%) surgery. Reported ureteral injury rates are 2.2% for routine hysterectomy and pelvic gynecologic procedures, up to 30% for radical hysterectomy, and up to 6% for laparoscopic hysterectomy specifically.<sup>[[11]](#ref11)</sup>
- **Prophylactic ureteral stenting** to aid intraoperative identification during non-urologic surgery is associated with fewer ureteral injuries in gynecologic surgery (pooled OR 0.61; number needed to stent to prevent one injury, 224) but not in colorectal surgery, per a meta-analysis of 46 studies; laparoscopic hysterectomy itself carries a higher injury rate than open (OR 2.12).<sup>[[12]](#ref12)</sup>
- **Endoscopic stone extraction** is a distinct iatrogenic mechanism: aggressive basket or forceps extraction of a large stone can avulse the ureter, described in case series and case reports rather than a large cohort. Reported reconstructive options include ureteroneocystostomy, ileal substitution and other repairs selected by defect length.<sup>[[13]](#ref13)</sup> For endoscopic ureteral injury, AUA recommends a stent or nephrostomy when possible (Statement 13a; Recommendation, Grade C), and permits open repair if diversion is impossible or inadequate (Statement 13b; Expert Opinion). Its acute-trauma guidance advises against bowel interposition and renal autotransplantation in the acute setting.<sup>[[17]](#ref17)</sup>

## Imaging and Diagnosis

### CT with Delayed (Excretory) Phase

AUA recommends contrast-enhanced CT with delayed images in stable patients with suspected ureteral injury (Statement 9a; Recommendation, Grade C); its discussion describes 10-minute delays and further delayed images if ureteral opacification remains inadequate.<sup>[[17]](#ref17)</sup> The ACS guideline recommends a delayed phase when ureteral injury is suspected, either routinely or selectively according to the facility protocol. Delayed images are traditionally taken 3–20 minutes after contrast injection, and about 10 minutes is reported as optimal for collecting-system injury:<sup>[[4]](#ref4)</sup>

| Finding | Significance |
|---|---|
| Contrast extravasation on delayed phase | Diagnostic of collecting system / ureteral injury |
| Periureteral fluid, retroperitoneal hematoma | Indirect sign |
| Ipsilateral delayed or absent nephrogram | Suggests UPJ avulsion or vascular compromise |
| Lack of ureteral opacification distal to injury | Raises concern for obstruction or disruption; inadequate timing/excretion can also prevent opacification |

**Ureteral Injury on Standard Trauma CT.** A standard trauma CT (portal venous phase only) can miss ureteral injuries. If ureteral injury is suspected, explicitly request or add a delayed-phase acquisition (about 10 minutes). Do not rely on a single-shot IV pyelogram at laparotomy to exclude ureteral injury; only 40% of excretory urograms were diagnostic in the cited external-trauma series.<sup>[[2]](#ref2)[[4]](#ref4)</sup> EAU likewise reports that one-shot IVP is negative in up to 60% of patients and names CT urography as the examination of choice when ureteral injury is suspected.<sup>[[9]](#ref9)</sup>

### Retrograde Pyelography (RPG)

RPG is the most accurate imaging test when CT findings are equivocal. Urology performs it cystoscopically, and it shows the exact injury location, extent and degree of obstruction. It is indicated for:<sup>[[4]](#ref4)</sup>

- Equivocal CT findings
- Operative planning before repair
- Delayed-presentation injuries with uncertain level

**Surgical drain fluid.** When a drain is already in place after pelvic surgery, a spot drain-fluid creatinine above the serum creatinine is a described clue to urine leakage; it is not a validated ratio or stand-alone test, and the injury still needs to be localized with CT or RPG.<sup>[[14]](#ref14)</sup>

### Intraoperative Detection

AUA calls for direct ureteral inspection during laparotomy when injury is suspected and preoperative imaging was not obtained (Statement 9b; Clinical Principle). Adjuncts include the following:<sup>[[4]](#ref4)[[17]](#ref17)</sup>

- Direct visual inspection of entire ureter
- IV dye may assist inspection, but normal dye efflux does not exclude partial or evolving ischemic injury; check agent contraindications and the [visualization-agent hub](/docs/foundations/pharmacology/intraoperative-adjuncts/visualization-agents)
- Direct injection of dye into the renal pelvis
- Retrograde ureteral catheterization via cystotomy or cystoscopy

## AAST Organ Injury Scale — Ureter

| Grade | Type | Description |
|---|---|---|
| **I** | Hematoma | Contusion or hematoma without devascularization |
| **II** | Laceration | &lt;50% transection |
| **III** | Laceration | &gt;50% transection |
| **IV** | Laceration | Complete transection with &lt;2 cm devascularization |
| **V** | Laceration | Avulsion with >2 cm devascularization |

*Advance one grade for bilateral injuries (up to grade III).*<sup>[[5]](#ref5)</sup>

## Endoscopic Management

Minor contusions (AAST grade I, without devascularization) can be managed with ureteral stent placement alone. Avoid endoscopic management when a contusion is severe or spans a long segment.<sup>[[4]](#ref4)</sup> For a distal ureteral perforation recognized during endoscopic instrumentation, stent removal at approximately 4–6 weeks is a described practice; temporary Foley drainage to limit bladder reflux onto the stent is optional. Complete avulsion requires reconstruction; nephrostomy can provide initial drainage when immediate repair is infeasible.<sup>[[14]](#ref14)[[17]](#ref17)</sup>

**AAST Grade II Lacerations.** The ACS guideline does not extend its stent-alone recommendation past contusion (Grade I), and EAU strongly recommends immediate formal repair for iatrogenic injuries recognized during surgery, so a Grade II laceration found acutely at operation should generally be repaired rather than stented alone.<sup>[[4]](#ref4)[[9]](#ref9)</sup> Selected small, sharp partial lacerations discovered late or judged low-risk are a separate, individualized decision: in one blunt-trauma series, 7 of 9 partial ureteral transections were managed successfully with observation or a retrograde stent alone (89% treatment success), so stent-alone management of a partial laceration is a reasonable option in the right patient, not a rule limited strictly to contusion.<sup>[[10]](#ref10)</sup>

AUA advises stenting or resection with primary repair for traumatic contusions at laparotomy, according to ureteral viability and the clinical situation (Statement 10c; Expert Opinion).<sup>[[17]](#ref17)</sup>

**High-Velocity Ballistic Injuries.** Blast effect from high-velocity projectiles causes microvascular injury extending well beyond the visible wound. Delayed ureteral necrosis may occur 5–7 days after injury with subsequent stricture formation over weeks. Avoid endoscopic stenting alone for significant ballistic contusions; plan for early re-evaluation and low threshold for formal repair.<sup>[[4]](#ref4)</sup>

For delayed-presentation incomplete injuries, AUA recommends attempting retrograde stenting (Statement 11a; Recommendation, Grade C). If stenting fails or is impossible, place a nephrostomy with delayed repair as needed (Statement 11b; Recommendation, Grade C). Persistent leakage may require a periureteral drain or repair.<sup>[[17]](#ref17)</sup>

## Surgical Management

### Principles of Ureteral Repair

AUA recommends repair of traumatic ureteral lacerations during laparotomy in stable patients (Statement 10a; Recommendation, Grade C). Its proximal and distal repair guidance supports viable tissue, preserved blood supply, spatulation, a tension-free anastomosis and a stent (Statements 12a–12b). The following list also includes ACS technical practice:<sup>[[4]](#ref4)[[17]](#ref17)</sup>

1. Complete debridement of devitalized tissue to bleeding edges. As with high-velocity ballistic injury (above), thermal injury can compromise ureteral tissue beyond the visibly affected area; assess viability individually and debride back to healthy, bleeding tissue rather than assuming a fixed margin.<sup>[[14]](#ref14)</sup>
2. Preservation of periureteral adventitia (blood supply); balance mobilization against vascularity
3. Spatulation of both ureteral ends (creates wider anastomosis, reduces stricture risk)
4. Tension-free, watertight mucosa-to-mucosa anastomosis with fine absorbable suture
5. Double-J ureteral stent across the repair
6. Retroperitoneal closed-suction drain
7. Isolation of the repair from adjacent structures with peritoneum or omentum (EAU lists this among its principles of repair)<sup>[[9]](#ref9)</sup>

### Repair Strategy by Injury Location

#### Distal Ureter (Distal to Iliac Vessels)

| Option | Indication | Success Rate |
|---|---|---|
| **Ureteroneocystostomy** | Recommended for distal injuries whenever possible, and preferred when distal segment viability is in question. Refluxing or nonrefluxing, intravesical or extravesical techniques are all used; ACS calls the choice surgeon preference, notes that refluxing anastomoses are more common in trauma, and cites success rates of 91–98%<sup>[[4]](#ref4)</sup> | Outcomes depend on indication; a mixed 100-patient reconstruction series reported hydronephrosis resolution in 81%, not a trauma-specific cure rate<sup>[[7]](#ref7)</sup> |
| **Psoas hitch** | When tension-free direct reimplant is not possible; bladder mobilized and fixed to ipsilateral psoas tendon | Failure in 11/143 (8%) with postoperative imaging in a 166-patient mixed-indication cohort<sup>[[8]](#ref8)</sup>; EAU cites a 97% success rate for distal injuries<sup>[[9]](#ref9)</sup> |
| **Boari flap** | Long mid-to-lower defects beyond psoas hitch range. AUA permits a psoas hitch or Boari adjunct for tension-free acute repair when feasible<sup>[[17]](#ref17)</sup> | EAU reports 81–88% and describes Boari repair as time-consuming and unsuited to the acute setting<sup>[[9]](#ref9)</sup> |

AUA permits primary repair over a stent for selected distal injuries with viable tissue, favoring reimplantation when distal viability is doubtful (Statement 12b; Recommendation, Grade C).<sup>[[17]](#ref17)</sup>

#### Proximal Ureter (Proximal to Iliac Vessels)

| Option | Indication |
|---|---|
| **Ureteroureterostomy** | Primary spatulated anastomosis over stent for short-segment injuries; EAU states that defects shorter than 2–3 cm can usually be managed this way, with ureterocalicostomy considered when it is not feasible<sup>[[9]](#ref9)</sup> |
| **Heineke-Mikulicz conversion** | Partial transection at laparotomy — convert longitudinal tear to transverse repair over stent |
| **Resection + primary repair** | High-velocity GSW with blast effect: resect devitalized segment, then primary anastomosis |
| **Ureteropyelostomy** | UPJ avulsion: anastomose proximal ureter into renal pelvis |

AUA recommends proximal primary repair over a stent when feasible (Statement 12a; Recommendation, Grade C).<sup>[[17]](#ref17)</sup>

#### Long Segment / Complex Defects (Deferred)

- **Ileal ureter interposition.** Usually deferred complex reconstruction; bowel preparation is not a universal prerequisite. EAU advises avoiding it with impaired renal function or known intestinal disease and following serum chemistry for hyperchloremic acidosis<sup>[[9]](#ref9)</sup>
- **Transuretero-ureterostomy (TUU).** Selected defects with a suitable healthy recipient ureter; consider the risk to both renal units. EAU cites a stenosis rate of 4% and revision or re-intervention in 10%<sup>[[9]](#ref9)</sup>
- **Autotransplantation.** Rarely required. EAU also lists buccal mucosa ureteroplasty for long segments, especially after failed reconstruction (reported success 90%, limited experience)<sup>[[9]](#ref9)</sup>

### Damage Control Strategy

AUA permits temporary drainage followed by delayed definitive repair in unstable patients (Statement 10b; Clinical Principle), using ligation with nephrostomy or an externalized catheter secured to the proximal defect. ACS describes additional temporizing options:<sup>[[4]](#ref4)[[17]](#ref17)</sup>

| Option | Description |
|---|---|
| **Cutaneous ureterostomy** | Exteriorize proximal ureter through abdominal wall |
| **Ureteral ligation + PCN** | Ligate ureter; percutaneous nephrostomy placed in recovery |
| **Intubated ureterostomy** | Ureteral stent or small catheter secured to proximal end, externalized |
| **Intraperitoneal drainage** | Last-resort temporary damage control when instability precludes diversion; suction via temporary abdominal closure and prompt reassessment |

Definitive reconstruction is planned after resuscitation, ideally at a center with reconstructive urology expertise.

### Venous Thromboembolism Prophylaxis

Neither the ACS nor the EAU guideline gives a ureteral-injury-specific VTE recommendation. The EAU trauma guideline's general chapter notes trauma patients overall are at high DVT risk, favors compression stockings and low-molecular-weight heparin once bleeding risk allows, and reports that strict bed rest has fallen out of favor in patients able to mobilize.<sup>[[9]](#ref9)</sup> Apply the individual risk-based approach on the [VTE Prophylaxis](/docs/foundations/pharmacology/perioperative-eras/vte-prophylaxis) hub — polytrauma patients undergoing laparotomy and reconstruction typically sit at moderate-to-high baseline risk — rather than a fixed regimen.

### Delayed Diagnosis Management

For patients diagnosed late with no planned reoperation:<sup>[[4]](#ref4)</sup> EAU strongly recommends nephrostomy or double-J stent diversion for iatrogenic injuries diagnosed late, and immediate repair of iatrogenic injuries recognized during surgery.<sup>[[9]](#ref9)</sup>

- Prioritize retrograde ureteral stenting for partial injuries
- Percutaneous nephrostomy when stenting fails
- Assess healing with antegrade nephrostography before tube removal. An interval of about 6–8 weeks is common practice; neither the ACS nor the EAU guideline specifies a timing
- Plan formal surgical reconstruction if ongoing leak, urinoma, or stricture confirmed

One described approach to timing: when endoscopic management is inadequate, attempt early surgical repair if the injury is recognized within the first postoperative week and inflammation is limited; beyond that window, divert or stent while inflammation subsides and plan reconstruction after about 6 weeks, with earlier surgery still reasonable for uncontrolled infection or an undrainable collection. This is one textbook's attributed time-specific framework rather than a universal rule; a retrospective comparison of immediate, within-1-month, and 3-month repair after iatrogenic injury found no significant difference in operative time or complications across the three groups, supporting flexibility within roughly the first month rather than a strict 7-day cutoff.<sup>[[15]](#ref15)[[16]](#ref16)</sup>
