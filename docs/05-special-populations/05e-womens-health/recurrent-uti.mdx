# Recurrent UTI in Women

## At a glance

- **Assess:** confirm recurrent acute cystitis symptoms with pyuria and uropathogenic bacteria; review culture history, pelvic examination, menopausal status, intercourse association and possible incomplete emptying. Obtain urinalysis/culture for symptomatic episodes before treatment when feasible.<sup>[[3]](#ref3)</sup>
- **Options:** treat acute episodes with culture-informed short courses. Discuss vaginal estrogen when appropriate, cranberry, methenamine, and continuous or postcoital antibiotic prophylaxis using risks and preferences. D-mannose alone may not prevent recurrence.<sup>[[3]](#ref3)</sup><sup>[[6]](#ref6)</sup>
- **Reconsider the pathway:** fever/flank pain, pregnancy, catheter-associated infection, neurogenic dysfunction or a structural abnormality fall outside uncomplicated rUTI guidance. Persistent symptoms after microbiological clearance need another diagnostic explanation.<sup>[[3]](#ref3)</sup>
- **Follow-up:** review recurrences, adverse effects and ongoing need for prophylaxis. Do not obtain routine test-of-cure cultures in an asymptomatic patient; repeat cultures for persisting symptoms. Do not routinely add vitamin C to methenamine.<sup>[[3]](#ref3)</sup>

[Compare ALTAR and MERIT](#selected-evidence). Use the linked prescribing hubs for doses, contraindications and drug monitoring.

**Recurrent urinary tract infection (rUTI) in women** is defined as **≥2 UTIs in 6 months or ≥3 UTIs in 12 months** (symptomatic episodes supported by pyuria and uropathogenic bacteria), affecting nearly **25% of women** who experience an initial UTI.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[2]](#ref2)</sup> The 2025 AUA / CUA / SUFU update emphasizes symptom-linked diagnosis, antimicrobial stewardship and informed choices among preventive options.<sup>[[3]](#ref3)[[4]](#ref4)</sup>

---

## Part I: Epidemiology

Over **50% of adult women** experience at least one UTI in their lifetime.<sup>[[5]](#ref5)[[1]](#ref1)</sup> Among those with a first UTI, approximately **27–44%** will experience recurrence within 12 months.<sup>[[2]](#ref2)</sup>

**Microbiology.** *Escherichia coli* causes approximately **75–80%** of recurrent UTIs. Other common organisms include *Enterococcus faecalis*, *Proteus mirabilis*, *Klebsiella* species, and *Staphylococcus saprophyticus*, particularly in patients with risk factors for complicated UTI.<sup>[[5]](#ref5)</sup>

## Part II: Pathophysiology — evolving understanding

### A. Traditional model — fecal-perineal-urethral ascent

The classic model holds that uropathogenic bacteria from the gastrointestinal tract colonize the periurethral area, ascend the urethra, and cause cystitis. This model explains the association with sexual intercourse, spermicide use, and anatomic factors.<sup>[[8]](#ref8)[[4]](#ref4)</sup>

### B. Intracellular bacterial communities (IBCs) and quiescent intracellular reservoirs (QIRs)

Experimental models show that UPEC can **invade urothelial cells** and form intracellular bacterial communities. The reservoir and reactivation sequence below is supported primarily by animal and organoid studies:<sup>[[9]](#ref9)[[10]](#ref10)[[11]](#ref11)</sup>

1. **IBCs** — UPEC binds to and invades superficial umbrella cells via type 1 pili, then replicates intracellularly to form biofilm-like communities. IBCs are transient and occur primarily during acute infection.<sup>[[9]](#ref9)</sup>
2. **QIRs** — after IBC dispersal, bacteria penetrate into **deeper, immature urothelial layers** where they enter a quiescent, non-replicating state. These QIRs are **protected from antibiotics and immune surveillance**.<sup>[[9]](#ref9)[[12]](#ref12)[[11]](#ref11)</sup>
3. **Reactivation** — as immature host cells differentiate and migrate toward the bladder lumen, dormant bacteria can reactivate and seed recurrent infection — potentially **weeks to months** after the initial episode.<sup>[[12]](#ref12)[[11]](#ref11)</sup>

Rosen et al. (2007) found evidence consistent with IBC formation in humans, without proving the entire reservoir/reactivation pathway — IBCs were found in **18% (14/80)** of urine specimens from women with acute cystitis, and filamentous bacteria (a hallmark of IBC dispersal) were found in **41%**.<sup>[[10]](#ref10)</sup> Approximately **35% of clinical UPEC isolates** demonstrate quorum-dependent quiescence in vitro.<sup>[[13]](#ref13)</sup>

### C. The urinary microbiome paradigm

Updated models recognize that the bladder harbors a **complex microbiome**, and interactions between the urinary and vaginal environments and immune mechanisms at the bladder mucosal surface influence infection susceptibility. Postmenopausal women experience microbiome shifts (loss of protective *Lactobacillus* species) that increase vulnerability to recurrent infections.<sup>[[4]](#ref4)</sup>

## Part III: Risk factors

| Population | Risk factors |
|---|---|
| **Premenopausal** | Sexual intercourse ≥3× / week, spermicide use, new / multiple sex partners, UTI before age 15, history of UTI in first-degree female relative |
| **Postmenopausal** | Vaginal atrophy / hypoestrogenism, urinary incontinence, cystocele, elevated postvoid residual, prior UTI history |
| **Genetic** | Blood-group antigen non-secretor phenotype, P1 phenotype, lower CXCR1 (IL-8 receptor) expression, family history of UTI in first-degree female relatives |
| **Not established** | Precoital / postcoital voiding patterns, wiping patterns, specific beverage types, tampon use, douching, hot tub use, type of underwear, BMI |

## Part IV: Diagnosis

The 2025 AUA / CUA / SUFU guideline update emphasizes several key diagnostic principles:<sup>[[3]](#ref3)[[1]](#ref1)[[14]](#ref14)</sup>

**Urine testing.** Obtain urinalysis and culture with susceptibility testing during **each symptomatic acute cystitis episode**, before treatment when feasible. Document symptoms together with pyuria and uropathogenic bacteria; an isolated positive culture or one historical culture does not explain every subsequent episode. Interpret colony count in the clinical and collection context, and repeat a contaminated specimen.<sup>[[3]](#ref3)</sup>

**Urinalysis.** The 2025 update highlights the value of a **negative urinalysis to lower the probability of acute cystitis** in the index population. A negative urinalysis (negative nitrite and &lt;5 WBC/hpf) helps exclude acute cystitis, whereas a positive one adds little diagnostic accuracy. Interpret it with symptoms, sample quality and clinical context; neither bacteriuria alone nor a negative test is an automatic treatment decision.<sup>[[3]](#ref3)</sup>

**Distinguishing relapse vs. reinfection:**<sup>[[5]](#ref5)</sup>

- **Reinfection** — commonly used for a different organism or a later recurrence after apparent resolution. Timing and organism identity do not prove that bacteria originated outside the urinary tract.
- **Relapse / persistence** — same organism within 2 weeks; suggests occult source (calculi, diverticula, foreign body) requiring further evaluation.

## Part V: Role of imaging and cystoscopy

ACR 2026 distinguishes uncomplicated, complicated and pregnant populations; AUA uncomplicated-rUTI guidance supports selective investigation:<sup>[[5]](#ref5)[[15]](#ref15)[[16]](#ref16)</sup>

**Uncomplicated rUTI (no risk factors)** — **imaging is usually not appropriate**. AUA 2025 advises against routine cystoscopy and upper tract imaging in the index patient (Expert Opinion) because the yield of anatomic abnormalities is low; reserve them for a specific indication such as gross hematuria, suspected pyelonephritis or stones, poor response to treatment, or rapid recurrence with the same organism.<sup>[[3]](#ref3)[[5]](#ref5)[[16]](#ref16)</sup>

**Complicated rUTI or red flags** — imaging should be considered when:<sup>[[5]](#ref5)[[15]](#ref15)</sup>

- Rapid recurrence within 2 weeks (relapse / persistence).
- Nonresponse to conventional therapy.
- Hematuria persisting after infection resolution.
- Known risk factors (calculi, prior surgery, anatomic abnormalities, pneumaturia, fecaluria).
- Select imaging for the suspected cause: ultrasound is a reasonable screen, and **CT urography** provides comprehensive assessment when appropriate. In pregnancy, use ultrasound first and noncontrast MRI selectively; avoid treating all complicated presentations as a universal CTU/MRU indication.<sup>[[15]](#ref15)</sup>

**Flowmetry and postvoid residual (PVR)** — useful when impaired emptying is suspected. In a retrospective two-hospital referral cohort, abnormalities occurred in 134 / 593 women; diagnostic testing changed treatment in 117, mostly because of flow/PVR findings. Absent follow-up data, this does not prove improved outcomes from routine testing of every woman.<sup>[[16]](#ref16)</sup>

## Part VI: Treatment of acute episodes

Treatment of an acute rUTI episode follows the same principles as sporadic uncomplicated cystitis:<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[2]](#ref2)[[17]](#ref17)</sup>

**First-line agents** (per IDSA / AUA guidelines):

Choose nitrofurantoin, TMP-SMX or fosfomycin according to cultures, local resistance, allergy and renal function, using the shortest appropriate course, generally no longer than seven days (AUA 2025: first-line agent choice by local antibiogram is a Strong Recommendation, Grade B; short duration is a Moderate Recommendation, Grade B). Use the [UTI treatment antibiotics hub](/docs/foundations/pharmacology/infection-prophylaxis/uti-treatment-antibiotics) for dosing; prevention and acute-treatment regimens are different.<sup>[[3]](#ref3)[[17]](#ref17)[[18]](#ref18)</sup>

## Part VII: Prevention — non-antibiotic strategies

The 2025 AUA / CUA / SUFU update expands counselling on **non-antibiotic preventive options** alongside discussion of antibiotic benefits and harms.<sup>[[3]](#ref3)[[4]](#ref4)</sup>

### A. Vaginal estrogen (postmenopausal women) — first-line

The AUA / CUA / SUFU guideline issues a **Moderate Recommendation** that peri- and postmenopausal women with rUTI should receive **vaginal estrogen therapy** to reduce future UTIs, if no contraindication exists.<sup>[[14]](#ref14)</sup>

- **Raz and Stamm (1993)** — landmark NEJM placebo-controlled trial of intravaginal estriol; cumulative protection from UTI markedly higher in the estrogen group than placebo, establishing topical estrogen as a foundational rUTI-prevention strategy in postmenopausal women.<sup>[[22]](#ref22)</sup>
- **Meta-analysis of 5 RCTs** (1,936 patients) — vaginal estrogen reduced rUTIs by **58%** (RR 0.42; 95% CI 0.30–0.59) compared with placebo. Oral estrogen showed **no benefit** (RR 1.11; 95% CI 0.92–1.35).<sup>[[20]](#ref20)</sup>
- **Tan-Kim et al. (2023)** — in a retrospective cohort of 5,638 women prescribed vaginal estrogen for rUTI, UTI frequency decreased by **51.9%** (from 3.9 to 1.8 episodes / year; p&lt;0.001).<sup>[[21]](#ref21)</sup>

**Formulations.** Choose cream, low-dose ring or insert according to preference, feasibility and product-specific safety. Use the [vaginal-estrogen prescribing hub](/docs/foundations/pharmacology/hormonal-therapies/vaginal-topical-estrogen) for loading and maintenance regimens.<sup>[[23]](#ref23)</sup>

**Application update (TAPER, July 2026):** a small randomized trial supports discussing periurethral cream when intravaginal applicator use is difficult, with a wide noninferiority margin. See the [estrogen hub](/docs/foundations/pharmacology/hormonal-therapies/vaginal-topical-estrogen#recurrent-uti-prevention--the-strongest-urologic-indication) for regimens, results, and limitations.<sup>[[35]](#ref35)</sup>

### B. Methenamine hippurate

AUA 2025 permits methenamine hippurate prophylaxis for women with rUTI (**Conditional Recommendation, Grade C**). It is a urinary antiseptic that can reduce reliance on daily antibiotics; it is not resistance-proof. **Do not routinely add vitamin C:** urinary acidifiers have not been shown to lower urinary pH significantly or to enhance methenamine in RCTs, so AUA advises against combining them. For the regimen, contraindications, and pH/label distinction, use the [methenamine prescribing hub](/docs/foundations/pharmacology/infection-prophylaxis/non-antibiotic-uti-prevention#methenamine-hippurate).<sup>[[3]](#ref3)[[24]](#ref24)[[27]](#ref27)</sup>

- **ALTAR trial** (Harding et al., 2022; n = 240) — multicenter RCT demonstrated methenamine hippurate **non-inferior to daily low-dose antibiotics** for rUTI prevention (1.38 vs. 0.89 episodes / person-year; absolute difference 0.49, within the predefined non-inferiority margin of 1 UTI / person-year). During treatment, resistance to at least one antibiotic in *E. coli* from **perineal swabs** was found in 46/64 antibiotic participants versus 39/70 methenamine participants (72% vs. 56%; p = 0.05). This is a colonization endpoint, not the proportion of all UTIs resistant to treatment.<sup>[[24]](#ref24)[[25]](#ref25)</sup>
- **ImpresU trial** (Heltveit-Olsen et al., 2025; 289 women recruited, 281 analyzed; age ≥70 years) — methenamine hippurate reduced antibiotic treatments for UTI by **25%** during the 6-month treatment period (IRR 0.75; 95% CI 0.57–1.0; p = 0.049). After discontinuation, the methenamine group had a **higher incidence of antibiotic-treated episodes** during the six-month follow-up (IRR 1.7; 95% CI 1.3–2.3).<sup>[[26]](#ref26)</sup>
- **Emerging concern.** Hodgkinson et al. (2026) identified growth above the study-defined formaldehyde threshold in **11/191 ALTAR-derived *E. coli* isolates (5.8%)**, with *frmR* variants or plasmid mechanisms. The genomic and laboratory findings support possible resistance evolution; 5.8% is an isolate proportion, not a clinical methenamine-failure rate.<sup>[[27]](#ref27)</sup>

### C. Cranberry products

A network meta-analysis of 50 RCTs (10,495 subjects) found cranberry products reduced UTI incidence by **28%** (RR 0.72; 95% CI 0.60–0.87) compared with placebo.<sup>[[28]](#ref28)</sup> The WikiGuidelines consensus (2024) provides a clear recommendation supporting cranberry for rUTI prevention in women, children, and individuals susceptible to UTIs after interventions.<sup>[[29]](#ref29)</sup> Cochrane 2023 supports cranberry for women with rUTI (8 trials, 1,555 participants; RR 0.74, 95% CI 0.55–0.99), but direct comparisons with antibiotics remain too limited to establish superiority or equivalence. Benefit was not shown in institutionalized older adults, pregnancy or neuromuscular bladder dysfunction with incomplete emptying. AUA 2025 advises clinicians to offer cranberry as an option (Moderate Recommendation). Optimal product and dose remain uncertain.<sup>[[3]](#ref3)[[36]](#ref36)</sup>

### D. D-mannose

**AUA/CUA/SUFU 2025:** counsel that D-mannose alone may not prevent recurrence. Favorable rankings from older/smaller trials in a network meta-analysis should not outweigh the large blinded MERIT trial.<sup>[[3]](#ref3)[[28]](#ref28)</sup>

However, the **MERIT RCT** (Hayward et al., 2024; *JAMA Intern Med*) — the first large, placebo-controlled trial — found that D-mannose 2 g daily for 6 months **did not significantly reduce UTI recurrence** vs. placebo in a primary-care population. The authors concluded that the evidence does not support recommending D-mannose for rUTI prevention.<sup>[[6]](#ref6)</sup>

### E. Probiotics

*Lactobacillus*-containing probiotics reduced UTI incidence by **31%** (RR 0.69; 95% CI 0.50–0.94) in the network meta-analysis.<sup>[[28]](#ref28)</sup> Older consensus discussions considered probiotics,<sup>[[23]](#ref23)</sup> but **AUA 2025 cannot recommend Lactobacillus prophylaxis**: there are no data showing benefit compared with other available agents, and clinicians generally cannot obtain the specific strains used in the trials. A favourable pooled estimate does not establish efficacy of an over-the-counter product.<sup>[[3]](#ref3)</sup>

### F. Behavioral modifications

- Discuss increased water intake in premenopausal women who drink little: in an open-label RCT of 140 women (usual intake below 1.5 L/day), an extra 1.5 L of water daily reduced cystitis episodes (mean 1.7 vs 3.2 per year). Individualize for fluid restrictions.<sup>[[3]](#ref3)[[19]](#ref19)</sup>
- Do not present postcoital voiding or hygiene changes as proven prevention.
- Avoidance of spermicidal contraceptives.<sup>[[5]](#ref5)</sup>

### G. Immunoprophylaxis

- **MV140** has randomized evidence: a placebo-controlled trial allocated 240 women to three or six months of vaccine or placebo. During the nine-month efficacy period after the first three months, UTI-free proportions were 56%, 58% and 25%, respectively. This supports efficacy in the studied population, without establishing superiority to standard prophylaxis.<sup>[[37]](#ref37)</sup>
- **EAU 2026** recommends immunomodulatory prophylaxis only within a well-regulated clinical trial (weak recommendation), reflecting limited and heterogeneous evidence. OM-89 and MV140 are different products; positive pooled vaccine results do not validate every preparation. Older guideline comparisons should not substitute for this current recommendation.<sup>[[30]](#ref30)[[38]](#ref38)</sup>

## Part VIII: Prevention — antibiotic prophylaxis

AUA 2025 permits antibiotic prophylaxis after discussing benefits, harms and alternatives (**Conditional Recommendation, Grade B**). EAU recommends it after unsuccessful non-antimicrobial approaches; distinguish these guideline frameworks.<sup>[[3]](#ref3)[[38]](#ref38)</sup>

### Continuous or intercourse-associated prophylaxis

Discuss expected benefits, drug toxicity, resistance, previous cultures and preferences. Continuous prophylaxis may suit infections without a consistent trigger; postcoital prophylaxis can limit exposure when recurrences track intercourse. The guideline does not require failure of every non-antibiotic option before this discussion.<sup>[[3]](#ref3)[[17]](#ref17)[[2]](#ref2)</sup>

Use the [UTI suppressive/prophylactic antibiotics hub](/docs/foundations/pharmacology/infection-prophylaxis/uti-suppressive-prophylactic) for agent selection, regimens, monitoring and contraindications. **Fluoroquinolones are not routine prophylaxis:** AUA 2025 notes that tendon rupture (boxed warning), QT prolongation, seizures and *C. difficile* infection generally outweigh their benefit for localized UTI.<sup>[[3]](#ref3)</sup>

Trials generally evaluated **6–12 months**; periodically reassess ongoing need. Recurrence may return after stopping. Years of use should not be described as proven safe or resistance-free. Treat an active episode, but do not require a routine negative test-of-cure culture in an asymptomatic patient before prevention.<sup>[[3]](#ref3)[[29]](#ref29)</sup>

## Part IX: Special populations

**Postmenopausal women** — include vaginal estrogen when appropriate and discuss the other preventive options according to eligibility and preference:<sup>[[3]](#ref3)[[14]](#ref14)[[21]](#ref21)</sup>

1. **Vaginal estrogen** (first-line).
2. Non-antibiotic prevention (prescription methenamine or cranberry products, according to eligibility).
3. Antibiotic prophylaxis when its benefits, risks and patient preferences support it.

**Women with anatomic / functional abnormalities** — cystocele, elevated PVR, urethral diverticula, or mesh erosion require targeted evaluation and treatment of the underlying condition.<sup>[[5]](#ref5)[[32]](#ref32)</sup>

**Antibiotic-resistant infections** — multidisciplinary management involving infectious-disease specialists is recommended. Selected intravesical therapies may limit systemic drug exposure, but evidence is heterogeneous and resistance can still emerge.<sup>[[31]](#ref31)</sup>

## Part X: Emerging therapies

### A. Bacteriophage therapy

The 2024 **ELIMINATE part 1** report described 39 women in an uncontrolled, open-label phase II dose-finding study of LBP-EC01 plus TMP-SMX. No serious adverse events occurred, but higher intravenous doses produced tolerability signals and the protocol changed. Clinical and bacterial clearance in 16 evaluable participants cannot establish phage efficacy separately from the concurrent antibiotic. This remains investigational evidence.<sup>[[7]](#ref7)</sup>

Joshi et al. (2026) showed that lytic phage ΦHP3 reduced UPEC adhesion and invasion of vaginal and bladder epithelial cells in vitro, and daily intravaginal phage administration significantly reduced vaginal UPEC burden in humanized-microbiota mice.<sup>[[33]](#ref33)</sup>

### B. UTI vaccines

See [immunoprophylaxis](#g-immunoprophylaxis) for the MV140 randomized trial and current EAU restriction. A network estimate combining different vaccine preparations (RR 0.65, 95% CI 0.52–0.82) does not establish class-wide effectiveness or routine availability.<sup>[[28]](#ref28)[[31]](#ref31)[[38]](#ref38)</sup>

### C. Fecal microbiota transplantation (FMT)

Initial findings suggest FMT may interrupt the rUTI cycle by restoring a healthy gut microbiome and reducing intestinal colonization with uropathogenic organisms, though clinical data remain preliminary.<sup>[[34]](#ref34)</sup>

### D. Intravesical therapies

Intravesical glycosaminoglycan (GAG) layer replenishment (hyaluronic acid ± chondroitin sulfate) aims to restore the protective bladder lining. Some evidence supports reduced UTI recurrence, though data quality is limited.<sup>[[31]](#ref31)</sup>

## Part XI: Management algorithm

Based on the 2025 AUA / CUA / SUFU update and multisociety consensus:<sup>[[3]](#ref3)[[1]](#ref1)[[23]](#ref23)</sup>

1. **Confirm the diagnosis** — relate recurrent acute symptoms to pyuria and uropathogenic bacteria; obtain urine testing for each acute episode and rule out other causes of LUTS.
2. **Treat the acute episode** — short-course antibiotics (nitrofurantoin, TMP-SMX, or fosfomycin).
3. **Identify and modify risk factors** — spermicide avoidance, behavioral modifications, adequate hydration.
4. **Discuss preventive choices:**
   - **Postmenopausal** — vaginal estrogen (first-line) ± methenamine hippurate ± cranberry.
   - **Premenopausal** — behavioral modifications, cranberry, consider methenamine hippurate.
5. **Antibiotic strategies** when selected after counselling:
   - Intercourse-related — postcoital single-dose prophylaxis.
   - Non-intercourse-related — continuous prophylaxis with planned review.
   - Selected reliable patients may start treatment themselves while a culture is pending (Conditional Recommendation, Grade C). Use agents chosen from prior cultures and the local antibiogram, still obtain a culture for symptomatic recurrences when feasible, and record how often self-start is used.
6. **Further evaluation** (if refractory):
   - Flowmetry and PVR measurement.
   - Imaging (CTU / MRU) if complicated features present.
   - Cystoscopy only if red flags (hematuria, suspected structural abnormality).
   - Multidisciplinary referral (urology, infectious disease, urogynecology).

## Part XII: Key paradigm shifts (2025 update)

The 2025 AUA / CUA / SUFU guideline update and contemporary reviews highlight several important shifts:<sup>[[3]](#ref3)[[4]](#ref4)</sup>

- **Avoid treating microbial detection alone** — protect against unnecessary antibiotics while treating symptomatic infection. Microbiome mechanisms do not establish routine microbiome testing or a validated microbiome-directed treatment.
- **More preventive choices** — vaginal estrogen, methenamine and cranberry can reduce antibiotic exposure; prophylaxis selection remains a shared decision, not a mandatory sequence.
- **From microbial detection to clinical judgment** — a negative urinalysis lowers the likelihood of acute cystitis in the index population; treatment decisions should weigh individual risks and benefits rather than relying solely on culture results.
- **From routine cystoscopy to selective evaluation** — cystoscopy and imaging are reserved for complicated presentations, not routine rUTI.
- **Antimicrobial stewardship** — recognition that repetitive antibiotic use causes "collateral damage" — rising resistance, microbiome disruption, and adverse effects — driving the search for alternatives.
