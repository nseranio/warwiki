# Breast Cancer Screening

Breast cancer is the most common malignancy in women worldwide. Randomized trials showed that screening mammography can reduce breast cancer mortality by detecting cancers before clinical presentation.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[10]](#ref10)</sup> Screening is risk-stratified. It begins with formal risk assessment, ideally by age 25, and modality, frequency and age of initiation depend on whether the patient is at average or elevated risk.<sup>[[2]](#ref2)</sup> Urogynecologists and pelvic reconstructive surgeons encounter these patients routinely during preoperative assessment and long-term follow-up, particularly women on hormonal therapies, those with prior pelvic irradiation, and BRCA carriers being counseled about opportunistic risk-reducing surgery.

---

## Average-risk screening

Major guidelines agree that screening mammography reduces breast cancer mortality but differ on when to start, how often to screen, and when to stop. **Digital breast tomosynthesis (DBT)** is preferred when available. NCCN cites lower callback rates and higher cancer detection than with standard 2D mammography alone.<sup>[[2]](#ref2)</sup>

| Guideline | Start age | Frequency | Stop / Upper limit |
|---|---|---|---|
| **USPSTF (2024)**<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup> | 40 | Biennial | Age 74; insufficient evidence ≥75 |
| **NCCN (2026)**<sup>[[2]](#ref2)</sup> | 40 | Annual | No upper limit; consider discontinuation when life expectancy ≤10 years |
| **ACS**<sup>[[3]](#ref3)</sup> | 45 (option to start at 40) | Annual 45–54; biennial or continued annual ≥55 | Continue with good health and ≥10-year life expectancy |
| **ACR**<sup>[[4]](#ref4)</sup> | 40 | Annual | Continue beyond 74 unless severe comorbidity limits life expectancy |
| **ACP (2026)**<sup>[[5]](#ref5)</sup> | 50 (shared decision-making 40–49) | Biennial | Age 74; discuss stopping at ≥75 or limited life expectancy |

Guideline positions:

- **USPSTF (2024).** The task force recommends biennial screening mammography for ages 40–74 (B recommendation). The 2016 version had individualized the decision for ages 40–49. Evidence is insufficient for screening at age 75 or older and for supplemental screening in dense breasts.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup>
- **NCCN (2026).** NCCN recommends annual mammography with tomosynthesis starting at age 40 (Category 1). Clinical encounters are advised every 1–3 years for ages 25–39 and annually from age 40. There is no established upper age limit; discontinuation is considered when life expectancy is ≤10 years.<sup>[[2]](#ref2)</sup>
- **ACS.** Annual mammography is advised for ages 45–54, with the option to begin at 40. From age 55, screening is biennial or continues annually while the patient is in good health with a life expectancy of at least ten years.<sup>[[3]](#ref3)</sup>
- **ACR.** ACR advises annual mammography starting at age 40, continuing beyond age 74 unless severe comorbidity limits life expectancy. The cited ACR/SBI statement gives no fixed 5–7-year threshold.<sup>[[15]](#ref15)</sup><sup>[[4]](#ref4)</sup>
- **ACP (2026).** ACP guidance is biennial mammography for ages 50–74, a shared decision at ages 40–49 (biennial screening if the patient prefers it), and a discussion of discontinuation at age 75 or older or with limited life expectancy. Supplemental DBT may be considered in BI-RADS C or D density, and supplemental MRI or ultrasound is not advised in average-risk patients with dense breasts.<sup>[[5]](#ref5)</sup>

## Increased-risk categories and screening

Risk assessment should be performed by age 25 and periodically reassessed.<sup>[[2]](#ref2)</sup> NCCN defines several increased-risk categories, each with its own screening protocol.<sup>[[2]](#ref2)[[6]](#ref6)</sup>

### Residual lifetime risk ≥20% (by comprehensive family-history models)

Annual mammography with tomosynthesis plus annual breast MRI with contrast, beginning no later than age 40. Mammography should not begin before age 30; MRI should not begin before age 25. Alternatively, begin 10 years before the youngest affected family member's diagnosis.<sup>[[2]](#ref2)</sup>

### Known genetic predisposition (e.g., BRCA1/2)

- **Ages 25–29.** Annual breast MRI with contrast (mammography only if MRI unavailable).
- **Ages 30–75.** Annual mammography with tomosynthesis plus annual breast MRI with contrast.
- Risk-reducing mastectomy counseling is part of management.<sup>[[6]](#ref6)</sup>

### Prior chest radiation (ages 10–30)

Use a dedicated high-risk pathway that accounts for treatment age and elapsed time. The ACR 2025 criteria specify mammography eight years after young-age thoracic/upper-abdominal radiation, not before age 25, with supplemental MRI according to the risk-based schedule. Do not combine age cutoffs from different organizations into a single rule.<sup>[[16]](#ref16)</sup><sup>[[2]](#ref2)</sup>

### 5-year Gail risk ≥1.7%, atypical ductal hyperplasia (ADH), or lobular neoplasia (LCIS / ALH)

Annual mammography with tomosynthesis; consider annual MRI. Clinical encounters every 6–12 months.<sup>[[2]](#ref2)</sup>

### Dense breast tissue (heterogeneously or extremely dense)

Recommendations differ. ACR supports supplemental contrast MRI for appropriate dense-breast risk categories, whereas USPSTF finds evidence insufficient to determine the overall benefit–harm balance and ACP 2026 recommends against supplemental MRI/ultrasound for average-risk dense breasts. Discuss the patient's total risk, local guideline and additional recalls/biopsies; density alone is not a universally agreed MRI mandate.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[5]](#ref5)[[16]](#ref16)</sup>

In the DENSE trial, women aged 50–75 with extremely dense breasts and a normal mammogram who were invited to MRI had fewer interval cancers (2.5 vs 5.0 per 1,000 screens; 59% accepted the invitation). The endpoint was interval cancer, not mortality. The NCCN 2026 category-specific pathway for dense breasts should be confirmed in the current guideline before it is applied.<sup>[[2]](#ref2)[[7]](#ref7)[[17]](#ref17)</sup>

## Supplemental screening modalities

When mammography alone is insufficient, particularly in dense breasts or high-risk patients, several supplemental modalities are available.<sup>[[2]](#ref2)[[7]](#ref7)[[8]](#ref8)</sup>

| Modality | Incremental CDR per 1,000 | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| **Breast MRI with contrast** | DENSE: 16.5 per 1,000 among women screened with MRI | Highest incremental CDR of the supplemental modalities in a meta-analysis; interval cancers 2.5 vs 5.0 per 1,000 in DENSE; increases false positives and biopsy rate<sup>[[7]](#ref7)[[8]](#ref8)[[9]](#ref9)[[10]](#ref10)</sup> |
| **Abbreviated breast MRI** | Invasive cancers 11.8 vs 4.8 per 1,000 with DBT (Comstock) | Sensitivity 95.7% vs 39.1% and specificity 86.7% vs 97.4% compared with DBT; shorter scan times than full-protocol MRI<sup>[[2]](#ref2)[[11]](#ref11)</sup> |
| **Contrast-enhanced mammography (CEM)** | Population-dependent | An option in selected patients; do not assume equivalent CDR/recall to MRI across studies; iodinated contrast and radiation considerations<sup>[[2]](#ref2)[[7]](#ref7)</sup> |
| **Molecular breast imaging (MBI)** | Population-dependent | Selected use when other modalities are unsuitable; MRI equivalence is not established; systemic radiation exposure matters<sup>[[2]](#ref2)</sup> |
| **Whole-breast ultrasound** | Lower than MRI | Lower incremental CDR than MRI in the meta-analysis; increases false positives<sup>[[2]](#ref2)[[8]](#ref8)</sup> |

A simulation study estimated the benefits and harms of density-targeted MRI strategies. Adding MRI for women with extremely dense breasts to biennial DBT at ages 50–74 averted 7.6 vs 7.4 breast cancer deaths and produced 919 vs 884 false-positive recalls per 1,000 simulated women. These are model-predicted deaths averted, not observed trial mortality, and the preferred strategy depends on the assumptions and the weight given to false positives.<sup>[[9]](#ref9)</sup>

## Risk assessment tools

Formal risk assessment should be performed by age 25 and updated periodically. NCCN recognizes several validated risk models, each with distinct inputs and use cases.<sup>[[2]](#ref2)[[12]](#ref12)</sup>

| Model | Key inputs | Best use case |
|---|---|---|
| **Tyrer-Cuzick (IBIS)** | Personal / family history, BRCA probability, mammographic density, hormonal factors | Includes extended pedigree and reproductive/density factors; no universally best model |
| **BCSC** | Age, race/ethnicity, family history (1st-degree), prior biopsy, breast density | Highest AUC in the density-available subgroup of the cited cohort<sup>[[13]](#ref13)</sup> |
| **Gail (BCRAT)** | Age, family history (1st-degree only), prior biopsy, ethnicity | Widely used; lower discrimination; drives 5-year risk ≥1.7% threshold |
| **BOADICEA** | Personal / family history, germline mutation results, tumor pathology | Includes detailed pedigree and genetic risk; use a version validated for the clinical population |

In the cited 35,921-person mammography cohort, the tested models showed moderate discrimination (AUC about 0.61–0.64); this is not universal validation of every model or population. The BCSC model had the highest AUC among women with available breast density information.<sup>[[13]](#ref13)</sup>

## Emerging evidence: risk-based screening

The **WISDOM trial** randomized 28,372 participants aged 40–74 with median 5.1-year follow-up. Risk-based screening met noninferiority for the rate of stage ≥IIB cancers (difference −18.0 per 100,000 person-years; 95% CI −40.2 to 4.1), but did not reduce the co-primary biopsy outcome (difference 98.7 per 100,000 person-years; 95% CI −17.9 to 215.3), despite fewer mammograms.<sup>[[14]](#ref14)</sup> Risk-based screening integrated:

- Genetic testing (9 susceptibility genes plus polygenic risk score)
- BCSC risk model
- Four screening tiers: deferred screening (low risk) to alternating mammography / MRI every 6 months (highest risk)

Among 18,031 participants who declined randomization and entered the observational cohort, 89% chose risk-based screening. This self-selected preference is not a population-wide acceptability estimate.<sup>[[14]](#ref14)</sup>

## Special populations

- **Age ≥75.** Evidence is insufficient to assess benefits and harms of continued screening (USPSTF I statement). NCCN recommends weighing comorbidities and life expectancy, using ≤10 years as a threshold for discontinuation.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[2]](#ref2)</sup>
- **Prior breast cancer.** Surveillance recommendations are covered in the NCCN Breast Cancer treatment guidelines, not the screening guidelines.<sup>[[2]](#ref2)</sup>
- **BRCA carriers (male).** Clinical breast examination is advised annually from age 35, and annual mammography can be considered from age 50 (or 10 years before the earliest known male breast cancer in the family), particularly for BRCA2 carriers.<sup>[[6]](#ref6)</sup>
- **Transgender individuals.** NCCN endorses ACR Appropriateness Criteria for screening guidance in transgender patients.<sup>[[2]](#ref2)</sup>
- **Racial disparities.** Non-Hispanic Black women have 40% higher breast cancer mortality than White women despite lower incidence. Disparities have been documented in time to diagnostic imaging and biopsy after an abnormal screening result.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[2]](#ref2)[[10]](#ref10)</sup>
