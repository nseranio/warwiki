# Osteoporosis Screening in the Reconstructive Urology & Urogynecology Patient

Reconstructive practices care for patients at risk of bone loss from menopause, GnRH therapy, aromatase inhibitors, androgen deprivation and systemic glucocorticoids used for other conditions.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup> The AUA advises against long-term systemic oral glucocorticoids as primary IC/BPS therapy (Recommendation; Grade C).<sup>[[13]](#ref13)</sup> Vertebral fractures can impair mobility and complicate operative positioning; hip fractures in this population carry substantial morbidity and mortality. The 2025 USPSTF reaffirmed a Grade B recommendation for dual-energy x-ray absorptiometry (DXA) screening in postmenopausal women aged ≥ 65 years without known osteoporosis, prior fragility fracture, or secondary osteoporosis, and in younger postmenopausal women at increased fracture risk as estimated by clinical risk assessment, making osteoporosis screening a core component of the comprehensive preventative-care visit.<sup>[[2]](#ref2)</sup>

---

## Who to Screen

### Universal threshold: ≥ 65 years

DXA screening is recommended for postmenopausal women aged ≥65 years without known osteoporosis, prior fragility fracture, or secondary osteoporosis, without additional risk-factor assessment.<sup>[[2]](#ref2)</sup> For men aged ≥40 years without known osteoporosis, prior fragility fracture, or secondary osteoporosis, the USPSTF finds evidence insufficient for population screening; ISCD indicates DXA at age ≥70 years or earlier with low-bone-mass risk factors, and androgen-deprivation therapy warrants targeted bone-risk assessment.<sup>[[2]](#ref2)[[8]](#ref8)[[14]](#ref14)</sup>

### Younger postmenopausal women (&lt; 65 years)

A practical two-step approach applies to younger postmenopausal women:

1. Identify clinical risk factors such as low weight, parental hip fracture, smoking or excess alcohol.
2. If risk factors are present, use a validated osteoporosis or fracture-risk tool to decide on DXA. USPSTF 2025 does not define 9.3% FRAX as a screening threshold; that figure illustrates a reference patient’s risk.<sup>[[2]](#ref2)</sup> Do not confuse screening decisions with thresholds for pharmacologic treatment.<sup>[[2]](#ref2)[[3]](#ref3)</sup>

### Risk factors warranting earlier screening

| Category | Examples |
|---|---|
| **GU / gynecologic treatments** | GnRH agonists (preoperative priming, endometriosis, fibroids), aromatase inhibitors (breast cancer survivors), prolonged estrogen deprivation |
| **Other medications** | Systemic glucocorticoids ≥2.5 mg/day prednisone-equivalent for &gt;3 months warrant dedicated risk assessment<sup>[[10]](#ref10)</sup>, anticonvulsants, depot medroxyprogesterone acetate<sup>[[4]](#ref4)</sup> |
| **Anthropometric** | Low body weight (&lt;127 lb / 57.6 kg), BMI &lt;20 kg/m² |
| **Lifestyle** | Current smoking, alcohol &gt;3 drinks/day |
| **Medical conditions** | Rheumatoid arthritis, type 1 or 2 diabetes, hyperparathyroidism, celiac disease / malabsorption syndromes |
| **Family history** | Parental history of hip fracture |
| **Reproductive** | Premature ovarian insufficiency (&lt;40 years), early menopause (40–44 years), premenopausal bilateral salpingo-oophorectomy, prolonged amenorrhea |

At diagnosis of premature ovarian insufficiency, the 2024 international guideline strongly recommends baseline DXA where available.<sup>[[18]](#ref18)</sup> After premenopausal risk-reducing salpingo-oophorectomy, the 2023 international consensus strongly recommends DXA within the first year.<sup>[[19]](#ref19)</sup>

**GnRH therapy.** Bone loss, treatment-duration limits and add-back depend on the product and indication. Leuprolide 11.25 mg IM every three months may be given with or without norethindrone acetate 5 mg daily for an initial course of up to six months; retreatment requires add-back and prior BMD assessment, with total therapy limited to 12 months. The fibroid indication is a three-month course with iron for preoperative anemia and is not labeled for use with add-back. Do not automatically prescribe estrogen-progestin or a bisphosphonate for every short course.<sup>[[11]](#ref11)</sup>

## Diagnosis

In postmenopausal women and men aged 50 or older, osteoporosis assessment includes:<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[7]](#ref7)</sup>

- DXA T score ≤ −2.5 at the lumbar spine, total hip, femoral neck, or 33% radius when hip or spine cannot be measured or interpreted, in hyperparathyroidism, or above the DXA table weight limit.<sup>[[8]](#ref8)</sup>
- Hip or vertebral fragility fracture supports treatment regardless of BMD. Fractures at other sites require assessment of BMD, mechanism and clinical risk rather than an automatic diagnosis from any fracture.<sup>[[9]](#ref9)</sup>

**Osteopenia** is defined as a T score between −1.0 and −2.5; fracture history and, when appropriate, FRAX estimates guide treatment decisions in this range.<sup>[[9]](#ref9)</sup>

T scores are compared to a young-adult reference population (female, age 20–29); Z scores compare with age-matched peers and are preferred in premenopausal women and men younger than 50. T scores are used in men aged 50 or older; BMD alone does not diagnose osteoporosis in a younger man.<sup>[[8]](#ref8)</sup>

When the T score is &lt;−1.0, ISCD indicates DXA vertebral fracture assessment or lateral spine radiography for women aged ≥70 or men aged ≥80, or for patients with historical height loss &gt;4 cm, a self-reported but undocumented prior vertebral fracture, or glucocorticoid therapy at a prednisone-equivalent dose ≥5 mg/day for ≥3 months.<sup>[[8]](#ref8)</sup>

## FRAX and Risk Stratification

FRAX combines age, sex, BMI, and selected clinical risk factors to estimate 10-year hip and major osteoporotic fracture probabilities; its omission of falls and type 2 diabetes may underestimate risk for affected patients.<sup>[[2]](#ref2)</sup> It can be run with or without femoral-neck BMD; adding femoral-neck BMD improves predictive accuracy. In cancer survivors starting androgen-deprivation therapy, aromatase inhibitors or GnRH agonists, FRAX may underestimate treatment-related fracture risk; ASCO advises recording secondary osteoporosis when using FRAX in these groups.<sup>[[14]](#ref14)</sup>

For postmenopausal women and men aged ≥50 years, BHOF advises considering pharmacologic treatment when any of the following criteria is met:<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[7]](#ref7)[[9]](#ref9)</sup>

| Criterion | Threshold |
|---|---|
| Fragility fracture | Hip or vertebral fracture: consider treatment regardless of BMD; proximal humerus, pelvis, or distal forearm fracture with osteopenia: consider treatment.<sup>[[9]](#ref9)</sup> |
| DXA T score | ≤ −2.5 at femoral neck, total hip, lumbar spine, or 33% radius when indicated (with some uncertainty for the radius threshold).<sup>[[8]](#ref8)[[9]](#ref9)</sup> |
| Osteopenia + FRAX | Femoral-neck or total-hip T score between −1.0 and −2.5 **and either** 10-year hip fracture probability ≥3% **or** major osteoporotic fracture probability ≥20% (US-adapted FRAX)<sup>[[9]](#ref9)</sup> |

Risk is then stratified into high versus very high risk to guide agent selection (see Treatment below).

## Screening and monitoring intervals

USPSTF 2025 supports screening women aged 65 or older and younger postmenopausal women at increased risk, but does not specify a single rescreening interval. Its average-risk screening framework does not replace evaluation of secondary osteoporosis or an existing fragility fracture.<sup>[[2]](#ref2)</sup>

Separate repeat screening from treatment monitoring. Age, baseline BMD, new fractures, medication exposure and expected rate of change determine timing. Observational 10–15-year estimates concern older women with near-normal BMD; individual rescreening timing depends on clinical risk.<sup>[[2]](#ref2)</sup> BHOF suggests reassessment one to two years after starting or changing osteoporosis therapy, then individualized intervals.<sup>[[4]](#ref4)[[5]](#ref5)[[6]](#ref6)[[9]](#ref9)</sup>

For ongoing systemic glucocorticoids, the American College of Rheumatology recommends risk assessment for adults taking ≥2.5 mg/day for more than three months, including BMD and vertebral assessment; reassessment is commonly every one to two years according to risk. This is distinct from the American College of Radiology imaging criteria.<sup>[[10]](#ref10)</sup>

## Treatment Overview

Full pharmacologic management is outside the scope of this article; the following framework supports risk stratification at the preventative-care visit.

### High fracture risk

Agent choice depends on fractures, BMD, renal function, contraindications and adherence. Antiresorptives are commonly used:<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[7]](#ref7)</sup>
- **Oral bisphosphonates** (alendronate 70 mg weekly, risedronate 35 mg weekly) are recommended initial options for postmenopausal women at high fracture risk when appropriate, with low cost and extensive safety data.<sup>[[9]](#ref9)</sup>
- **IV zoledronic acid** 5 mg annually is an option when oral adherence or GI tolerance is a concern. Before infusion, correct hypocalcemia, ensure hydration, and check creatinine clearance; infuse over at least 15 minutes. Reclast is contraindicated with hypocalcemia, creatinine clearance &lt;35 mL/min, or acute renal impairment.<sup>[[16]](#ref16)</sup>
- **Denosumab** 60 mg SC every six months.<sup>[[9]](#ref9)</sup> In advanced CKD, especially dialysis or CKD-MBD, FDA warns of severe hypocalcemia; CKD-MBD specialist assessment includes calcium and bone-disease evaluation, correction of hypocalcemia, calcium and activated vitamin D supplementation, and close calcium monitoring for 2–10 weeks after each injection.<sup>[[12]](#ref12)</sup> Discontinuation or delayed dosing requires a subsequent antiresorptive plan because of rebound vertebral fracture risk.<sup>[[9]](#ref9)[[12]](#ref12)</sup>
- **Raloxifene** (SERM) has a modest vertebral-fracture benefit and no demonstrated hip-fracture benefit; it may be considered in selected postmenopausal women at increased breast-cancer risk.<sup>[[9]](#ref9)</sup> It is contraindicated with current or prior venous thromboembolism; the label directs interruption at least 72 hours before and throughout prolonged immobilization, with resumption only once fully ambulatory.<sup>[[17]](#ref17)</sup> In the randomized RUTH trial of 10,101 postmenopausal women with coronary disease or elevated coronary risk (median follow-up 5.6 years), fatal stroke occurred in 59/5,044 (1.2%) taking raloxifene versus 39/5,057 (0.8%) taking placebo; the label advises weighing this risk in women at risk for stroke.<sup>[[17]](#ref17)</sup>

### Very high fracture risk

Recent or multiple fractures, very low BMD and other major risk factors may favor an anabolic-first strategy. A remote single fracture does not automatically assign every patient to the same category:<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup>
- **Teriparatide** duration and osteosarcoma-risk precautions require specialist review; use beyond two years is considered only when fracture risk remains or becomes high, and use should be avoided after skeletal radiation or with bone metastases or a history of skeletal malignancy.<sup>[[9]](#ref9)</sup> Avoid a blanket treatment algorithm for all cancer survivors.
- **Romosozumab** (anti-sclerostin) 210 mg SC monthly for 12 months has dual anabolic and antiresorptive effects. Do not initiate it in patients who had myocardial infarction or stroke within the preceding year.<sup>[[9]](#ref9)</sup>
- Follow with antiresorptive therapy after anabolic course to consolidate gains.

### Calcium and vitamin D

For adults aged 50 years or older receiving osteoporosis therapy, age- and sex-specific total calcium intake (1,000–1,200 mg/day) and vitamin D 800–1,000 IU/day as needed to achieve sufficiency are adjuncts, with dietary calcium preferred.<sup>[[9]](#ref9)</sup> Some studies have linked calcium supplements with cardiovascular risk, but the evidence is conflicting.<sup>[[9]](#ref9)</sup>

## GU-Specific Considerations

- **Hormone exposure.** Low-dose vaginal estrogen is not a treatment for osteoporosis. For GnRH therapy, follow the indication-specific label and assess individual bone risk rather than requiring the same add-back and DXA schedule for every short course.<sup>[[11]](#ref11)</sup>
- **Mobility and positioning.** Review vertebral pain, falls, balance and ability to tolerate operative positioning. Avoid asserting that a vertebral fracture necessarily increases lumbar lordosis or directly worsens prolapse.
- **Cancer survivors on AIs or ADT.** Assess fracture risk and BMD, then choose bone-directed treatment according to risk and oncology context. ASCO generally supports DXA about every two years, with shorter intervals when clinically indicated; annual DXA and automatic antiresorptive co-treatment are not required for every patient.<sup>[[14]](#ref14)</sup> For women with hormone-sensitive breast cancer receiving aromatase inhibitors, the 2025 joint position statement recommends antiresorptive therapy when the lowest spine or hip T score is &lt;−2.0, at least two specified risk factors are present, or annual BMD falls ≥10% at any site (≥4–5% with baseline osteopenia) on the same DXA machine; rapid loss prompts evaluation for secondary causes.<sup>[[15]](#ref15)</sup> Women with T score &gt;−2.0 and no additional risk factors undergo fracture-risk and BMD review at 1–2-year intervals.<sup>[[15]](#ref15)</sup>
- **Chronic systemic glucocorticoids.** Use the dedicated rheumatology risk framework and reassess exposure, BMD, vertebral fractures and age. Do not apply a universal T-score −1.5 treatment trigger or assume chronic oral steroids are indicated for Hunner lesions.<sup>[[10]](#ref10)[[13]](#ref13)</sup>
