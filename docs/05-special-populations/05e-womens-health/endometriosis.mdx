# Endometriosis

Endometriosis is a chronic, estrogen-dependent inflammatory disease defined by endometrial-like tissue outside the uterine lining, affecting approximately 10% of reproductive-age women (approximately 9 million in the U.S.). Endometriosis is a common cause of pelvic pain in women; approximately 26% of women with endometriosis report infertility. There is currently no medical or surgical cure, and diagnosis is delayed an average of 5 to 12 years from symptom onset.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup>

---

## Pathophysiology

The most widely accepted theory is **retrograde menstruation** (Sampson's theory), in which shed endometrial tissue travels through the fallopian tubes into the peritoneal cavity, where it implants, proliferates, and provokes an inflammatory response.<sup>[[3]](#ref3)</sup> Additional mechanisms include:

- **Epigenetic reprogramming** of endometrial mesenchymal progenitor / stem cells, with overexpression of steroidogenic factor-1 driving excessive local estrogen production via estrogen receptor-β, and progesterone receptor deficiency causing progesterone resistance.<sup>[[3]](#ref3)</sup>
- **Immune dysregulation** with proinflammatory cytokines, altered circulating immune cell populations, and aberrant microRNA expression creating a systemic inflammatory environment.<sup>[[4]](#ref4)</sup>
- **Neurogenesis** within lesions, with new nerve fiber growth contributing to nociceptive and neuropathic pain mechanisms; central sensitization (nociplastic pain) explains why lesion burden correlates poorly with pain severity.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup>

Three pain mechanisms coexist and explain the disconnect between disease stage and symptom severity: **nociceptive** (lesion-driven inflammation), **neuropathic** (lesion-associated nerve fiber injury and infiltration), and **nociplastic** (central sensitization).

---

## Clinical Presentation

Approximately 90% of women with endometriosis report pelvic pain, and 50% report moderate to severe fatigue.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup> Symptoms include:

- **Dysmenorrhea.** Progressive, often starting in adolescence
- **Nonmenstrual chronic pelvic pain**
- **Deep dyspareunia**
- **Dyschezia.** Painful defecation, often cyclic
- **Dysuria.** Cyclic
- **Infertility.** Reported by 26.2% of 940 survey respondents with surgically confirmed endometriosis.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup>

Endometriosis exists in three phenotypes: **superficial peritoneal lesions**, **ovarian endometriomas**, and **deep infiltrating endometriosis** (involving the rectovaginal septum, bladder, ureters, or bowel).<sup>[[2]](#ref2)[[4]](#ref4)</sup>

---

## Diagnosis

A clinical diagnosis can now be made based on symptoms, physical examination, and imaging; surgical confirmation is not required to initiate empiric treatment. ACOG 2026 supports a presumptive diagnosis and treatment while imaging evaluation continues.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[5]](#ref5)[[7]](#ref7)</sup> Diagnostic considerations:

- **Physical examination.** Tenderness in the posterior fornix, uterosacral nodularity, fixed retroverted uterus, or adnexal masses; a normal exam does not exclude the diagnosis.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup>
- **Transvaginal ultrasound (TVUS)** is first-line imaging and can identify endometriomas and deep infiltrating endometriosis in experienced hands. A negative scan does not exclude superficial disease.<sup>[[19]](#ref19)[[5]](#ref5)</sup>
- **Pelvic MRI** maps deep infiltrating endometriosis and is most useful when surgical planning is needed or TVUS is inconclusive.<sup>[[5]](#ref5)</sup>
- **Laparoscopy with biopsy** can establish histology and treat disease when indicated, including when imaging is negative and empiric treatment has been unsuccessful or is inappropriate. It is not a prerequisite for all clinical diagnoses.<sup>[[19]](#ref19)[[7]](#ref7)</sup>
- **Biomarkers.** No serum biomarker, including CA-125, has sufficient sensitivity or specificity for clinical use.<sup>[[3]](#ref3)</sup>

---

## Treatment: Medical Management

Hormonal therapy is the mainstay of treatment for endometriosis-associated pain in women not seeking immediate pregnancy. Treatments suppress ovarian activity, act locally on endometriotic tissue, or both; not every regimen reliably inhibits ovulation or provides contraception. A network meta-analysis of 15 RCTs (1,680 participants) found placebo-adjusted mean pain reductions of 12.6–17.7 points on a 0–100 VAS for the combined contraceptives, progestins, levonorgestrel IUD and GnRH agonists studied, with little difference among these regimens.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup>

### First-Line Therapies

- **Combined oral contraceptives** (20–30 μg ethinyl estradiol). Continuous use provides better pain control than cyclic regimens. Mean pain reduction versus placebo: 15.1 points (95% CI, −20.8 to −9.3).<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[8]](#ref8)</sup>
- **Progestin-only options.** Norethindrone acetate (oral), depot medroxyprogesterone acetate (IM), etonogestrel implant, or the 52-mg levonorgestrel IUD. The LNG-IUD reduced pain by 17.7 points versus placebo but does not suppress ovulation.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[8]](#ref8)</sup>
- **NSAIDs.** Commonly used adjunctively, though a Cochrane review found insufficient evidence that they significantly reduce endometriosis-specific pain.<sup>[[8]](#ref8)[[9]](#ref9)</sup>

### Second-Line Therapies: GnRH Agonists and Antagonists

Two oral GnRH antagonist regimens are FDA-approved for endometriosis-associated pain: Orilissa (elagolix) and Myfembree (relugolix combination therapy).<sup>[[10]](#ref10)[[21]](#ref21)</sup>

- **Elagolix (Orilissa).** With normal liver function or mild hepatic impairment, low dose 150 mg daily (up to 24 months) or high dose 200 mg twice daily (up to 6 months).<sup>[[10]](#ref10)</sup> Moderate hepatic impairment permits only 150 mg daily for up to 6 months; severe hepatic impairment and known osteoporosis are contraindications. In the phase 3 trials EM-1 and EM-2, month-3 dysmenorrhea response rates were 43–46% (150 mg daily) and 72–76% (200 mg twice daily) versus 20–23% with placebo.<sup>[[10]](#ref10)</sup> High-dose elagolix with add-back therapy (estradiol 1 mg / norethindrone acetate 0.5 mg) has phase 3 efficacy and BMD data; this study does not extend the approved Orilissa monotherapy duration or establish zero bone loss.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[10]](#ref10)[[11]](#ref11)</sup>
- **Relugolix combination therapy (Myfembree).** Relugolix 40 mg plus estradiol 1 mg plus norethindrone acetate 0.5 mg, once daily.<sup>[[21]](#ref21)</sup> At 24 weeks in the SPIRIT 1 and 2 trials, 75% met dysmenorrhea responder criteria versus 27–30% with placebo; a separate extension reported outcomes through 2 years among eligible trial completers.<sup>[[12]](#ref12)[[13]](#ref13)</sup> Add-back reduces but does not eliminate bone loss: US treatment is limited to 24 months, with baseline and annual DXA for endometriosis, and thromboembolic contraindications must be reviewed.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[12]](#ref12)[[13]](#ref13)[[21]](#ref21)</sup>

A meta-analysis of five phase 3 trials (2,060 participants; elagolix 200 mg twice daily, relugolix combination therapy and linzagolix with add-back) found a pooled NNT of 2 for dysmenorrhea response and 5 for nonmenstrual pelvic pain response, with no statistical heterogeneity. Treatment lasted up to 24 weeks.<sup>[[14]](#ref14)</sup>

GnRH agonists (leuprolide acetate) remain an option but require injection, cause an initial flare, and are limited to 12 months with add-back therapy due to bone loss concerns.<sup>[[8]](#ref8)[[9]](#ref9)[[2]](#ref2)</sup>

These GnRH regimens are not contraceptives. The labels require pregnancy exclusion and nonhormonal contraception, and ovulation suppression or amenorrhea does not exclude pregnancy.<sup>[[10]](#ref10)[[21]](#ref21)</sup>

### Third-Line Therapy

- **Aromatase inhibitors** (off-label) block estrogen production within lesions and are reserved for refractory cases. In premenopausal patients, combine with an appropriate ovarian-suppression regimen to limit follicular stimulation; specialist selection is required. Limited by bone loss with prolonged use.<sup>[[9]](#ref9)[[8]](#ref8)</sup>

Approximately 11–19% of patients have no pain reduction with hormonal medications, and 25–34% experience recurrent pain within 12 months of discontinuation.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[8]](#ref8)</sup>

## Treatment: Surgical Management

Surgery may be considered when hormonal therapy is ineffective or contraindicated, for a large or suspicious endometrioma when symptoms, malignancy concern, or follicle access warrant intervention, or for deep lesions that threaten organ function.<sup>[[5]](#ref5)[[8]](#ref8)</sup> For infertility, benefit depends on disease phenotype and the fertility plan: treating superficial disease may improve spontaneous pregnancy, whereas routine endometrioma surgery solely to improve ART live birth is not recommended.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[19]](#ref19)</sup>

- **Laparoscopic excision or ablation** is the standard surgical approach. For endometriomas, cystectomy reduces recurrence versus drainage or coagulation; CO₂ laser vaporization is another option, with ovarian-reserve tradeoffs. Superficial-disease comparisons differ.<sup>[[19]](#ref19)</sup>
- **Recurrence and repeat surgery are different outcomes.** A July 2026 meta-analysis of 57 studies (22,644 patients) estimated approximately 11% repeat surgery, with very high heterogeneity and varying follow-up.<sup>[[22]](#ref22)</sup> This is not a universal individual risk or a pain-recurrence rate. Postoperative suppression may be offered when immediate pregnancy is not desired.<sup>[[22]](#ref22)[[6]](#ref6)</sup>
- **Hysterectomy** is considered when all other treatments fail and future pregnancy is not desired. Approximately 25% of patients who undergo hysterectomy for endometriosis experience recurrent pelvic pain, and 10% undergo additional surgery to treat pain.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup> The ovarian-removal decision requires individualized counseling about recurrent disease, fertility goals, early menopause, and hormone therapy; hysterectomy does not guarantee pain cure.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[9]](#ref9)</sup>
- **Endometrioma excision and fertility.** Cystectomy adversely affects ovarian reserve (lower AMH, reduced antral follicle counts), requiring careful risk-benefit discussion before surgery in women desiring fertility.<sup>[[9]](#ref9)</sup>

In a large population-based cohort (n = 84,885, median follow-up 10 years), up to 1 in 4 women who underwent minor conservative surgery and 1 in 5 who underwent major conservative surgery with ovarian preservation required additional endometriosis surgery.<sup>[[15]](#ref15)</sup>

### Urologic involvement

Urinary involvement may be silent. When deep endometriosis or an endometrioma is suspected, the 2025 DGGG/OEGGG/SGGG guideline calls for bilateral renal ultrasound (expert consensus) to detect silent obstruction. If preoperative imaging shows ureteral or renal-pelvic dilation, repeat upper-tract imaging three months after ureterolysis to document relief; hydronephrosis also warrants assessment of renal function.<sup>[[30]](#ref30)</sup> Surgery should be planned with an experienced multidisciplinary team. Bladder lesions may require partial cystectomy; ureterolysis is appropriate in selected extrinsic disease, while obstructing intrinsic disease or failed ureterolysis may require resection with ureteroureterostomy or reimplantation according to location and length. Extrinsic ureteral disease is confined to the adventitia, while intrinsic disease extends into the muscularis; this wall-layer distinction underlies the choice between ureterolysis and resection, though no validated depth threshold defines it.<sup>[[23]](#ref23)</sup> Because deep posterior-compartment excision near the uterosacral ligaments and ureter can place the pelvic autonomic plexus at risk, a nerve-sparing dissection that separates the connective tissue and fascia surrounding the nerves from the underlying peritoneum and endometriotic lesions has been described; in one center's reported experience, none of 51 patients required intermittent self-catheterization after this technique. See [Pelvic Neuroanatomy](/docs/foundations/anatomy-physiology/pelvis-support/pelvic-neuroanatomy) for the plexus anatomy.<sup>[[24]](#ref24)</sup> Injection of sterile water or saline beneath an implant adjacent to bowel, bladder, or ureter has also been described to create a dissection plane and a heat sink that protects the underlying wall during laser vaporization or excision.<sup>[[25]](#ref25)</sup> The ESGE/ESHRE/WES surgical recommendations advise renal ultrasound follow-up after ureteral surgery and during conservative management of suspected deep disease to avoid missing silent hydronephrosis.<sup>[[20]](#ref20)</sup> See [Ureteral Stricture](/docs/clinical-conditions/03e-upper-tract/ureteral-stricture) and [ureteral reconstruction](/docs/surgical-techniques/04d-upper-tract-reconstruction).

**Postoperative voiding dysfunction.** Colorectal surgery for deep endometriosis carries a meaningful risk of lower urinary tract dysfunction that varies by technique. A systematic review included 60 studies in its qualitative synthesis; nine studies (792 patients) contributed to the meta-analysis of voiding dysfunction within 30 days. Disc excision was associated with higher odds than rectal shaving (OR 12.9, 95% CI 1.40-119.34), while segmental resection did not differ significantly from shaving (OR 3.05, 95% CI 0.55-16.87) or discoid resection (OR 0.99, 95% CI 0.54-1.85).<sup>[[26]](#ref26)</sup> A separate systematic review and meta-analysis of 13 studies found lower odds of postoperative voiding dysfunction with rectal shaving than with segmental resection (OR 0.34, 95% CI 0.18-0.63) or discoid excision (OR 0.22, 95% CI 0.09-0.51), and lower odds of self-catheterization beyond one month versus segmental resection (OR 0.30, 95% CI 0.14-0.66); discoid excision and segmental resection did not differ significantly.<sup>[[27]](#ref27)</sup> These are separate pooled comparisons rather than a single trial, and the choice between techniques still depends on the anatomy of the disease. In a single-center retrospective cohort of 198 women who underwent surgery for posterior-compartment deep infiltrating endometriosis, 41% initially had voiding dysfunction (postvoid residual &gt;100 mL at the second bladder scan); among the 17 who needed self-catheterization after discharge, the median time to a postvoid residual under 100 mL was 41 days.<sup>[[28]](#ref28)</sup> Preoperative ENZIAN compartment-B nodules were associated with postoperative dysfunction (p = 0.001), and stage B3 (a compartment-B nodule &gt;3 cm in lateral diameter) had the highest hazard ratio for elevated residual urine (HR 6.43, 95% CI 2.3-18.2).<sup>[[28]](#ref28)</sup> A multicenter series identified persistent urinary retention (postvoid residual &gt;100 mL requiring self-catheterization beyond 30 days) in 16 of 881 women (1.8%) after deep pelvic endometriosis surgery, compared with a 3.5%-14.3% range cited from prior posterior-compartment surgery literature; among the 16 retention cases, 93.8% underwent colorectal resection and 87.5% colpectomy, and the authors suggested that bilateral uterosacral-ligament resection with posterior colpectomy tended to increase risk.<sup>[[29]](#ref29)</sup> Eleven of the 16 still required self-catheterization at one year.<sup>[[29]](#ref29)</sup>

## Endometriosis and Infertility

Infertility occurs in 30–50% of women with endometriosis through multiple mechanisms: chronic inflammation impairing folliculogenesis and implantation, distorted pelvic anatomy, diminished ovarian reserve, and altered endometrial receptivity.<sup>[[16]](#ref16)[[4]](#ref4)</sup>

- **Ovarian suppression does not improve natural fertility.** ESHRE no longer recommends extended GnRH-agonist pretreatment before ART to improve live birth because benefit is uncertain.<sup>[[19]](#ref19)</sup>
- **Minimal–mild disease (rASRM I–II).** Surgical excision or ablation may improve spontaneous pregnancy rates. Ovulation induction with IUI increases fecundity (limit to 3–4 cycles).<sup>[[4]](#ref4)[[17]](#ref17)</sup>
- **Moderate–severe disease (rASRM III–IV).** IVF is the most effective treatment. ART outcomes are similar to other causes of infertility in stage I–II disease but show reduced live birth rates and oocyte yields in stage III–IV.<sup>[[16]](#ref16)[[18]](#ref18)</sup>
- **Endometriomas and IVF.** Do not routinely operate solely to improve ART live birth: benefit is unproven and ovarian reserve may be harmed. Consider surgery for pain, suspicious findings, or follicle access, with individualized counseling.<sup>[[19]](#ref19)</sup>
- The **Endometriosis Fertility Index (EFI)** can help guide management decisions after surgery.<sup>[[16]](#ref16)</sup>

## Systemic Comorbidities and Long-Term Risks

Endometriosis is increasingly recognized as a systemic inflammatory disease with effects extending beyond the pelvis:<sup>[[4]](#ref4)[[1]](#ref1)</sup>

- **Ovarian cancer.** Risk is 93% greater overall, with a 3.4-fold risk of clear-cell and a 2.3-fold risk of endometrioid subtypes. Ovarian-cancer incidence is approximately 1.1% among all women, and the absolute risk in women with endometriosis remains low; guidelines do not recommend prophylactic bilateral salpingo-oophorectomy for normal-appearing ovaries.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup>
- **Cardiovascular disease.** Significantly elevated risk of MI and stroke, partly mediated by chronic inflammation and partly by high rates of surgical menopause.<sup>[[9]](#ref9)[[1]](#ref1)</sup>
- **Autoimmune conditions.** Increased risk of SLE, rheumatoid arthritis, and other autoimmune diseases.<sup>[[9]](#ref9)[[1]](#ref1)</sup>
- **Mental health.** Depression and anxiety, with evidence of altered brain gene expression and decreased volume in emotional-processing regions.<sup>[[4]](#ref4)</sup>
- **Coexisting pain conditions.** Migraine, irritable bowel syndrome, interstitial cystitis / painful bladder syndrome, and fibromyalgia are common comorbidities, reflecting shared central-sensitization pathways.<sup>[[9]](#ref9)</sup>

---

## Emerging Concepts

- **Classification.** The revised ASRM staging system correlates poorly with symptoms and prognosis. Newer frameworks including the ENZIAN classification for deep endometriosis and the Endometriosis Fertility Index aim to better guide clinical decisions.<sup>[[6]](#ref6)[[16]](#ref16)</sup>
- **Noninvasive diagnosis.** Research into circulating biomarkers (miRNAs, proteomics) and advanced imaging continues, though none have reached clinical utility.<sup>[[3]](#ref3)[[4]](#ref4)</sup>
- **Multimodal pain management.** Because central sensitization contributes to pain, interdisciplinary approaches that incorporate pelvic-floor physical therapy, cognitive behavioral therapy and neuromodulatory agents are used alongside hormonal treatment.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[9]](#ref9)</sup>
