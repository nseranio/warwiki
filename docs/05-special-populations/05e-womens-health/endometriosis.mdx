# Endometriosis

Endometriosis is a chronic, estrogen-dependent inflammatory disease defined by endometrial-like tissue outside the uterine lining, affecting approximately 10% of reproductive-age women (approximately 9 million in the U.S.). It is the most common cause of chronic pelvic pain in women and is associated with infertility in 30–50% of affected individuals. There is currently no medical or surgical cure, and diagnosis is delayed an average of 5 to 12 years from symptom onset.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup> For the urogynecologist and pelvic reconstructive surgeon, endometriosis is relevant because deep infiltrating disease causes ureteral obstruction and bladder-detrusor invasion that drive partial cystectomy and ureteroneocystostomy referrals, IC/PBS frequently coexists through shared central-sensitization pathways, and repeat-operation risk varies substantially by disease phenotype, surgery, postoperative suppression, and follow-up.

---

## Pathophysiology

The most widely accepted theory is **retrograde menstruation** (Sampson's theory), in which shed endometrial tissue travels through the fallopian tubes into the peritoneal cavity, where it implants, proliferates, and provokes an inflammatory response.<sup>[[3]](#ref3)</sup> Additional mechanisms include:

- **Epigenetic reprogramming** of endometrial mesenchymal progenitor / stem cells, with overexpression of steroidogenic factor-1 driving excessive local estrogen production via estrogen receptor-β, and progesterone receptor deficiency causing progesterone resistance.<sup>[[3]](#ref3)</sup>
- **Immune dysregulation** with proinflammatory cytokines, altered circulating immune cell populations, and aberrant microRNA expression creating a systemic inflammatory environment.<sup>[[4]](#ref4)</sup>
- **Neurogenesis** within lesions, with new nerve fiber growth contributing to nociceptive and neuropathic pain mechanisms; central sensitization (nociplastic pain) explains why lesion burden correlates poorly with pain severity.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup>

Three pain mechanisms coexist and explain the disconnect between disease stage and symptom severity: **nociceptive** (lesion-driven inflammation), **neuropathic** (lesion-associated nerve fiber injury and infiltration), and **nociplastic** (central sensitization).

## Clinical Presentation

Approximately 90% of women with endometriosis report pelvic pain, and 50% report moderate to severe fatigue.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup> Symptoms include:

- **Dysmenorrhea.** Progressive, often starting in adolescence
- **Nonmenstrual chronic pelvic pain**
- **Deep dyspareunia**
- **Dyschezia.** Painful defecation, often cyclic
- **Dysuria.** Cyclic
- **Infertility.** Reported in 26% at diagnosis<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup>

Endometriosis exists in three phenotypes: **superficial peritoneal lesions**, **ovarian endometriomas**, and **deep infiltrating endometriosis** (involving the rectovaginal septum, bladder, ureters, or bowel).<sup>[[2]](#ref2)[[4]](#ref4)</sup>

---

## Diagnosis

A clinical diagnosis can now be made based on symptoms, physical examination, and imaging; surgical confirmation is not required to initiate empiric treatment. ACOG 2026 supports a presumptive diagnosis and treatment while imaging evaluation continues.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[5]](#ref5)[[7]](#ref7)</sup> Diagnostic considerations:

- **Physical examination.** Tenderness in the posterior fornix, uterosacral nodularity, fixed retroverted uterus, or adnexal masses; a normal exam does not exclude the diagnosis.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup>
- **Transvaginal ultrasound (TVUS)** is first-line imaging and can identify endometriomas and deep infiltrating endometriosis in experienced hands. A negative scan does not exclude superficial disease.<sup>[[19]](#ref19)[[5]](#ref5)</sup>
- **Pelvic MRI** maps deep infiltrating endometriosis and is most useful when surgical planning is needed or TVUS is inconclusive.<sup>[[5]](#ref5)</sup>
- **Laparoscopy with biopsy** can establish histology and treat disease when indicated, including when imaging is negative and empiric treatment has been unsuccessful or is inappropriate. It is not a prerequisite for all clinical diagnoses.<sup>[[19]](#ref19)[[7]](#ref7)</sup>
- **Biomarkers.** No serum biomarker, including CA-125, has sufficient sensitivity or specificity for clinical use.<sup>[[3]](#ref3)</sup>

## Treatment: Medical Management

Hormonal therapy is the mainstay of treatment for endometriosis-associated pain in women not seeking immediate pregnancy. Treatments suppress ovarian activity, act locally on endometriotic tissue, or both; not every regimen reliably inhibits ovulation or provides contraception. A network meta-analysis of 1,680 women from 15 RCTs found that all hormonal treatments produced clinically significant pain reduction (13–18 points on a 0–100 VAS) compared with placebo, with little difference among classes.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup>

### First-Line Therapies

- **Combined oral contraceptives** (20–30 μg ethinyl estradiol). Continuous use provides better pain control than cyclic regimens. Mean pain reduction versus placebo: 15.1 points (95% CI, −20.8 to −9.3).<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[8]](#ref8)</sup>
- **Progestin-only options.** Norethindrone acetate (oral), depot medroxyprogesterone acetate (IM), etonogestrel implant, or the 52-mg levonorgestrel IUD. The LNG-IUD reduced pain by 17.7 points versus placebo but does not suppress ovulation.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[8]](#ref8)</sup>
- **NSAIDs.** Commonly used adjunctively, though a Cochrane review found insufficient evidence that they significantly reduce endometriosis-specific pain.<sup>[[8]](#ref8)[[9]](#ref9)</sup>

### Second-Line Therapies: GnRH Agonists and Antagonists

Two oral GnRH antagonists are FDA-approved for endometriosis-associated pain:

- **Elagolix (Orilissa).** With normal liver function or mild hepatic impairment, low dose 150 mg daily (up to 24 months) or high dose 200 mg twice daily (up to 6 months). Moderate hepatic impairment permits only 150 mg daily for up to 6 months; severe hepatic impairment and known osteoporosis are contraindications. In the phase 3 trials EM-1 and EM-2, month-3 dysmenorrhea response rates were 43–46% (150 mg daily) and 72–76% (200 mg twice daily) versus 20–23% with placebo. High-dose elagolix with add-back therapy (estradiol 1 mg / norethindrone acetate 0.5 mg) has phase 3 efficacy and BMD data; this study does not extend the approved Orilissa monotherapy duration or establish zero bone loss.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[10]](#ref10)[[11]](#ref11)</sup>
- **Relugolix combination therapy (Myfembree).** Relugolix 40 mg plus estradiol 1 mg plus norethindrone acetate 0.5 mg, once daily. In the SPIRIT 1 and 2 trials, 75% had clinically significant improvement in dysmenorrhea versus 27–30% placebo, with extension data through 2 years. Add-back reduces but does not eliminate bone loss: US treatment is limited to 24 months, with baseline and annual DXA for endometriosis, and thromboembolic contraindications must be reviewed.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[12]](#ref12)[[13]](#ref13)[[21]](#ref21)</sup>

A meta-analysis of five phase 3 trials (2,060 participants; elagolix 200 mg twice daily, relugolix combination therapy and linzagolix with add-back) found a pooled NNT of 2 for dysmenorrhea response and 5 for nonmenstrual pelvic pain response, with no statistical heterogeneity. Treatment lasted up to 24 weeks.<sup>[[14]](#ref14)</sup>

GnRH agonists (leuprolide acetate) remain an option but require injection, cause an initial flare, and are limited to 12 months with add-back therapy due to bone loss concerns.<sup>[[8]](#ref8)[[9]](#ref9)[[2]](#ref2)</sup>

These GnRH regimens are not contraceptives. The labels require pregnancy exclusion and nonhormonal contraception, and ovulation suppression or amenorrhea does not exclude pregnancy.<sup>[[10]](#ref10)[[21]](#ref21)</sup>

### Third-Line Therapy

- **Aromatase inhibitors** (off-label) block estrogen production within lesions and are reserved for refractory cases. In premenopausal patients, combine with an appropriate ovarian-suppression regimen to limit follicular stimulation; specialist selection is required. Limited by bone loss with prolonged use.<sup>[[9]](#ref9)[[8]](#ref8)</sup>

Approximately 11–19% of patients have no pain reduction with hormonal medications, and 25–34% experience recurrent pain within 12 months of discontinuation.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[8]](#ref8)</sup>

## Treatment: Surgical Management

Surgery is indicated when medical therapy is ineffective or contraindicated, for large endometriomas, deep infiltrating lesions causing organ compromise, or for infertility.<sup>[[5]](#ref5)[[8]](#ref8)</sup>

- **Laparoscopic excision or ablation** is the standard surgical approach. For endometriomas, cystectomy reduces recurrence versus drainage or coagulation; CO₂ laser vaporization is another option, with ovarian-reserve tradeoffs. Superficial-disease comparisons differ.<sup>[[19]](#ref19)</sup>
- **Recurrence and repeat surgery are different outcomes.** A July 2026 meta-analysis of 57 studies (22,644 patients) estimated approximately 11% repeat surgery, with very high heterogeneity and varying follow-up. This is not a universal individual risk or a pain-recurrence rate. Postoperative suppression may be offered when immediate pregnancy is not desired.<sup>[[22]](#ref22)[[6]](#ref6)</sup>
- **Hysterectomy** is considered when all other treatments fail and future pregnancy is not desired. Approximately 25% of patients who undergo hysterectomy for endometriosis experience recurrent pelvic pain, and 10% require additional surgery. The ovarian-removal decision requires individualized counseling about recurrent disease, fertility goals, early menopause, and hormone therapy; hysterectomy does not guarantee pain cure.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[9]](#ref9)</sup>
- **Endometrioma excision and fertility.** Cystectomy adversely affects ovarian reserve (lower AMH, reduced antral follicle counts), requiring careful risk-benefit discussion before surgery in women desiring fertility.<sup>[[9]](#ref9)</sup>

In a large population-based cohort (n = 84,885, median follow-up 10 years), up to 1 in 4 women who underwent minor conservative surgery and 1 in 5 who underwent major conservative surgery with ovarian preservation required additional endometriosis surgery.<sup>[[15]](#ref15)</sup>

### Urologic involvement

Urinary involvement may be silent. Suspected deep disease warrants assessment of the bladder, ureters, and kidneys; hydronephrosis requires evaluation of renal function and obstruction. Surgery should be planned with an experienced multidisciplinary team. Bladder lesions may require partial cystectomy; ureterolysis is appropriate in selected extrinsic disease, while obstructing intrinsic disease or failed ureterolysis may require resection with ureteroureterostomy or reimplantation according to location and length. The ESGE/ESHRE/WES surgical recommendations advise renal ultrasound follow-up after ureteral surgery and during conservative management of suspected deep disease to avoid missing silent hydronephrosis.<sup>[[20]](#ref20)</sup> See [Ureteral Stricture](/docs/clinical-conditions/03e-upper-tract/ureteral-stricture) and [ureteral reconstruction](/docs/surgical-techniques/04d-upper-tract-reconstruction).

## Endometriosis and Infertility

Infertility occurs in 30–50% of women with endometriosis through multiple mechanisms: chronic inflammation impairing folliculogenesis and implantation, distorted pelvic anatomy, diminished ovarian reserve, and altered endometrial receptivity.<sup>[[16]](#ref16)[[4]](#ref4)</sup>

- **Ovarian suppression does not improve natural fertility.** ESHRE no longer recommends extended GnRH-agonist pretreatment before ART to improve live birth because benefit is uncertain.<sup>[[19]](#ref19)</sup>
- **Minimal–mild disease (rASRM I–II).** Surgical excision or ablation may improve spontaneous pregnancy rates. Ovulation induction with IUI increases fecundity (limit to 3–4 cycles).<sup>[[4]](#ref4)[[17]](#ref17)</sup>
- **Moderate–severe disease (rASRM III–IV).** IVF is the most effective treatment. ART outcomes are similar to other causes of infertility in stage I–II disease but show reduced live birth rates and oocyte yields in stage III–IV.<sup>[[16]](#ref16)[[18]](#ref18)</sup>
- **Endometriomas and IVF.** Do not routinely operate solely to improve ART live birth: benefit is unproven and ovarian reserve may be harmed. Consider surgery for pain, suspicious findings, or follicle access, with individualized counseling.<sup>[[19]](#ref19)</sup>
- The **Endometriosis Fertility Index (EFI)** can help guide management decisions after surgery.<sup>[[16]](#ref16)</sup>

## Systemic Comorbidities and Long-Term Risks

Endometriosis is increasingly recognized as a systemic inflammatory disease with effects extending beyond the pelvis:<sup>[[4]](#ref4)[[1]](#ref1)</sup>

- **Ovarian cancer.** Risk is 93% greater overall, with a 3.4-fold risk of clear-cell and a 2.3-fold risk of endometrioid subtypes. Absolute lifetime risk remains low (1.8% versus 1.1% in the general population), and prophylactic oophorectomy is not recommended.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup>
- **Cardiovascular disease.** Significantly elevated risk of MI and stroke, partly mediated by chronic inflammation and partly by high rates of surgical menopause.<sup>[[9]](#ref9)[[1]](#ref1)</sup>
- **Autoimmune conditions.** Increased risk of SLE, rheumatoid arthritis, and other autoimmune diseases.<sup>[[9]](#ref9)[[1]](#ref1)</sup>
- **Mental health.** Depression and anxiety, with evidence of altered brain gene expression and decreased volume in emotional-processing regions.<sup>[[4]](#ref4)</sup>
- **Coexisting pain conditions.** Migraine, irritable bowel syndrome, interstitial cystitis / painful bladder syndrome, and fibromyalgia are common comorbidities, reflecting shared central-sensitization pathways.<sup>[[9]](#ref9)</sup>

---

## Emerging Concepts

- **Classification.** The revised ASRM staging system correlates poorly with symptoms and prognosis. Newer frameworks including the ENZIAN classification for deep endometriosis and the Endometriosis Fertility Index aim to better guide clinical decisions.<sup>[[6]](#ref6)[[16]](#ref16)</sup>
- **Noninvasive diagnosis.** Research into circulating biomarkers (miRNAs, proteomics) and advanced imaging continues, though none have reached clinical utility.<sup>[[3]](#ref3)[[4]](#ref4)</sup>
- **Multimodal pain management.** Because central sensitization contributes to pain, interdisciplinary approaches that incorporate pelvic-floor physical therapy, cognitive behavioral therapy and neuromodulatory agents are used alongside hormonal treatment.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[9]](#ref9)</sup>
