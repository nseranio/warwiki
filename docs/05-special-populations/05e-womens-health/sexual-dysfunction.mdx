# Female Sexual Dysfunction

Approximately 43% of American women report sexual problems, and 12% report a sexual problem bothersome enough to cause personal distress; distress is about 10% at ages 18–44, peaks at about 15% at 45–64 and is about 9% at 65–85.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup> ACOG recommends that obstetrician-gynecologists initiate clinical discussion of sexual function during routine care visits to identify issues and destigmatize the topic.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup> For urogynecologists and pelvic reconstructive surgeons, FSD intersects with daily practice: dyspareunia and genito-pelvic pain drive surgical referrals, vaginal estrogen is a perioperative adjunct, and many patients undergoing prolapse or incontinence repair have overlapping sexual concerns that require evaluation and counseling.

## Prevalence and epidemiology

FSD is highly prevalent. A primary-care meta-analysis estimated overall prevalence at 39%.<sup>[[3]](#ref3)</sup> In a community survey of Australian women aged 18–39, 50.2% had sexually related personal distress and 20.6% had at least one FSD, defined as low function in a domain plus distress.<sup>[[4]](#ref4)</sup> A 2024 *NEJM* review highlighted that low desire progressively increases with age, but sexually associated distress concurrently declines, so that the peak in **hypoactive sexual desire disorder (HSDD)** emerges during midlife.<sup>[[5]](#ref5)</sup> In a large Australian community-based study, 27.4% of women aged 18–24 and 58.9% of women aged 45–49 had low desire, but hypoactive sexual desire dysfunction peaked during midlife.<sup>[[5]](#ref5)</sup>

Unspecified arousal dysfunction has been reported in population-based studies in approximately 3–9% of women aged 18–44 and 5–7.5% of women aged 45–64.<sup>[[5]](#ref5)</sup> In a community survey of Australian women aged 18–39 years, 20.6% had at least one FSD, with sexual self-image dysfunction (11.1%) the most prevalent subtype.<sup>[[4]](#ref4)</sup> Psychotropic medication use was significantly associated with all FSD subtypes in this population.<sup>[[4]](#ref4)</sup>

## DSM-5 classification

The DSM-5 identifies four types of female sexual dysfunction.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[6]](#ref6)</sup> All diagnoses require ≥6 months duration (except substance-induced), clinically significant personal distress, and exclusion of another mental disorder, relationship distress, or medical condition as primary explanation.

| DSM-5 Disorder | Core features |
|---|---|
| **Female Sexual Interest/Arousal Disorder (FSIAD)** | Absent/reduced interest, thoughts/fantasies, initiation/responsiveness, excitement, or genital/nongenital sensations during activity |
| **Female Orgasmic Disorder** | Absent, delayed, infrequent, or markedly reduced intensity of orgasm |
| **Genito-pelvic Pain/Penetration Disorder (GPPPD)** | Difficulty with penetration, vulvovaginal/pelvic pain, fear/anxiety about pain, or pelvic floor muscle tensing; combines former diagnoses of vaginismus and dyspareunia |
| **Substance/Medication-Induced Sexual Dysfunction** | Temporally linked to a substance or medication |

The **International Society for the Study of Women's Sexual Health / International Consultation on Sexual Medicine (ISSWSH / ICSM)** classification separates desire from arousal (cognitive versus genital arousal), adds **persistent genital arousal disorder (PGAD)** (see [PGAD/GPD](/docs/clinical-conditions/03h-pelvic-pain/pgad-gpd)), and further expands orgasm-disorder subtypes by frequency, intensity, timing, and pleasure dimensions.<sup>[[2]](#ref2)</sup> These frameworks overlap but are not interchangeable; distinguish pain, penetration difficulty, unwanted arousal and distress about low desire.

## Evaluation

The initial evaluation may require an extended visit and should include a comprehensive history and physical examination.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup> A detailed sexual history should cover:

- Sexual and gender identity
- Symptom nature, duration, and onset (acquired versus lifelong; generalized versus situational)
- Personal distress level
- Partner factors and relationship quality
- History of abuse or trauma
- Genito-pelvic activities and injuries
- Sleep quality and body image concerns

**Validated instruments.** ACOG describes the Female Sexual Function Index (FSFI) and Female Sexual Distress Scale (FSDS) as research tools that may be useful clinical adjuncts.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup> The **Decreased Sexual Desire Screener (DSDS)** is a brief HSDD screener;<sup>[[25]](#ref25)</sup> the **SIDI-F** measures HSDD severity;<sup>[[26]](#ref26)</sup> **PROMIS SexFS v2.0** includes genital discomfort domains;<sup>[[27]](#ref27)</sup> and **PISQ-IR** is designed for pelvic-floor-disorder populations and includes sexually inactive respondents.<sup>[[28]](#ref28)</sup> Scores support clinical assessment rather than independently establish a diagnosis. For the full instrument catalog with comparison table, scoring details, psychometric data, and a "which instrument when" practical-guidance block see [Assessment Tools — Female Sexual Function](/docs/evaluation/history-physical/assessment-tools#female-sexual-function).

Laboratory testing is typically not necessary unless an undiagnosed medical etiology is suspected.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup>

**Physical examination.** A structured, outside-in genital exam complements the history and gives a documented baseline before treatment starts: pubic hair pattern; labia majora for skin or lichenoid changes and postmenopausal flattening from labial fat-pad loss; labia minora for resorption or fusion (GSM findings).<sup>[[29]](#ref29)[[34]](#ref34)</sup> Record oral-contraceptive use, breastfeeding, and breast-cancer endocrine therapy in the history as possible contributors to genital dryness or vulvar pain.<sup>[[29]](#ref29)[[32]](#ref32)[[35]](#ref35)</sup> Retract the clitoral hood to visualize the glans corona; incomplete visualization suggests adhesions or phimosis, which may coexist with lichen sclerosus, and light cotton-swab contact can localize glans pain or hypersensitivity.<sup>[[19]](#ref19)[[29]](#ref29)</sup> Inspect the urethral meatus for telescoping, caruncles, or polyps and the vestibule for erythema. Map vestibular and periurethral-gland tenderness systematically with a cotton swab after separating the labia, distinguishing it from pain in surrounding vulvar skin; palpate the urethra, bladder neck, and bladder separately to localize pain.<sup>[[19]](#ref19)[[29]](#ref29)[[31]](#ref31)</sup> Vestibular tenderness prompts assessment for GSM, infection, dermatoses, provoked vestibulodynia, and pelvic-floor hypertonicity.<sup>[[32]](#ref32)[[35]](#ref35)</sup> Hormonal factors and neuroproliferation are potential associated factors of vulvodynia.<sup>[[32]](#ref32)</sup> Inspect the vagina with a speculum, rotating it 90° to visualize the anterior and posterior walls.<sup>[[33]](#ref33)</sup> Palpate the pelvic floor circumferentially with one digit, including the levator ani, to assess tenderness or hypertonicity.<sup>[[31]](#ref31)[[35]](#ref35)</sup>

## Risk factors and contributing conditions

FSD is multifactorial, requiring a biopsychosocial framework:<sup>[[2]](#ref2)[[5]](#ref5)</sup>

- **Biological:** menopause / hypoestrogenism (genitourinary syndrome of menopause, GSM), diabetes, cardiovascular disease, neurologic conditions, pelvic-floor dysfunction, endometriosis, chronic pain, surgical history (hysterectomy, oophorectomy)
- **Medications:** SSRIs/SNRIs, antipsychotics, hormonal contraceptives, antiepileptics, opioids, antihypertensives
- **Psychological:** depression, anxiety, history of sexual trauma, body image concerns, stress
- **Relational:** partner sexual dysfunction, relationship conflict, communication barriers
- **Sociocultural:** cultural or religious beliefs, inadequate sex education

## ACOG 2019 evidence-graded recommendations

These grades summarize the 2019 ACOG bulletin. The 2025 AUA/SUFU/AUGS GSM guideline recommends offering local low-dose vaginal estrogen for dyspareunia (Strong Recommendation; Grade C), offering vaginal DHEA (Moderate Recommendation; Grade C), and permits ospemifene (Conditional Recommendation; Grade C).<sup>[[29]](#ref29)</sup> For subsequent FDA labeling changes, including flibanserin eligibility, use the current prescribing information summarized below.

### Level A (good and consistent evidence)<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup>

- **Low-dose vaginal estrogen** is the preferred hormonal treatment for FSD due to GSM
- **Low-dose systemic hormone therapy** (estrogen ± progestin) is an alternative for women with both dyspareunia from GSM and vasomotor symptoms
- **Ospemifene** is an alternative to vaginal estrogen for dyspareunia caused by GSM
- **Systemic DHEA** is not effective and is not recommended for sexual interest/arousal disorders

### Level B (limited or inconsistent evidence)<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup>

- **Psychological interventions** (cognitive behavioral therapy [CBT], mindfulness-based therapy, couples therapy, sexual skills training) are recommended as part of treatment
- **Short-term [transdermal testosterone](/docs/foundations/pharmacology/sexual-medicine-andrology/female-testosterone-hsdd)** can be considered for postmenopausal women with sexual interest / arousal disorders after appropriate counseling about risks and unknown long-term effects
- **[Flibanserin](/docs/foundations/pharmacology/sexual-medicine-andrology/flibanserin)** can be considered for HSDD in premenopausal women without depression, with counseling about alcohol-related risks
- **Intravaginal prasterone, low-dose vaginal estrogen, and ospemifene** can all be used for moderate-to-severe dyspareunia from GSM
- **Sildenafil** should not be used for female interest/arousal disorders outside clinical trials
- **Vaginal CO₂ laser** should not be used outside a research setting

### Level C (consensus/expert opinion)<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup>

- [**Pelvic floor physical therapy**](/docs/surgical-techniques/04f-incontinence-procedures/procedures/pelvic-floor-pt) is recommended for genito-pelvic pain/penetration disorders
- **Lubricants, topical anesthesia, and moisturizers** may help reduce dyspareunia
- If transdermal testosterone is used, a 3–6 month trial is recommended with baseline and follow-up testosterone levels to confirm levels remain in the normal premenopausal range; discontinue at 6 months if no response

## Pharmacologic treatments

### [Flibanserin (Addyi)](/docs/foundations/pharmacology/sexual-medicine-andrology/flibanserin)

FDA-approved for acquired, generalized HSDD in women younger than 65 years, including postmenopausal women (label expanded December 2025).<sup>[[7]](#ref7)</sup> Exclude desire loss primarily explained by another condition, relationship problems, or medication effects.<sup>[[7]](#ref7)</sup> Mechanism: modulates serotonin, dopamine, and norepinephrine pathways (5-HT1A agonist / 5-HT2A antagonist). Efficacy is modest: a meta-analysis of 8 trials (5,914 participants) showed an increase of about 0.5 **satisfying sexual events (SSEs)** per month over placebo, with significant side effects including dizziness, somnolence, nausea, and fatigue.<sup>[[5]](#ref5)</sup> For alcohol safety, wait at least 2 hours after 1–2 standard drinks before the bedtime dose; skip the dose after 3 or more drinks. After taking it, avoid alcohol until the next day. Moderate or strong CYP3A4 inhibitors and hepatic impairment are contraindications.<sup>[[7]](#ref7)</sup> See the [Flibanserin pharmacology page](/docs/foundations/pharmacology/sexual-medicine-andrology/flibanserin) for the full prescribing framework.

### [Bremelanotide (Vyleesi)](/docs/foundations/pharmacology/sexual-medicine-andrology/bremelanotide)

FDA-approved for acquired, generalized HSDD in premenopausal women.<sup>[[8]](#ref8)</sup> Mechanism: melanocortin-4 receptor agonist administered subcutaneously (1.75 mg) as needed at least 45 minutes before anticipated sexual activity.<sup>[[18]](#ref18)</sup> The 24-week RECONNECT phase 3 trials randomized 1,267 women; in the modified intention-to-treat efficacy population (n = 1,202), the integrated placebo-adjusted FSFI-desire domain change was 0.35 (p &lt; 0.001), with a reduction in distress.<sup>[[9]](#ref9)</sup> Among the 684 women who entered the 52-week open-label extension, the most common drug-related treatment-emergent adverse events were nausea (40%), flushing (21%), and headache (12%).<sup>[[10]](#ref10)</sup> In the 52-week open-label extension, 684 enrolled and 272 completed follow-up; sustained improvement and no new safety signals were reported, with interpretation limited by attrition and no concurrent control.<sup>[[10]](#ref10)</sup> Most prespecified age, weight, BMI and testosterone subgroups favored treatment, with exceptions; identical benefit across subgroups was not established.<sup>[[11]](#ref11)</sup> See the [Bremelanotide pharmacology page](/docs/foundations/pharmacology/sexual-medicine-andrology/bremelanotide) for the full prescribing framework.

### [Transdermal testosterone](/docs/foundations/pharmacology/sexual-medicine-andrology/female-testosterone-hsdd)

The only evidence-based indication for testosterone in women is postmenopausal HSDD, per the 2019 Global Consensus Position Statement.<sup>[[12]](#ref12)</sup> A meta-analysis of 36 RCTs (8,480 participants) showed that testosterone significantly increased SSEs by about 0.85/month in postmenopausal women, with improvements in desire, arousal, orgasm, pleasure, and reduced distress.<sup>[[13]](#ref13)</sup>

No FDA-approved female testosterone formulation exists in the United States. A transdermal 1% testosterone cream is approved in Australia.<sup>[[5]](#ref5)</sup> When prescribed off-label, a fractionated dose of a regulator-approved male formulation is preferred over compounded preparations, with regular monitoring of serum testosterone and clinical assessment for androgen excess.<sup>[[5]](#ref5)[[12]](#ref12)</sup> More than 2 million testosterone prescriptions are written annually for women in the US.<sup>[[5]](#ref5)</sup> Oral testosterone raised LDL cholesterol and lowered HDL cholesterol in the meta-analysis, whereas non-oral routes did not.<sup>[[13]](#ref13)</sup> Transdermal routes are preferred.<sup>[[5]](#ref5)</sup> Side effects include acne, increased facial or body hair, and weight gain; long-term safety data are lacking.<sup>[[12]](#ref12)</sup> A 2025 review confirmed that two clinical guidelines now provide expert guidance on testosterone treatment and monitoring for HSDD in women.<sup>[[14]](#ref14)</sup> See the [Female Testosterone for HSDD pharmacology page](/docs/foundations/pharmacology/sexual-medicine-andrology/female-testosterone-hsdd) for the full prescribing and monitoring framework.

### Medication-induced sexual dysfunction (SSRI/SNRI)

For SSRI- or SNRI-associated orgasmic or sexual dysfunction, options include a weekend drug holiday or a gradual dose reduction, both of which risk recurrence of the underlying depression or anxiety or of discontinuation symptoms and require counseling and close monitoring. An alternative is switching to a dopaminergic/noradrenergic antidepressant such as bupropion, or adding bupropion to the existing SSRI as a second agent, an approach supported by a placebo-controlled trial and an open-label switch study.<sup>[[20]](#ref20)[[21]](#ref21)</sup> A randomized controlled trial found a statistically significant improvement in the FSFI orgasm domain with sildenafil in women with antidepressant-associated sexual dysfunction, suggesting a PDE5 inhibitor may have a role specifically for orgasmic dysfunction in women taking an SSRI.<sup>[[22]](#ref22)</sup>

## Non-pharmacologic treatments

A 2025 systematic review included 36 studies (26 randomized and 10 single-arm), excluded sexual pain conditions, and found that mindfulness-based CBT significantly improved total FSFI scores and subscales of desire, arousal, and orgasm, and that all three modalities (CBT, flibanserin, and bremelanotide) reduced distress.<sup>[[15]](#ref15)</sup> No included study directly compared CBT with pharmacotherapy; the review cannot rank those treatments head to head or establish effects in patients with sexual pain.

| Modality | Target disorder | Evidence level | Notes |
|---|---|---|---|
| [**CBT / mindfulness-based therapy**](/docs/surgical-techniques/04j-sexual-dysfunction/psychosexual-therapy) | Desire, arousal, orgasm | Level B | May improve sexual function and distress; select according to the psychological and relational formulation.<sup>[[15]](#ref15)</sup> |
| [**Couples therapy / sex therapy**](/docs/surgical-techniques/04j-sexual-dysfunction/psychosexual-therapy) | Desire, arousal, relational | Level B | Addresses partner and communication factors; integrate with treatment of contributing physical symptoms.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup> |
| [**Pelvic floor physical therapy**](/docs/surgical-techniques/04f-incontinence-procedures/procedures/pelvic-floor-pt) | GPPPD | Level C | Recommended for genito-pelvic pain/penetration disorder<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup> |
| **Vaginal dilators** | GPPPD / introital narrowing | Not graded | Self-dilation for vaginismus, trigger points, or stenosis after radiation or other injury; data on optimal use are scant<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup> |
| **Lubricants / moisturizers** | Dyspareunia | Level C | OTC; adjunctive to all strategies<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup> |
| **Clitoral vacuum-suction device** | Genital arousal disorder | Eros authorization; separate Fiera physiology data | The prescription Eros clitoral therapy device received FDA marketing authorization in 2000; its vacuum pump applies gentle suction over the clitoris to increase blood flow.<sup>[[23]](#ref23)</sup> A separate single-visit Fiera thermography study enrolled women without sexual dysfunction and does not establish treatment benefit for arousal disorder.<sup>[[24]](#ref24)</sup> |
