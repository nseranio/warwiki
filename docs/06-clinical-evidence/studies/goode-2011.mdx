# Goode 2011: Behavioral Therapy for Persistent Postprostatectomy Incontinence

<TrialBottomLine id="goode-2011" />

**Goode 2011** was a three-site randomized superiority trial in men with incontinence persisting after radical prostatectomy that compared behavioral therapy, behavioral therapy augmented by biofeedback and pelvic floor electrical stimulation, and delayed treatment; at 8 weeks, both active programs reduced diary-recorded incontinence episodes more than delayed treatment, and augmentation did not improve the result over behavioral therapy alone.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup>

<TrialQuickHit id="goode-2011" />

## Design

The 208 community-dwelling participants were aged 51–84 years and had been incontinent for 1–17 years after radical prostatectomy. Recruitment took place at a university and two Veterans Affairs continence clinics. Men with an artificial urinary sphincter or suburethral sling, previous structured behavioral therapy, or poor bladder emptying were excluded. Participants were stratified by clinic, incontinence type and frequency. Patients and treating clinicians knew the assignment; study staff who scored the 7-day bladder diaries were blinded.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup>

The primary endpoint was each participant's percentage reduction in weekly incontinence episodes on a 7-day diary after 8 weeks, analyzed by intention to treat. Pairwise testing followed a significant overall group test, with a prespecified alpha of .0125 for the comparisons. Missing posttreatment diaries for 36/208 men were multiply imputed.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup>

## Results

Mean weekly episodes fell from 28 to 13 with behavioral therapy, from 26 to 12 with augmented therapy, and from 25 to 21 with delayed treatment: mean percentage reductions of 55%, 51% and 24%. The behavioral-versus-control difference was 30.65 percentage points (95% CI 12.22–49.08; P = .001); the augmented-versus-control difference was 26.74 points (95% CI 7.14–46.35; P = .01). Both passed the prespecified pairwise threshold. The difference between active programs was 3.91 points (95% CI −15.07 to 22.88; P = .69).<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup>

Complete continence, defined as no episode during the 7-day diary, occurred in 11/70 (15.7%) behavioral, 12/70 (17.1%) augmented and 4/68 (5.9%) control participants at 8 weeks. Among the active-arm participants with 12-month diaries, mean episode reductions from baseline were 50.4% and 59.0%, respectively (P = .32); the control participants had been offered treatment after the initial comparison.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup>

## Surgery and treatment delivery

Physician investigators or nurse practitioners delivered behavioral therapy in four visits approximately 2 weeks apart. They taught pelvic floor contraction using anal palpation, then prescribed three daily exercise sessions of 15 contractions. Bladder strategies included contraction before a cough or lift and repeated contractions during urgency; diaries, fluid management and caffeine advice were also part of treatment.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup>

The augmented arm added in-office dual-channel electromyographic feedback and daily home stimulation using an anal probe: 20 Hz, 1-ms pulse width, 5 seconds on and 15 seconds off for 15 minutes, with current up to 100 mA. Its exercise schedule used two daily sessions. Of 208 randomized men, 176 (84.6%) completed the 8-week treatment period; 87/112 (77.7%) active-treatment completers were followed for one year. Across the active arms, reported adherence at 8 weeks was 100% for exercises and 93% for bladder control strategies.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup>

## Adverse events

Two of 70 men receiving electrical stimulation (2.9%) developed transient hemorrhoidal irritation; these were the report's study-related adverse events.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup>

## Limitations

The treatment assignment was open to patients and clinicians. The behavioral program combined muscle exercises, bladder strategies, fluid advice and self-monitoring, so its individual components were not randomized. The 12-month results include only the active arms and 81 men with diary data, after delayed-treatment controls were offered treatment.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup>

## Guidelines and approvals

The AUA/GURS/SUFU Incontinence after Prostate Treatment guideline, amended 2024, says clinicians should offer pelvic floor muscle exercises or training to patients seeking treatment for incontinence after radical prostatectomy <Rec>(Moderate Recommendation; Evidence Level: Grade B)</Rec>. Its discussion of that statement cites this trial.<sup>[[2]](#ref2)</sup>
