# ATLAS: Ambulatory Treatments for Leakage Associated with Stress Incontinence

<TrialBottomLine id="atlas" />

**ATLAS** was a nine-site, assessor-blinded randomized trial in women seeking nonsurgical care for stress-predominant urinary incontinence that compared a fitted continence pessary, behavioral pelvic-floor therapy, and their combination, finding at three months that behavioral therapy improved the no-bothersome-symptoms endpoint versus pessary while the global-improvement comparison was not significant and combination therapy failed its prespecified superiority rule.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup>

<TrialQuickHit id="atlas" />

## Design

Eligible women had stress-only or stress-predominant mixed incontinence. Randomization was stratified by incontinence type and the frequency of episodes on a seven-day diary. Participants and interventionists knew the assignment; staff performing examinations and collecting telephone outcomes were blinded. Withdrawal or use of another incontinence treatment counted as failure in the intention-to-treat analysis.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup>

The coprimary outcomes at three months were a Patient Global Impression of Improvement (PGI-I) rating of “much better” or “very much better” and absence of bothersome stress symptoms on the Pelvic Floor Distress Inventory (PFDI) stress subscale. Combination therapy qualified as superior only if it significantly exceeded both single treatments. The final PGI-I comparison used a 0.0492 significance level after a planned interim analysis; the other comparisons used 0.05.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup>

## Results

On PGI-I, 72/146 women (49.3%) assigned to behavioral therapy and 59/149 (39.6%) assigned to pessary reported being much or very much better at three months, a 9.7-percentage-point difference (P = 0.09). The PFDI stress endpoint was met by 71/146 (48.6%) and 49/149 (32.9%), respectively, a 15.7-percentage-point difference (P = 0.006).<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup>

In the combination arm, 80/150 (53.3%) met the PGI-I endpoint and 66/150 (44.0%) had no bothersome stress symptoms. Combination exceeded pessary on those measures (P = 0.02 and P = 0.05) but did not exceed behavioral therapy (P = 0.49 and P = 0.42), so neither comparison met the trial's combination-superiority rule. Treatment satisfaction at three months was 110/146 (75.3%) with behavioral therapy, 94/149 (63.1%) with pessary, and 118/150 (78.7%) with combination; the behavioral-versus-pessary comparison had P = 0.03 in the Results and Table 3 footnote.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup>

At 12 months, global improvement was reported by 48/146 (32.9%), 47/149 (31.5%), and 49/150 (32.7%), respectively. No bothersome stress symptoms were reported by 59/146 (40.4%), 52/149 (34.9%), and 49/150 (32.7%). Neither endpoint differed significantly among groups at that assessment.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup>

A secondary analysis of 295 women in the pessary and behavioral arms found improvement in pelvic symptom distress and condition-specific quality of life in both arms at three months, without a significant between-group difference in the analyzed scores.<sup>[[2]](#ref2)</sup> An adjusted secondary analysis among 445 women across all three arms associated no prior incontinence surgery with greater odds of stress-symptom success (OR 3.15, 95% CI 1.04–9.53) and fewer than 14 weekly episodes at baseline with greater odds of treatment satisfaction (OR 1.97, 95% CI 1.21–3.19).<sup>[[3]](#ref3)</sup> The investigators found no significant interaction between these predictors and treatment assignment.<sup>[[3]](#ref3)</sup>

## Surgery and treatment delivery

There was no surgical intervention. The main trial allowed up to three visits at one- to two-week intervals to obtain a usable continence ring or dish fit. Trained nurses, nurse practitioners, and physical therapists delivered behavioral treatment in four standardized visits about two weeks apart. They prescribed daily pelvic-floor exercises and taught active muscle strategies for stress and urge leakage, followed by an individualized home program after the eight-week supervised period.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup>

In the prespecified fitting analysis of the pessary-containing arms, 235/255 women (92%) with a recorded final outcome were fitted successfully. Trained nurses, nurse practitioners, or physical therapists selected a ring or dish; a usable fit allowed a finger between device and vaginal wall and retained the device while the woman stood, coughed, and strained. Of the successful fittings, 122 used a ring and 113 a dish. POP-Q total vaginal length, point C, and point D did not predict final device diameter.<sup>[[4]](#ref4)</sup>

By three months, withdrawal had occurred in 39/149 (26.2%) assigned to pessary, 22/146 (15.1%) assigned to behavioral therapy, and 18/150 (12.0%) assigned to combination. Twelve pessary-arm withdrawals were attributed to inadequate fit. At one year, 45% reported continued pessary use and 57% reported continued pelvic-floor exercises.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup>

## Adverse events

Vaginal discharge was reported at three months by 16% in the pessary arm, 6% in the behavioral arm, and 9% in the combination arm. The report also recorded vaginal yeast infection in 7% overall and no vaginal erosions. Ten unexpected urologic events comprised seven urinary tract infections, two possible infections, and one possible urinary retention event; the counts were 3/149 with pessary, 2/146 with behavioral therapy, and 5/150 with combination.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup>

## Limitations

The interventions could not be blinded to participants, and early withdrawal was greatest with pessary. Missing outcomes and withdrawals were generally treated as failures, making continued treatment use important when interpreting the 12-month estimates. Combination-arm participants could remain in follow-up while using only one component. The behavioral regimen comprised four supervised visits, so its results describe that treatment intensity.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup>

## Guidelines and approvals

The European Association of Urology 2026 female lower urinary tract symptoms guideline summarizes evidence comparing pelvic-floor muscle training with continence pessaries, including the ATLAS comparison through its cited evidence review. It recommends supervised intensive pelvic-floor muscle training for at least three months as first-line therapy for women with stress or mixed urinary incontinence <Rec>(Strong)</Rec>.<sup>[[5]](#ref5)</sup>
