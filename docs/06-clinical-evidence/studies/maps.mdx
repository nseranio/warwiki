# MAPS: Pelvic Floor Training for Incontinence After Prostate Surgery

<TrialBottomLine id="maps" />

**MAPS** comprised two parallel UK randomized trials in 853 men with urinary incontinence identified around six weeks after radical prostatectomy or TURP, comparing therapist-led pelvic floor muscle training (PFMT) with indicated bladder training against standard care; neither trial showed a statistically significant reduction in self-reported incontinence at 12 months.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup>

<TrialQuickHit id="maps" />

## Design

Men were recruited through 34 UK centres and randomized within separate radical-prostatectomy and TURP trials. Allocation used a remote computer system and was minimized by centre, age and incontinence before surgery. Men and therapists knew the assignment; clerical staff entering the participant questionnaires were blinded. Men already referred for formal PFMT, those scheduled for early radiotherapy and those undergoing channel TURP for advanced cancer were excluded.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[2]](#ref2)</sup>

Both groups received a lifestyle advice leaflet. Controls could obtain routine NHS care, including non-specialist pelvic floor exercise advice and locally available leaflets. The intervention added a standardized therapist program delivered by a specialist continence physiotherapist or nurse. The primary clinical endpoint was any leakage reported on either the frequency or amount question of the ICIQ-UI Short Form at 12 months. Each trial was powered around a prespecified 15-percentage-point reduction in persistent incontinence. The primary economic measure was incremental cost per quality-adjusted life-year (QALY).<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[2]](#ref2)</sup>

## Results

Formal therapy showed no statistically significant reduction in 12-month incontinence in either trial. After radical prostatectomy, 148/196 men (75.5%) in the intervention group and 151/195 (77.4%) in the control group reported leakage: unadjusted risk difference −1.9 percentage points (95% CI −10 to 6); adjusted intention-to-treat RR 0.97 (95% CI 0.87–1.09; p=0.64).<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup> After TURP, the corresponding figures were 126/194 (64.9%) and 125/203 (61.6%): unadjusted risk difference 3.4 points (95% CI −6 to 13); adjusted RR 1.06 (95% CI 0.91–1.23; p=0.47).<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup> The trials were powered to detect a 15-point reduction.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[2]](#ref2)</sup>

Severe leakage at least daily in moderate or large amounts also persisted at 12 months: 74/196 (38%) versus 78/195 (40%) after radical prostatectomy (adjusted RR 0.93, 95% CI 0.73–1.19; p=0.58), and 48/194 (25%) versus 49/203 (24%) after TURP (adjusted RR 1.03, 95% CI 0.73–1.45; p=0.88). Pad use was 63/196 (32%) versus 68/195 (35%) after radical prostatectomy (adjusted RR 0.91, 95% CI 0.69–1.20; p=0.50) and 24/194 (12%) versus 24/203 (12%) after TURP (adjusted RR 1.05, 95% CI 0.62–1.78; p=0.86).<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup>

Therapist assignment increased self-reported PFMT practice: 128/191 (67%) versus 95/189 (50%) after radical prostatectomy (adjusted RR 1.30, 95% CI 1.09–1.53; p=0.003 in the HTA report) and 122/188 (65%) versus 39/193 (20%) after TURP (adjusted RR 3.20, 95% CI 2.37–4.32; p=0.001 in the HTA report).<sup>[[2]](#ref2)</sup> Mean NHS costs were £181 higher per patient after radical prostatectomy (95% CI £107–£255) and £209 higher after TURP (95% CI £147–£271), without a QALY advantage in either trial.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[2]](#ref2)</sup>

## Surgery and treatment delivery

The radical-prostatectomy cohort included abdominal, perineal and laparoscopic approaches, with no robotic procedures; the TURP cohort included standard and laser resections, with no HoLEP. These were the index operations, performed before randomization.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup>

The therapist program began with a 60-minute assessment, then three approximately 45-minute visits at about 2, 6 and 12 weeks. Digital anal examination supplied verbal feedback on contraction strength; machine-mediated biofeedback was used in 17 men after radical prostatectomy and 10 after TURP across five centres. The home program prescribed three maximum-strength contractions separated by 10-second breaks in lying, sitting and standing positions, twice daily, a slight pelvic-floor lift while walking, and contraction before activities that provoked leakage and after voiding. Men with urgency received bladder training and urge-suppression instruction.<sup>[[2]](#ref2)</sup>

At least one therapist visit was attended by 189/205 men (92%) after radical prostatectomy and 189/220 (86%) after TURP; 175/205 (85%) and 158/220 (72%), respectively, attended the final visit.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup>

## Adverse events

No adverse effects were reported by participants in either trial.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup>

## Limitations

The primary endpoint was participant-reported leakage, without objective pad-weight testing. Participants and therapists were unmasked, and pelvic floor advice remained available in standard care. Randomization followed identification of incontinence around six weeks after surgery; mean time from surgery to randomization was approximately eight weeks. The economic analysis used UK NHS costs.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[2]](#ref2)</sup>

## Guidelines and approvals

The EAU's 2026 male LUTS guideline cites MAPS in its pelvic floor training discussion and recommends offering PFMT alone or with biofeedback and/or electrical stimulation to men undergoing radical prostatectomy to speed recovery from incontinence <Rec>(Weak)</Rec>.<sup>[[3]](#ref3)</sup>
