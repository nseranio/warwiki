# OPAL: Pelvic Floor Muscle Training With or Without Electromyographic Biofeedback

<TrialBottomLine id="opal" />

**OPAL** was a multicenter randomized superiority trial in 600 women with stress or mixed urinary incontinence that compared supervised pelvic floor muscle training (PFMT) with versus without clinic and home electromyographic (EMG) biofeedback and found no improvement in 24-month incontinence severity with biofeedback.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup>

<TrialQuickHit id="opal" />

## Design

Women were newly presenting for continence care at 23 community and secondary care centers in England and Scotland. Eligibility required a demonstrable pelvic floor muscle contraction. Exclusions included urgency incontinence alone, prolapse greater than stage II, recent formal PFMT instruction, pregnancy or recent childbirth, neurological disease, pelvic cancer treatment, and nickel sensitivity. Randomization was balanced by incontinence type, center, age, and baseline severity. Participants, treating therapists, and researchers knew the allocation; clinicians assessing pelvic floor function at six months were masked.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup>

Experienced physiotherapists, nurses, or other continence clinicians offered both groups six appointments over 16 weeks. They assessed contraction, taught an individualized home programme aiming for three exercise sets daily, and used behavior change techniques. In the intervention group, EMG from a vaginal probe was displayed during clinic training; women also received a home biofeedback unit with instructions for operation, cleaning, and interpretation. The comparison group received the same supervised PFMT protocol without the device.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup>

The primary endpoint was the International Consultation on Incontinence Questionnaire–Urinary Incontinence Short Form (ICIQ-UI SF) score at 24 months, on which higher scores indicate greater severity. The prespecified analysis tested superiority at a two-sided 5% significance level; a 3-point between-group difference informed sample-size planning. Secondary definitions separated self-reported cure (no leakage frequency or quantity) from improvement (at least a 3-point score reduction).<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup>

## Results

At 24 months, mean ICIQ-UI SF scores were 8.2 among 225 women assigned biofeedback and 8.5 among 235 assigned PFMT alone. The adjusted mean difference was −0.09 points (95% CI −0.92 to 0.75; P=0.84), missing the superiority threshold. Self-reported cure occurred in 18/229 (7.9%) versus 20/238 (8.4%); improvement occurred in 135/225 (60.0%) versus 147/235 (62.6%).<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup>

The secondary self-efficacy score favored biofeedback: 63.1 versus 60.9, adjusted mean difference 2.36 points (95% CI 0.04 to 4.68; P=0.05).<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup>

The health technology assessment found an incremental cost of £50 and a gain of 0.0009 quality-adjusted life-years in its imputed 24-month base case; mean costs were £956 versus £906 and mean quality-adjusted life-years were 1.568 versus 1.567. The incremental cost-effectiveness ratio was £56,617 per quality-adjusted life-year, with a 48.8% probability of cost effectiveness at a £20,000 threshold.<sup>[[2]](#ref2)</sup>

## Surgery and treatment delivery

At least four appointments were attended by 198/295 (67.1%) in the biofeedback group and 192/298 (64.4%) in the comparison group.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup> Mean total appointment time was 176 versus 152 minutes. The additional time and home unit were part of the intervention being tested.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup> Treatment as allocated under the prespecified protocol definition occurred in 198/266 (74.4%) versus 230/276 (83.3%); a post hoc definition allowing biofeedback to begin by the second appointment gave 222/266 (83.5%) in that arm.<sup>[[2]](#ref2)</sup>

The HTA process evaluation found that therapists delivered additional biofeedback components while providing the basic PFMT components in both groups. Any adherence to the prescribed home programme between appointments during treatment was recorded in 220/281 (78.3%) versus 241/297 (81.1%); at 24 months, 90/173 (52.0%) versus 87/188 (46.3%) reported exercising at least weekly. Among women with complete 24-month treatment-uptake data, 10/81 (12.3%) versus 8/86 (9.3%) reported incontinence surgery (adjusted OR 1.25, 95% CI 0.35 to 4.46).<sup>[[2]](#ref2)</sup>

## Adverse events

Adverse events were reported by 34 women assigned biofeedback and 14 assigned PFMT alone; 21 and 2, respectively, had events considered related or possibly related to treatment. One nickel allergy in the biofeedback group was judged definitely related and led to stopping the intervention. Serious adverse events numbered 6 and 2; all were judged unrelated to treatment.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup>

## Limitations

The primary outcome was self-reported, and participants and treating clinicians were unblinded. Primary scores were available for 225/300 women assigned biofeedback and 235/300 assigned PFMT alone. Women unable to contract their pelvic floor muscles were excluded, and the intervention tested a particular clinic and home EMG device within a standardized therapist-led programme.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup>

## Guidelines and approvals

The EAU 2026 female LUTS guideline cites an early OPAL report in its PFMT evidence review and lists no added benefit from combining PFMT with biofeedback (level of evidence 1b). It recommends supervised intensive PFMT for at least three months as first-line treatment for women with stress or mixed incontinence <Rec>(Strong)</Rec>.<sup>[[3]](#ref3)</sup>
