# TOM: Testosterone in Older Men with Mobility Limitations

<TrialBottomLine id="tom-trial" />

**TOM** was a three-site, double-blind randomized trial of daily testosterone gel versus placebo gel in 209 older men with low testosterone and mobility limitation. Leg-press strength improved more with testosterone, but cardiovascular-related adverse events occurred in 23/106 versus 5/103 men, prompting early discontinuation of enrollment and treatment.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup>

<TrialQuickHit id="tom-trial" />

## Design

Eligibility required age at least 65 years, difficulty walking two blocks or climbing 10 steps, a Short Physical Performance Battery score of 4–9, and morning total testosterone of 100–350 ng/dL or free testosterone below 50 pg/mL. Men with uncontrolled hypertension, unstable angina, myocardial infarction within three months, class III–IV heart failure, active cancer, severe lower urinary tract symptoms, elevated hematocrit, or elevated prostate-specific antigen were excluded. The prespecified primary efficacy endpoint was change in maximal leg-press force at 24 weeks.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup>

Cardiovascular events were collected as adverse events. After an imbalance emerged, the safety board requested a broader cardiovascular-related category encompassing cardiac disorders, revascularization, edema, elevated blood pressure, arrhythmias, ECG changes, stroke, and syncope. An atherosclerosis-related category included myocardial infarction, sudden death, revascularization, and stroke.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup>

## Results

Among men with leg-press assessments, mean force rose 156.9 N with testosterone (n=49) and 27.1 N with placebo (n=54), an unadjusted mean difference in change of 129.8 N (95% CI 43.9–215.6; P=0.003). Chest-press force rose 34.7 N (n=38) and 0.28 N (n=44), respectively (mean difference 34.5 N, 95% CI 13.2–55.8; P=0.002).<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup>

The subsequent physical-function analysis found that 43% of testosterone recipients versus 18% of placebo recipients reached its participant-anchored minimally important difference in leg-press strength (P=0.01); for loaded stair-climbing power, the proportions were 28% versus 10% (P=0.03). The reported numbers needed to treat for those improvements were 4.0 and 5.5, respectively. Walking speed, self-reported function, physical activity, and fatigue did not change significantly. The analysis included 165 men with at least one follow-up efficacy measure (82 testosterone, 83 placebo); 138 completed six months (69 per arm).<sup>[[2]](#ref2)</sup>

Posttreatment total lean mass was 56.1 kg among 77 testosterone recipients and 56.2 kg among 80 placebo recipients; the reported between-arm difference in change from baseline was 1.5 kg (95% CI 0.4–2.2; P&lt;0.0001).<sup>[[2]](#ref2)</sup>

## Surgery and treatment delivery

Participants applied Testim 1% gel at 10 g daily, providing 100 mg of testosterone, for a planned six months. After two weeks, dosing changed to 5 g if measured testosterone exceeded 1000 ng/dL or 15 g if it remained below 500 ng/dL. Among testosterone recipients, 29 used 5 g, 61 used 10 g, and 16 used 15 g after titration; tube-count adherence exceeded 90% in both arms. Enrollment stopped at 209 of 252 planned participants; 129 completed six months and 176 had at least one postrandomization efficacy assessment in the initial report.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup>

The physical-function report used a modified testing battery for men at high cardiovascular risk or with limited exercise tolerance; the other men underwent exercise-tolerance testing before loaded strength and function assessments.<sup>[[2]](#ref2)</sup>

## Adverse events

The cardiovascular-related category included 23/106 testosterone recipients (21.7%) and 5/103 placebo recipients (4.9%); the unadjusted odds ratio was 5.4 (95% CI 2.0–14.9). The original MedDRA cardiac-disorders category included 10/106 versus 1/103 men, and the narrower atherosclerosis-related category included 7/106 versus 1/103. The safety board recommended stopping study treatment and enrollment on December 31, 2009.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup>

In the physical-function report's 165-man efficacy sample, cardiovascular-related adverse events occurred in 17/82 testosterone recipients and 5/83 placebo recipients; outcomes did not differ significantly between participants with and without these events.<sup>[[2]](#ref2)</sup>

## Limitations

Cardiovascular events were not a prespecified endpoint or systematically adjudicated; the broad category was assembled after the safety imbalance appeared. Its events varied in severity, and the small event count limited precision. Early termination reduced the strength analysis sample below the randomized population. Hyperlipidemia and statin use were more common at baseline in the testosterone arm.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup>

## Guidelines and approvals

The **AUA testosterone-deficiency guideline (2018)** cites TOM in its cardiovascular evidence discussion. It advises counseling patients that available evidence could not definitively establish whether testosterone therapy increases or decreases cardiovascular events <Rec>(Moderate Recommendation; Evidence Level: Grade B)</Rec>. It also advises waiting three to six months after a cardiovascular event before starting therapy <Rec>(Expert Opinion)</Rec>.<sup>[[3]](#ref3)</sup>
