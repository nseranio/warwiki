# PREVPROL: Pelvic Floor Muscle Training for Secondary Prevention of Pelvic Organ Prolapse

<TrialBottomLine id="prevprol" />

**PREVPROL** was a three-center randomized trial in women from a postchildbirth cohort with untreated stage I–III prolapse; supervised pelvic floor muscle training (PFMT), Pilates-based classes and a lifestyle leaflet produced a lower two-year prolapse symptom score than the leaflet alone (adjusted mean difference −1.01 points, 95% CI −1.70 to −0.33).<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup>

<TrialQuickHit id="prevprol" />

## Design

The cohort's index births occurred in 1993–1994, and trial recruitment took place 14–17 years later. Eligibility depended on POP-Q stage I–III prolapse without prior prolapse treatment. Women with stage 0 or IV prolapse, recent formal PFMT instruction, planned pregnancy, current pregnancy or recent delivery were excluded. Nearly all analyzed participants had prolapse above or at the hymen: 399/412 (96.8%).<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup>

The primary endpoint was the patient-completed Pelvic Organ Prolapse Symptom Score (POP-SS) at two years. Its seven items score symptom frequency over the preceding four weeks, with totals from 0 to 28; lower is better. Randomization was minimized by center, parity, prolapse position relative to the hymen and delivery mode. Participants and physiotherapists knew the assignment; data entry staff and the trial statistician were masked. The prespecified analysis used a repeated-measures model with a two-sided alpha of 0.05.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup>

## Results

At two years, mean POP-SS was 3.2 (SD 3.4; n=161) with PFMT and 4.2 (SD 4.4; n=180) with leaflet alone; the adjusted mean difference was −1.01 (95% CI −1.70 to −0.33; p=0.004). The primary endpoint met the prespecified significance threshold. The observed difference was smaller than the 1.52-point minimally important change derived from a surgical prolapse cohort.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup>

Any prolapse-related treatment within two years was reported by 8/135 (5.9%) versus 22/153 (14.4%) respondents (adjusted OR 0.29, 95% CI 0.12–0.71). This measure included consultations and referrals. Mean ICIQ-UI SF was 3.3 (SD 3.3; n=161) versus 4.1 (SD 4.1; n=175), with an adjusted mean difference of −0.83 (95% CI −1.44 to −0.22); any urine leakage and weekly pad use did not differ significantly.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup>

An ICS conference abstract reported three-year data for the UK subgroup, with 335 women analyzed. Mean POP-SS was 3.6 (SD 4.1; n=118) versus 4.0 (SD 4.3; n=139), with an adjusted mean difference of −0.08 (95% CI −0.86 to 0.70; p=0.834). Any prolapse-related treatment within three years was reported by 8/102 (7.8%) versus 31/127 (24.4%; OR 0.27, 95% CI 0.12–0.61).<sup>[[2]](#ref2)</sup>

## Surgery and treatment delivery

Specialist women's health physiotherapists offered five individual visits over 16 weeks. They assessed pelvic floor contraction, taught technique and prescribed individualized home exercises three times daily. Participants were also offered two six-week blocks of physiotherapist-led Pilates-based classes, a home exercise DVD and individual reviews at one and two years. Both groups received the same leaflet covering weight, lifting, constipation, cough and high-impact activity. In the intervention group, 19 women attended no individual visits and 88 attended all five; the mean was 3.5 visits (SD 1.7). At two years, 77% versus 53% reported pelvic floor exercises during the preceding four weeks.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup>

Surgery was a subsequent treatment, not part of the assigned intervention. Within two years, surgery was reported by 0/131 versus 1/143 respondents, and pessary use by 0/135 versus 2/153.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup>

## Adverse events

Three adverse events were reported in the PFMT group and none in the leaflet group: a fall before a Pilates class, tailbone pain during PFMT, and chest pain with shortness of breath during PFMT.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup>

## Limitations

The primary outcome was self-reported by unmasked participants. Two women were excluded after randomization, and the primary endpoint analysis used responses from 161 PFMT and 180 control participants. Some UK entry examinations predated trial recruitment, whereas New Zealand participants had a new POP-Q examination. The intervention combined individual training, classes, home exercise and annual reviews. The three-year abstract analyzed only UK participants and had a 70% questionnaire response rate.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup><sup>[[2]](#ref2)</sup>

## In conversation

<InConversation id="prevprol" />

POPPY enrolled women seeking treatment for symptomatic prolapse and assessed a 12-month training course; PREVPROL recruited women with anatomical prolapse who had not sought treatment and assessed symptoms at two years.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup>

## Guidelines and approvals

The EAU guideline on non-neurogenic female lower urinary tract symptoms (limited update, March 2026) cites PREVPROL in its review of PFMT and urinary symptoms. It recommends informing women with prolapse who do not need a pessary or surgery about potential lower urinary tract symptom relief from PFMT <Rec>(Strong)</Rec>.<sup>[[3]](#ref3)</sup>
