# Cardenas hydrophilic-catheter SCI trial: Intermittent catheterization after acute spinal cord injury

<TrialBottomLine id="hydrophilic-catheter-sci" />

In this open-label, multicenter randomized trial of 224 adults with acute traumatic spinal cord injury, single-use hydrophilic-coated SpeediCath delayed first antibiotic-treated symptomatic urinary tract infection compared with uncoated Conveen (HR 0.666, 95% CI 0.453–0.978; P = .038).<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup>

<TrialQuickHit id="hydrophilic-catheter-sci" />

## Design

The trial enrolled adults with traumatic spinal cord injury less than three months old, neurogenic bladder dysfunction, and a need for intermittent catheterization at least three times daily at 15 North American centers. Participants with UTI symptoms or taking prophylactic antibiotics were excluded. Randomization within ten days of starting intermittent catheterization assigned 108 participants to SpeediCath and 116 to Conveen; catheter appearance and handling prevented participant and staff blinding. Follow-up covered hospital or rehabilitation care and up to three months after discharge, with a six-month maximum.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup>

The main outcome measures were time to first UTI and total symptomatic UTIs during the study. Time to first UTI ran from first assigned-catheter use to the first day of symptoms. The clinical definition required a prescription for antibiotic treatment; the strict definition also required bacteriuria ≥10² CFU/mL, a listed symptom, and a positive leukocyte-esterase dipstick. Kaplan–Meier and Cox analyses assessed time to first UTI, and Poisson regression compared UTI episodes per observed patient-month. Tests used a two-sided .05 threshold.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup>

## Results

First antibiotic-treated symptomatic UTI occurred later with SpeediCath by the clinical definition: HR 0.666 (95% CI 0.453–0.978; P = .038), a 33% lower daily hazard. Of 219 participants who used a study catheter, 31 were excluded from the covariate-adjusted analysis because of missing covariates or a UTI before first assigned-catheter use. The strict-definition time-to-first-UTI comparison did not meet the .05 threshold (HR 0.640, 95% CI 0.395–1.032; P = .067).<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup>

During institutional care, clinical-definition UTI incidence was 0.539 per patient-month with SpeediCath (60 episodes/111.3 patient-months) versus 0.682 with Conveen (97/142.2), a 21% lower rate (reported RR 1.40, uncoated versus coated; P = .038). Strict-definition incidence was 0.189 (21/111.3) versus 0.295 (42/142.2) per patient-month (reported RR 1.67, uncoated versus coated; P = .022). Over the full institutional and community study period, clinical-definition incidence was 0.479 (99/206.7) versus 0.478 (167/349.2) per patient-month (P = .426), and strict-definition incidence was 0.198 (41/206.7) versus 0.218 (76/349.2) (P = .248).<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup>

## Surgery and treatment delivery

SpeediCath was a ready-to-use hydrophilic-coated polyurethane Nelaton catheter supplied in sterile water-based solution; uncoated polyvinyl chloride Conveen required gel from a single-use sachet. Both were sterile and discarded after use. Participants self-catheterized when feasible; otherwise nursing staff performed catheterization. Of 224 randomized participants, 114 completed the study, five stopped before first assigned-catheter use, and 105 discontinued prematurely after starting. Change in bladder management accounted for 34 coated-arm and 29 uncoated-arm discontinuations.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup>

## Adverse events

Urethral bleeding was reported more often with SpeediCath: 14 adverse-event reports versus six with Conveen (P &lt; .05), including seven versus two that led to premature discontinuation. Ten of 14 coated-arm bleeding reports occurred within the first eight days. In institutional weeks three and four, daily dipsticks were positive for microhematuria in 23% of coated-arm tests versus 34% of uncoated-arm tests (P &lt; .0001).<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup>

## Limitations

The open-label design allowed clinical judgment about antibiotic treatment to vary by arm. Premature discontinuation was 57% among coated-catheter users versus 39% among uncoated-catheter users, and only 114 of 224 randomized participants completed follow-up. Institutional and community care differed in monitoring and UTI ascertainment; follow-up lasted at most six months.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup>

## Guidelines and approvals

The AUA/SUFU 2021 adult neurogenic lower urinary tract dysfunction guideline cites this trial in the evidence discussion for intermittent catheterization and says hydrophilic catheters may be associated with lower UTI and urethral trauma rates, specifically in patients with spinal cord injury. Its statement 35 recommends intermittent rather than indwelling catheterization for bladder emptying <Rec>(Strong Recommendation; Evidence Level: Grade C)</Rec>; that statement compares drainage approaches, not catheter coatings.<sup>[[2]](#ref2)</sup>

The EAU 2026 urological infections guideline recommends hydrophilic-coated catheters to reduce catheter-associated UTI <Rec>(Strong)</Rec>, drawing on a meta-analysis of randomized catheter comparisons.<sup>[[3]](#ref3)</sup>
