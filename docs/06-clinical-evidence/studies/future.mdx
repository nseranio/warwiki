# FUTURE: Invasive Urodynamics Before Treatment for Refractory Overactive Bladder Symptoms

<TrialBottomLine id="future" />

**FUTURE** was a multicenter, open-label superiority randomized trial in women with refractory overactive bladder or urgency-predominant mixed urinary incontinence considering invasive treatment; adding invasive urodynamics to comprehensive clinical assessment did not improve final participant-reported success over assessment alone.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup>

<TrialQuickHit id="future" />

## Design

Participants had not responded to conservative management and at least two pharmacological treatments unless contraindicated. Exclusions included predominant stress incontinence, neurogenic bladder, bladder pain syndrome, recent urodynamics, previous botulinum toxin A or sacral neuromodulation for incontinence, and prolapse beyond the introitus. The trial ran in UK secondary and tertiary hospitals without masking participants or clinicians.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup>

Both groups had a history, examination, 3-day bladder diary, post-void residual scan, and optional noninvasive uroflowmetry. The urodynamics group also underwent cystometry and uroflowmetry, with optional pressure-flow studies. Treatment followed the assigned diagnosis.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup>

The primary endpoint was “very much improved” or “much improved” on the Patient Global Impression of Improvement (PGI-I) at final follow-up: 15 months, or 24 months for women whose treatment was delayed during COVID-19. The trial was powered to detect a 10-percentage-point superiority advantage. The primary analysis used the intention-to-treat population after postrandomization exclusions.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup>

## Results

Final PGI-I success did not differ after 1,103 women were randomized and four excluded: 117/496 (23.6%) with urodynamics versus 114/503 (22.7%) with assessment alone; adjusted odds ratio (OR) 1.12 (95% CI 0.73–1.74; P = 0.60). The analysis included 550 and 549 women, respectively. Per-protocol success was 113/454 (24.9%) versus 111/483 (23.0%); OR 1.22 (95% CI 0.78–1.91; P = 0.39).<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup>

Earlier PGI-I success at 3 months favored assessment alone: 34/417 (8.2%) versus 77/433 (17.8%); adjusted OR 0.28 (95% CI 0.16–0.51; P &lt; 0.0001). With the broader prespecified success definition, final responses were 217/496 (43.8%) versus 209/503 (41.6%); OR 1.14 (95% CI 0.79–1.65; P = 0.47). In a prespecified secondary analysis after botulinum toxin A, success two months after injection was 88/138 (63.8%) versus 99/165 (60.0%); OR 1.17 (95% CI 0.73–1.89; P = 0.52). Condition-specific symptom and quality-of-life scores improved in both groups without a consistent final between-group advantage.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup>

The within-trial, multiple-imputation economic analysis of 1,099 women estimated 24-month NHS costs of £3,907.33 with urodynamics versus £3,444.78 with assessment alone, a difference of £462.55 (95% CI £48.10–£877.01). Estimated quality-adjusted life-years (QALYs) were 1.315 versus 1.304, a difference of 0.011 (95% CI −0.044 to 0.065). The incremental cost-effectiveness ratio was £42,643 per QALY gained, with a 33.82% probability of cost-effectiveness at £20,000 per QALY; the complete-case sensitivity analysis gave 67.26%.<sup>[[2]](#ref2)</sup>

The report's primary lifetime economic model, which extrapolated beyond trial follow-up using external evidence, estimated £37,818 and 11.245 QALYs per participant with urodynamics versus £36,438 and 11.247 QALYs with assessment alone. Urodynamics was dominated in this model: £1,380 higher cost and 0.002 fewer QALYs, with a 23.4% probability of cost-effectiveness at £20,000 per QALY.<sup>[[2]](#ref2)</sup>

## Surgery and treatment delivery

Treatment was received by 467/550 (84.9%) versus 479/549 (87.2%). Among treated women, 277/467 (59.3%) versus 343/479 (71.6%) received intradetrusor botulinum toxin A; 16/467 (3.4%) versus 5/479 (1.0%) underwent stress incontinence surgery; and 11/467 (2.4%) versus 8/479 (1.7%) received sacral neuromodulation. Among 494 women with a urodynamic diagnosis, 287/494 (58.1%) had detrusor overactivity or associated incontinence, 65/494 (13.2%) urodynamic stress incontinence, 39/494 (7.9%) mixed incontinence, and 102/494 (20.6%) neither finding. An expert panel disagreed with 5/124 (4.0%) reviewed local diagnoses, which were changed.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup>

The assigned assessment was received by 491/550 (89.3%) in the urodynamics arm and 522/549 (95.1%) in the assessment-only arm. For women with detrusor overactivity, the treatment pathway allowed 100 units of botulinum toxin A or a sacral neuromodulation test after local discussion.<sup>[[2]](#ref2)</sup>

## Adverse events

Adverse events occurred in 113/550 (20.5%) versus 122/549 (22.2%). Urinary tract infection occurred in 39/550 (7.1%) versus 41/549 (7.5%); clean intermittent self-catheterization was required in 26/550 (4.7%) versus 32/549 (5.8%); and retention without catheterization occurred in 5/550 (0.9%) versus 11/549 (2.0%).<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup>

Nine of 1,099 participants had a serious adverse event: five deaths unrelated to trial participation, two instances of self-catheterization after botulinum toxin A, one hospitalization for possible pyelonephritis and urinary tract infection, and one anesthetic complication.<sup>[[2]](#ref2)</sup>

## Limitations

Treatment was open label, and the diagnostic strategy changed subsequent procedures and their timing. Most treated participants received botulinum toxin A, leaving small sacral neuromodulation groups. COVID-19 delays extended follow-up for some participants to 24 months; others ended at 15 months.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup>

The lifetime economic model assumed equal long-term treatment success and health-related quality of life across assessment arms and drew treatment outcomes from external cohorts; the authors identified potential bias in the cost assigned to “other care.”<sup>[[2]](#ref2)</sup>
