# Bø 1999: Pelvic Floor Exercises, Electrical Stimulation, Vaginal Cones, and No Treatment for Genuine Stress Incontinence

<TrialBottomLine id="bo-1999" />

**Bø 1999** was a multicenter, assessor-blinded randomized trial in 122 women with clinically and urodynamically confirmed genuine stress incontinence, comparing supervised pelvic floor muscle training (PFMT), vaginal electrical stimulation, weighted vaginal cones, and no active treatment; after six months, PFMT produced the greatest reduction in standardized pad-test leakage and patient-reported severity.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup>

<TrialQuickHit id="bo-1999" />

## Design

Five centers in southeast Norway enrolled women from a surgical waiting list and newspaper recruitment. Entry required stress leakage on a standardized pad test. Prior incontinence surgery, detrusor contractions during cystometry, abnormal emptying, active urinary infection, and concomitant treatment were exclusions. Randomization was stratified by baseline pad-test leakage with sealed envelopes. The main investigator and outcome-assessing physicians were blinded; participants and therapists were aware of treatment allocation.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup>

The primary outcome was change in leakage on a standardized pad test; women also rated incontinence severity on a five-category scale. For the pad test, the bladder was emptied by catheter and filled with 200 mL saline before 30 seconds of running in place and 30 seconds of jumping jacks. The main analysis included 107 treated women with final efficacy data; the intention-to-treat analysis assigned baseline values to women without final measurements. The statistical plan used a global four-group test and a significance threshold of P &lt; 0.05.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup>

## Results

Supervised PFMT produced the largest reduction in standardized pad-test leakage: −30.2 g (95% CI −43.3 to −16.9), versus −7.4 g (−20.9 to 6.1) with stimulation, −14.7 g (−27.6 to −1.8) with cones, and −12.7 g (−27.2 to 1.8) in controls (global P = 0.038). The reduction exceeded stimulation by 22.8 g (95% CI 3.8 to 41.8; P = 0.02). At treatment completion, 14/25 (56.0%) women assigned PFMT rated incontinence unproblematic, compared with 3/25 (12.0%), 2/27 (7.4%), and 1/30 (3.3%), respectively; change from baseline favored PFMT (P &lt; 0.001).<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup>

Objective cure, defined as &lt;2 g leaked on the standardized pad test, occurred in 11/25 (44.0%) with PFMT, 7/25 (28.0%) with stimulation, 4/27 (14.8%) with cones, and 2/30 (6.7%) controls (P = 0.02). Additional treatment was requested after the trial by 4/25 (16.0%), 19/25 (76.0%), 23/27 (85.2%), and 28/30 (93.3%), respectively (P &lt; 0.001). The secondary 24-hour home pad test showed no significant four-group difference: mean change was −6.6 g (95% CI −12.1 to −1.1) with PFMT, −0.5 g (−8.9 to 7.9) with stimulation, −22 g (−55.7 to 11.7) with cones, and −7.1 g (−20.2 to 6.0) in controls (global P = 0.684).<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup>

Pelvic floor muscle strength rose from 11.0 cm H2O (95% CI 7.7 to 14.3) to 19.2 (15.3 to 23.1) among 25 PFMT participants, from 14.8 (10.9 to 18.7) to 18.6 (13.3 to 23.9) among 25 stimulation participants, and from 11.8 (8.5 to 15.1) to 15.4 (11.1 to 19.7) among 27 cone participants; the PFMT increase exceeded those in the other active groups (P = 0.03).<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup>

## Surgery and treatment delivery

PFMT comprised 8–12 near-maximal contractions three times daily, held for 6–8 seconds with three or four fast contractions added, with a weekly 45-minute therapist-led class and a home diary. Vaginal stimulation used the MS 106 Twin at 50 Hz, 0.2 ms pulse width, and 0–120 mA for 30 minutes daily, with intensity and duty cycle adjusted to elicit a contraction. Mabella cones progressed through 20, 40, and 70 g weights for 20 minutes daily. Controls could use a continence guard; 14/30 used it, of whom four felt completely dry while wearing it and five felt somewhat better. All active-treatment groups had monthly therapist contact; only the PFMT group also attended weekly classes. Mean adherence was 93% (SE 1.5%) with PFMT, 75% (SE 2.8%) with stimulation, and 78% (SE 4.4%) with cones.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup>

## Adverse events

No adverse effects were reported with PFMT. Two women reported smarting with stimulation (one tenderness and bleeding, one discomfort); the cone group reported one case of abdominal pain, two of vaginitis, and one of bleeding. Eight women reported motivation or device-use problems with stimulation, and 14 reported such problems with cones. Pain or bleeding caused withdrawal in 3/32 randomized to stimulation and bleeding in 1/29 randomized to cones. Other withdrawals included difficulty with motivation, travel, unrelated circumstances, and use of other treatments.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup>

## Limitations

Weekly supervised classes gave the PFMT group more contact than the other groups. The no-treatment group had no monthly therapist visits, and the active comparators were not sham treatments. The main efficacy analysis excluded women without final measurements; the intention-to-treat sensitivity analysis carried their baseline values forward.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup>

## Guidelines and approvals

The EAU 2026 guideline cites this trial in its comparison of PFMT with vaginal electrical stimulation and cones. It recommends supervised, intensive PFMT for at least three months as first-line treatment for women with stress or mixed urinary incontinence <Rec>(Strong)</Rec>, and says to consider electrical stimulation for stress incontinence or to teach a pelvic floor contraction <Rec>(Weak)</Rec>.<sup>[[2]](#ref2)</sup>
