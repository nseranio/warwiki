# Darifenacin pivotal trial: Once-Daily Darifenacin for Overactive Bladder

<TrialBottomLine id="darifenacin-pivotal" />

**The darifenacin pivotal trial** was a multicenter, double-blind randomized comparison of once-daily darifenacin and placebo in 561 patients with overactive bladder; at 12 weeks, both formally tested doses reduced median weekly incontinence episodes more than placebo.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup>

<TrialQuickHit id="darifenacin-pivotal" />

## Design

Participants had symptoms for more than 6 months, 5–50 urge-incontinence episodes per week, at least 8 voids per day, and urgency at least once per day; some had used antimuscarinics, and previous nonresponse was allowed. After washout and a 2-week placebo run-in, they received controlled-release darifenacin 3.75 mg, 7.5 mg or 15 mg, or matching placebo; 53, 229, 115 and 164 patients were randomized to these arms, respectively. The 3.75 mg group served as a null-dose arm without formal sample sizing or statistical analysis. Electronic diaries recorded incontinence episodes, voiding frequency and volume, urgency, clothing or pad changes, and nocturnal awakenings before visits at weeks 2, 6 and 12. The primary endpoint was change in weekly incontinence episodes at week 12 in the full analysis set: randomized patients who took a dose and had baseline and post-baseline efficacy data. The 15 mg comparison with placebo was tested first at two-sided α = 0.05, followed by 7.5 mg if significant.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup>

## Results

Both prespecified primary comparisons were positive: at week 12, median weekly incontinence episodes fell by 9.0 (67.7%) with 7.5 mg and 10.4 (72.8%) with 15 mg, versus 7.6 (55.9%) with placebo. Week-12 medians were 5.0, 4.7 and 8.0 episodes per week, respectively. Median treatment differences versus placebo were −1.5 episodes/week (95% CI −3.0 to −0.4; P = 0.010) and −2.1 (95% CI −3.5 to −0.3; P = 0.017), respectively.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup>

Secondary diary measures also improved at week 12. Median daily micturitions fell by 1.6 with 7.5 mg and 1.7 with 15 mg versus 0.8 with placebo (both P &lt; 0.001); urgency episodes fell by 2.0 versus 2.0 versus 0.9 per day (P = 0.001 and 0.005), and urgency severity fell by 9.1 versus 7.9 versus 4.6 mm on a 100 mm scale (P = 0.001 and 0.002). Median voided volume rose by 14.9 versus 30.9 versus 7.6 mL (P = 0.040 and P &lt; 0.001). Weekly incontinence episodes requiring a pad or clothing change fell by 4.0 versus 4.7 versus 2.0 (P &lt; 0.001 and P = 0.002). Nocturnal awakenings due to overactive bladder did not differ significantly.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup>

## Treatment delivery

The once-daily controlled-release regimens were completed by 219/229 (7.5 mg), 106/115 (15 mg), 49/53 (3.75 mg), and 152/164 (placebo) patients. Tablet-count compliance exceeded 86% in all but two patients; 462/561 had 100% compliance.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup>

## Adverse events

Dry mouth and constipation were generally mild to moderate. Treatment-related dry mouth occurred in 43/229 (18.8%) with 7.5 mg, 36/115 (31.3%) with 15 mg, and 14/164 (8.5%) with placebo; constipation occurred in 33/229 (14.4%), 16/115 (13.9%), and 11/164 (6.7%), respectively. Constipation caused discontinuation in 2/229, 1/115, and 1/164, respectively; no patient withdrew because of dry mouth. Serious adverse events of any cause occurred in 6/229, 0/115, and 1/164, respectively. One 7.5 mg recipient had second-degree atrioventricular block, which investigators deemed unlikely related to treatment after reviewing prior history. No blurred vision was reported. The authors described CNS and cardiac safety profiles as comparable to placebo.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup>

## Limitations

Follow-up ended at 12 weeks. The 3.75 mg group was not formally sized or analyzed.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup>
