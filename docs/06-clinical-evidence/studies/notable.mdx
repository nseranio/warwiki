# NOTABLe: Percutaneous Tibial Nerve Stimulation Versus Sham for Fecal Incontinence in Women

<TrialBottomLine id="notable" />

**NOTABLe** was a multicenter, single-masked superiority trial of percutaneous tibial nerve stimulation (PTNS) versus sham stimulation in 166 women with moderate-to-severe fecal incontinence despite supervised pelvic muscle training and constipating medication; PTNS did not produce a significantly greater improvement in St. Mark's severity score after 12 weekly sessions.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup>

<TrialQuickHit id="notable" />

## Design

The Pelvic Floor Disorders Network randomized participants at nine US sites after a four-week education and bowel-diary run-in. Eligibility required symptoms for at least three months and a St. Mark's score of at least 12 after the run-in. Predominant uncontrolled diarrhea or severe constipation, unrepaired rectovaginal fistula or fourth-degree obstetric laceration, rectal prolapse, and prior colon resection were exclusions. Allocation was 2:1, stratified by site and diary type. Participants were masked; interventionists delivered the assigned procedure.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup>

The primary endpoint was adjusted change in the seven-item St. Mark's score after 12 sessions. Scores range from 0 to 24, with higher scores indicating worse symptoms; the cited within-patient minimal important difference was 3–5 points. The prespecified superiority test used a two-sided 0.05 threshold. Bowel-diary episode counts, responder rates, and quality-of-life measures were secondary outcomes without multiplicity adjustment.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup>

## Results

Adjusted mean St. Mark's scores fell by 5.3 points with PTNS and 3.9 points with sham; the adjusted mean difference in change was −1.3 points (95% CI −2.8 to 0.2; P = 0.09). The between-group primary endpoint did not meet the superiority threshold despite improvement in each arm. Weekly fecal incontinence episodes fell by 2.1 and 1.9, respectively (adjusted mean difference −0.26 episodes/week, 95% CI −1.85 to 1.33; P = 0.75). Among participants with complete diaries, 51/97 (52.6%) versus 19/48 (39.6%) had at least 50% fewer weekly episodes (unadjusted OR 1.7, 95% CI 0.81–3.56; P = 0.16).<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup>

A planned secondary analysis evaluated treatment interactions across St. Mark's response thresholds, patient global impression, and diary-defined response. Among 160 women with at least one evaluable definition, no baseline characteristic consistently identified greater benefit from PTNS than sham. Only 41/142 (29%) with complete data met all three types of success criterion.<sup>[[2]](#ref2)</sup>

## Surgery and treatment delivery

PTNS was delivered to one lower extremity through a needle electrode, with current titrated to toe twitch or sensation. The sham used a retractable placebo needle and surface electrodes at the fifth metatarsal connected to a transcutaneous stimulator to create sensation. Both groups attended 12 weekly, 30-minute sessions. Completion of at least 10 sessions within 14 weeks occurred in 99/111 (89.2%) assigned PTNS and 51/55 (92.7%) assigned sham. A nine-month maintenance phase for responders was planned, with discontinuation specified if the primary comparison failed superiority.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup>

## Adverse events

Serious adverse events occurred in 4/111 (4%) PTNS participants and 2/55 (4%) sham participants; none were treatment related. Paresthesia was reported in 11/111 (10%) and 2/55 (4%). Needle-site bleeding (8%) and pain (2%) were reported only with PTNS.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup>

## Limitations

The trial enrolled women only and assessed the specified weekly protocol after 12 sessions. No untreated group was included. Anorectal imaging and functional testing were not used to characterize sphincter defects or physiology. The secondary interaction models were exploratory, unadjusted for multiple testing, and lacked power for subgroup comparisons.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[2]](#ref2)</sup>

## In conversation

CONFIDeNT also compared PTNS with sham after 12 treatments but used a diary-defined reduction in weekly episodes as its primary endpoint and enrolled adults of both sexes. NOTABLe used change in St. Mark's severity score as its primary endpoint and enrolled women.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup>
