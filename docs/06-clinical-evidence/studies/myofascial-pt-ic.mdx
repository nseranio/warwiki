# ICCRN Myofascial PT: Myofascial Physical Therapy for Women With IC/BPS and Pelvic Floor Tenderness

<TrialBottomLine id="myofascial-pt-ic" />

**ICCRN Myofascial PT** was a multicenter, single-blind randomized trial in women with IC/BPS and pelvic floor tenderness that compared targeted myofascial physical therapy with global therapeutic massage; global symptom response was higher with targeted therapy at 12 weeks.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup>

<TrialQuickHit id="myofascial-pt-ic" />

## Design

At 11 North American centers, 81 women were randomized: 39 to myofascial physical therapy and 42 to massage. Eligibility required bladder pain, urgency and frequency each rated at least 3/10, symptoms lasting 3 months to 3 years, and pelvic floor tenderness on vaginal examination confirmed by a study physical therapist. Participants had previously received at least one standard IC/BPS treatment and had not received pelvic floor myofascial therapy. Participants were not told which treatment they received; physician examiners and research nurses assessing outcomes were masked.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup>

The primary endpoint was moderate or marked improvement on the seven-category Global Response Assessment at 12 weeks or study withdrawal. A participant who withdrew without a primary-outcome assessment counted as a nonresponder. The center-adjusted comparison used an exact conditional Mantel–Haenszel test. Pain, urgency, frequency and the O’Leary–Sant symptom and problem indexes were secondary measures; their changes were analyzed in participants with complete follow-up data.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup>

## Results

At 12 weeks, 23/39 women (59.0%) assigned to myofascial therapy and 11/42 (26.2%) assigned to massage reported moderate or marked improvement: a 32.8-percentage-point risk difference calculated from the reported arm rates (P = 0.0012). The prespecified primary comparison was met.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup>

Mean changes in pain ratings were −2.2 versus −1.5 (P = 0.27), urgency −2.1 versus −1.4 (P = 0.16), and frequency −2.2 versus −1.3 (P = 0.17), respectively. IC Symptom Index changes were −3.2 versus −2.2 (P = 0.31), and IC Problem Index changes were −3.6 versus −2.4 (P = 0.09). None of these secondary comparisons differed significantly between groups.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup>

## Surgery and treatment delivery

Study physical therapists were centrally trained and certified in both protocols. Myofascial treatment involved internal and external manipulation of muscles and connective tissue in the pelvic floor, hip girdle and abdomen. The control was full-body Western massage. Each arm was scheduled for up to ten 60-minute sessions over 12 weeks, without other changes to urologic care during treatment.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup>

Thirty-eight of 39 women assigned to myofascial therapy and 40 of 42 assigned to massage completed the treatment phase. Among completers, 21/38 and 15/40 received all ten sessions. Enrollment ended at 81 of a planned 88 because of slow accrual and limited funding. During the next 3 months, 4/30 myofascial-therapy and 8/28 massage patients with partial follow-up data obtained additional myofascial therapy.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup>

## Adverse events

At least one adverse event occurred in 25/39 myofascial-therapy patients (64.1%) and 25/42 massage patients (59.5%; P = 0.73). Across both arms, 12/81 (14.8%) had a severe adverse event. Pain, chiefly bladder or pelvic pain, was the most common event, reported by 14% of participants. No serious adverse events were reported.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup>

## Limitations

Enrollment stopped short of the planned 88 participants, and treatment was delivered by centrally trained therapists at academic centers. The trial enrolled women with pelvic floor tenderness and symptoms of no more than 3 years' duration. Only 23/39 myofascial-therapy patients and 11/42 massage patients completed the final Global Response Assessment after the post-treatment follow-up period; additional treatment and attrition precluded a reliable durability estimate.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup>

## Guidelines and approvals

The AUA IC/BPS guideline, amended in 2022, cites this trial in support of Statement 12: offer appropriate manual physical therapy to patients with pelvic floor tenderness when appropriately trained clinicians are available, and avoid pelvic floor strengthening exercises such as Kegels <Rec>(Standard (Evidence Strength: Grade A))</Rec>.<sup>[[2]](#ref2)</sup>
