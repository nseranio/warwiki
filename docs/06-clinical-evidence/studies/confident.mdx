# CONFIDeNT: Percutaneous Tibial Nerve Stimulation Versus Sham for Faecal Incontinence

<TrialBottomLine id="confident" />

**CONFIDeNT** was a multicenter, masked randomized trial in adults with faecal incontinence despite conservative treatment that compared percutaneous tibial nerve stimulation (PTNS) with sham electrical stimulation; PTNS did not significantly increase the proportion with at least 50% fewer weekly incontinence episodes after the treatment course.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup>

<TrialQuickHit id="confident" />

## Design

The trial randomized 227 adults in 17 UK specialist hospital units. Randomization was stratified by sex and, for women, center. Patients and outcome assessors were masked through the 14-week trial; staff delivering treatment were unmasked. The primary endpoint was at least a 50% reduction in weekly faecal incontinence episodes recorded in bowel diaries after 12 sessions. The analysis used intention to treat with multiple imputation for missing data.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup>

## Results

Among patients with complete bowel diaries, 39/103 (38%) assigned PTNS and 32/102 (31%) assigned sham met the primary response definition (adjusted OR 1.28, 95% CI 0.72–2.28; P = 0.396).<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup> Two weeks after the 12-week course, mean weekly episode counts were 6.4 with PTNS (n=105) and 9.1 with sham (n=104); the adjusted intention-to-treat difference in change was −2.262 episodes per week (95% CI −4.185 to −0.339; P = 0.021).<sup>[[2]](#ref2)</sup> The adjusted difference for urge episodes was −1.456 per week (95% CI −2.693 to −0.219; P = 0.021), while passive episodes did not differ (P = 0.228).<sup>[[2]](#ref2)</sup> St Mark's Continence Score did not differ (adjusted difference −0.047, 95% CI −1.033 to 0.939; P = 0.925), nor did quality-of-life measures.<sup>[[2]](#ref2)</sup> The patient-centred outcomes score favored PTNS (mean 7.8, n=87, versus 8.4, n=85; adjusted difference −0.545, 95% CI −1.081 to −0.008; P = 0.047).<sup>[[2]](#ref2)</sup>

A post hoc analysis of 205 participants found that obstructive-defecation symptoms predicted a poorer PTNS response and a greater sham response. After excluding participants with those symptoms, the exploratory responder comparison in 112 participants was 48.9% with PTNS versus 18.2% with sham (adjusted OR 4.71, 95% CI 1.71–12.93; P = 0.003).<sup>[[3]](#ref3)</sup>

## Surgery and treatment delivery

Specialist staff delivered Urgent PC stimulation for 30 minutes weekly over 12 treatments, placing a 34-gauge needle about 2 cm deep, 5 cm above the medial malleolus and 2 cm behind the tibia. The sham inserted the needle 2 mm at the same site and delivered foot stimulation at 10 Hz with a 200-microsecond pulse width.<sup>[[2]](#ref2)</sup> Treatment was completed by 108/115 PTNS and 107/112 sham participants; 197/227 met the per-protocol requirement of at least 10 treatments within 13 weeks, spaced 5–10 days apart.<sup>[[2]](#ref2)</sup> Seven of 115 PTNS patients and five of 112 sham patients withdrew from treatment or the trial; five of these 12 withdrawals were due to weekly visit commitments.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[2]](#ref2)</sup>

## Adverse events

Seven mild treatment-related events were reported in each arm, including needle-site pain in four PTNS and three sham patients. No serious treatment-related adverse events were reported. One patient in each arm withdrew after an adverse event unrelated to treatment.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup> Four serious adverse events occurred (2 events/115 PTNS participants; 2 events/112 sham participants); all were unrelated to treatment and resolved.<sup>[[2]](#ref2)</sup>

## Limitations

The randomized assessment ended after the 12-session course, leaving durability and maintenance treatment unmeasured. The responder proportions use complete diaries, while the adjusted comparison imputes missing data. The later obstructive-defecation finding arose from a post hoc subgroup analysis.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[3]](#ref3)</sup>
