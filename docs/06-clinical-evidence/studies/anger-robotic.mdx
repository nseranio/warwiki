# ACCESS: Robotic Compared With Laparoscopic Sacrocolpopexy

<TrialBottomLine id="anger-robotic" />

**ACCESS** was a two-center randomized trial of robotic versus conventional laparoscopic sacrocolpopexy in 78 women with symptomatic stage II or greater prolapse and substantial apical descent. Its primary cost comparison found higher hospital costs with robotics through six weeks; prolapse and recovery measures were secondary outcomes.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup>

<TrialQuickHit id="anger-robotic" />

## Design

Participants had an indication for sacrocolpopexy and apical descent at least halfway down the vaginal canal. Randomization occurred on the day of surgery at UCLA/Cedars-Sinai or Loyola. The five participating surgeons had each performed at least 10 operations by both routes. Both arms used two separate pieces of synthetic mesh and Gore-Tex sutures; the surgeon chose the mesh brand and whether to close peritoneum over it. Supracervical hysterectomy, posterior repair, and retropubic synthetic midurethral slings were allowed.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup>

The prespecified primary comparison was provider-side hospital and physician costs for the operation and any rehospitalization within six weeks, including allocated robot purchase and maintenance. Charges were converted with facility-specific cost-to-charge ratios. The trial was powered to detect a $2,500 cost difference with a two-sided 0.05 threshold; pain, POP-Q, symptom bother, quality of life, and adverse events were secondary outcomes.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup>

## Results

Mean hospital cost through six weeks was $20,898 after robotic surgery and $12,170 after conventional laparoscopy, a $8,728 difference (P &lt; 0.001). Initial day-of-surgery hospital cost was $19,616 versus $11,573, a $8,043 difference (P &lt; 0.001). Excluding robot purchase and maintenance reduced the six-week comparison to $13,867 versus $12,170, a $1,697 difference (P = 0.060).<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup>

At one week, mean pain with normal activities was 3.5 (SD 2.1) after robotic surgery and 2.6 (SD 2.2) after laparoscopy, a 0.9-point difference (P = 0.044); the difference diminished by two weeks. At six months, mean POP-Q point C was −7.97 cm versus −7.30 cm (treatment-effect P = 0.502). The prespecified cost sample was too small to establish equivalence of prolapse outcomes.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup>

The planned one-year analysis assessed 33/40 robotic participants (83%) and 33/38 laparoscopic participants (87%), or 66/78 (85%) overall. Patient-rated prolapse symptom improvement was reported by 33/33 (100%) versus 32/33 (97%) assessed women (P = 0.999). Mean POP-Q point C was −7.6 cm versus −8.7 cm (P = 0.424). Between-arm symptom and quality-of-life measures did not differ significantly in this underpowered comparison.<sup>[[2]](#ref2)</sup>

## Surgery and treatment delivery

The mesh pieces were attached to the anterior and posterior vagina and the anterior longitudinal ligament. In the primary report, 45/78 women (58%) underwent concomitant hysterectomy, 47/78 (60%) a retropubic midurethral sling, and 5/78 (6%) an anterior or posterior repair. Mean sacrocolpopexy procedure time was 202.8 minutes with robotics and 178.4 minutes with laparoscopy, a 24.4-minute difference (P = 0.030); total surgery time did not differ significantly.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[2]](#ref2)</sup>

## Adverse events

The primary report counted six adverse events after robotic surgery (one Dindo I, two II, two III, one IV) and 10 after laparoscopy (four II, six III); these are event counts, not numbers of affected patients. Each arm had an iliac venotomy repaired without changing access and a small-bowel obstruction. The robotic arm had a pulmonary embolism and an obstruction requiring exploration; the laparoscopic arm had an intraoperative cystotomy and a port-site hernia requiring reoperation. Three laparoscopic cases had vaginal granulation tissue or suture exposure.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup>

At one year, no sacrocolpopexy mesh complication or mesh-exposure reoperation was reported among the 33 assessed women in each arm. One asymptomatic Gore-Tex suture erosion was reported without arm assignment. The follow-up report described three bowel-related reoperations within the first six to eight weeks, without assigning them by arm.<sup>[[2]](#ref2)</sup>

## Limitations

The two-center cost analysis depended on each hospital's robot purchase price, maintenance expense, utilization, and cost-to-charge ratios. Surgeons were experienced in both routes, and all cases involved academic trainees. Clinical outcomes were secondary, with 66 of 78 participants assessed at one year; the study was not powered to resolve small differences in prolapse recurrence or uncommon mesh complications.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[2]](#ref2)</sup>

## In conversation

<InConversation id="anger-robotic" />

Paraiso and colleagues also randomized robotic versus conventional laparoscopic sacrocolpopexy and found greater robotic cost and operating time. Their trial enrolled women with posthysterectomy vault prolapse and made total operative time, rather than cost, its primary endpoint.<sup>[[3]](#ref3)</sup>
