# TTrials: Testosterone Treatment in Older Men With Low Testosterone Levels

<TrialBottomLine id="ttrials" />

**TTrials** was a double-blind randomized program in 790 symptomatic men aged at least 65 years with low testosterone, comparing titrated testosterone gel with placebo for one year; sexual activity improved, while the prespecified walking and vitality outcomes did not improve significantly in their qualifying cohorts.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup>

<TrialQuickHit id="ttrials" />

## Design

Men qualified for at least one of the Sexual Function, Physical Function, and Vitality Trials, and could enter more than one. The Sexual Function Trial required reduced libido, a low Derogatis sexual-desire score, and a partner willing to have intercourse at least twice monthly. Men with known hypogonadal causes, prior prostate cancer, high predicted prostate-cancer risk, an International Prostate Symptom Score (IPSS) above 19, or recent major cardiovascular instability were excluded. Randomization balanced trial participation, site, testosterone level, age, antidepressant use, and phosphodiesterase-5 inhibitor use.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup>

The sexual trial's primary endpoint was change in Psychosexual Daily Questionnaire sexual-activity score (PDQ-Q4). The physical trial tested a gain of at least 50 m on the six-minute walk; the vitality trial tested a gain of at least four FACIT-Fatigue points. Efficacy was assessed every three months. The reported treatment effects used longitudinal models averaging the postbaseline visits; the tabulated arm values below are the month-12 observations. The three primary outcomes were analyzed as independent trials without a multiplicity adjustment between them.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup>

## Results

In the Sexual Function Trial, mean PDQ-Q4 change at month 12 was +0.2 points with testosterone and −0.1 with placebo. The adjusted mean difference in change across visits was 0.58 points (95% CI 0.38–0.78; P &lt; 0.001), meeting the primary hypothesis. Sexual desire also improved (adjusted mean difference 2.93 points, 95% CI 2.13–3.74), as did IIEF erectile-function score (2.64 points, 95% CI 1.68–3.61). These were secondary outcomes.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup>

In the Physical Function Trial, 35/172 (20.3%) testosterone recipients and 20/165 (12.1%) placebo recipients gained at least 50 m at month 12; the longitudinal odds ratio was 1.42 (95% CI 0.83–2.45; P = 0.20). In the Vitality Trial, 147/203 (72.4%) versus 120/191 (62.8%) improved at least four FACIT-Fatigue points; the longitudinal odds ratio was 1.23 (95% CI 0.83–1.84; P = 0.30). Neither primary endpoint met its significance threshold. In the secondary analysis of all participants, the month-12 walking threshold was reached by 71/346 (20.5%) versus 41/326 (12.6%; longitudinal OR 1.76, 95% CI 1.21–2.57; P = 0.003).<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup>

A later analysis of the same Sexual Function Trial cohort found significant improvement on 10 of 12 sexual-activity measures versus placebo. Changes in total and free testosterone and estradiol tracked with sexual activity and desire, without an identified response threshold; those hormone-change associations were secondary analyses.<sup>[[2]](#ref2)</sup>

## Surgery and treatment delivery

The active treatment was AndroGel 1%, started at 5 g daily. Central testosterone assays at months 1, 2, 3, 6, and 9 guided dose changes toward the young-adult normal range; matched placebo dose changes maintained blinding. Of 790 enrolled men, 705 (89.2%) completed treatment through month 12. PSA and digital rectal examination were scheduled at months 3 and 12, with another PSA check at month 18; a confirmed PSA increase of at least 1.0 ng/mL or a nodule prompted urology referral.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup>

## Adverse events

During treatment, PSA increased at least 1.0 ng/mL in 23/394 (5.8%) testosterone recipients and 8/394 (2.0%) placebo recipients. Hemoglobin reached at least 17.5 g/dL in 7/394 (1.8%) versus 0/394 (0%). IPSS exceeded 19 in 27/394 (6.9%) versus 26/394 (6.6%). Adjudicated major cardiovascular events occurred in 7/394 (1.8%) in each arm. The report recorded one prostate-cancer diagnosis during treatment in the testosterone arm and none in the placebo arm.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup>

## Limitations

The exclusion of men with high prostate-cancer risk or substantial baseline urinary symptoms narrows the safety population. The trial's size and one-year treatment period were insufficient to estimate cardiovascular or prostate-cancer risk reliably. Sexual activity, desire, and erectile function were measured on different instruments.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup>

## Guidelines and approvals

The AUA's 2018 testosterone-deficiency guideline cites the Snyder trial in its evidence discussion and advises telling testosterone-deficient patients that treatment may improve erectile function and low sex drive <Rec>(Moderate Recommendation; Evidence Level: Grade B)</Rec>.<sup>[[3]](#ref3)</sup> The EAU's March 2026 sexual and reproductive health guideline discusses TTrials' sexual-function results and recommends testosterone therapy as first-line treatment for hypogonadal patients with mild erectile dysfunction <Rec>(Strong)</Rec>.<sup>[[4]](#ref4)</sup>
