# St Mark's biofeedback trial: Advice, sphincter exercises, and biofeedback for fecal incontinence

<TrialBottomLine id="st-marks-biofeedback" />

**The St Mark's biofeedback trial** randomized 171 patients with fecal incontinence to standard advice, advice plus sphincter exercise instruction, hospital-based pressure biofeedback, or hospital biofeedback plus a home electromyogram device. On the primary global rating of worse, same, improved, or cured, groups did not differ (P=0.54); 53% of 49 assigned hospital pressure biofeedback and 54% of 37 assigned advice reported improvement. The other primary rating differed (P=0.048) because the home-device group rated less improvement than the other groups.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup>

<TrialQuickHit id="st-marks-biofeedback" />

## Design

The four groups separated the effects of advice, explicit sphincter exercise instruction, instrumented hospital training, and additional home device use. The two primary outcomes were patients' global rating of worse, same, improved, or cured and change in bowel control on a −5 to +5 scale; noncompleters were counted as unchanged in intention-to-treat analyses. Secondary outcomes included a bowel diary, symptom and continence scores, quality-of-life and psychological scales, and anal manometry. Patients and therapists were unblinded; the repeat manometry physiologist was blinded.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup>

## Results

The second primary rating showed median changes of +3 with advice, +2 with exercise instruction, +3 with hospital pressure feedback, and +0.5 with hospital plus home feedback (P=0.048 across groups); the home-device arm had the poorer outcome. In pooled paired analyses, median diary incontinence episodes fell from 2 to 0 per week (P&lt;0.001; 39% of post-treatment diaries were missing), and the median continence score fell from 15 to 13 on a scale whose worst score is 20 (P≤0.001). Post-treatment median continence scores were 13, 11, 13, and 14, respectively (P=0.54 across groups). Disease-specific quality of life, selected SF-36 domains, psychological scores, and resting and squeeze pressures also improved.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup>

At 1 year, 106 of 160 eligible patients returned questionnaires (66%); 74% of respondents rated their bowel control improved, with no detected between-group difference.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup>

## Surgery and treatment delivery

All groups received nurse-led assessment, education, lifestyle and bowel advice, emotional support, and urge-resistance training during up to nine 40–60-minute sessions over 3–6 months; the median was five sessions. The exercise groups were instructed to perform at least 50 maximal sustained and 50 fast-twitch sphincter contractions daily. Hospital biofeedback displayed external sphincter contraction pressure; the additional home electromyogram device used an intra-anal electrode for 20 minutes daily. In all, 140 of 171 patients (82%) completed the protocol and end-of-treatment questionnaires. Nine patients underwent surgery for persistent fecal incontinence before the 1-year follow-up.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup>

## Limitations

The comparison used an active advice control and had no sham-feedback arm.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup>

## Guidelines and approvals

The American Society of Colon and Rectal Surgeons' 2023 fecal incontinence guideline cites this trial among the randomized comparisons underlying its statement that biofeedback may be considered for patients with fecal incontinence <Rec>(Conditional; Low)</Rec>.<sup>[[2]](#ref2)</sup>
