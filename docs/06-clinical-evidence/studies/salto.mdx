# SALTO: Laparoscopic versus Open Abdominal Sacrocolpopexy for Vaginal Vault Prolapse

<TrialBottomLine id="salto" />

**SALTO** was a multicenter, unblinded randomized superiority trial of laparoscopic versus open abdominal sacrocolpopexy in women with symptomatic post-hysterectomy vault prolapse; the prespecified 12-month UDI genital-prolapse domain did not favor laparoscopy (median 0.0 in each group; P = 0.93), while blood loss and hospital stay were lower after laparoscopy.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup>

<TrialQuickHit id="salto" />

## Design

Four teaching and two university hospitals in the Netherlands enrolled women who chose surgery for vaginal vault prolapse, with or without cystocele or rectocele. Prior vault-prolapse repair and medical contraindications to surgery excluded participation. Allocation was equal by sealed envelope, and participating gynecologists had performed at least 50 sacrocolpopexies before the trial. Participants and independent postoperative examiners were unblinded.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup>

The primary endpoint was the UDI genital-prolapse domain at 12 months. The sample size targeted a 10-point between-group difference with 80% power and two-sided alpha 0.05. The subsequently added apical composite required no prolapse beyond the hymen, no bothersome bulge, and no repeat prolapse surgery or pessary use; it was not prespecified.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup>

## Results

At 12 months, the primary UDI genital-prolapse score was 0.0 (IQR 0–0) in both groups, among 31 laparoscopic and 29 open respondents (P = 0.93). The trial's superiority hypothesis was not met. The added composite succeeded in 31/37 (83.8%) and 33/37 (89.2%), respectively. Patient-reported improvement of “much” or “very much” better occurred in 22/31 (71%) and 20/27 (74%; P = 0.563).<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup>

At a median 109 months (9.1 years), 22 laparoscopic and 19 open participants joined the observational follow-up. Median UDI genital-prolapse scores were 0.0 (IQR 0–17; n=20) and 0.0 (IQR 0–0; n=19; P = 0.175). Apical composite success was 11/14 (78.6%) versus 11/13 (84.6%; P = 0.686); apical surgical failure, defined as POP-Q stage 2 or higher or reintervention, was 2/16 (12.5%) versus 0/13 (0%; P = 0.186). Subsequent surgery occurred in 5/22 (22.7%) and 5/19 (26.3%; P = 0.729).<sup>[[2]](#ref2)</sup>

## Surgery and treatment delivery

Both approaches used anterior and posterior strips of type 1 polypropylene mesh secured to the vaginal walls with nonabsorbable multifilament sutures and to the sacral promontory, followed by reperitonealization. Laparoscopy used four ports and staple fixation at the promontory; the open operation generally used a Pfannenstiel incision, with promontory staples or nonabsorbable sutures at the surgeon's discretion. Concomitant vaginal prolapse repair or a midurethral sling could be added. One woman assigned to laparoscopy chose to continue pessary treatment; one assigned to open surgery underwent sacrospinous fixation.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup>

Median blood loss was 86 mL (IQR 10–100) after laparoscopy versus 200 mL (IQR 100–300) after laparotomy (P &lt; 0.001). Median admission was 2 days (IQR 2–3) versus 4 days (IQR 3–5; P &lt; 0.001). Median operative time was 125 versus 115 minutes (P = 0.31). By 12 months, surgery for prolapse or incontinence had occurred in 7/36 versus 4/37 (10.8%); prolapse reinterventions concerned the posterior compartment.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup>

## Adverse events

Two laparoscopic operations converted to open surgery, one after bladder injury and one after promontory hemorrhage; the open arm had no intraoperative injuries recorded in the trial table. During admission, complications occurred in 2/36 (5.6%) after laparoscopy and 7/37 (18.9%) after open surgery. The open-arm events included a fatal bowel perforation, two wound dehiscences requiring repair, pulmonary embolism, and three episodes of ileus. No vaginal mesh or suture exposure was observed at the 12-month examination.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup>

Among participants assessed for long-term complications, mesh exposure had been reported by 2/16 (12.5%) after laparoscopy and 1/13 (7.7%) after open surgery; one infected exposure in each arm required mesh removal. Among participants assessed for long-term complications, suture exposure had been reported in 1/16 (6.3%) after laparoscopy and 2/13 (15.4%) after open surgery. The open-arm mesh removal involved a vaginal-vault fistula and ileal resection; the laparoscopic-arm removal was robot assisted.<sup>[[2]](#ref2)</sup>

## Limitations

The primary study was powered for a 10-point UDI difference, and its nonsignificant comparison cannot establish equivalence of the approaches. Fourteen 12-month questionnaires were missing. Patients and examiners could identify the incision, and postoperative length of stay may also reflect local discharge practice and complications. The apical composite was added after the trial began.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup>

Long-term participation was 22/36 (61.1%) eligible laparoscopic and 19/35 (54.3%) eligible open patients; only 16 and 13 underwent pelvic examination. The follow-up required new consent and was observational, with unblinded examiners.<sup>[[2]](#ref2)</sup>
