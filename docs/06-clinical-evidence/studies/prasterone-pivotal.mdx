# ERC-238: Intravaginal Prasterone for Menopausal Dyspareunia

<TrialBottomLine id="prasterone-pivotal" />

**ERC-238** was a double-blind phase 3 randomized trial of nightly intravaginal prasterone 6.5 mg versus a matching placebo insert in postmenopausal women with moderate-to-severe dyspareunia; at 12 weeks, prasterone met all four coprimary endpoints covering dyspareunia, vaginal cell maturation, and pH.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup>

<TrialQuickHit id="prasterone-pivotal" />

## Design

The trial randomized 558 women at US and Canadian sites in a 2:1 ratio. Entry required age 40–80 years, dyspareunia as the woman's most bothersome vulvovaginal atrophy symptom, vaginal pH above 5, and no more than 5% superficial cells at both screening and baseline. Women could be sexually active at enrollment or have reduced sexual activity because of pain or dryness. Prior cancer other than nonmelanoma skin cancer, abnormal screening endometrial histology, and recent systemic or vaginal hormone therapy were exclusion criteria.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup>

The prespecified efficacy population contained 325 prasterone and 157 placebo recipients who received treatment and still met entry criteria at baseline. Cytology was read by a blinded central cytopathologist from a lateral vaginal wall smear; pH was measured on the opposite wall. Women scored symptoms from 0 (none) to 3 (severe). The analysis used baseline-adjusted ANCOVA with last observation carried forward for missing efficacy measurements. Superiority required two-sided P &lt; 0.05 for **each** coprimary endpoint; vaginal dryness was tested after dyspareunia in a prespecified sequence.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup>

## Results

At 12 weeks, mean dyspareunia severity fell from 2.54 to 1.13 with prasterone and from 2.56 to 1.50 with placebo, an adjusted between-group difference of −0.36 score units (P = 0.0002). The corresponding mean parabasal-cell proportions were 12.7% and 39.7% (adjusted difference −27.7 percentage points; P &lt; 0.0001), superficial-cell proportions 11.2% and 2.78% (+8.44 percentage points; P &lt; 0.0001), and vaginal pH 5.39 and 6.05 (−0.66 units; P &lt; 0.0001).<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup>

Among women with moderate-to-severe dryness at baseline (273 prasterone, 132 placebo), mean dryness severity fell from 2.30 to 0.86 and from 2.30 to 1.13, respectively. The adjusted difference was −0.27 score units (P = 0.004). At speculum examination, the investigators also graded secretions, epithelial integrity, surface thickness, and color; each favored prasterone at 12 weeks.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup>

An ERC-238 secondary analysis found a 2.59-point greater improvement in total Female Sexual Function Index score with prasterone than placebo at 12 weeks (P = 0.0006). Each of the six domain comparisons favored prasterone; their reported P values ranged from 0.047 to 0.0005.<sup>[[2]](#ref2)</sup>

## Surgery and treatment delivery

Participants inserted one 0.50% prasterone ovule or matching placebo ovule before bed each day for 12 weeks using a supplied single-use applicator. Study staff checked returned containers and daily dosing diaries. Among the efficacy population, 311/325 prasterone recipients (96%) and 152/157 placebo recipients (97%) completed the study.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup>

## Adverse events

In the safety population, any treatment-emergent adverse event occurred in 179/374 prasterone recipients (47.9%) and 77/180 placebo recipients (42.8%). Application-site vaginal discharge occurred in 23/374 (6.1%) and 10/180 (5.6%); two women stopped treatment because of discharge. Urinary tract infection was reported in 17/374 (4.5%) and 5/180 (2.8%). The investigators attributed discharge to melting of the insert vehicle, possibly with increased vaginal secretions. Serum steroids measured by liquid chromatography–tandem mass spectrometry remained within the reported postmenopausal ranges at 12 weeks.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup>

## Limitations

The primary efficacy analysis excluded 72 treated women whose screening eligibility was no longer present at baseline. Symptom scores were patient reported, the placebo was an intravaginal ovule, and treatment lasted 12 weeks. The trial excluded women with prior cancer other than nonmelanoma skin cancer. The FDA label states that Intrarosa has not been studied in women with a history of breast cancer and identifies estrogen as a prasterone metabolite.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[3]](#ref3)</sup>

## Guidelines and approvals

The AUA/SUFU/AUGS 2025 GSM guideline cites the ERC-238 publication in its evidence review and says clinicians should offer vaginal DHEA as an option for vulvovaginal dryness or dyspareunia <Rec>(Moderate Recommendation; Evidence Level: Grade C)</Rec>. Its guidance against endometrial surveillance solely because of vaginal DHEA use is <Rec>(Expert Opinion)</Rec>.<sup>[[4]](#ref4)</sup>

The FDA approved Intrarosa on November 16, 2016, for moderate-to-severe dyspareunia due to menopausal vulvar and vaginal atrophy.<sup>[[5]](#ref5)</sup>
