# Coaptite pivotal: Multicenter Prospective Randomized 52-Week Trial of Calcium Hydroxylapatite Versus Bovine Dermal Collagen for Stress Urinary Incontinence

<TrialBottomLine id="coaptite-pivotal" />

**Coaptite pivotal** was a multicenter, single-blind randomized noninferiority trial in 296 treated women with stress incontinence from intrinsic sphincter deficiency and no urethral hypermobility; calcium hydroxylapatite injection met the prespecified noninferiority criterion against bovine dermal collagen for Stamey-grade improvement at 12 months.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[2]](#ref2)</sup>

<TrialQuickHit id="coaptite-pivotal" />

## Design

Eligible women had a straining urethral angle of 35° or less, bladder capacity &gt;250 mL without detrusor instability, negative urine cultures and no prior urethral bulking.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup> Women received urethral sphincter augmentation with Coaptite or Contigen. The primary endpoint was improvement of at least one Stamey grade from baseline at 12 months. The study planned a 95% confidence interval within 15% of the anticipated 70% collagen response to establish equivalence.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup> Randomization occurred at study entry. Baseline and 12-month Stamey grades were available for 231/296 treated women (131 Coaptite, 100 collagen).<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup>

## Results

Coaptite met the prespecified noninferiority endpoint: among women with baseline and 12-month Stamey scores, 83/131 (63.4%) versus 57/100 (57.0%) improved by at least one grade (P = 0.34 for the between-group comparison).<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup> In the intent-to-treat analysis with last observation carried forward, improvement occurred in 58% versus 51% (P = 0.24). Stamey grade 0 (dry) was reached by 39% versus 37% (P = 0.78), and a 50% or greater reduction in 24-hour pad weight by 62% versus 54% (P = 0.23).<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup>

## Surgery and treatment delivery

Experienced investigators injected at the bladder neck to achieve visual coaptation, choosing anesthesia and a transurethral or periurethral route. The initial injection was transurethral in 145/158 (91.8%) Coaptite patients and 123/138 (89.1%) collagen patients; 13/158 (8.2%) and 15/138 (10.9%), respectively, received periurethral injections.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup> Repeat treatment was permitted during the first six months, up to five sessions. Across the treated population, 60/158 (38.0%) versus 36/138 (26.1%) received only one injection (P = 0.034).<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup> Mean volume at the first injection was 2.15 versus 3.39 mL, and mean total injected volume was 4.0 versus 6.6 mL during the study (P &lt; 0.0001 for both).<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup>

## Adverse events

Transient retention occurred in 41% of Coaptite recipients and 33% of collagen recipients, with no permanent retention. Post-treatment urge incontinence occurred in 5.7% versus 12% (P &lt; 0.05).<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup> Serious adverse events affected 11/158 Coaptite and 12/138 collagen recipients; two events were considered treatment related, both after Coaptite injection. One involved vaginal-wall erosion and a later suburethral sling; the other involved dissection beneath the trigonal mucosa without intervention. No systemic adverse events or chronic persistent urinary tract infections were reported with either agent.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup>

## Limitations

The complete-case efficacy analysis excludes treated women without a 12-month assessment. Injection route was left to investigators, and the small periurethral subgroup cannot establish route-specific outcomes.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[2]](#ref2)</sup>

## Guidelines and approvals

The FDA approved Coaptite on November 10, 2005 for soft-tissue augmentation in adult women with stress urinary incontinence due to intrinsic sphincter deficiency.<sup>[[3]](#ref3)</sup>
