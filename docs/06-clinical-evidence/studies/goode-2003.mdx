# Goode 2003: Behavioral Training With or Without Pelvic Floor Electrical Stimulation for Stress Incontinence in Women

<TrialBottomLine id="goode-2003" />

**Goode 2003** was a single-center, three-arm randomized trial of clinic-based behavioral training with or without home pelvic floor electrical stimulation (PFES) versus a self-help booklet in women with stress-predominant incontinence; both clinic programs reduced diary leakage more than the booklet, while stimulation added no detected reduction.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup>

<TrialQuickHit id="goode-2003" />

## Design

The 200 participants were ambulatory, nondemented, community-dwelling women aged 40–78 years with stress-predominant leakage demonstrated on urodynamic stress testing; 133/200 had mixed stress and urge incontinence. The control booklet contained the same core exercise, diary, and bladder-control instructions as the clinic program, but no professional training or equipment. The trial therefore tested both the added clinic support and the added stimulation within the clinic program.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup>

The prespecified primary outcome was each woman's percentage reduction in diary-recorded incontinent episodes. The investigators tested the three groups together, then made post hoc pairwise comparisons. The intention-to-treat analysis carried forward the most recent diary for participants who left treatment early. Participants and treating staff knew the assigned program, and patients recorded their own leakage.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup>

## Results

After treatment, mean reductions in weekly diary episodes were 68.6% with behavioral training, 71.9% with behavioral training plus PFES, and 52.5% with the booklet (three-group P = .005). In post hoc comparisons, each clinic arm exceeded the booklet (P = .02 and P = .002, respectively), while the two clinic arms did not differ (P = .60). The three-group test met the trial's significance threshold; the comparison central to the stimulation question did not show added benefit.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup>

Among patients who provided outcome ratings, 27/47 (57.4%) in the behavioral arm, 36/47 (76.6%) in the PFES arm, and 12/40 (30.0%) in the booklet arm said they were “much better” (three-group P &lt; .001). The post hoc PFES-versus-behavioral comparison favored PFES (P = .048). Incontinence Impact Questionnaire scores improved across the groups without a detected between-group difference.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup>

## Surgery and treatment delivery

Specially trained nurse practitioners delivered four clinic visits at 2-week intervals. Initial anorectal manometric biofeedback taught selective pelvic floor contraction; the home program prescribed three daily sessions of 15 contractions. Subsequent visits reinforced contraction during cough, lifting, or other leakage triggers and taught an urge-suppression strategy for mixed symptoms.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup>

The PFES arm used a Hollister InCare vaginal probe at home: biphasic 20-Hz pulses, 1-ms pulse width, a 1:1 train-to-rest ratio, and current increased to comfort, up to 100 mA. Women stimulated for 15 minutes every other day while voluntarily contracting with each pulse; on alternate days they performed the usual exercise sessions. Treatment completion was 54/66 (81.8%) with behavioral training, 59/67 (88.1%) with PFES, and 42/67 (62.7%) with the booklet.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup>

## Adverse events

Four of 67 women assigned PFES (6%) reported vaginal irritation: three episodes of probe slippage with pinching and one reaction to the conduction gel. No participant withdrew because of an adverse effect.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup>

## Limitations

Booklet-arm attrition was 37.3%, compared with 18.2% and 11.9% in the two clinic arms. Carrying forward diary values after withdrawal could favor programs that retained more participants; among completers, episode reductions did not differ significantly across groups. The added stimulation was unblinded, which complicates interpretation of the patient-rated improvement. The study measured outcomes shortly after treatment and did not establish long-term persistence.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup>
