# Paraiso 2011: Laparoscopic Compared With Robotic Sacrocolpopexy for Vaginal Prolapse

<TrialBottomLine id="paraiso-lap-robotic" />

**Paraiso 2011** was a single-center, patient- and assessor-blinded randomized comparison of robotic-assisted and conventional laparoscopic sacrocolpopexy in 78 women with posthysterectomy stage 2–4 apical prolapse. The prespecified incision-to-closure operative time was 265 versus 199 minutes, a 66-minute increase with robotic assistance (95% CI 43–90; P &lt; 0.001).<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup>

<TrialQuickHit id="paraiso-lap-robotic" />

## Design

Women seeking minimally invasive repair were enrolled at the Cleveland Clinic. Prior sacrocolpopexy, prior or planned rectal-prolapse surgery, suspected adnexal malignancy, pelvic inflammatory disease, inability to receive general anesthesia, and morbid obesity excluded participation. Allocation used computer-generated blocks stratified by surgeon; the surgical scheduler opened opaque envelopes before scheduling because the procedures required different equipment. Patients and follow-up examiners were blinded, while the operative team knew the assignment.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup>

The primary endpoint was total time from first incision to skin closure for all procedures. The analysis included randomized patients who underwent surgery and used a two-sided test for a difference. A sensitivity analysis imputed operative time for patients who did not undergo surgery. Pain and activity were assessed during the first six postoperative weeks; POP-Q support and condition-specific function were reassessed at six months and one year. Health-system costs included surgery, hospitalization, and related care through six weeks, excluding robot purchase and maintenance.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup>

## Results

The primary comparison was statistically significant and favored conventional laparoscopy. Robotic sacrocolpopexy suturing took 31 minutes longer on average (95% CI 20–41; P &lt; 0.001). The imputation analysis did not change the operative-time finding.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup>

Robotic participants reported greater pain at rest and during normal activities in postoperative weeks 3–5. Mean health-system cost was $16,278 with robotic surgery and $14,342 with laparoscopy, a difference of $1,936 (95% CI $417–$3,454; P = 0.008). Both groups improved in vaginal support and functional measures at one year, without a reported between-group difference.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup>

## Surgery and treatment delivery

Two attending urogynecologic surgeons operated with fellows. Both attendings were experienced with the two approaches and had performed at least 10 robotic-assisted procedures before enrollment. Both approaches used two separate 4×15-cm polypropylene mesh pieces. Conventional laparoscopy used four ports and extracorporeal knot tying; the robotic approach used five ports in a shallow W, a cart docked between the legs, and intracorporeal knots. The robotic ports included three 8-mm lower-quadrant ports, with 12-mm umbilical and right subcostal ports.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup>

Concomitant incontinence surgery was performed in 25/35 robotic and 23/33 laparoscopic operations; rectocele repair in 10/35 and 16/33, respectively. The primary surgeon selected concomitant reconstructive procedures.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup>

## Adverse events

Robotic participants used nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory drugs for a median 20 days (IQR 9–36) versus 11 days (IQR 3–21) after laparoscopy (P &lt; 0.005); narcotic-use duration was similar. Three of 35 robotic operations converted to another approach: one laparotomy after two cystotomies and sigmoid injury, and two laparoscopies after robot malfunction. Two of 33 laparoscopic operations converted, one to laparotomy and one to a vaginal approach. The report found no between-group difference in intraoperative or postoperative complication frequency.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup>

## Limitations

The trial was powered for a difference in operative time, not small differences in complications or long-term anatomic and functional outcomes. The single high-volume center used surgeons experienced in both approaches. Surgeon ergonomics were not measured. The cost analysis omitted robot purchase, maintenance, patient costs, and societal costs.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup>
