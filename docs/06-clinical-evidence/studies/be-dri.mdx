# BE-DRI: Behavioral Therapy to Enable Women with Urge Incontinence to Discontinue Drug Treatment

<TrialBottomLine id="be-dri" />

**BE-DRI** was a two-stage, multicenter randomized trial in women with urge-predominant urinary incontinence that compared tolterodine ER plus supervised behavioral training with tolterodine ER alone, then stopped drug treatment in both groups; adding training did not demonstrate an improvement in the prespecified eight-month drug-free continence outcome.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup>

<TrialQuickHit id="be-dri" />

## Design

The trial randomized 307 women at nine university-affiliated outpatient centers. Entry required at least seven weekly incontinence episodes on a bladder diary, symptoms lasting at least three months, and no current antimuscarinic use or neurologic or systemic cause of incontinence. Nearly all participants also reported stress-incontinence symptoms. Randomization was stratified by site, urge-only versus mixed symptoms, and baseline leakage frequency. Participants and interventionists knew the assignment; outcome assessors were blinded.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup>

Both groups received ten weeks of active treatment, followed by planned drug discontinuation and assessment six months later. Success required no drug or other treatment for urge incontinence **and** at least a 70% reduction in diary-recorded leakage episodes from baseline. The primary analysis estimated cumulative success with life-table methods. Following two planned interim analyses, the final significance threshold was 0.033.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup>

## Results

At eight months, life-table success was 41% with combined therapy and 41% with drug alone: a risk difference of 0 percentage points (95% CI −12 to 12), which did not meet the trial's superiority threshold. The raw counts of observed successes were 43/154 and 41/153, but 36 and 34 participants, respectively, had missing final status; these counts are distinct from the life-table estimates. When all missing outcomes were counted as failures, success was 28% versus 27% (risk difference 1 percentage point, 95% CI −9 to 11).<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup>

Among 269 women with evaluable ten-week diaries, at least a 70% reduction in leakage episodes occurred in 69% with combined therapy and 58% with drug alone (risk difference 11 percentage points, 95% CI −0.3 to 22.1). At eight months, complete satisfaction was reported by 33% versus 20% (difference 13 percentage points, 95% CI 2 to 24), and perceived improvement by 69% versus 43% (difference 26 percentage points, 95% CI 14 to 38). These were secondary outcomes.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup>

## Surgery and treatment delivery

Tolterodine ER was prescribed at 4 mg daily, with reduction to 2 mg for adverse effects or substitution of another antimuscarinic if needed. Four visits over ten weeks supported both groups. In the combined arm, a nurse practitioner, nurse specialist, or physical therapist taught pelvic-floor contraction using vaginal palpation, urgency suppression, delayed voiding when appropriate, and individualized fluid management for excessive urine output. Participants continued the behavioral program after drug withdrawal. Both arms kept daily bladder diaries and received general fluid advice.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup>

Overall, 290/307 attended at least three treatment visits, 208/307 took at least 80% of prescribed drug, and eight-month outcome status was known for 237/307.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup>

## Adverse events

Twelve events occurred in six participants, three in each group (3/154 with combined therapy; 3/153 with drug alone). One participant in the combined arm had a serious drug reaction including blurred vision and syncope. Small-bowel obstruction occurred in one participant in each arm. There were no deaths.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup>

## Limitations

The drug-only group also kept daily diaries and received general fluid-management advice, components that could have reduced the added effect of supervised training. Eight-month status was unavailable for 70 randomized women. Assigned treatment was completed by 68%, principally because of incomplete medication use; the missing-as-failure analysis still showed little between-group difference.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup>
