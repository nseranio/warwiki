# van Ophoven amitriptyline trial: Oral Amitriptyline for Interstitial Cystitis

<TrialBottomLine id="amitriptyline-van-ophoven" />

The van Ophoven trial randomized patients meeting interstitial cystitis symptom criteria to self-titrated amitriptyline or placebo under double blinding; after four months, the primary O'Leary-Sant symptom/problem score improved more with amitriptyline.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup>

<TrialQuickHit id="amitriptyline-van-ophoven" />

## Design

The single-center trial enrolled 44 women and 6 men meeting National Institute of Diabetes, Digestive and Kidney Diseases symptom criteria; all had received prior conservative treatment, and prior amitriptyline use excluded enrollment. The primary endpoint was change in the O'Leary-Sant IC symptom and problem index. Secondary measures were pain and urgency visual analog scales, plus frequency and functional bladder capacity from a 48-hour voiding log. The reported efficacy analysis used the 24 patients available in each arm after one withdrawal per arm.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup>

## Results

The primary score improved more with amitriptyline among 24 evaluable patients per arm: mean scores fell from 26.9 to 18.5 versus 27.6 to 24.1, with mean changes of −8.4 ± 7.2 versus −3.5 ± 5.4 points. The arithmetic between-arm difference in mean change was −4.9 points; the trial's Mann-Whitney U comparison gave p = 0.005.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup>

Pain intensity changed by −22.8 ± 26.1 versus +1.0 ± 14.8 mm, and urgency intensity by −43.8 ± 23.5 versus −0.1 ± 3.2 mm on visual analog scales (both p &lt; 0.001). Frequency changed by −4.0 ± 5.1 versus −0.6 ± 5.8 voids per 24 hours (p = 0.063), and functional bladder volume by +19.0 ± 54.62 versus −7.7 ± 47.5 mL (p = 0.083). Good or excellent treatment satisfaction was reported by 15/24 (62.5%) versus 1/24 (4.2%) patients (p &lt; 0.001).<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup>

## Surgery and treatment delivery

Patients started amitriptyline at 25 mg at bedtime and could increase weekly to 50, 75 and 100 mg if not symptom-free, maintaining the lowest dose that gave satisfactory relief; placebo followed the same protocol. At four months, 7/24 (29.2%) took 25 mg, 9/24 (37.5%) took 50 mg, 5/24 (20.8%) took 75 mg and 3/24 (12.5%) took 100 mg.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup>

## Adverse events

Anticholinergic effects occurred in 22/24 (92%) evaluable amitriptyline patients and 5/24 (21%) placebo patients. Dry mouth occurred in 19/24 (79%) versus 2/24 (8%), weight gain in 15/24 (62.5%) versus 2/24 (8.3%), and constipation in 11/24 (45.8%) versus 2/24 (8.3%). Five amitriptyline patients had grade 2 weight gain and three had grade 2 constipation; no grade 3 or higher adverse effects were reported. One participant per randomized arm (1/25) withdrew because of side effects. Adverse effects were graded by Common Toxicity Criteria.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup>

## Limitations

The single-center efficacy comparison included 48 of 50 randomized patients and covered four months.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup>

## In conversation

<InConversation id="amitriptyline-van-ophoven" />

The later ICRN trial enrolled treatment-naïve patients and gave both arms education and behavioral modification. Its intention-to-treat global response endpoint did not differ significantly between amitriptyline and placebo; van Ophoven instead measured change in a symptom/problem index.<sup>[[2]](#ref2)</sup>

## Guidelines and approvals

AUA's 2022 IC/BPS guideline cites this trial and includes amitriptyline among the oral medications that may be administered, listed alphabetically with no hierarchy <Rec>(Option (Evidence Strength: Grades B, B, C, and B))</Rec>. Amitriptyline carries the first Grade B in that sequence.<sup>[[3]](#ref3)</sup>
