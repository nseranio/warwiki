# TOPSY: Pessary Self-Management versus Clinic-Based Care for Pelvic Organ Prolapse

<TrialBottomLine id="topsy" />

**TOPSY** was a multicenter, open-label superiority randomized trial in 340 women at 21 UK National Health Service pessary clinics who had retained a pessary for at least 2 weeks, comparing taught self-management with routine clinic-based care; the clinical primary endpoint was the Pelvic Floor Impact Questionnaire-7 (PFIQ-7) at 18 months and the economic primary endpoint was incremental net benefit. PFIQ-7 did not differ (32.3 versus 32.5; adjusted difference −0.03, 95% CI −9.32 to 9.25), and self-management had a lower mean cost (£578.30 versus £728.84 per participant), with an incremental net benefit of £564.32.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup>

<TrialQuickHit id="topsy" />

## Design

Eligible women were 18 or older and used a pessary of any material or type except shelf, Gellhorn or cube, retained for at least 2 weeks; new users (3 months or less of use) and existing users were both included. Women with limited manual dexterity, a cognitive deficit that precluded consent or self-management, pregnancy, or insufficient English (the teaching was available only in English) were excluded. Allocation was 1:1 by a web-based system, minimized on age (under 65 or 65 and older), user type and centre. Participants, intervention deliverers, researchers and the statistician were not masked.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup>

Of 2514 women screened between 16 May 2018 and 7 February 2020, 340 were randomized. Randomized women were younger (64 versus 67 years) and less often existing users (68% versus 80%) than eligible women who did not take part, and 39% of those who declined randomization had a treatment preference (33% for clinic-based care, 6% for self-management). Baseline groups were similar: mean age 63.7 years, 106 of 340 (31.2%) new users, and a ring pessary in about 88% of each arm.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup>

The sample size of 330 gave 90% power to detect a 20-point PFIQ-7 difference at 18 months (standard deviation 50, two-sided alpha 0.05, 20% loss to follow-up). The 20 points came from consensus among patient representatives and clinical collaborators, since no minimal clinically important difference had been published for this population. PFIQ-7 ranges from 0 to 300, with higher scores indicating worse pelvic floor-specific quality of life. The economic primary endpoint was incremental net benefit at £20,000 per quality-adjusted life-year (QALY) from a health-sector perspective, where a positive value implies self-management is cost-effective. Analysis was by intention to treat with a longitudinal analysis of covariance adjusted for age group, user type and baseline PFIQ-7.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup>

## Results

At 18 months, valid PFIQ-7 data were available for 139 of 169 self-management women (82.2%) and 152 of 171 clinic-care women (88.9%). Mean scores were 32.3 and 32.5; the adjusted difference of −0.03 (95% CI −9.32 to 9.25) did not show superiority, and the interval excludes the 20-point difference the trial was powered to detect. Differences at 6 months (−5.90, 95% CI −15.00 to 3.20) and 12 months (−3.45, 95% CI −12.71 to 5.82) were also nonsignificant. Sensitivity analyses for missing data, crossover and previous hysterectomy, and subgroups defined by age, user type, hysterectomy and local oestrogen use, gave no significant difference.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup>

Over 18 months, mean total cost was £578.30 with self-management and £728.84 with clinic-based care, and QALYs were 1.241 and 1.221. The incremental net benefit of £564.32 (standard error £581.50; 95% CI −£576 to £1704) was positive, which met the trial's criterion for cost-effectiveness, although the interval includes zero. Mean pessary-related clinic-visit cost was £16.81 versus £77.45 per participant, and the teaching appointment cost a mean £31.77. Among 125 self-management and 139 clinic-care women with EQ-5D-5L data, mean index scores at 18 months were 0.823 and 0.819.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup>

Self-managing women reported a lower mean percentage of applicable complication types (16.7% versus 22.0%; adjusted difference −3.83, 95% CI −6.86 to −0.81), more often rated their pessary care as better (42 of 126, 33.3%, versus 35 of 143, 24.5%), and were more confident inserting (81.28 versus 48.36) and removing (85.28 versus 52.63) the pessary; the adjusted odds ratio from ordinal regression across PGI-I response categories was 3.23 (95% CI 1.47 to 7.13). General self-efficacy (31.3 versus 32.0) and PFDI-20 (98.07 versus 102.04) did not differ.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup>

The 4-year report followed participants who opted in: 186 of 340 (55%) responded, 86 of 169 self-management (51%) and 100 of 171 clinic-care women (58%). Mean PFIQ-7 was 32.9 versus 31.4 (adjusted difference 4.86, 95% CI −6.41 to 16.12; p = 0.398). An equivalence test added after the null 18-month result rejected a 20-point difference (p = 0.0108). The incremental net benefit was £2240.15 (bootstrapped standard error £1530.25), with a probability of cost-effectiveness of 92.97% at £20,000 per QALY, based on 78 self-management and 91 clinic-care women with complete data. At 4 years, the mean percentage of applicable complication types reported per participant was 17.91% versus 21.97% (reported adjusted difference 3.01, 95% CI −0.58 to 6.61; p = 0.101). Confidence to insert (82.3 versus 55.9) and remove (85.5 versus 59.6) the pessary remained higher with self-management.<sup>[[2]](#ref2)</sup>

## Surgery and treatment delivery

Self-management consisted of a 30-minute appointment in which the woman removed, cleaned and reinserted her own pessary under supervision, an information leaflet, a telephone call at 2 weeks and a clinic number for support. Each centre's specialist nurse, physiotherapist or doctor was trained at a site visit and given a manual. Clinic-based care followed local practice, usually every 6 months; excluding COVID-19 lockdown periods, the median change interval was 186 days (interquartile range 165 to 201).<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup>

Pessary type at baseline was a ring in 148 of 168 self-management women (88.1%) and 151 of 171 clinic-care women (88.3%); silicone devices were used in 54 of 167 (32.3%) and 52 of 167 (31.1%), and PVC in 90 of 167 (53.9%) and 98 of 167 (58.7%). Mean pessary size was 71.0 and 70.7 mm. Local oestrogen was used by 98 of 340 women (28.8%), 38 (11.2%) had a previous hysterectomy and 39 (11.5%) previous pelvic floor surgery.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup>

By 18 months, 141 of 169 self-management women (83.4%) were classed as on treatment, against 103 of 171 clinic-care women (60.2%) classed as on treatment in that arm. Thirty-four self-management women (20.1%) had changed to clinic-based care, including 11 who could not remove the pessary. No clinic-care woman formally crossed over, but of 55 who said they had removed their pessary, 27 (49%) reported receiving self-management training. Pessary use had stopped in 16 of 169 and 26 of 171 women. Intention to continue was 130 of 138 (94.2%) with self-management and 131 of 148 (88.5%) with clinic-based care, and 101 of 134 (75.4%) versus 92 of 143 (64.3%) found pessary changes convenient.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup>

At 4 years, 79 of 86 self-management and 94 of 100 clinic-care responders had used a pessary in the previous 6 months. Among those currently using a pessary, 60 of 75 original self-management women (80%) still self-managed, while 35 of 90 original clinic-care women (39%) had changed to self-management. Eight responders (4%) had any surgery: 4 prolapse operations (1 self-management, 3 clinic-care), 1 incontinence operation and 3 hysterectomies.<sup>[[2]](#ref2)</sup>

## Adverse events

Serious adverse events occurred in 17 of 169 self-management and 14 of 171 clinic-care women, all judged unrelated to trial participation, and there were no suspected unexpected serious adverse reactions. Between 18 months and 4 years, 11 self-management and 8 clinic-care women reported serious adverse events; one event in the self-management group, urinary and chest infections requiring hospitalization, was possibly related.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[2]](#ref2)</sup>

Participant-reported complications at 18 months, self-management versus clinic-based care, were difficulty emptying the bladder 25 of 141 (17.7%) versus 41 of 147 (27.9%), difficulty emptying the bowels 34 of 140 (24.3%) versus 55 of 151 (36.4%), pessary falling out 6 of 138 (4.3%) versus 19 of 145 (13.1%) and difficulty having sex 5 of 134 (3.7%) versus 16 of 148 (10.8%). Vaginal discharge (29.1% versus 32.5%), smell (18.4% versus 22.0%), non-menstrual bleeding (10.9% versus 14.7%) and urine infection (12.1% versus 10.6%) were similar. On examination, ulceration, granulation or another vaginal tissue concern was found in 27 of 152 (17.8%) and 43 of 160 (26.9%).<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup>

At 18 months, 15 of 140 self-management women (10.7%) versus 11 of 150 clinic-care women (7.3%) reported being unable to remove the pessary, and 27 of 139 (19.4%) versus 12 of 149 (8.1%) reported difficulty removing it.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup> Of the 15 who could not remove it, 11 had reverted to clinic-based care. At 4 years the same items were 5 of 86 (5.8%) versus 4 of 100 (4.0%) and 14 of 85 (16.5%) versus 9 of 98 (9.2%), while difficulty emptying the bladder (12 of 85, 14.1%, versus 27 of 98, 27.6%) and difficulty having sex (1 of 84, 1.2%, versus 9 of 97, 9.3%) again favored self-management.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[2]](#ref2)</sup>

## Limitations

Participants and clinicians were unmasked, and the primary outcome, the complication outcome and the confidence outcomes were participant-reported. Complications came from a 15-item study-specific questionnaire, and the authors call for a validated pessary complications instrument. Response at 18 months was lower with self-management (82.2% versus 88.9%), and 20.1% of self-management women changed to clinic-based care while some clinic-care women self-managed, which the authors note could dilute the intention-to-treat contrast; the on-treatment analysis agreed with it.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup> Both arms used a pessary, so the trial compares two ways of delivering the same device, and about 91% of participants were White.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup> Women who agreed to randomization were younger and less often existing users than those who declined. The 4-year report rests on 55% of the cohort who opted in, and the authors suggest the loss of power may explain the nonsignificant complications difference.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[2]](#ref2)</sup>
