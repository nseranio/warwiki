# PRIDE: Weight Loss to Treat Urinary Incontinence in Overweight and Obese Women

<TrialBottomLine id="pride" />

**PRIDE** was a two-center, assessor-masked randomized trial in overweight and obese women with frequent urinary incontinence that found a larger six-month reduction in weekly leakage episodes with an intensive diet, activity, and behavior-change weight-loss program than with structured education, driven mainly by stress episodes.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup>

<TrialQuickHit id="pride" />

## Design

Women were eligible from age 30 with BMI 25–50 kg/m² and at least 10 leakage episodes in a seven-day diary. Prior incontinence or urethral surgery, recent incontinence or weight-loss medication, active infection, and several medical conditions were exclusions. Allocation was 2:1, stratified by center. Participants knew their assignment; staff collecting outcomes were masked. The prespecified primary endpoint was percentage change in diary-recorded total weekly episodes at six months. Diaries classified leakage with exertion as stress incontinence and leakage with a strong need to void as urge incontinence.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup>

Both arms received the same bladder-control booklet, including pelvic-floor exercises and urgency-suppression instructions. Continence was not otherwise discussed in the group sessions. The comparison therefore tested the added weight-loss program against education and shared self-help advice.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup>

## Results

At six months, mean weekly total episodes fell 47% with weight loss and 28% with education, a 19-percentage-point difference (P = 0.01). Stress episodes fell 58% and 33% (P = 0.02); urge episodes fell 42% and 26% (P = 0.14). Among questionnaire respondents, 166/219 (75.8%) in the intervention arm and 44/94 (46.8%) in the control arm were moderately or very satisfied with their change in incontinence (P &lt; 0.001). The 24-hour pad-weight reductions, 45% and 34%, did not differ significantly (P = 0.23).<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup>

The follow-up report found mean stress-episode reductions of 66% versus 45% at 12 months (P &lt; 0.001), while total-episode reductions of 57% versus 45% did not meet its significance threshold (P = 0.08). At 18 months, total-episode reductions were 62% versus 55% (P = 0.30). The modeled proportion achieving at least a 70% reduction in urge episodes was 47% versus 34% (P = 0.03).<sup>[[2]](#ref2)</sup>

A pooled post-randomization analysis across both arms associated 5% to &lt;10% weight loss with larger decreases in weekly incontinence episodes than weight gain; it did not detect further improvement at ≥10% loss. Weight-change categories were observed after randomization.<sup>[[3]](#ref3)</sup>

## Surgery and treatment delivery

Nutrition and behavior-change specialists led weekly one-hour groups for six months. The intervention prescribed 1,200–1,500 kcal/day, meal replacements, gradual progression toward at least 200 minutes of activity weekly, self-monitoring, stimulus control, and problem-solving. Controls attended four one-hour sessions on diet, activity, and healthy eating. Mean weight loss at six months was 8.0% (7.8 kg) versus 1.6% (1.5 kg).<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup>

The intervention continued with biweekly maintenance groups for another year; the control arm was offered seven education sessions over 18 months. Mean weight loss was 7.5% versus 1.7% at 12 months and 5.5% versus 1.6% at 18 months.<sup>[[2]](#ref2)</sup>

## Adverse events

The published reports did not provide adverse-event rates for either program.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[2]](#ref2)</sup>

## Limitations

The primary endpoint relied on unblinded participants' voiding diaries, although assessors were masked. Diaries were completed by 304/338 women at six months, with different completion by arm (214/226 versus 90/112); missing outcomes were imputed. The shared continence booklet and improvement in controls affect the size of the between-group contrast. Pad-weight change did not corroborate the diary result. The selected participants were able to walk unassisted and monitor diet and activity before enrollment. The combined diet, activity, and behavior program did not isolate weight change from its other components.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup>

## Guidelines and approvals

The EAU's 2026 female LUTS guideline recommends encouraging overweight and obese women with LUTS or stress urinary incontinence to lose weight and maintain the loss <Rec>(Strong)</Rec>. Its OAB/urinary-incontinence section also recommends weight loss and maintenance for overweight and obese adults <Rec>(Strong)</Rec>.<sup>[[4]](#ref4)</sup>

The AUA/SUFU 2024 idiopathic OAB guideline cites PRIDE in its obesity discussion and advises assessment of comorbid conditions that may contribute to urgency or urgency incontinence, with counseling about the role of managing them <Rec>(Expert Opinion)</Rec>.<sup>[[5]](#ref5)</sup>
