# NASHA Dx: Submucosal Dextranomer–Hyaluronic Acid Injection for Fecal Incontinence

<TrialBottomLine id="nasha-dx" />

**NASHA Dx** was a multicenter, double-blind, sham-controlled randomized superiority trial in 206 adults with fecal incontinence, comparing transanal submucosal dextranomer in stabilized hyaluronic acid with sham injection. Active treatment increased the six-month proportion with at least a 50% reduction in incontinence episodes.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup>

<TrialQuickHit id="nasha-dx" />

## Design

Patients had fecal incontinence for at least 12 months despite supervised conservative treatment, a Cleveland Clinic Florida Fecal Incontinence Score (CCFIS) of at least 10, and at least four episodes over a two-week diary. Eligible ages were 18–75 years. Exclusions included complete external sphincter disruption, significant rectal prolapse, active anorectal sepsis or fissure, prior pelvic radiotherapy, and an existing anorectal implant. Recruitment occurred at US and European centers. Patients and investigators remained masked through the six-month assessment; randomization was stratified by sex and region.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[2]](#ref2)</sup>

The primary responder definition was a reduction of at least 50% in fecal-incontinence episodes recorded in a two-week diary. The FDA's study review described three prespecified efficacy criteria: superiority over sham at six months, an active-arm six-month responder estimate above a minimum threshold, and persistence of a lesser response threshold at 12 months. All three criteria were met in that review.<sup>[[3]](#ref3)</sup>

## Results

At six months, 71/136 (52%) NASHA Dx recipients and 22/70 (31%) sham recipients met the responder definition (odds ratio 2.36, 95% CI 1.24–4.47; P = 0.0089).<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup>

The later report followed the 136 patients originally assigned to NASHA Dx. At 36 months, the responder proportion was 52% with last observation carried forward. Of the 112 patients assessed at that visit, 59% met the responder definition; counting all missing patients as failures gave 44.9%. Mean CCFIS decreased from 14 at baseline to 11 at 36 months (P &lt; 0.001). These were within-arm outcomes after the masked comparison ended.<sup>[[2]](#ref2)</sup>

## Surgery and treatment delivery

Clinicians used an anoscope in an outpatient setting without anesthesia. They injected 1 mL of NASHA Dx into each of four submucosal quadrants, about 5 mm above the dentate line, and retained the needle for 10 seconds after each injection to limit backflow. Patients with CCFIS at least 10 and no persistent adverse effects could receive another treatment after one month; 112/136 (82%) did. The same proportion completed a 36-month assessment.<sup>[[2]](#ref2)</sup>

## Adverse events

Among NASHA Dx recipients during the first six months, proctalgia occurred in 19/136 (14.0%) and injection-site bleeding in 7/136 (5.1%). A rectal abscess in 1/136 (0.7%) required surgery; a prostatic abscess in 1/136 (0.7%) resolved with antibiotics. From after six months through 36 months, proctalgia and injection-site nodules each occurred in 3/133 (2.3%); no further treatment-related serious adverse events were reported.<sup>[[2]](#ref2)</sup>

## Limitations

The randomized sham comparison ended at six months. The three-year analysis followed only the originally treated arm, most of whom received a second injection, and its response estimate varied with the handling of missing follow-up data. Complete external sphincter disruption and significant rectal prolapse were excluded.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[2]](#ref2)</sup>

## Guidelines and approvals

The American Society of Colon and Rectal Surgeons' 2023 guideline says injection of biocompatible bulking agents into the anal canal is not routinely recommended for fecal incontinence <Rec>(Conditional; Low)</Rec>.<sup>[[4]](#ref4)</sup> The FDA approved Solesta on May 27, 2011, for adults with fecal incontinence who have failed conservative therapy.<sup>[[3]](#ref3)</sup>
