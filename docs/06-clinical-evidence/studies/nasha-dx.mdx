# NASHA Dx: Submucosal Dextranomer–Hyaluronic Acid Injection for Fecal Incontinence

<TrialBottomLine id="nasha-dx" />

**NASHA Dx** was a multicenter, double-blind, sham-controlled randomized superiority trial in 206 adults with fecal incontinence, comparing transanal submucosal dextranomer in stabilized hyaluronic acid with sham injection. Active treatment increased the six-month proportion with at least a 50% reduction in incontinence episodes.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup>

<TrialQuickHit id="nasha-dx" />

## Design

Patients had fecal incontinence for at least 12 months despite supervised conservative treatment, a Cleveland Clinic Florida Fecal Incontinence Score (CCFIS) of at least 10, and at least four episodes over a two-week diary. Eligible ages were 18–75 years. Exclusions included complete external sphincter disruption, significant rectal prolapse, active anorectal sepsis or fissure, prior pelvic radiotherapy, and an existing anorectal implant. Recruitment occurred at US and European centers. Patients and outcome assessors remained masked through the six-month assessment; the clinicians giving injections were unmasked. Randomization was stratified by sex and region.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[2]](#ref2)</sup>

The primary objective had three prespecified criteria: a between-arm difference in at least 50% diary-episode responders at six months (P &lt; 0.05); a six-month active-arm responder estimate with a lower 95% confidence limit above 35%; and a 12-month active-arm estimate for at least 25% episode reduction with a lower 95% confidence limit above 50%.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup> The study used intention-to-treat analysis with last observation carried forward. All three criteria were met.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[3]](#ref3)</sup>

## Results

At six months, 71/136 (52%) NASHA Dx recipients and 22/70 (31%) sham recipients met the responder definition (odds ratio 2.36, 95% CI 1.24–4.47; P = 0.0089). The model estimated response at 52.7% (95% CI 39.8–65.2) and 32.1% (20.2–46.9), respectively; 132/136 and 65/70 patients had six-month assessments. At 12 months, 69.1% of the 136 randomized active-arm patients met the at least 25% reduction threshold (95% CI 61.4–76.9); sham patients had been unmasked after six months. Mean incontinence-free days over two weeks increased by 3.1 (SD 4.05) versus 1.7 (SD 3.50) at six months (P = 0.0156).<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup>

The later report followed the 136 patients originally assigned to NASHA Dx. At 36 months, the responder proportion was 52% with last observation carried forward. Of the 112 patients assessed at that visit, 59% met the responder definition; counting all missing patients as failures gave 44.9%. Mean CCFIS decreased from 14 at baseline to 11 at 36 months (P &lt; 0.001). These were within-arm outcomes after the masked comparison ended.<sup>[[2]](#ref2)</sup>

## Surgery and treatment delivery

Clinicians used an anoscope in an outpatient setting without anesthesia. They injected 1 mL of NASHA Dx into each of four submucosal quadrants, about 5 mm above the dentate line, and retained the needle for 10 seconds after each injection to limit backflow.<sup>[[2]](#ref2)</sup> Patients with CCFIS at least 10 and no persistent adverse effects could receive another treatment after one month; 112/136 (82%) did, while 61/70 (87%) sham recipients had a repeat sham procedure.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup> The same proportion of active-arm patients completed a 36-month assessment.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[2]](#ref2)</sup>

## Adverse events

During the six-month blinded phase, proctalgia occurred in 19/136 (14.0%) NASHA Dx recipients versus 2/70 (2.9%) sham recipients, and injection-site bleeding in 7/136 (5.1%) versus 12/70 (17.1%).<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup> Fever occurred in 11/136 (8.1%) versus 0/70; rectal hemorrhage in 10/136 (7.4%) versus 1/70 (1.4%).<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup> A rectal abscess in 1/136 (0.7%) required surgery; a prostatic abscess in 1/136 (0.7%) resolved with antibiotics; neither occurred in the sham arm.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup> From after six months through 36 months, proctalgia and injection-site nodules each occurred in 3/133 (2.3%); no further treatment-related serious adverse events were reported.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[2]](#ref2)</sup>

## Limitations

The randomized sham comparison ended at six months. The three-year analysis followed only the originally treated arm, most of whom received a second injection, and its response estimate varied with the handling of missing follow-up data. Complete external sphincter disruption and significant rectal prolapse were excluded.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[2]](#ref2)</sup>

## Guidelines and approvals

The American Society of Colon and Rectal Surgeons' 2023 guideline says injection of biocompatible bulking agents into the anal canal is not routinely recommended for fecal incontinence <Rec>(Conditional; Low)</Rec>.<sup>[[4]](#ref4)</sup> The FDA approved Solesta on May 27, 2011, for adults with fecal incontinence who have failed conservative therapy.<sup>[[3]](#ref3)</sup>
