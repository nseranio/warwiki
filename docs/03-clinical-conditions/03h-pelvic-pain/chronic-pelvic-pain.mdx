# Chronic Pelvic Pain

**Chronic pelvic pain (CPP)** is a highly prevalent, multifactorial syndrome — not a single disease — that affects roughly **15–26% of women** and **~9.3% of men** (as chronic prostatitis / chronic pelvic pain syndrome, CP/CPPS). It is defined as pain perceived to originate from pelvic structures lasting **≥ 6 months** (or ≥ 3 months in men per NIH criteria), often associated with urinary, bowel, sexual, and psychological dysfunction. The origin is **not gynecologic in ~80% of female patients**, and the most common contributors across both sexes are **irritable bowel syndrome, bladder pain syndrome / interstitial cystitis, myofascial pelvic floor dysfunction, and endometriosis** (in women). The cornerstone of management is a **biopsychosocial, multimodal, interdisciplinary approach**.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[2]](#ref2)[[3]](#ref3)[[4]](#ref4)[[5]](#ref5)</sup>

For the bladder-specific phenotype, see [IC/PBS](/docs/clinical-conditions/03h-pelvic-pain/ic-pbs); for menopause-related pelvic and urogenital pain, see [Genitourinary Syndrome of Menopause](/docs/special-populations/05e-womens-health/gsm).

## Part I — Conceptual Framework

### Definition

- **Women:** pain perceived to originate from pelvic organs/structures lasting &gt; 6 months, with negative cognitive, behavioral, sexual, and emotional consequences. Cyclical pain (e.g., dysmenorrhea) is included if it carries significant functional impact.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[3]](#ref3)</sup>
- **Men (CP/CPPS):** pelvic pain or discomfort for ≥ 3 months in the absence of identifiable infection, cancer, urinary obstruction, or retention.<sup>[[4]](#ref4)[[6]](#ref6)</sup>

### Epidemiology

- **Women** — prevalence 5.7–26% globally; accounts for **40% of diagnostic laparoscopies** and **12–39% of hysterectomies** in the US<sup>[[2]](#ref2)[[7]](#ref7)</sup>
- **Men** — CP/CPPS lifetime prevalence ~9.3%; ~267,000 US men diagnosed annually; risk rises after age 50<sup>[[4]](#ref4)[[6]](#ref6)</sup>

### Pathophysiology — the biopsychosocial model

CPP is best understood as a **chronic pain syndrome** with three interacting domains:<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[2]](#ref2)</sup>

1. **Peripheral pain generators** — visceral (gynecologic, urologic, GI) and somatic (musculoskeletal, neural) nociceptive input
2. **Central sensitization** — amplified spinal/brain pain signaling, decreased descending inhibition, viscero-visceral and viscero-somatic convergence
3. **Psychosocial modulation** — mood disorders, trauma, catastrophizing, sleep disruption, social stressors

**Central sensitization** explains why pain persists after treating the primary lesion (e.g., endometriosis excision), why CPP overlaps with IBS / IC/BPS / fibromyalgia / migraine, and why purely organ-targeted treatment frequently fails.<sup>[[2]](#ref2)[[5]](#ref5)</sup>

## Part II — Etiology by Organ System

### A. Gynecologic (women)

| Condition | Notes |
|---|---|
| **Endometriosis** | Dysmenorrhea, dyspareunia, non-menstrual pelvic pain; affects ~10% of reproductive-age women; **33–50% of CPP** cases<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[8]](#ref8)[[9]](#ref9)</sup> |
| **Adenomyosis** | Dysmenorrhea, heavy bleeding, diffusely enlarged uterus; often coexists with endometriosis<sup>[[3]](#ref3)</sup> |
| **Vulvodynia** | Chronic vulvar pain (burning, stinging) without identifiable cause; provoked or unprovoked; 8–16% of women |
| **Pelvic masses** | Fibroids, ovarian cysts — pressure / bulk-related pain |
| **Chronic pelvic infection** | History of PID; tubal damage and adhesions |

**Endometriosis** deserves emphasis — affects ~10% of reproductive-age women (~9 million in the US); **90% report pelvic pain**; diagnostic delay averages **5–12 years**. Pain mechanisms include nociceptive (inflammation), neuropathic (nerve infiltration / neurogenesis), and **nociplastic (central sensitization)** — explaining why lesion burden correlates poorly with pain severity and why ~25% have recurrent pain after hysterectomy.<sup>[[8]](#ref8)[[9]](#ref9)</sup>

### B. Urologic (both sexes)

#### Bladder pain syndrome / interstitial cystitis (BPS/IC)

- Persistent or chronic discomfort perceived as bladder origin, with urgency or frequency, lasting ≥ 6 weeks with negative urine cultures<sup>[[10]](#ref10)</sup>
- More commonly diagnosed in women; increasingly recognized in men (often misdiagnosed as CP/CPPS)<sup>[[10]](#ref10)[[11]](#ref11)</sup>
- **Hunner lesions** (~5–10%) represent a distinct phenotype with different treatment responses<sup>[[10]](#ref10)[[12]](#ref12)</sup>
- The 2022 AUA IC/BPS guideline replaced tiered treatment lines with categorical management — see [IC/PBS](/docs/clinical-conditions/03h-pelvic-pain/ic-pbs) for the full algorithm<sup>[[10]](#ref10)[[11]](#ref11)[[13]](#ref13)</sup>

#### Chronic prostatitis / chronic pelvic pain syndrome (CP/CPPS) — men

- The most common urologic diagnosis in men &lt; 50<sup>[[4]](#ref4)[[14]](#ref14)</sup>
- **Diagnosis of exclusion** — made when history, exam, urine culture, and PVR exclude infection, cancer, obstruction, and retention<sup>[[4]](#ref4)</sup>
- **NIH-CPSI** measures symptom severity (0–43 scale); a **6-point change** is clinically meaningful<sup>[[4]](#ref4)</sup>
- **Pelvic floor myalgia / tenderness in 27–64%** of CP/CPPS patients<sup>[[4]](#ref4)</sup>
- Higher rates of fibromyalgia, chronic fatigue, IBS, depression, anxiety, and panic disorder<sup>[[6]](#ref6)</sup>

### C. Gastrointestinal

- **Irritable bowel syndrome (IBS)** — most common GI cause of CPP; present in 35–50% of CPP patients<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[2]](#ref2)</sup>
- Diverticular disease, IBD, chronic constipation may all contribute<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup>

### D. Musculoskeletal and myofascial

- **Pelvic-floor myofascial dysfunction** is the **most commonly overlooked contributor** — present in **50–90% of women** at specialized CPP centers and **27–64% of men** with CP/CPPS when a structured pelvic-floor exam is performed.<sup>[[2]](#ref2)[[4]](#ref4)</sup> Focus on visceral causes alone leads to prolonged pain and unnecessary surgery.<sup>[[2]](#ref2)</sup>
- For the dedicated technical reference — pelvic-floor exam, PFPT down-training, trigger-point injection protocols, onabotulinumtoxinA dosing and outcomes, and compounded intravaginal diazepam / baclofen / ketamine — see [Myofascial Pelvic Pain](/docs/clinical-conditions/03h-pelvic-pain/myofascial-pelvic-pain).
- **Abdominal-wall myofascial pain** — trigger points in rectus and obliques; positive Carnett sign.
- **Sacroiliac, hip, and lumbar spine** disorders may refer pain to the pelvis.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup>

### E. Neurologic

#### Pudendal neuralgia

Chronic neuropathic pain in the pudendal nerve distribution (perineum, vulva/scrotum, perianal region).<sup>[[15]](#ref15)[[16]](#ref16)[[17]](#ref17)</sup>

**Nantes diagnostic criteria:**<sup>[[15]](#ref15)[[16]](#ref16)</sup>

1. Pain in the anatomical territory of the pudendal nerve
2. Pain worsened by sitting
3. Pain does not wake the patient at night
4. No objective sensory loss on exam
5. Positive response to a diagnostic pudendal nerve block

**Etiologies:** entrapment (Alcock canal, sacrospinous / sacrotuberous ligaments), childbirth, pelvic surgery, cycling, sacroiliac abnormalities.<sup>[[15]](#ref15)[[17]](#ref17)[[18]](#ref18)</sup>

**Stepwise management:**<sup>[[15]](#ref15)[[16]](#ref16)[[17]](#ref17)</sup>

1. **Conservative** — avoid prolonged sitting, cushion, pelvic floor PT
2. **Pharmacologic** — TCAs or gabapentinoids first-line; opioids ineffective<sup>[[16]](#ref16)</sup>
3. **Pudendal nerve blocks** — diagnostic and therapeutic; response rates up to **94%**<sup>[[15]](#ref15)</sup>
4. **Pulsed radiofrequency** — pain reduction in up to **95%** of refractory cases; long-term durability uncertain<sup>[[15]](#ref15)[[19]](#ref19)</sup>
5. **Surgical decompression** — refractory cases (transgluteal, transischiorectal, transperineal)<sup>[[15]](#ref15)[[16]](#ref16)</sup>
6. **Sacral or pudendal neuromodulation** — refractory cases<sup>[[16]](#ref16)[[17]](#ref17)</sup>

**Other neuralgias** — ilioinguinal, iliohypogastric, genitofemoral entrapment, particularly after pelvic / abdominal surgery or mesh.<sup>[[2]](#ref2)</sup>

### F. Vascular — pelvic congestion syndrome (PCS)

- Chronic pelvic pain associated with pelvic varicosities from incompetent ovarian / pelvic vein valves<sup>[[20]](#ref20)[[21]](#ref21)[[22]](#ref22)[[23]](#ref23)</sup>
- Estimated to account for **up to 30%** of CPP in women, although **ACOG notes the evidence is insufficient** to confirm a causal relationship<sup>[[20]](#ref20)[[1]](#ref1)</sup>
- **Phenotype:** dull aching pelvic pain worsened by **prolonged standing**, **post-coital pain**, **premenstrual exacerbation**; predominantly premenopausal, parous women<sup>[[20]](#ref20)[[21]](#ref21)[[23]](#ref23)</sup>
- **Pathophysiology:** ovarian/pelvic vein valve incompetence → retrograde flow → venous hypertension and dilation; may be secondary to nutcracker (left renal vein compression) or May-Thurner (iliac vein compression)<sup>[[21]](#ref21)[[22]](#ref22)</sup>
- **Diagnosis:**<sup>[[20]](#ref20)[[22]](#ref22)[[24]](#ref24)</sup>
  - **TVUS** — first-line; ovarian vein diameter &gt; 7–8 mm, slow / retrograde flow, dilated (&gt; 5 mm) pelvic veins
  - **Venography** — gold standard but invasive
  - **MRI / CT** — dilated pelvic veins seen in 12% of women on CT (21% premenopausal, 10% postmenopausal)<sup>[[24]](#ref24)</sup>
  - **Caveat:** laparoscopy has limited sensitivity because CO₂ pneumoperitoneum compresses the veins<sup>[[20]](#ref20)</sup>
- **Treatment:**<sup>[[21]](#ref21)[[22]](#ref22)[[23]](#ref23)</sup>
  - **Ovarian vein embolization** — minimally invasive, first-choice; excellent technical and clinical success
  - **Hormonal suppression** — medroxyprogesterone may reduce pain
  - **Venous stenting** — for obstructive nutcracker / May-Thurner
  - **Hysterectomy + BSO** — historical; largely replaced by embolization

### G. Psychosocial

- **Depression** in **12–33%** of women with CPP<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup>
- **Trauma history** — pelvic pain and dyspareunia more prevalent in women with abuse, military sexual trauma, or adverse childhood events<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[2]](#ref2)</sup>
- **Catastrophizing and anxiety** independently predict pain severity and disability<sup>[[25]](#ref25)</sup>
- Psychosocial factors do not change the physical pain generators but **amplify the symptom burden** and impair recovery<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup>

## Part III — Clinical Phenotyping (Men): UPOINT

The UPOINT system is the most widely adopted clinical phenotyping tool for CP/CPPS, sorting patients into six domains to drive individualized, multimodal therapy.<sup>[[25]](#ref25)[[26]](#ref26)[[27]](#ref27)[[28]](#ref28)[[29]](#ref29)</sup>

| Domain | Description | Targeted treatment |
|---|---|---|
| **U — Urinary** | Voiding / storage symptoms | Alpha-blockers (tamsulosin, alfuzosin) |
| **P — Psychosocial** | Depression, catastrophizing, anxiety, maladaptive coping | CBT, antidepressants, psychological support |
| **O — Organ-specific** | Prostate / bladder tenderness, Hunner lesions | Anti-inflammatories, quercetin, intravesical therapy |
| **I — Infection** | Documented localized infection | **Targeted antibiotics — only if proven** |
| **N — Neurologic / Systemic** | Widespread pain, fibromyalgia, central sensitization | Gabapentinoids, SNRIs, pain-mechanism education |
| **T — Tenderness** | Pelvic floor muscle tenderness, trigger points | Pelvic floor PT, myofascial release |

The number of positive UPOINT domains correlates with NIH-CPSI severity. Cluster analysis identifies a **pelvic-predominant cluster** (U, O, T) and a **systemic cluster** (N, I, P); the dominant pain drivers across patients are **pelvic floor tenderness, depression, and catastrophizing**.<sup>[[25]](#ref25)</sup> A sexual-dysfunction domain (S) has been added (UPOINTS) to improve stratification.<sup>[[26]](#ref26)</sup>

## Part IV — Diagnostic Evaluation

### Women

1. **Biopsychosocial history** — pain (onset, location, quality, severity, triggers, menstrual relationship), urinary / GI / sexual symptoms, trauma history, mood / sleep, prior treatments<sup>[[2]](#ref2)[[3]](#ref3)[[5]](#ref5)</sup>
2. **Red-flag screening** — acute abdomen, unintentional weight loss, postmenopausal bleeding, hematuria, rectal bleeding, new neurologic deficit → urgent evaluation
3. **Physical exam** — abdominal (Carnett test for abdominal-wall pain); **pelvic musculoskeletal exam** of levator ani, obturator internus, and piriformis for trigger points and hypertonicity; neurosensory mapping; speculum and bimanual exam for masses, tenderness, prolapse, endometriosis nodularity
4. **Basic diagnostics** — pregnancy test, UA / culture, STI screen, transvaginal ultrasound
5. **Advanced imaging** — MRI for suspected endometriosis, adenomyosis, complex masses; TVUS with vascular assessment for PCS
6. **Assess for central sensitization** — widespread pain, multiple pain syndromes, allodynia / hyperalgesia, sleep / mood disturbance

### Men

1. **History** — pain location / quality / duration, voiding symptoms, sexual dysfunction, psychosocial impact<sup>[[4]](#ref4)[[28]](#ref28)</sup>
2. **Physical exam** — DRE (prostate tenderness), pelvic floor palpation (external and internal)
3. **Urine culture** (exclude infection); **PVR** (exclude retention)
4. **NIH-CPSI** for symptom severity
5. **UPOINT phenotyping** to guide treatment
6. **Consider** cystoscopy, urodynamics, transrectal ultrasound if uncertainty

## Part V — Management

### Overarching principles (both sexes)<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[2]](#ref2)[[3]](#ref3)[[30]](#ref30)</sup>

- **Multimodal interdisciplinary treatment** is superior to single-agent therapy or surgery alone
- **Patient education** about pain mechanisms (especially central sensitization) is essential
- **Shared decision-making** with functional goal-setting
- **Treat all contributing domains simultaneously** — visceral, myofascial, neurologic, psychosocial
- **Opioids are not recommended** for CPP

### A. Non-pharmacologic therapies

| Therapy | Evidence |
|---|---|
| **Pelvic floor PT** | High-certainty evidence in women (SMD −1.69 for short-term pain); effective in both sexes; recommended by ACOG, AUA, EAU<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[30]](#ref30)[[31]](#ref31)[[32]](#ref32)</sup> |
| **CBT** | Small-to-moderate benefit for chronic pain syndromes; addresses catastrophizing, depression, anxiety<sup>[[2]](#ref2)[[30]](#ref30)</sup> |
| **Sex therapy** | Adjunct to PT for dyspareunia and genito-pelvic pain<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup> |
| **Self-management** | Moderate physical activity, stress management, mindfulness, dietary modification<sup>[[2]](#ref2)</sup> |
| **Acupuncture** | May be considered for musculoskeletal CPP (ACOG Level C); evidence inconsistent<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[33]](#ref33)</sup> |

### B. Pharmacologic therapies

| Class | Agents | Evidence / indication |
|---|---|---|
| **Alpha-blockers** | Tamsulosin, alfuzosin | First-line for CP/CPPS with urinary symptoms; ΔNIH-CPSI −10.8 to −4.8 vs placebo<sup>[[4]](#ref4)[[26]](#ref26)</sup> |
| **NSAIDs** | Ibuprofen, naproxen | Modest benefit in CP/CPPS (ΔNIH-CPSI −2.5 to −1.7); modest benefit in dysmenorrhea; no proven benefit in endometriosis<sup>[[4]](#ref4)[[8]](#ref8)[[9]](#ref9)</sup> |
| **SNRIs** | Duloxetine, venlafaxine | Recommended for neuropathic CPP (ACOG Level B); no CPP-specific RCTs<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[2]](#ref2)</sup> |
| **Gabapentinoids** | Gabapentin, pregabalin | Recommended for neuropathic CPP (ACOG Level B); pregabalin ΔNIH-CPSI −2.4 in CP/CPPS; one large CPP RCT showed no significant pain improvement<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[2]](#ref2)[[34]](#ref34)</sup> |
| **TCAs** | Amitriptyline, nortriptyline | First-line for IC/BPS and pudendal neuralgia; weaker evidence in general CPP<sup>[[2]](#ref2)[[10]](#ref10)</sup> |
| **Hormonal therapy** | COCs, progestins, GnRH agonists / antagonists | First-line for endometriosis-related CPP; continuous use preferred<sup>[[8]](#ref8)[[9]](#ref9)[[34]](#ref34)[[35]](#ref35)</sup> |
| **Phytotherapy** | Pollen extract (Cernilton) | Modest benefit in CP/CPPS (ΔNIH-CPSI −2.49)<sup>[[26]](#ref26)</sup> |
| **PDE5 inhibitors** | Tadalafil | Limited evidence in CP/CPPS<sup>[[26]](#ref26)</sup> |
| **Muscle relaxants** | Cyclobenzaprine, intravaginal diazepam | Insufficient / inconsistent evidence for myofascial CPP<sup>[[2]](#ref2)</sup> |

### C. Hormonal therapy for endometriosis-related CPP<sup>[[8]](#ref8)[[9]](#ref9)[[34]](#ref34)[[35]](#ref35)</sup>

- **First-line:** combined oral contraceptives (continuous use preferred) or progestins (norethindrone acetate, dienogest, LNG-IUS) — clinically significant pain reduction (MD −12.6 to −17.7 on 0–100 VAS vs placebo)
- **Second-line:** GnRH agonists (leuprolide) or antagonists (elagolix, relugolix combination) with add-back therapy
- **Third-line:** aromatase inhibitors (letrozole, anastrozole) — off-label
- **Limitation:** 25–34% recur within 12 months of stopping hormonal therapy; 11–19% have no pain reduction with hormonal medications

### D. Interventional therapies

- **Trigger point injections** (saline, anesthetic, steroid, or botulinum toxin A) for myofascial CPP — ACOG Level B; for protocols see [Myofascial Pelvic Pain](/docs/clinical-conditions/03h-pelvic-pain/myofascial-pelvic-pain)<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[2]](#ref2)</sup>
- **Pudendal nerve blocks** — diagnostic and therapeutic; up to 94% response in pudendal neuralgia<sup>[[15]](#ref15)</sup>
- **Sacral neuromodulation** — emerging evidence for refractory IC/BPS and CPP<sup>[[13]](#ref13)</sup>
- **Pulsed radiofrequency** — 95% pain reduction in refractory pudendal neuralgia<sup>[[15]](#ref15)[[19]](#ref19)</sup>
- **Ovarian vein embolization** — first-choice for PCS<sup>[[21]](#ref21)[[22]](#ref22)</sup>

### E. Surgical therapies

**Women:**

- **Laparoscopic excision of endometriosis** when hormonal therapies are ineffective or contraindicated; ~25% have recurrent pain even after hysterectomy<sup>[[8]](#ref8)[[36]](#ref36)</sup>
- **Hysterectomy** — refractory endometriosis / adenomyosis with other treatments exhausted; ovarian preservation in premenopausal women when feasible<sup>[[8]](#ref8)[[36]](#ref36)</sup>
- **Laparoscopic adhesiolysis is NOT recommended** — no benefit and may worsen outcomes (ACOG Level A)<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[30]](#ref30)</sup>
- **LUNA and presacral neurectomy do NOT improve CPP**<sup>[[30]](#ref30)</sup>
- **Pudendal nerve decompression** — refractory pudendal neuralgia after failed conservative / interventional therapy<sup>[[15]](#ref15)[[16]](#ref16)</sup>

**Men:**

- The 2025 AUA male CPP guideline recommends **initial non-surgical therapy** with shared decision-making; surgery is not first-line<sup>[[32]](#ref32)</sup>
- **Transurethral procedures (e.g., TURP) are not recommended** for CP/CPPS

### F. AUA male CPP guideline (2025) — multimodal pathway<sup>[[4]](#ref4)[[28]](#ref28)[[32]](#ref32)</sup>

1. **Alpha-blockers** for urinary symptoms
2. **NSAIDs** for pain / inflammation
3. **Pregabalin** for neurologic / systemic symptoms
4. **Pollen extract** as a phytotherapy option
5. **Pelvic floor PT** for the tenderness domain (27–64% of patients)
6. **CBT / psychological support** for the psychosocial domain
7. **Antibiotics are NOT recommended** without proven infection
8. **Referral** to specialists for psychosocial impairment and chronic non-urologic pain (fibromyalgia, IBS)

## Part VI — Special Topics

### Chronic overlapping pain conditions (COPCs)

CPP frequently coexists with other chronic pain syndromes, reflecting shared central-sensitization mechanisms:<sup>[[2]](#ref2)[[5]](#ref5)[[6]](#ref6)</sup>

- Fibromyalgia
- IBS
- IC/BPS
- Chronic fatigue syndrome
- Migraine / tension headaches
- Temporomandibular disorders
- Vulvodynia

The presence of multiple COPCs suggests a **nociplastic phenotype** in which central sensitization dominates — these patients benefit more from centrally-acting therapies (gabapentinoids, SNRIs, CBT) than from organ-specific peripheral treatments.<sup>[[2]](#ref2)[[5]](#ref5)</sup>

### Sex-specific differences

| | Women | Men |
|---|---|---|
| **Prevalence** | 15–26% | ~9.3% (CP/CPPS) |
| **Most common causes** | IBS, IC/BPS, endometriosis, myofascial | CP/CPPS, IC/BPS, myofascial |
| **Hormonal contribution** | Major (endometriosis, adenomyosis, cyclic pain) | Minor |
| **Pelvic floor involvement** | 50–90% | 27–64% |
| **Phenotyping tool** | Biopsychosocial framework | UPOINT(S) system |
| **Surgical options** | Endometriosis excision, hysterectomy (refractory) | Not first-line; no established surgical role |
| **Psychological burden** | Depression 12–33%; trauma history common | Depression, anxiety, panic disorder elevated |

## Key Principles

- **CPP is a syndrome, not a single disease** — most patients have multiple contributing conditions<sup>[[2]](#ref2)[[3]](#ref3)</sup>
- **Central sensitization** is the unifying mechanism explaining pain persistence, COPC overlap, and poor response to organ-specific treatment alone<sup>[[2]](#ref2)</sup>
- **Musculoskeletal / myofascial dysfunction is present in 50–90% of women and 27–64% of men** — and is the most commonly overlooked contributor<sup>[[2]](#ref2)[[4]](#ref4)</sup>
- **Biopsychosocial, multimodal, interdisciplinary treatment** is essential<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[2]](#ref2)</sup>
- **Multimodal pelvic floor PT** has the strongest evidence (high certainty) among non-pharmacologic treatments<sup>[[31]](#ref31)</sup>
- **Opioids are not recommended** for CPP in either sex<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[2]](#ref2)</sup>
- **Hormonal therapy** is first-line for endometriosis-related CPP but does not address non-gynecologic pain generators<sup>[[8]](#ref8)</sup>
- **Alpha-blockers** are first-line for CP/CPPS with urinary symptoms<sup>[[4]](#ref4)</sup>
- **Antibiotics should not be used** for CP/CPPS without proven infection<sup>[[28]](#ref28)</sup>
- **Laparoscopic adhesiolysis, LUNA, and presacral neurectomy are not recommended** — they don't improve CPP and may worsen outcomes<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[30]](#ref30)</sup>
- **Pelvic congestion syndrome** may account for up to 30% of CPP in women but remains controversial — ACOG considers the evidence insufficient to confirm causation<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[20]](#ref20)</sup>
