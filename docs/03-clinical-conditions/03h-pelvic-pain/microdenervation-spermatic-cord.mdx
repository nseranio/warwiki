# Microdenervation of the Spermatic Cord (MDSC)

**Microdenervation of the spermatic cord (MDSC)**, also reported as microsurgical spermatic cord denervation (MSCD), is a testis-sparing neuroablative operation for chronic scrotal content pain in which the nerve-bearing tissues of the spermatic cord are divided under the operating microscope while the testicular arteries, a set of lymphatics and usually the vas deferens and its vessels are preserved. AUA 2025 states that clinicians may recommend MDSC for chronic scrotal content pain, especially after a favorable response to spermatic cord block (Statement 34; Conditional Recommendation, Grade C).<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[2]](#ref2)</sup> EAU recommends offering microsurgical denervation when testicular pain improves after a spermatic cord block (weak recommendation).<sup>[[3]](#ref3)</sup>

For the definition, differential diagnosis, evaluation and nonsurgical ladder, see [Chronic Scrotal Content Pain](/docs/clinical-conditions/03h-pelvic-pain/chronic-scrotal-content-pain). Block technique is on [Nerve Blocks](/docs/foundations/perioperative-care/intraoperative-care/nerve-blocks#spermatic-cord-block).

---

## Rationale

[Levine](/docs/roots/surgeons/h-r/laurence-levine) and colleagues described microsurgical denervation in 1996 as a surgical alternative to orchiectomy for chronic orchialgia, and in 2001 reported it as the primary surgical treatment in men with temporary relief after cord block.<sup>[[4]](#ref4)[[5]](#ref5)</sup> Heidenreich and colleagues reported a parallel European experience in 2002.<sup>[[6]](#ref6)</sup>

The histologic basis is the distribution of injured nerves in the cord. In biopsies from 57 denervation procedures, Wallerian degeneration was found in 84% of orchialgia specimens and 20% of controls, concentrated in three sites: the cremasteric muscle layer (posterior portion most affected), the perivasal tissues and vasal sheath, and the posterior periarterial lipomatous tissue.<sup>[[7]](#ref7)</sup> Immunohistochemical mapping found about 50% of cord nerves near the vas and 20% in the spermatic fascia, with sensory and sympathetic fibers co-localized in the same nerves.<sup>[[8]](#ref8)</sup> Complete denervation divides all of this tissue; targeted denervation divides the three sites of the "trifecta nerve complex" and leaves the rest of the cord intact.<sup>[[7]](#ref7)[[20]](#ref20)</sup>

## Indications and Patient Selection

Candidates have chronic scrotal content pain of at least 3 months, pain localized to the testis or cord, a negative evaluation for structural, infectious and referred causes, and failure of conservative treatment.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[18]](#ref18)[[21]](#ref21)</sup>

| Selection factor | Evidence |
|---|---|
| Positive spermatic cord block | Temporary relief predicted sustained improvement (p = 0.05) and was an independent predictor of response (p = 0.03) in 74 men (77 units)<sup>[[9]](#ref9)</sup>. In placebo-controlled screening of 180 men, 37% had a positive response and were offered surgery<sup>[[10]](#ref10)</sup>. A multicenter trial selected 50 men by response to a series of blocks that included a saline sham<sup>[[11]](#ref11)</sup> |
| Idiopathic pain | The population in most series<sup>[[5]](#ref5)[[18]](#ref18)</sup> |
| Post-vasectomy pain syndrome | 71% surgical success in 28 units, median follow-up 10 months<sup>[[13]](#ref13)</sup>; a systematic review found nine retrospective denervation studies involving 213 men with chronic orchialgia, but only one PVPS-specific study (17 men, without long-term follow-up)<sup>[[14]](#ref14)</sup> |
| Prior failed ipsilateral surgery | Complete relief in 50% of men with prior failed surgery, compared with 64% of men without prior surgery<sup>[[12]](#ref12)</sup>. In a separate retrospective cohort of 45 block-responsive men with persistent pain after varicocelectomy, 7/45 reported complete relief, 24/45 reached NRS ≤1, and 38/45 had &gt;50% pain reduction at mean 14-month follow-up.<sup>[[33]](#ref33)</sup> |
| Prior inguinal hernia repair | First exclude recurrent hernia and mesh-related nerve entrapment; a targeted-denervation cohort excluded men with pain or paresthesia confined to the lateral scrotum and medial thigh.<sup>[[20]](#ref20)</sup> A retrospective post-hernia series reported complete resolution in 20 of 47 cords (43%) and improvement in 41 of 47 (87%) at median 7 weeks.<sup>[[15]](#ref15)</sup> HerniaSurge 2023 weakly suggests MDSC only in research settings for chronic postoperative inguinal pain.<sup>[[31]](#ref31)</sup> |
| Pelvic floor muscle spasm | Independently predicted failure (OR 3.95, p = 0.02); failure in 47% with spasm and 23% without<sup>[[16]](#ref16)</sup> |
| Fertility wishes | Determine whether the vas is stripped and preserved or ligated<sup>[[17]](#ref17)</sup> |

Perineal, bilateral scrotal, penile or suprapubic pain, or voiding or ejaculatory symptoms, point to the CP/CPPS phenotype rather than isolated CSCP.<sup>[[2]](#ref2)</sup> Because pelvic floor muscle spasm independently predicted failure, a pelvic floor examination is part of preoperative selection.<sup>[[16]](#ref16)</sup>

## Preoperative Counseling

- **Expected result.** Complete relief in 49–80% of followed cases, testicular units or patients across most cited series (96% of 35 men in one), with partial relief or at least 50% pain reduction in a further 9–34%; outcome definitions and follow-up differ between series (see [Outcomes](#outcomes)).<sup>[[5]](#ref5)[[6]](#ref6)[[11]](#ref11)[[18]](#ref18)[[21]](#ref21)</sup>
- **Failure and recurrence.** No change in pain in 12% of 95 testicular units and 17% of 860 robotic cases with follow-up; in another series, 5 patients had recurrent pain more than 6 months after surgery among 103 procedures in 93 patients.<sup>[[16]](#ref16)[[18]](#ref18)[[21]](#ref21)</sup>
- **Testis.** MDSC aims to preserve the testis, but ischemia requiring orchiectomy has been reported in one patient in the cited robotic series. Atrophy (2 of 79 men in one conventional series) and hydrocele are also reported (see [Complications](#complications)).<sup>[[18]](#ref18)[[31]](#ref31)</sup>
- **Fertility.** Ligation of the vas ends fertility on that side; stripping preserves the vas. In a retrospective multicenter comparison of full denervation in block responders without prior vasectomy, hernia or mesh-related nerve entrapment, complete pain resolution was recorded in 25/37 after stripping and 30/45 tabulated outcomes after ligation at a median 12 months; the groups differed in prior inguinoscrotal surgery.<sup>[[17]](#ref17)</sup>
- **Sensation.** Excision of an ilioinguinal segment can alter sensation in that nerve's cutaneous territory; Strom and Levine reported no complaints of hypoesthesia or hyperesthesia among 79 men, and the robotic intra-abdominal approach was described as a way to preserve the ilioinguinal nerve.<sup>[[18]](#ref18)[[19]](#ref19)[[22]](#ref22)</sup>
- **Evidence quality.** All outcome data are retrospective or uncontrolled case series; there is no sham-controlled surgical trial.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[14]](#ref14)</sup>

## Approaches

| Approach | Description | Reported results |
|---|---|---|
| Low inguinal microsurgical, complete | Strom and Levine used a low inguinal incision at the external ring, then delivered and skeletonized the cord under the microscope with micro-Doppler identification of the arteries; Tatem and Kovac also describe a subinguinal alternative<sup>[[18]](#ref18)[[19]](#ref19)</sup> | Complete relief in 67 of 95 units (71%)<sup>[[18]](#ref18)</sup> |
| Subinguinal microsurgical, targeted | Division limited to cremasteric fibers, perivasal tissue and posterior lipomatous tissue<sup>[[20]](#ref20)</sup> | Resolution 69.8% targeted compared with 66.7% full; operative time 21 compared with 53 minutes<sup>[[20]](#ref20)</sup> |
| Robot-assisted microsurgical, targeted | Robotic platform used for the microsurgical trifecta ligation<sup>[[21]](#ref21)</sup> | Significant reduction in 83% of 860 cases; complete resolution 49%, median follow-up 24 months<sup>[[21]](#ref21)</sup> |
| Robotic intra-abdominal (targeted robotic intra-abdominal denervation) | Division of genitofemoral and inferior hypogastric contributions above the internal ring, preserving the ilioinguinal nerve; described for failed cord denervation and post-orchiectomy pain<sup>[[22]](#ref22)</sup> | Single-group experience; limited published data<sup>[[22]](#ref22)</sup> |

## Operative Technique

Levine and Matkov previously reported complete denervation.<sup>[[5]](#ref5)</sup> The steps below describe the later low inguinal operation reported by Strom and Levine; Tatem and Kovac also describe a subinguinal alternative, and surgeons vary the dissection sequence.<sup>[[18]](#ref18)[[19]](#ref19)</sup>

1. **Anesthesia and positioning.** Supine, under general or regional anesthesia; a spermatic cord block can supplement either (see [Nerve Blocks](/docs/foundations/perioperative-care/intraoperative-care/nerve-blocks#spermatic-cord-block)).
2. **Incision.** Make a low inguinal incision to expose the external inguinal ring in the Strom and Levine approach; Tatem and Kovac also describe a subinguinal alternative modeled on microsurgical varicocelectomy.<sup>[[18]](#ref18)[[19]](#ref19)</sup>
3. **Ilioinguinal nerve.** Identify the nerve on the surface of the cord as it emerges from the ring. Excise a 2–3 cm segment and ligate the cut ends. With the inguinal approach, burying the proximal stump under the external ring has been described to reduce neuroma formation.<sup>[[19]](#ref19)</sup>
4. **Cord delivery.** Deliver the cord into the wound and bring it under the operating microscope.<sup>[[19]](#ref19)</sup>
5. **Microscope and Doppler.** Under the operating microscope, open the external and internal spermatic fascia. Identify and protect the testicular, cremasteric and deferential arteries and their branches with micro-Doppler, noting their location, tubular morphology and visible pulsation; systematically identify the veins, vas and lymphatics.<sup>[[18]](#ref18)[[19]](#ref19)</sup>
6. **Lymphatics.** Identify and preserve several lymphatic channels to reduce the risk of hydrocele.<sup>[[8]](#ref8)[[19]](#ref19)</sup>
7. **Vas deferens.** Preserve the vas and the deferential artery and strip the perivasal sheath and adventitia over a segment of the vas to remove the perivasal nerves. El-Achkar and colleagues describe ligation and division of the vas with its artery as an alternative in men without fertility wishes; their retrospective comparison recorded complete pain resolution in 25/37 after stripping and 30/45 tabulated after ligation at median 12 months, without establishing equivalent efficacy.<sup>[[17]](#ref17)[[19]](#ref19)</sup>
8. **Division of the remaining cord.** Ligate and divide the cremasteric fibers, the internal spermatic veins, the posterior lipomatous and periarterial tissue and all other tissue that does not contain the identified arteries, preserved lymphatics or vas. The genital branch of the genitofemoral nerve travels within the cord and is divided with this tissue.<sup>[[7]](#ref7)[[18]](#ref18)[[19]](#ref19)</sup>
9. **Closure.** At the end only the arteries, preserved lymphatics and the vas (if preserved) remain in continuity. Confirm pulsatile flow in the preserved arteries with micro-Doppler before closure; Strom and Levine applied topical papaverine when arterial spasm reduced flow. Return the cord and close the wound.<sup>[[18]](#ref18)[[19]](#ref19)</sup>

**Targeted variant.** Only the three trifecta sites are ligated: the cremasteric fibers, the perivasal sheath and the posterior lipomatous tissue. Targeted denervation addresses the trifecta nerve complex.<sup>[[7]](#ref7)</sup> In a sequential retrospective comparison of 43 targeted and 39 full denervations, complete pain resolution was 69.8% and 66.7% (p = 0.88), and operative times were 21 and 53 minutes, respectively.<sup>[[20]](#ref20)</sup> In a 2026 prospective nonrandomized comparison of men with unilateral idiopathic orchialgia, 50 of 60 enrolled men completed 12-month follow-up; complete relief occurred in 74.1% (20/27) after standard and 60.9% (14/23) after targeted denervation.<sup>[[34]](#ref34)</sup>

**Pearls.**

- Doppler identification of the arteries before any tissue is divided is intended to prevent inadvertent arterial ligation and testicular ischemia.<sup>[[19]](#ref19)</sup>
- Lymphatic preservation is intended to reduce postoperative hydrocele.<sup>[[8]](#ref8)[[19]](#ref19)</sup>
- After inguinal hernia repair, complete resolution was reported in 43% of cords at a median 7 weeks; Strom and Levine reported 71% of units in a mixed-etiology cohort at a mean 20.3 months.<sup>[[15]](#ref15)[[18]](#ref18)</sup>

## Postoperative Care

Postoperative care resembles that after microsurgical varicocelectomy: outpatient discharge, scrotal support, cold packs and short-course non-opioid analgesia. Pain is reassessed with the scale used before surgery. Strom and Levine proposed stopping chronic medication as a possible benefit of successful denervation but did not report medication cessation as a measured outcome.<sup>[[18]](#ref18)</sup>

## Outcomes

| Series | Design and population | Follow-up | Results |
|---|---|---|---|
| Levine and Matkov 2001<sup>[[5]](#ref5)</sup> | 27 men, 33 units, all with temporary relief after cord block | Mean 20 months | Complete relief 76%, partial 9.1%, none 15% |
| Heidenreich 2002<sup>[[6]](#ref6)</sup> | 35 men | Mean 31.5 months | 34 of 35 (96%) pain-free; no atrophy or hydrocele |
| Strom and Levine 2008<sup>[[18]](#ref18)</sup> | 79 men, 95 units, positive cord block | Mean 20.3 months | Complete relief 71%, partial 17%, unchanged 12%; no patient worse |
| Benson 2013<sup>[[9]](#ref9)</sup> | 74 men, 77 units | Mean 10 months | Mean pain 8 before block; mean 2 after MDSC (73% decrease) |
| Larsen 2013<sup>[[12]](#ref12)</sup> | 68 men (70 units) treated 2006–2010; 31 units after prior failed ipsilateral surgery | Retrospective; mean 10 months | Complete relief in 64% of men without prior surgery and 50% of men after failed surgery |
| Oomen 2014<sup>[[10]](#ref10)</sup> | 180 men screened with double-blind placebo-controlled blocks; 58 testicular units underwent denervation, including three after negative blocks | Mean 42.8 months | 86.2% had at least 50% pain reduction; 51.7% pain-free |
| Marconi 2015<sup>[[11]](#ref11)</sup> | Prospective multicenter open-label, 50 men | 6 months | Pain-free 80%, partial 12%, no change 8% |
| Calixte 2018<sup>[[21]](#ref21)</sup> | Robot-assisted targeted, 860 cases with follow-up | Median 24 months | Significant reduction 83% (complete 49%, at least 50% reduction 34%); no change 17% |
| Tan 2018<sup>[[13]](#ref13)</sup> | PVPS, 27 men, 28 units | Median 10 months | Surgical success 71% |
| Kavoussi 2019<sup>[[20]](#ref20)</sup> | Targeted compared with full, single surgeon | Retrospective | Resolution 69.8% targeted and 66.7% full; failure 7.0% and 15.4% (not significant) |
| Murthy 2020<sup>[[16]](#ref16)</sup> | 103 procedures | Retrospective | Success (complete resolution of bothersome pain) 73%; 77% excluding pelvic floor muscle spasm |
| El-Achkar 2025<sup>[[17]](#ref17)</sup> | 85 men, multicenter, vas stripping (37) or ligation (48) | Median 12 months | Complete response in 25/37 (67.6%) after stripping and 30/45 with tabulated responses (66.7%) in the 48-man ligation arm; no testicular atrophy |
| Romanelli 2026<sup>[[15]](#ref15)</sup> | 42 men, 47 cords, after inguinal hernia repair | Median 7 weeks | Complete resolution 43%, improvement 87% |

The series share limitations. They are retrospective or uncontrolled, mostly from high-volume microsurgeons, and define success differently (complete relief, at least 50% reduction, or resolution of bothersome pain).<sup>[[16]](#ref16)[[21]](#ref21)</sup> Most series selected patients who responded to cord block, so their success rates describe block responders.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[9]](#ref9)[[14]](#ref14)</sup> AUA 2025 cites a 57% negative predictive value for a negative block in a retrospective cohort of 1,112 and says nonresponders may still be considered after counseling that relief is less likely.<sup>[[2]](#ref2)</sup> In the robotic series, PIQ-6 improvement was reported in 67% of patients assessed at six months and 83% assessed at four years; median cohort follow-up was 24 months (range 1–70).<sup>[[21]](#ref21)</sup>

## Complications

| Complication | Reported frequency and notes |
|---|---|
| Testicular atrophy or ischemic loss | Atrophy in 2 of 79 men (95 operated units) in the Strom and Levine series; none in the Heidenreich series or either arm of the El-Achkar comparison. In the cited robotic cohort, two testicular artery injuries were repaired intraoperatively and one ischemic testis required orchiectomy.<sup>[[6]](#ref6)[[17]](#ref17)[[18]](#ref18)[[31]](#ref31)</sup> |
| Hydrocele | 2 of 79 men (95 operated units) in Strom and Levine; none in Heidenreich; one of 50 men in Marconi required reoperation for hydrocele; El-Achkar reported 0/37 after vas stripping and 3/48 after vas ligation.<sup>[[6]](#ref6)[[11]](#ref11)[[17]](#ref17)[[18]](#ref18)</sup> |
| Hematocele | One of 50 men in Marconi required reoperation for hematocele.<sup>[[11]](#ref11)</sup> |
| Incisional hematoma or seroma | One incisional hematoma and one spontaneously draining seroma among 79 men (95 operated units)<sup>[[18]](#ref18)</sup> |
| Wound infection | 2 superficial wound infections among 79 men (95 operated units)<sup>[[18]](#ref18)</sup> |
| Altered cutaneous sensation | Excision of an ilioinguinal segment can alter sensation in its cutaneous territory; Strom and Levine reported no complaints of hypoesthesia or hyperesthesia among 79 men after conventional MDSC with ilioinguinal excision. The intra-abdominal approach was described as a way to preserve the ilioinguinal nerve.<sup>[[18]](#ref18)[[19]](#ref19)[[22]](#ref22)</sup> |
| Persistent pain | No change in 12% of 95 testicular units and 17% of 860 followed robotic cases<sup>[[18]](#ref18)[[21]](#ref21)</sup> |
| Recurrent pain | 5 patients more than 6 months after surgery among 103 procedures in 93 patients<sup>[[16]](#ref16)</sup> |
| Loss of fertility on the operated side | After vas ligation, by design<sup>[[17]](#ref17)</sup> |

After failed denervation, the evaluation is repeated for a second pain generator before further surgery. In the Cleveland Clinic series, two men whose denervation failed improved after genitofemoral nerve block.<sup>[[16]](#ref16)</sup>

## Comparison With Other Surgical Options

| Operation | Best indication | AUA 2025 position | Reported outcome | Testis and fertility |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| MDSC | Cord-localized idiopathic pain or PVPS with positive cord block | Statement 34, Conditional, Grade C<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup> | Complete relief in 34/35 men after microsurgical testicular denervation at mean 31.5 months<sup>[[6]](#ref6)</sup>, 67/95 testicular units (71%) after conventional MDSC at mean 20.3 months<sup>[[18]](#ref18)</sup>, and 426/860 cases after targeted robotic-assisted MDSC at median 24 months<sup>[[21]](#ref21)</sup> | Testis preserved; vas preserved unless ligated |
| Vasectomy reversal | PVPS, especially pain exacerbated by ejaculation; AUA discussion suggests bilateral vasovasostomy and cautions that vasoepididymostomy may aggravate pain in some men<sup>[[2]](#ref2)</sup> | Statement 35, Expert Opinion<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup> | Among 31 survey responders from 123 vasectomy-reversal procedures, 34% reported complete pain resolution; mean preoperative pain was 6.4 and median postoperative pain was 2.7.<sup>[[23]](#ref23)</sup> A 2026 review of five case series (123 patients) reported pain-score reductions of 60–83%, a distinct endpoint.<sup>[[32]](#ref32)</sup> | May restore fertility; a review reported mean patency of 87% and pregnancy of 49% after microsurgical vasectomy reversal.<sup>[[35]](#ref35)</sup> |
| Epididymectomy | Pain and tenderness focal to the epididymis | Statement 39, Expert Opinion<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup> | Cured or improved in 162 of 225 men (72%) at last documented visit; median follow-up 12 weeks<sup>[[24]](#ref24)</sup> | Ends fertility on that side |
| Inguinal triple neurectomy | Post-herniorrhaphy neuropathic pain | Not addressed by AUA; HerniaSurge 2018 supports selected use<sup>[[30]](#ref30)</sup>, while its 2023 update weakly suggests tailored neurectomy, mesh removal or both for chronic postoperative inguinal pain and suggests spermatic cord denervation only in research settings<sup>[[31]](#ref31)</sup> | Described in 49 patients<sup>[[25]](#ref25)</sup> | Cord not mobilized |
| Varicocelectomy | Painful varicocele concordant with symptoms | Not addressed | Dull pain resolves more often than dragging or sharp pain; microsurgical subinguinal repair favored<sup>[[26]](#ref26)</sup> | Preserves testis |
| Inguinal orchiectomy with entire cord | Last resort after failed less invasive treatment and a pain-relieving spermatic cord block<sup>[[2]](#ref2)</sup> | Statement 40, Expert Opinion; inguinal rather than scrotal<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup> | Complete pain relief in 11/15 men (73%) after inguinal and 5/9 (reported as 55%) after scrotal orchiectomy in Davis 1990<sup>[[27]](#ref27)</sup>; one review reports 20–75% for significant pain reduction after orchiectomy<sup>[[22]](#ref22)</sup>, and another summarizes orchiectomy success as 55–75%<sup>[[28]](#ref28)</sup> | Testis lost |

Because orchiectomy fails in a substantial minority, reviews place cord denervation before extirpative surgery in idiopathic pain.<sup>[[28]](#ref28)[[29]](#ref29)</sup>
