# Pelvic Venous Disorders (PeVD) and Pelvic Congestion Syndrome

**Pelvic venous disorders (PeVD)** encompass a spectrum of conditions caused by pelvic venous reflux, obstruction, or both. Their best-characterized clinical phenotype, pelvic congestion syndrome (PCS) or pelvic venous insufficiency (PVI), is implicated in up to 30% of chronic pelvic pain (CPP) presentations in women and remains substantially underdiagnosed.<sup>[[12]](#ref12)</sup> PeVD is a potentially treatable cause of chronic pelvic pain to consider alongside urologic, gynecologic, and myofascial causes.<sup>[[12]](#ref12)</sup> The deep uterine vein lies close to the ureter and inferior hypogastric plexus, informing uterine transplantation and nerve-sparing radical hysterectomy.<sup>[[21]](#ref21)[[22]](#ref22)</sup> Selected retrospective cohorts also report pelvic venous abnormalities among patients with postural orthostatic tachycardia syndrome (POTS) or orthostatic intolerance.<sup>[[28]](#ref28)</sup>

For the broader differential of pelvic pain, see [Chronic Pelvic Pain](/docs/clinical-conditions/03h-pelvic-pain/chronic-pelvic-pain).

## Normal Pelvic Venous Anatomy

The female pelvic venous system is highly interconnected and variable between patients, with rich collateral potential that has both protective and diagnostic implications.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[2]](#ref2)</sup>

### Principal drainage pathways

- **Ovarian (gonadal) veins.** Drain the ovaries, fallopian tubes, and superior uterus. The left ovarian vein drains into the left renal vein; the right ovarian vein drains directly into the IVC, typically within 25 mm below the right renal vein ostium.<sup>[[3]](#ref3)</sup> The longer left ovarian vein column and its perpendicular insertion into the renal vein predispose to reflux and congestion on the left.<sup>[[4]](#ref4)[[5]](#ref5)</sup>
- **Uterine (inferior uterine) veins.** Drain the inferior uterus and cervix; course laterally through the base of the broad ligament to the internal iliac veins. Typically plexiform.<sup>[[6]](#ref6)</sup>
- **Utero-ovarian arcade.** In six postmenopausal cadavers, the arcade averaged 3.71 mm in diameter, similar to the uterine and ovarian veins; the authors inferred that these vessels form a shared venous pool without preferential drainage.<sup>[[2]](#ref2)</sup>
- **Internal iliac veins.** Receive uterine, vaginal plexus, vesical plexus, and rectal plexus drainage; tributaries include obturator, gluteal, and pudendal veins.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup>
- **Ramus communicans.** A cross-pelvic anastomosis between the left and right parametrial plexuses enabling contralateral drainage.<sup>[[2]](#ref2)</sup>
- **Fallopian tube drainage.** Via three separate veins (internal, median, external tubal).<sup>[[2]](#ref2)</sup>

### Embryologic basis for left/right asymmetry

The pelvic venous system derives from the caudal cardinal veins (CCVs) during weeks 4–10. The infrarenal IVC develops from the right CCV; the renal segment arises from subcardinal veins; the iliac veins form by gradual caudal CCV extension.<sup>[[18]](#ref18)[[19]](#ref19)</sup> Embryologic remodeling of the caudal cardinal and subcardinal veins produces the asymmetric IVC and renal venous system.<sup>[[18]](#ref18)[[19]](#ref19)</sup> In adults, the left gonadal vein usually drains into the left renal vein and the right directly into the IVC.<sup>[[3]](#ref3)</sup> Nutcracker and May-Thurner syndromes involve separate arterial compressions of the left renal and left common iliac veins, respectively.<sup>[[10]](#ref10)[[31]](#ref31)</sup>

### The deep uterine vein (DUV) — surgical landmark

The deep uterine vein arises from the merger of cervical, vesical, and vaginal veins, courses through the paracervix in a descending or posterior direction, and drains into the internal iliac vein.<sup>[[20]](#ref20)</sup> In a dissection of eight fresh female cadavers (16 hemipelves), a separate superficial uterine vein accompanied the larger deep uterine vein in 25% of cases.<sup>[[21]](#ref21)</sup>

The DUV is operatively important because it divides the lateral uterine aspect into two zones: a superior vascular zone (uterine artery, ureter) and an inferior nervous zone (autonomic pathway or inferior hypogastric plexus).<sup>[[20]](#ref20)[[22]](#ref22)</sup> Technical videos describe early identification of the DUV–pelvic splanchnic nerve crossing point as a landmark for nerve-sparing radical hysterectomy and propose that a vascular-centered approach may limit bleeding; neither report provides comparative blood-loss outcomes.<sup>[[22]](#ref22)[[23]](#ref23)</sup> In uterine transplantation, the spatial relationship of the DUV with the ureter (passing posterior to the superficial uterine vein and anterior to the DUV) and the inferior hypogastric plexus is an indispensable guide for safe graft procurement.<sup>[[21]](#ref21)</sup>

### Uterine venous nomenclature for transplantation

The US Uterus Transplant Consortium has proposed a standardized nomenclature renaming the four veins originating from the uterus as superior and inferior uterine veins:<sup>[[6]](#ref6)</sup>

- **Inferior uterine veins.** Drain the inferior uterus, plexiform, course laterally through the broad ligament base to the internal iliac veins (equivalent to conventional "uterine veins").
- **Superior uterine veins.** Drain the superior uterus to the ovaries through the suspensory ligament (previously lumped with "ovarian veins" but functionally distinct).

### Collateral pathways

| Pathway | Components |
| --- | --- |
| Deep | Lumbar veins, sacral veins, vertebral venous plexuses |
| Superficial | Circumflex iliac, epigastric vessels |
| Intermediate | Gonadal veins, ovarian / uterine plexuses |
| Iliofemoral | Various pathways bypassing iliac obstruction |
| Portosystemic | Rectal venous plexuses and veins of Retzius connect mesenteric and systemic veins, especially with portal hypertension<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup> |

## Pathophysiology

PeVD is classified into two principal mechanisms (which frequently coexist):<sup>[[7]](#ref7)[[8]](#ref8)</sup>

1. **Primary venous reflux.** Most commonly involving the ovarian veins from incompetent valves. Reflux is common in symptomatic pelvic varices, but reported proportions depend on selection and imaging methods.<sup>[[5]](#ref5)[[9]](#ref9)</sup> Among 22 asymptomatic renal-donor candidates in a small MRI series, the left gonadal vein was visualized in 21 and passive left renal-to-gonadal reflux was suggested in 8 of those 21 examinations (38%); reflux on imaging alone is insufficient for diagnosis.<sup>[[5]](#ref5)</sup>
2. **Secondary venous obstruction.** Two classic entities:
   - **Nutcracker syndrome.** Compression of the left renal vein between the aorta and superior mesenteric artery, with elevated pressure transmitted to the left ovarian vein.<sup>[[10]](#ref10)[[11]](#ref11)</sup>
   - **May-Thurner (iliac vein compression) syndrome.** Compression of the left common iliac vein by the overlying right common iliac artery, with reflux into the ipsilateral internal iliac vein.<sup>[[10]](#ref10)[[11]](#ref11)</sup>

### Hormonal contribution

Hormonal effects on pelvic venous distensibility remain uncertain.<sup>[[26]](#ref26)</sup>

- **Estrogen.** In an observational study of 90 menopausal women, upper-tertile estradiol was associated with lower-limb varicose veins (OR 3.6, 95% CI 1.1–11.6) and increased lower-limb venous distensibility (OR 4.4, 95% CI 1.2–15.5).<sup>[[24]](#ref24)</sup> Immunohistochemistry of normal and varicose saphenous veins detected estrogen and progesterone receptors in the intima and media.<sup>[[45]](#ref45)</sup>
- **Progesterone and pregnancy.** Progesterone relaxes venous smooth muscle, the gravid uterus exerts mechanical compression, and blood volume rises during pregnancy. Among women who develop varicose-vein problems during pregnancy, symptoms appear in the first trimester in 70–80%, often within 2–3 weeks of conception.<sup>[[25]](#ref25)</sup> Increased uterine blood flow during pregnancy may distend pelvic veins enough to separate valve leaflets, allowing reflux that can persist postpartum.<sup>[[26]](#ref26)</sup>
- **Postmenopausal pattern.** The premenopausal-parous predominance of PCS, with relative postmenopausal rarity, supports the hormonal hypothesis.<sup>[[8]](#ref8)</sup>

## Clinical Presentation

PCS occurs predominantly in premenopausal parous women, although nulliparous patients are well-described.<sup>[[12]](#ref12)[[15]](#ref15)</sup> Cardinal features:

- **Dull, aching pelvic pain** worsened by prolonged standing, intercourse, and the premenstrual period.
- **Post-coital pain.** Suggestive in context, but not pathognomonic.<sup>[[12]](#ref12)</sup>
- Dyspareunia, dysmenorrhea, dysuria.
- **Vulvar and perivulvar varicosities** (especially post-pregnancy); reported in about 22% of pregnant women, about 10% of non-pregnant women, and up to 34% of women with incompetent pelvic venous plexuses.<sup>[[27]](#ref27)</sup>
- Urologic symptoms: hematuria and urinary frequency in the absence of infection.<sup>[[13]](#ref13)[[14]](#ref14)</sup>
- Lower extremity varicose veins of pelvic origin (posteromedial thigh, vulva).

Imaging prevalence anchors. In 1,042 abdominal and pelvic CT scans of women aged 25–65, 143 (13.7%) showed ovarian-vein dilation by the study’s grading scheme, which included mild dilation (&lt;6 mm); 96 of 143 positive scans were bilateral.<sup>[[4]](#ref4)</sup> Chronic pelvic pain of unclear etiology with dilated veins was documented in 8% of premenopausal women in a large imaging series.<sup>[[14]](#ref14)</sup>

### Pelvic escape points and extra-pelvic varicose veins

Reflux from the pelvis can reach vulvar and leg veins through four main escape-point families comprising seven to eight described individual sites:<sup>[[9]](#ref9)[[27]](#ref27)</sup>

| Escape point | Route | Clinical signature |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Inguinal** | Round ligament → groin / vulva | Vulvar / labial varix |
| **Obturator** | Obturator canal → medial thigh | Medial-thigh varicose veins |
| **Pudendal (including perineal)** | Clitoral, intermediate-labial and perineal branches to vulva / perineum | Vulvar and perineal varices<sup>[[27]](#ref27)</sup> |
| **Gluteal** | Greater sciatic foramen → posterior thigh / buttock | Posterior-thigh varix; can include intra/peri-neural (sciatic, tibial) varices from inferior gluteal tributary |

Most patients with extra-pelvic varicose veins of pelvic origin do not report pelvic symptoms; pelvic pain is reported in under 10% (7% in one cited series).<sup>[[27]](#ref27)</sup> A 2023 SVS/AVF/AVLS consensus statement advises against pelvic venous imaging for medial-thigh or vulvar varices when pelvic venous symptoms are absent.<sup>[[46]](#ref46)</sup> For extra-pelvic varices of pelvic origin without pelvic symptoms, a "bottom-up" treatment approach (US-guided foam sclerotherapy or ligation of escape points and extra-pelvic varicosities) is usually sufficient initially; ovarian vein embolization or iliac vein stenting may be considered if local treatment fails with rapid recurrence or pelvic symptoms develop.<sup>[[27]](#ref27)</sup>

## Classification — The SVP System

The American Vein & Lymphatic Society Symptoms-Varices-Pathophysiology (SVP) classification standardizes nomenclature for PeVD, complementing the CEAP system used for lower-extremity venous disease.<sup>[[9]](#ref9)</sup> The pelvis is partitioned into four anatomic zones:

| Zone | Anatomy |
| --- | --- |
| **Zone 1** | Left renal vein |
| **Zone 2** | Gonadal and internal iliac veins; pelvic venous plexuses |
| **Zone 3** | Pelvic-origin extra-pelvic transitional veins (escape points) |
| **Zone 4** | Lower extremity veins (classified per CEAP) |

SVP notation is structured as S (symptoms) V (varices) P (pathophysiology), with P incorporating Anatomy, Hemodynamics, and Etiology as subscripts.<sup>[[9]](#ref9)</sup>

| Example | SVP notation |
| --- | --- |
| Bilateral ovarian reflux with pelvic pain | S₂V₂P(BGV,R,NT) |
| Nutcracker syndrome | S₁V₁P(LRV,O,NT) |
| Asymptomatic left common iliac vein compression anatomy | S₀V₀P(LCIV,O,NT); not itself a symptomatic syndrome |

## Diagnosis

Pelvic venous engorgement and gonadal vein reflux can be seen in asymptomatic women, so imaging findings must be correlated with the clinical syndrome.<sup>[[5]](#ref5)[[10]](#ref10)</sup>

### Imaging hierarchy

| Modality | Role | Key criteria / yield |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Transvaginal US (TVUS)** | First-line | Assess reflux, caliber and collateral pathways dynamically. Proposed markers include ovarian vein diameter above 7–8 mm, slow flow (&lt;3 cm/s) and myometrial vein dilatation above 5 mm; thresholds are not standardized.<sup>[[12]](#ref12)</sup> One study confirmed PeVD on venography in 187/195 TVUS-positive patients (95.9%); this is not sensitivity/specificity in an unselected population<sup>[[12]](#ref12)[[16]](#ref16)</sup> |
| **Catheter venography** | Historic gold standard | Direct visualization of reflux; pressure measurements<sup>[[10]](#ref10)[[17]](#ref17)</sup> |
| **CT / MRI** | Structural causes; planning | Nutcracker / May-Thurner anatomy; ovarian vein grading on CT — mild (< 6 mm), moderate (6–8 mm), severe (> 8 mm)<sup>[[4]](#ref4)</sup> |
| **IVUS** | Invasive assessment of suspected iliac obstruction when considering intervention | Complements venography; anatomic narrowing alone does not establish a treatment indication<sup>[[30]](#ref30)</sup> |
| **Laparoscopy** | Low sensitivity | CO₂ pneumoperitoneum and Trendelenburg compress pelvic veins<sup>[[12]](#ref12)</sup> |

### Intravascular ultrasound — diagnostic upgrade

IVUS complements venography for lesion assessment and stent planning in patients already being considered for iliac intervention; it is not routine invasive screening for all pelvic pain.<sup>[[29]](#ref29)[[30]](#ref30)[[31]](#ref31)</sup>

The 2024 VIVA/AVF/AVLS consensus recommends dynamic assessment and cautions against treating transient compression. The consensus cites &gt;50% IVUS area reduction as a historical selection threshold; in a VIDIO subgroup of 48 patients with nonthrombotic iliac lesions and advanced lower-extremity venous disease, &gt;61% diameter stenosis predicted clinical improvement after stenting, whereas area reduction did not. The panel advises against intervention below its stated thresholds and bases treatment decisions on symptoms plus venography and IVUS.<sup>[[31]](#ref31)</sup> There is no basis for routine balloon angioplasty simply to search for a lesion after otherwise negative testing.<sup>[[31]](#ref31)</sup>

## Management

Treatment is directed at the underlying mechanism: reflux, obstruction, or both. Reflux and obstruction can coexist in PeVD.<sup>[[10]](#ref10)[[11]](#ref11)</sup> The 2026 SIR practice guidance does not establish a mandatory sequence for combined reflux and iliac obstruction; select staged or combined treatment with a venous specialist according to symptoms and mechanism.<sup>[[42]](#ref42)</sup> When left renal-vein compression accompanies ovarian reflux in women with venous-origin chronic pelvic pain, assess the renal-vein-to-IVC gradient during temporary ovarian-vein occlusion: the 2026 SIR panel considers embolization appropriate if the gradient does not rise above 3 mm Hg and advises close clinical follow-up for new renal symptoms after embolization.<sup>[[42]](#ref42)</sup> For hemodynamically significant left renal-vein compression, discuss surgical options before embolization because the ovarian vein may provide renal outflow or be needed for transposition; its closure may cause flank pain, hematuria, or renal-vein thrombosis.<sup>[[42]](#ref42)</sup>

### Medical therapy

Flavonoids are a conservative option for symptomatic PeVD when intervention is unsuitable, declined, or awaited; a 2026 review found 11 studies (748 women), including three randomized trials, two at high risk of bias.<sup>[[44]](#ref44)</sup> Micronized purified flavonoid fraction (MPFF, 1000 mg daily for 2 months) improved quality-of-life and symptom scores versus placebo in a single-blind randomized study of 83 women; in a separate 125-woman dose-comparison study, 60 received 1000 mg twice daily for 1 month followed by 1000 mg daily for 1 month, and 2/60 (3.3%) reported gastrointestinal symptoms.<sup>[[37]](#ref37)[[38]](#ref38)</sup> Medroxyprogesterone acetate or GnRH agonists may produce short-term pain reduction by suppressing ovarian function; long-term efficacy is unproven.<sup>[[13]](#ref13)</sup>

### Specialist interventions

- **Ovarian or pelvic vein embolization** (coils, plugs, sclerosant) is a standard approach for symptomatic primary reflux; the SVS/AVF guideline suggests coil embolization, plugs, or transcatheter sclerotherapy, alone or combined (GRADE 2B).<sup>[[13]](#ref13)</sup> For women planning pregnancy, the 2026 SIR guidance considers ovarian-vein embolization safe for future pregnancy on limited reproductive-outcome data.<sup>[[42]](#ref42)</sup> In a 21-patient nulliparous series, technical success was complete in all patients, with mean VAS pain falling from 8.5 to 2.4 at 90 days; 19/21 (90.5%) reported satisfaction during follow-up averaging 23.2 months.<sup>[[15]](#ref15)</sup> A systematic review included 970 women in 20 studies of isolated coil embolization; a pooled VAS analysis of seven studies found a mean improvement of 5.5 points.<sup>[[39]](#ref39)</sup> Recurrent pain at 1–2 years ranged from 5.9% to 25%, and coil migration (19 cases) was the most common complication.<sup>[[39]](#ref39)</sup>
- **Iliac stenting:** may have a role for selected patients with quality-of-life-limiting venous symptoms, but is inappropriate for minimal or absent symptoms. Incidental compression is common.<sup>[[31]](#ref31)</sup> After stenting a nonthrombotic iliac lesion, arrange early and long-term clinical and imaging surveillance for migration, stenosis, or thrombosis; tailor antithrombotic treatment to thrombotic risk, as routine anticoagulation or antiplatelet therapy without prior venous thromboembolism lacks consensus.<sup>[[31]](#ref31)</sup>
- **Nutcracker syndrome:** the urologic presentation. In a systematic review of 384 patients, hematuria (69.5%), left flank or abdominal pain (48.4%), pelvic pain (23.1%) and left varicocele in men (15.8%) were the commonest features; left renal vein stenosis above 80% was the most-used imaging threshold, and venography with renocaval gradient (with IVUS where available) is advised before intervention.<sup>[[41]](#ref41)</sup> Distinguish symptomatic renal venous hypertension from incidental compression. The 2025 Delphi panel favored conservative care first, found no consensus on a diagnostic stenosis cutoff, and preferred left renal-vein transposition when an operation is required because of stent-migration risk.<sup>[[36]](#ref36)</sup> The 2025 ESVS guideline recommends conservative care for established nutcracker syndrome with mild symptoms (Class I, Level C); for severe symptoms, it says transposition should be considered (Class IIa, Level C), while stenting may be considered in selected patients (Class IIb, Level C).<sup>[[47]](#ref47)</sup>
- A patient-blinded RCT randomized 60 women with CPP and pelvic vein incompetence after other pathology was excluded to venography alone or venography plus occlusion; it stopped at 60 of 100 planned participants during COVID-19, and 9/60 (15%) were lost to 12-month follow-up (7 controls, 2 treated). At 12 months it reported:<sup>[[32]](#ref32)</sup>
  - 12-month median SF-MPQ pain rating index 3 versus 9 (p = 0.016 for baseline-adjusted between-arm change)<sup>[[32]](#ref32)</sup>
  - VAS pain 15 versus 53 (p = 0.002)
  - 12-month EQ-5D VAS median 80 versus 70 (p = 0.01 for baseline-adjusted between-arm change)<sup>[[32]](#ref32)</sup>
  - No major complications
- A 2025 prospective study analyzed 40 women with venous-origin CPP (VO-CPP) treated with coil and foam embolization; all 40 provided mid-term outcomes and 37 provided long-term outcomes at mean 29.6 months. Symptom scores improved from baseline with no significant mid- to long-term difference; among long-term respondents, 81% reported satisfaction and 86% would repeat treatment.<sup>[[33]](#ref33)</sup>
- Systematic review of 21 prospective case series and one poor-quality randomized trial (1,308 women); early substantial pain relief in about 75%, generally sustained, with a coil-migration risk under 2%; the authors rate the evidence quality as low.<sup>[[34]](#ref34)</sup>
- **Embolization complications.** A 2024 systematic review of 25 studies (2,038 women) reported 183 complications (9.0%); postembolization syndrome, usually self-limited pain, was the most common. Nineteen major events occurred, of which 15 required another intervention; the underlying studies used heterogeneous techniques and were of low quality.<sup>[[43]](#ref43)</sup>
- A 2026 single-center, open-label randomized trial assigned 40 women with imaging-confirmed gonadal-vein reflux and noncyclic pelvic pain (20 per arm) to cyanoacrylate embolization or MPFF: mean pelvic-pain VAS at 12 months was 3.2 versus 5.1 (p &lt; 0.001), and symptom recurrence occurred in 2/20 (10%) versus 9/20 (45%). Clinical success, defined as a ≥2-point VAS reduction sustained at 3 and 6 months without a major treatment-related complication, was 19/20 (95%) versus 14/20 (70%; p = 0.091).<sup>[[40]](#ref40)</sup>

### Surgical options

Surgical approaches have been described; when less invasive treatment is unavailable or has failed, the SVS/AVF guideline suggests ovarian-vein ligation and excision for symptomatic pelvic venous reflux (GRADE 2B).<sup>[[13]](#ref13)</sup> Hysterectomy with oophorectomy is not an automatic salvage step for venous pain: irreversible loss of reproductive and ovarian function requires separate justification and careful multidisciplinary selection.<sup>[[13]](#ref13)</sup>

## Emerging Association: PeVD and POTS / Orthostatic Intolerance

A 2026 report combined two retrospective studies. In the diagnostic cohort, 107/129 (83%) patients already selected for both POTS/OI and symptoms suggestive of PeVD had venous abnormalities on at least one imaging modality. This is not the prevalence of PeVD among all people with POTS.<sup>[[28]](#ref28)</sup>

A separate cohort of 271 women receiving iliac stents reported improved symptom and quality-of-life scores. The uncontrolled stenting cohort cannot isolate the mechanism of improvement or compare stenting with standard autonomic care. The authors called for additional trials. A subsequent mechanistic commentary is hypothesis-generating, not a treatment guideline.<sup>[[28]](#ref28)[[35]](#ref35)</sup>

## Practical Notes for the Reconstructive Urologist / Urogynecologist

- **Consider PeVD when symptoms and examination fit**, particularly postural pelvic aching and vulvar varices. Incidental dilated veins alone do not require invasive testing or treatment.
- **Imaging alone does not diagnose PeVD.** Gonadal-vein reflux is also seen in asymptomatic women.<sup>[[5]](#ref5)</sup> The diagnosis is clinical-radiologic.
- **Individualize combined obstruction and reflux treatment** with a venous specialist; avoid a universal procedure sequence.<sup>[[10]](#ref10)[[11]](#ref11)[[31]](#ref31)</sup>
- **The DUV is a surgical landmark, not just a venographic finding.** Anatomic literacy of the paracervical venous plexus protects the ureter and inferior hypogastric plexus during radical hysterectomy and uterine transplantation.<sup>[[20]](#ref20)[[21]](#ref21)[[22]](#ref22)</sup>
- **Persistent genital arousal.** For pelvic varices proposed as a trigger of persistent genital arousal, and the controlled data that weigh against a general causal role, see [PGAD/GPD](/docs/clinical-conditions/03h-pelvic-pain/pgad-gpd).
- **POTS/OI associations remain preliminary**; they do not justify routine venous stenting or abandoning autonomic evaluation.<sup>[[28]](#ref28)[[35]](#ref35)</sup>
