# Pelvic Venous Disorders (PeVD) and Pelvic Congestion Syndrome

**Pelvic venous disorders (PeVD)** encompass a spectrum of conditions caused by pelvic venous reflux, obstruction, or both. Their best-characterized clinical phenotype, pelvic congestion syndrome (PCS) or pelvic venous insufficiency (PVI), is implicated in up to 30% of chronic pelvic pain (CPP) presentations in women and remains substantially underdiagnosed.<sup>[[12]](#ref12)</sup> For the urogynecologist and reconstructive urologist, PeVD matters for three reasons: (1) it is one potentially treatable cause of CPP to consider alongside urologic, gynecologic and myofascial causes; (2) the gonadal and internal iliac venous outflow shares surgical real estate with the ureter, pelvic plexus, and uterine vasculature, so PeVD anatomy is directly relevant to radical hysterectomy, uterine transplantation, and complex pelvic floor reconstruction; and (3) selected retrospective cohorts suggest an association with postural orthostatic tachycardia syndrome (POTS), without proving that venous obstruction causes POTS or that stenting is standard POTS treatment.<sup>[[28]](#ref28)</sup>

For the broader differential of pelvic pain, see [Chronic Pelvic Pain](/docs/clinical-conditions/03h-pelvic-pain/chronic-pelvic-pain).

## Normal Pelvic Venous Anatomy

The female pelvic venous system is highly interconnected and variable between patients, with rich collateral potential that has both protective and diagnostic implications.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[2]](#ref2)</sup>

### Principal drainage pathways

- **Ovarian (gonadal) veins.** Drain the ovaries, fallopian tubes, and superior uterus. The left ovarian vein drains into the left renal vein; the right ovarian vein drains directly into the IVC, typically within 25 mm below the right renal vein ostium.<sup>[[3]](#ref3)</sup> The longer left ovarian vein column and its perpendicular insertion into the renal vein predispose to reflux and congestion on the left.<sup>[[4]](#ref4)[[5]](#ref5)</sup>
- **Uterine (inferior uterine) veins.** Drain the inferior uterus and cervix; course laterally through the base of the broad ligament to the internal iliac veins. Typically plexiform.<sup>[[6]](#ref6)</sup>
- **Utero-ovarian arcade.** Anastomotic connection between uterine and ovarian systems with caliber similar to both (about 3.7 mm), suggesting the internal genital organs function as a venous pool without preferential drainage.<sup>[[2]](#ref2)</sup>
- **Internal iliac veins.** Receive uterine, vaginal plexus, vesical plexus, and rectal plexus drainage; tributaries include obturator, gluteal, and pudendal veins.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup>
- **Ramus communicans.** A cross-pelvic anastomosis between the left and right parametrial plexuses enabling contralateral drainage.<sup>[[2]](#ref2)</sup>
- **Fallopian tube drainage.** Via three separate veins (internal, median, external tubal).<sup>[[2]](#ref2)</sup>

### Embryologic basis for left/right asymmetry

The pelvic venous system derives from the caudal cardinal veins (CCVs) during weeks 4–10. The infrarenal IVC develops from the right CCV; the renal segment arises from subcardinal veins; the iliac veins form by gradual caudal CCV extension.<sup>[[18]](#ref18)[[19]](#ref19)</sup> Regression of left-sided embryonic conduits with preservation of right-sided structures explains why the left gonadal vein takes the long, perpendicular renal-vein route and why left-sided compression syndromes (nutcracker, May-Thurner) predominate.

### The deep uterine vein (DUV) — surgical landmark

The deep uterine vein arises from the merger of cervical, vesical, and vaginal veins, courses through the paracervix in a descending or posterior direction, and drains into the internal iliac vein.<sup>[[20]](#ref20)</sup> Cadaveric studies have identified an inconstant duality (a larger-caliber deep uterine vein with a separate superficial uterine vein) in approximately 25% of cases.<sup>[[21]](#ref21)</sup>

The DUV is operatively important because it divides the lateral uterine aspect into two zones: a superior vascular zone (uterine artery, ureter) and an inferior nervous zone (autonomic pathway or inferior hypogastric plexus).<sup>[[20]](#ref20)[[22]](#ref22)</sup> Early identification of the DUV–pelvic splanchnic nerve crossing point during radical hysterectomy enables nerve-sparing dissection and reduces intraoperative bleeding.<sup>[[22]](#ref22)[[23]](#ref23)</sup> In uterine transplantation, the spatial relationship of the DUV with the ureter (passing posterior to the superficial uterine vein and anterior to the DUV) and the inferior hypogastric plexus is an indispensable guide for safe graft procurement.<sup>[[21]](#ref21)</sup>

### Uterine venous nomenclature for transplantation

The US Uterus Transplant Consortium has proposed a standardized nomenclature renaming the four veins originating from the uterus as superior and inferior uterine veins:<sup>[[6]](#ref6)</sup>

- **Inferior uterine veins.** Drain the inferior uterus, plexiform, course laterally through the broad ligament base to the internal iliac veins (equivalent to conventional "uterine veins").
- **Superior uterine veins.** Drain the superior uterus to the ovaries through the suspensory ligament (previously lumped with "ovarian veins" but functionally distinct).

### Collateral pathways

| Pathway | Components |
| --- | --- |
| Deep | Lumbar veins, sacral veins, vertebral venous plexuses |
| Superficial | Circumflex iliac, epigastric vessels |
| Intermediate | Gonadal veins, ovarian / uterine plexuses |
| Iliofemoral | Various pathways bypassing iliac obstruction |
| Portosystemic | Activated only in severe / chronic obstruction |

## Pathophysiology

PeVD is classified into two principal mechanisms (which frequently coexist):<sup>[[7]](#ref7)[[8]](#ref8)</sup>

1. **Primary venous reflux.** Most commonly involving the ovarian veins from incompetent valves. Reflux is common in symptomatic pelvic varices, but reported proportions depend on selection and imaging methods.<sup>[[5]](#ref5)[[9]](#ref9)</sup> Passive left renal vein to left gonadal vein reflux was seen in 8 of 21 (38%) asymptomatic women in a small MRI series of renal-donor candidates, so reflux on imaging alone is insufficient for diagnosis.<sup>[[5]](#ref5)</sup>
2. **Secondary venous obstruction.** Two classic entities:
   - **Nutcracker syndrome.** Compression of the left renal vein between the aorta and superior mesenteric artery, with elevated pressure transmitted to the left ovarian vein.<sup>[[10]](#ref10)[[11]](#ref11)</sup>
   - **May-Thurner (iliac vein compression) syndrome.** Compression of the left common iliac vein by the overlying right common iliac artery, with reflux into the ipsilateral internal iliac vein.<sup>[[10]](#ref10)[[11]](#ref11)</sup>

### Hormonal contribution

Hormonal milieu is a major modifier of pelvic venous distensibility:

- **Estrogen.** High endogenous estradiol is independently associated with increased venous distensibility and clinical varicose veins (OR 3.6, 95% CI 1.1–11.6; venous distensibility OR 4.4, 95% CI 1.2–15.5 in menopausal women). Both ER and PR are expressed in saphenous vein walls.<sup>[[24]](#ref24)</sup>
- **Progesterone and pregnancy.** Progesterone relaxes venous smooth muscle, the gravid uterus exerts mechanical compression, and circulating volume rises 40–50%. 70–80% of pregnancy-related varicose veins appear in the first trimester, often within 2–3 weeks of conception.<sup>[[25]](#ref25)</sup> Uterine blood flow during gestation pushes valve leaflets apart, enabling retrograde flow that may persist postpartum and worsen with each successive pregnancy.<sup>[[26]](#ref26)</sup>
- **Postmenopausal pattern.** The premenopausal-parous predominance of PCS, with relative postmenopausal rarity, supports the hormonal hypothesis.<sup>[[8]](#ref8)</sup>

## Clinical Presentation

PCS occurs predominantly in premenopausal parous women, although nulliparous patients are well-described.<sup>[[12]](#ref12)[[15]](#ref15)</sup> Cardinal features:

- **Dull, aching pelvic pain** worsened by prolonged standing, intercourse, and the premenstrual period.
- **Post-coital pain.** Suggestive in context, but not pathognomonic.<sup>[[12]](#ref12)</sup>
- Dyspareunia, dysmenorrhea, dysuria.
- **Vulvar and perivulvar varicosities** (especially post-pregnancy); reported in about 22% of pregnant women, about 10% of non-pregnant women, and up to 34% of women with incompetent pelvic venous plexuses.<sup>[[27]](#ref27)</sup>
- Urologic symptoms: hematuria and urinary frequency in the absence of infection.<sup>[[13]](#ref13)[[14]](#ref14)</sup>
- Lower extremity varicose veins of pelvic origin (posteromedial thigh, vulva).

Imaging prevalence anchors. CT demonstrates ovarian vein dilation (≥ 6 mm) in approximately 13.7% of women aged 25–65, most commonly bilateral.<sup>[[4]](#ref4)</sup> Chronic pelvic pain of unclear etiology with dilated veins was documented in 8% of premenopausal women in a large imaging series.<sup>[[14]](#ref14)</sup>

### Pelvic escape points and extra-pelvic varicose veins

Reflux from the pelvis can manifest in the leg via four recognized escape points:<sup>[[9]](#ref9)[[27]](#ref27)</sup>

| Escape point | Route | Clinical signature |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Inguinal** | Round ligament → groin / vulva | Vulvar / labial varix |
| **Obturator** | Obturator canal → medial thigh | Medial-thigh varicose veins |
| **Perineal** | Direct to vulva / perineum | Perineal varix |
| **Gluteal** | Greater sciatic foramen → posterior thigh / buttock | Posterior-thigh varix; can include intra/peri-neural (sciatic, tibial) varices from inferior gluteal tributary |

Most patients with extra-pelvic varicose veins of pelvic origin do not report pelvic symptoms; pelvic pain is reported in under 10% (7% in one cited series).<sup>[[27]](#ref27)</sup> A "bottom-up" treatment approach (US-guided foam sclerotherapy or ligation of escape points and extra-pelvic varicosities) is often sufficient as first-line therapy; ovarian vein embolization or iliac vein stenting is reserved for failure or development of pelvic symptoms.<sup>[[27]](#ref27)</sup>

## Classification — The SVP System

The American Vein & Lymphatic Society Symptoms-Varices-Pathophysiology (SVP) classification standardizes nomenclature for PeVD, complementing the CEAP system used for lower-extremity venous disease.<sup>[[9]](#ref9)</sup> The pelvis is partitioned into four anatomic zones:

| Zone | Anatomy |
| --- | --- |
| **Zone 1** | Left renal vein |
| **Zone 2** | Gonadal and internal iliac veins; pelvic venous plexuses |
| **Zone 3** | Pelvic-origin extra-pelvic transitional veins (escape points) |
| **Zone 4** | Lower extremity veins (classified per CEAP) |

SVP notation is structured as S (symptoms) V (varices) P (pathophysiology), with P incorporating Anatomy, Hemodynamics, and Etiology as subscripts.<sup>[[9]](#ref9)</sup>

| Example | SVP notation |
| --- | --- |
| Bilateral ovarian reflux with pelvic pain | S₂V₂P(BGV,R,NT) |
| Nutcracker syndrome | S₁V₁P(LRV,O,NT) |
| Asymptomatic left common iliac vein compression anatomy | S₀V₀P(LCIV,O,NT); not itself a symptomatic syndrome |

## Diagnosis

Pelvic venous engorgement and gonadal vein reflux can be seen in asymptomatic women, so imaging findings must be correlated with the clinical syndrome.<sup>[[5]](#ref5)[[10]](#ref10)</sup>

### Imaging hierarchy

| Modality | Role | Key criteria / yield |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Transvaginal US (TVUS)** | First-line | Assess reflux, caliber and collateral pathways dynamically. Proposed markers include ovarian vein diameter above 7–8 mm, slow flow (&lt;3 cm/s) and myometrial vein dilatation above 5 mm; thresholds are not standardized.<sup>[[12]](#ref12)</sup> One study confirmed PeVD on venography in 187/195 TVUS-positive patients (95.9%); this is not sensitivity/specificity in an unselected population<sup>[[12]](#ref12)[[16]](#ref16)</sup> |
| **Catheter venography** | Historic gold standard | Direct visualization of reflux; pressure measurements<sup>[[10]](#ref10)[[17]](#ref17)</sup> |
| **CT / MRI** | Structural causes; planning | Nutcracker / May-Thurner anatomy; ovarian vein grading on CT — mild (< 6 mm), moderate (6–8 mm), severe (> 8 mm)<sup>[[4]](#ref4)</sup> |
| **IVUS** | Invasive assessment of suspected iliac obstruction when considering intervention | Complements venography; anatomic narrowing alone does not establish a treatment indication<sup>[[30]](#ref30)</sup> |
| **Laparoscopy** | Low sensitivity | CO₂ pneumoperitoneum and Trendelenburg compress pelvic veins<sup>[[12]](#ref12)</sup> |

### Intravascular ultrasound — diagnostic upgrade

IVUS complements venography for lesion assessment and stent planning in patients already being considered for iliac intervention; it is not routine invasive screening for all pelvic pain.<sup>[[29]](#ref29)[[30]](#ref30)[[31]](#ref31)</sup>

The 2024 VIVA/AVF/AVLS consensus recommends dynamic assessment and cautions against treating transient compression. Reported IVUS thresholds (>50% area reduction or >61% diameter stenosis) support selection but do not independently predict benefit. Below these thresholds, intervention is not recommended. There is no basis for routine balloon angioplasty simply to search for a lesion after otherwise negative testing.<sup>[[31]](#ref31)</sup>

## Management

Treatment is directed at the underlying mechanism: reflux, obstruction, or both. When both coexist, specialist assessment determines whether staged or combined treatment is appropriate; an obligatory obstruction-first sequence is not established.<sup>[[10]](#ref10)[[11]](#ref11)</sup>

### Medical therapy

Venoactive drugs are the usual first medical step. Micronized purified flavonoid fraction (MPFF, 1000 mg daily for 2 months) improved quality-of-life and symptom scores versus placebo in a single-blind randomized study of 83 women; a higher-dose regimen (1000 mg twice daily for 1 month) has been used in more severe pain but caused more gastrointestinal side effects.<sup>[[37]](#ref37)[[38]](#ref38)</sup> Medroxyprogesterone acetate or GnRH agonists may produce short-term pain reduction by suppressing ovarian function; long-term efficacy is unproven.<sup>[[13]](#ref13)</sup>

### Specialist interventions

- **Ovarian or pelvic vein embolization** (coils, plugs, sclerosant) is the standard approach for primary reflux; the SVS/AVF guideline suggests coil embolization, plugs or transcatheter sclerotherapy, alone or combined (GRADE 2B).<sup>[[13]](#ref13)</sup> In a 21-patient nulliparous series, technical success was complete in all patients, with mean VAS pain falling from 8.5 to 2.4 at 90 days and 90% satisfaction.<sup>[[15]](#ref15)</sup> A pooled analysis of 970 women in 20 coil-embolization studies reported a mean VAS improvement of 5.5 points; recurrent pain at 1–2 years ranged from 5.9% to 25%, and coil migration (19 cases) was the most common complication.<sup>[[39]](#ref39)</sup>
- **Iliac stenting:** may have a role for selected, quality-of-life-limiting venous symptoms, but is inappropriate for minimal or absent symptoms. Incidental compression is common.<sup>[[31]](#ref31)</sup>
- **Nutcracker syndrome:** the urologic presentation. In a systematic review of 384 patients, hematuria (69.5%), left flank or abdominal pain (48.4%), pelvic pain (23.1%) and left varicocele in men (15.8%) were the commonest features; left renal vein stenosis above 80% was the most-used imaging threshold, and venography with renocaval gradient (with IVUS where available) is advised before intervention.<sup>[[41]](#ref41)</sup> Distinguish symptomatic renal venous hypertension from incidental compression. A 2025 international Delphi consensus favors left renal vein transposition as the first operative choice and highlights stent migration risk. A small retrospective series describing renal balloon angioplasty does not establish it as standard treatment.<sup>[[11]](#ref11)[[36]](#ref36)</sup>
- A patient-blinded RCT randomized 60 women with CPP and pelvic vein incompetence after exclusion of other pathology to venography alone or venography plus occlusion. At 12 months it reported:<sup>[[32]](#ref32)</sup>
  - Median SF-MPQ pain 2 versus 9 (p = 0.016)
  - VAS pain 15 versus 53 (p = 0.002)
  - EQ-5D QoL 0.79 to 0.84 (p = 0.008)
  - No major complications
- A 2025 prospective study of 40 women with venous-origin CPP (VO-CPP) treated with coil and foam embolization demonstrated sustained relief at mean 29.6-month follow-up, with no significant difference between mid- and long-term scores; 81% patient satisfaction, 86% willing to repeat.<sup>[[33]](#ref33)</sup>
- Systematic review of 21 prospective case series and one poor-quality randomized trial (1,308 women); early substantial pain relief in about 75%, generally sustained, with a coil-migration risk under 2%; the authors rate the evidence quality as low.<sup>[[34]](#ref34)</sup>
- A 2026 randomized trial of 40 women compared cyanoacrylate embolization with MPFF: 12-month pelvic pain VAS was 3.2 versus 5.1 (p &lt; 0.001) and recurrence 10% versus 45%, while clinical success (95% versus 70%) was not significantly different. The trial is small and single-center.<sup>[[40]](#ref40)</sup>

### Surgical options

Ovarian vein ligation and other surgical approaches have been described. Hysterectomy with oophorectomy is not an automatic salvage step for venous pain: irreversible loss of reproductive and ovarian function requires separate justification and careful multidisciplinary selection.<sup>[[13]](#ref13)</sup>

## Emerging Association: PeVD and POTS / Orthostatic Intolerance

A 2026 report combined two retrospective studies. In the diagnostic cohort, 107/129 (83%) patients already selected for both POTS/OI and symptoms suggestive of PeVD had venous abnormalities on at least one imaging modality. This is not the prevalence of PeVD among all people with POTS.<sup>[[28]](#ref28)</sup>

A separate cohort of 271 women receiving iliac stents reported improved symptom and quality-of-life scores. Without randomized controls, improvement cannot establish a causal mechanism or prove that stenting outperforms standard autonomic care. The authors called for additional trials. A subsequent mechanistic commentary is hypothesis-generating, not a treatment guideline.<sup>[[28]](#ref28)[[35]](#ref35)</sup>

## Practical Notes for the Reconstructive Urologist / Urogynecologist

- **Consider PeVD when symptoms and examination fit**, particularly postural pelvic aching and vulvar varices. Incidental dilated veins alone do not require invasive testing or treatment.
- **Imaging alone does not diagnose PeVD.** Gonadal-vein reflux is also seen in asymptomatic women.<sup>[[5]](#ref5)</sup> The diagnosis is clinical-radiologic.
- **Individualize combined obstruction and reflux treatment** with a venous specialist; avoid a universal procedure sequence.<sup>[[10]](#ref10)[[11]](#ref11)[[31]](#ref31)</sup>
- **The DUV is a surgical landmark, not just a venographic finding.** Anatomic literacy of the paracervical venous plexus protects the ureter and inferior hypogastric plexus during radical hysterectomy and uterine transplantation.<sup>[[20]](#ref20)[[21]](#ref21)[[22]](#ref22)</sup>
- **POTS/OI associations remain preliminary**; they do not justify routine venous stenting or abandoning autonomic evaluation.<sup>[[28]](#ref28)[[35]](#ref35)</sup>
