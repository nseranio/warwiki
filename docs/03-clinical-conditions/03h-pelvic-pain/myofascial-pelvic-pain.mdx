# Myofascial Pelvic Pain

**Myofascial pelvic pain (MFPP)** is a chronic regional pain condition driven by hyperirritable trigger points within taut bands of the pelvic floor musculature, most commonly the levator ani (puborectalis, pubococcygeus and iliococcygeus), adjacent coccygeus, and obturator internus. Across heterogeneous chronic pelvic pain cohorts, myofascial involvement of the lower abdominal wall, pelvic-floor muscles, or both has been reported in 22–94% of cases, depending on case mix and examination methods; pelvic-floor involvement may be missed without a structured pelvic-floor myofascial exam.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[2]](#ref2)[[3]](#ref3)</sup> In men, EAU 2026 cites muscle tenderness in 51% of men with prostatitis versus 7% of controls, and pelvic floor dysfunction in 92.2% of men attending a tertiary pain center.<sup>[[45]](#ref45)</sup>

For the broader chronic pelvic pain framework (etiology by organ system, UPOINT phenotyping, pelvic congestion, pudendal neuralgia), see [Chronic Pelvic Pain](/docs/clinical-conditions/03h-pelvic-pain/chronic-pelvic-pain). For the bladder-specific phenotype, see [IC/PBS](/docs/clinical-conditions/03h-pelvic-pain/ic-pbs). First-line management centers on [Pelvic Floor Physical Therapy](/docs/surgical-techniques/04f-incontinence-procedures/procedures/pelvic-floor-pt) (down-training, not strengthening).

---

## Pathophysiology and Presentation

MFPP arises from dysfunction of the pelvic-floor muscles with development of myofascial trigger points (MTrPs) producing localized and referred pain. It may exist independently or co-occur with endometriosis, IC/BPS, vulvodynia, IBS, dyspareunia or vaginismus, and CP/CPPS in men, with viscero-somatic convergence amplifying both the pelvic-floor and visceral symptom burden.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[2]](#ref2)[[4]](#ref4)</sup>

A recently described entity, myofascial urinary frequency syndrome, captures the subset of patients in whom pelvic-floor myofascial dysfunction drives lower-urinary-tract symptoms (frequency, urgency, sensation of incomplete emptying, suprapubic or bladder pressure) that are routinely misclassified as overactive bladder or IC/BPS.<sup>[[5]](#ref5)</sup>

Typical clinical features:

- Pelvic, perineal, vulvar, or suprapubic pain
- Dyspareunia, post-coital pain, sexual dysfunction
- Urinary frequency, urgency, hesitancy, or incomplete emptying
- Pain with prolonged sitting
- Referred pain to low back, thighs, abdomen, and rectum

## Diagnosis

Diagnosis is clinical and exam-based. There is no validated imaging, biomarker, or laboratory test.<sup>[[6]](#ref6)</sup>

### Pelvic-floor myofascial examination

AUA 2025 recommends digital transrectal palpation of pelvic-floor muscles in men to identify tenderness suggesting pelvic-floor myalgia (Statement 5; Expert Opinion).<sup>[[50]](#ref50)</sup>

- **Single-digit transvaginal (or transrectal) palpation** of the levator-ani complex and obturator internus, sweeping each muscle group and laterality.<sup>[[6]](#ref6)</sup>
- Assess for taut bands, trigger points, hypertonicity, and reproduction of the patient's typical pain with focal pressure.
- **Clock-face topography.** One published vaginal screening protocol palpates the right obturator internus from 9 to 12 o'clock and the left from 12 to 3 o'clock, confirming each by resisted ipsilateral thigh abduction; it then palpates each levator ani belly and along its fibers while recording pain on a 0–10 scale.<sup>[[52]](#ref52)</sup> The ischial spine marks the sacrospinous-ligament attachment, but the pudendal nerve's course varies; do not treat a fixed clock position or distance as its location.<sup>[[46]](#ref46)</sup>
- **A myofascial trigger point** is defined by four features: a palpable taut band, a highly tender nodule within that band, reproduction of the patient's pain on palpation of the nodule, and pain-limited stretch or restricted range of motion.<sup>[[47]](#ref47)</sup>
- The ACOG Practice Bulletin 218 explicitly directs particular attention to the abdominal and pelvic neuromusculoskeletal exam.<sup>[[7]](#ref7)</sup>
- **Grading muscle tone.** Pelvic-floor muscle firmness has been described by comparison with the resting compressibility of the examiner's own thenar eminence, with moderate and severe increased firmness compared to the firmness at the thumb-opposition point with the first and fifth digits, respectively. This is a descriptive teaching comparison, not a validated scale or diagnostic cutoff, and no accepted standard defines myofascial trigger points.<sup>[[48]](#ref48)</sup>

### Persistency Index

The Persistency Index (Ackerman 2023) is a screening tool to help identify patients who warrant examination for myofascial dysfunction, incorporating the persistent nature of incomplete-emptying sensation, dyspareunia, and age. A score ≥ 7 discriminated myofascial urinary frequency syndrome from other LUTS in a 719-patient validation cohort (AUC 0.74; sensitivity 80%, specificity 61%), so it is a triage aid toward a structured pelvic-floor exam, not a diagnostic test.<sup>[[5]](#ref5)</sup>

## Stepwise Management

ACOG supports multimodal management and the AUA IC/BPS guideline supports manual PT for IC/BPS with pelvic-floor tenderness.<sup>[[7]](#ref7)[[8]](#ref8)[[2]](#ref2)</sup> In men with pelvic-floor or abdominopelvic myalgia, AUA 2025 permits individualized internal and external manual physical therapy (Statement 29; Conditional Recommendation, Grade C) and EMG biofeedback when pelvic-floor tone is increased (Statement 30; Expert Opinion).<sup>[[49]](#ref49)</sup> The 2024 high-tone pelvic-floor algorithm is expert Delphi consensus; PFPT is first line in that consensus, and subsequent options require reassessment.<sup>[[40]](#ref40)</sup>

EAU 2026 weakly recommends myofascial treatment as first-line therapy for pelvic floor dysfunction (level 1b, biofeedback as an adjunct level 1a), and strongly recommends biofeedback with muscle exercises for anal pain from an overactive pelvic floor. It weakly recommends actively looking for trigger points in chronic primary pelvic pain, while noting that no accepted standard defines them. EAU also finds it neither supported nor refuted that needling beats placebo, and finds no evidence that manual trigger point techniques beat no treatment. It recommends that physical therapy be delivered by therapists trained in the musculoskeletal, psychological and central nervous system aspects of chronic pain.<sup>[[45]](#ref45)</sup>

| Step | Intervention | Trigger to escalate |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | **PFPT**, relaxation and a home program | First-line; address access barriers with guided home or virtual care |
| 2 | **Local-anesthetic TPIs**, cognitive behavioral therapy, selected muscle-relaxant trials | Persistent symptoms or inability to tolerate manual therapy; medication evidence is limited |
| 3 | **Off-label onabotulinumtoxinA** | Selected refractory patients; discuss negative larger RCTs and phenotype-specific uncertainty |
| 4 | **Specialist reassessment** | Reconsider diagnosis and comorbid pain mechanisms; the consensus reserves SNM mainly for coexisting urgency/frequency |

Avoid unsupervised Kegel strengthening during symptomatic hypertonicity. PFPT initially emphasizes relaxation and down-training; later exercise is individualized to examination findings.<sup>[[9]](#ref9)[[7]](#ref7)</sup> Opioids are not recommended.<sup>[[7]](#ref7)[[2]](#ref2)</sup>

## I. Pelvic Floor Physical Therapy

PFPT is the cornerstone and the consensus first-line treatment. Evidence from multimodal physiotherapy in female CPP supports short-term pain reduction, but should not be generalized to every isolated modality or patient phenotype.<sup>[[7]](#ref7)[[2]](#ref2)[[9]](#ref9)</sup>

| Modality | Technique | Goal |
|---|---|---|
| **Internal myofascial release** | Single-digit transvaginal/transrectal palpation of levator ani and obturator internus with sustained pressure to taut bands and MTrPs; 30–60 min sessions, 1–2× per week for 8–12 weeks | Release fascial restrictions, reduce trigger-point irritability |
| **Biofeedback (down-training)** | Surface or intracavitary EMG with real-time feedback | Train pelvic-floor **relaxation**, not strength |
| **Electrical stimulation** | Low-frequency TENS or interferential current, external or intravaginal | Pain modulation, muscle relaxation |
| **Pelvic-floor stretching / home program** | Reverse Kegels, child's pose, deep squat, diaphragmatic breathing | Sustain in-clinic gains |

**Randomized evidence for manual myofascial PT** comes from the urologic pelvic pain literature. In the multicenter IC/PBS trial (81 women with pelvic-floor tenderness and symptoms for no more than 3 years; 10 sessions), the global response rate was 59% with myofascial physical therapy versus 26% with global therapeutic massage (p = 0.0012); pain, urgency and frequency and symptom-index scores improved in both arms without a significant between-group difference.<sup>[[43]](#ref43)</sup> The earlier feasibility trial (47 men and women with CP/CPPS or IC/PBS) reported responders of 57% versus 21% (p = 0.03).<sup>[[44]](#ref44)</sup> In the male subgroup, response rates were 64% with myofascial therapy versus 40% with massage (p = 0.39); urinary subscores favored myofascial therapy (p = 0.007). AUA judged the evidence very low certainty because the trial was open-label, small and imbalanced at baseline.<sup>[[49]](#ref49)</sup> The 2022 AUA IC/BPS guideline (Statement 12; Standard, Evidence Strength Grade A) states that appropriate manual physical therapy should be offered to patients with pelvic-floor tenderness when appropriately trained clinicians are available, and that pelvic-floor strengthening such as Kegel exercises should be avoided.<sup>[[8]](#ref8)</sup>

In a 165-woman RCT limited to postpartum pelvic-floor hypertonia, adding myofascial manipulation to lifestyle advice, electrical stimulation, and biofeedback yielded a reported 'total effective rate' of 94.3%, versus 87.0% without manipulation and 37.7% with lifestyle advice alone. The abstract does not define that composite endpoint, so these figures should not be read as pain-response rates or a general MFPP protocol.<sup>[[10]](#ref10)</sup>

## II. Trigger Point Injections (TPIs)

ACOG Practice Bulletin 218 recommends TPIs (Level B) to improve pain and functional ability in myofascial CPP, alone or with other modalities.<sup>[[7]](#ref7)</sup> In Langford's prospective 18-woman series, 13/18 (72%) met the study's composite success definition after the first levator ani trigger-point injection: at least 50% VAS pain reduction plus satisfaction and cure scores of at least 60%. Six of 18 (33%) were pain-free at a mean 3-month follow-up.<sup>[[12]](#ref12)[[45]](#ref45)</sup> Needling and contextual effects may contribute to benefit; small heterogeneous studies do not establish equivalence of every injectant.<sup>[[7]](#ref7)[[11]](#ref11)</sup>

### Injectant options

| Injectant | Notes | Evidence |
|---|---|---|
| **Local anesthetic** (lidocaine, bupivacaine, levobupivacaine, mepivacaine) | Most commonly used; well-studied | Multiple cohorts and RCTs<sup>[[12]](#ref12)[[13]](#ref13)[[14]](#ref14)</sup> |
| **Local anesthetic + corticosteroid** (triamcinolone, betamethasone) | Common in postpartum and refractory MFPP | An underenrolled 2025 postpartum RCT (57/arm; 124/arm planned) found similar six-month VAS scores with levobupivacaine alone and levobupivacaine plus betamethasone; formal noninferiority remains uncertain<sup>[[13]](#ref13)</sup>; ASRA/AAPM guidance favors considering anesthetic alone because routine steroid addition offers little established benefit; its earlier evidence search preceded the postpartum RCT<sup>[[15]](#ref15)</sup> |
| **Magnesium-based formulation** | Novel approach | Small underpowered RCT (44 participants; magnesium, lidocaine and waitlist arms) did not establish superiority over lidocaine<sup>[[11]](#ref11)</sup> |
| **Normal saline** | Mechanical / needling effect | May produce a needling/contextual response; absence of superiority does not establish universal equivalence<sup>[[7]](#ref7)</sup> |
| **OnabotulinumtoxinA** | For refractory / repeated-TPI patients | See Section III |

### Office-based transvaginal TPI — published protocols

| Protocol | Injectant | Volume per TrP | Delivery |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Langford 2007** | 0.25% bupivacaine 10 mL + 2% lidocaine 10 mL + triamcinolone 40 mg / 1 mL | **5 mL** per trigger point | 5.5" Iowa-trumpet pudendal needle guide; office, no sedation<sup>[[12]](#ref12)</sup> |
| **Leitch 2022 (RCT)** | 1% lidocaine 10 mL **or** magnesium-based formulation | Per trigger point | Transvaginal, office; 8 sessions over 12 weeks<sup>[[11]](#ref11)</sup> |
| **Solano Calvo 2025 (RCT)** | Levobupivacaine 5 mg/mL ± betamethasone 3 mg/mL | 2 mL per trigger point | Transvaginal, 5 mm needle depth; hemostasis checked<sup>[[13]](#ref13)</sup> |
| **Moya Esteban 2019** | Mepivacaine 2% (8 mL) + betamethasone acetate (2 mL) | 10 mL prepared mixture; do not assume 10 mL per point | Transvaginal injections in 27 postpartum women<sup>[[14]](#ref14)</sup> |

These are historical study formulations, not universal prescribing instructions. Calculate the total dose across every injection site and any accompanying block, accounting for additive local-anesthetic toxicity, body size and comorbidity. Steroid is not routinely required.<sup>[[15]](#ref15)</sup>

### Step-by-step technique

1. **Position:** dorsal lithotomy; sedation usually not required.
2. **Exam:** single-digit transvaginal palpation of pubococcygeus, iliococcygeus, coccygeus, and obturator internus bilaterally; identify trigger points by reproduction of the patient's pain.<sup>[[12]](#ref12)[[6]](#ref6)</sup>
3. **Needle guidance:** Iowa-trumpet pudendal needle guide (5.5") or pudendal-block kit (≈ 1 cm depth of penetration); the guide is held transvaginally with the index finger directing the needle tip onto the trigger point.<sup>[[12]](#ref12)</sup>
4. **Injection:** advance into the trigger point under digital guidance; aspirate and inject incrementally with monitoring. Negative aspiration does not exclude intravascular placement. Choose aliquots and the number of sites within the calculated total-dose limit.
5. **Post-procedure:** brief observation; teach pelvic-floor stretching for home use; resume PFPT.<sup>[[12]](#ref12)</sup>

### Frequency and pairing with PFPT

- Frequency is individualized; the Leitch RCT used 8 sessions over 12 weeks, not a universal every-2–4-week regimen.<sup>[[11]](#ref11)</sup>
- In a retrospective cohort of 87 women, TPI followed immediately by myofascial-release PFPT was associated with a median VAS reduction of 4 points versus 2 points with TPI alone (p = 0.042); 77% versus 45% achieved ≥ 3-point improvement (p = 0.008).<sup>[[16]](#ref16)</sup>

## III. OnabotulinumtoxinA (Botox)

OnabotulinumtoxinA (BTA) may be discussed for selected refractory patients after PFPT; access barriers alone are not evidence of benefit or an indication to escalate.<sup>[[17]](#ref17)[[18]](#ref18)</sup> A 2026 expert Delphi panel favored third-line use, typically 100–200 U total (20–50 U per muscle group per side in divided 1–2 mL injection sites), and opposed local-anesthetic test injections as predictors of response; these are technique preferences amid mixed trial results.<sup>[[53]](#ref53)</sup> This is an off-label use; pelvic-floor MFPP is not on the FDA label.<sup>[[17]](#ref17)</sup> Before injection, screen for prior hypersensitivity to any botulinum toxin preparation or formulation component and infection at the proposed sites (labeled contraindications); review neuromuscular disease, aminoglycosides, and muscle relaxants because of possible systemic weakness. Counsel about distant toxin spread, including swallowing or breathing difficulty.<sup>[[54]](#ref54)</sup> For men, AUA 2025 finds the evidence too weak to recommend botulinum toxin for CP/CPPS and notes that pelvic-floor injection is off-label. This is an ungraded Future Directions discussion.<sup>[[49]](#ref49)</sup>

### Dosing

| Dose | Dilution | Approach | Anesthesia | Note |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **200 U** (published protocol) | 200 U in **20 mL normal saline** (10 U/mL) | Transvaginal | Conscious sedation or general | Whitmore 2025 protocol<sup>[[17]](#ref17)</sup>; Dessie RCT<sup>[[19]](#ref19)</sup> |
| **80 U** | Variable | Transvaginal | General | Abbott 2006 RCT<sup>[[20]](#ref20)</sup> |
| **50–200 U range** | Variable | Transvaginal | Variable | Observational dosing experience; an optimal dose and reliable dose-response relationship remain unestablished<sup>[[21]](#ref21)[[22]](#ref22)</sup> |

### Step-by-step technique (Whitmore 2025)

1. **Awake exam.** Palpate pubococcygeus, iliococcygeus, and obturator internus bilaterally; mark hypertonic or tender targets.<sup>[[17]](#ref17)</sup>
2. **Sedation.** Conscious sedation or general anesthesia; some centers use local-only office protocols.
3. **Reconstitution.** 200 U onabotulinumtoxinA in 20 mL normal saline (10 U/mL).
4. **Needle.** Pudendal-block kit, about 1 cm depth of penetration.
5. **Injection.** Sequentially deliver 1–2 mL (10–20 U) at multiple sites along each affected muscle, distributed across pubococcygeus, iliococcygeus, and obturator internus bilaterally.
6. **Image-guided alternative.** 4D transperineal ultrasound can confirm intramuscular needle placement and visualize fluid tracking along muscle fibers.<sup>[[23]](#ref23)</sup>
7. **Standardized ten-point method.** A retrospective study published in the 2026 volume compared a fixed ten-site pattern with trigger-point injection (91 patients; 64 propensity-matched). No significant outcome difference establishes neither equivalence nor a superior learning curve in a randomized comparison.<sup>[[24]](#ref24)</sup>

### Concurrent pudendal nerve block — limited benefit

- A 12-year, 182-patient retrospective cohort found no difference in 30-day clinic, ER or message contacts, or in time to repeat injection, between BTA alone and BTA and pudendal block.<sup>[[18]](#ref18)</sup>
- A 96-event subgroup analysis found no difference in PACU pain (VAS 1.7 versus 1.9), PACU time, or opioid requirements.<sup>[[25]](#ref25)</sup>

### Onset, duration, retreatment

- **Assess response at 2–4 weeks**, as suggested by the expert consensus; studies also assess outcomes at 6–12 weeks. These assessment times are not a universal six-week pharmacologic onset or a reason to withhold PFPT.<sup>[[17]](#ref17)[[26]](#ref26)[[40]](#ref40)</sup>
- **Duration:** about 3–6 months; median time to re-intervention 6 months.<sup>[[17]](#ref17)[[18]](#ref18)</sup>
- **Mean number of total injections per patient:** 1.6–1.7 in a large cohort; many require retreatment.<sup>[[18]](#ref18)</sup>

### Efficacy — mixed

| Study | Design / N | Result |
|---|---|---|
| Spruijt 2025 RCT | n = 94; 100 U vs placebo, then four PFPT sessions | ≥33% pain reduction: 33% vs 20% (p=0.19); global improvement 17% in both groups. Resting activity improved, but pain benefit was not demonstrated.<sup>[[41]](#ref41)</sup> |
| Karp 2026 phase 2 RCT | n = 29 with endometriosis-associated pain and pelvic-floor spasm despite treatment; 100 U vs saline | At one month, self-reported benefit 11/15 (73%) vs 4/14 (29%), p=0.027. Small, selected phenotype; subsequent open injections prevent interpreting one-year outcomes as a sustained randomized comparison.<sup>[[42]](#ref42)</sup> |
| Dessie 2019 RCT | n = 59; 200 U BTA vs saline (all received PFPT from week 4) | **Not superior** to saline for pain on palpation at 2/4/12 weeks; BTA more likely to report **PGI-I global improvement** at 4 weeks (p = 0.03)<sup>[[19]](#ref19)</sup> |
| Abbott 2006 RCT | n = 60; 80 U BTA vs saline | Resting pelvic-floor pressure fell 49 → 32 cmH₂O with BTA vs 44 → 39 with placebo; non-menstrual pelvic pain improved only in the BTA arm, while dyspareunia improved in both (VAS 66 → 12 vs 64 → 27)<sup>[[20]](#ref20)</sup> |
| Jha 2021 observational | 48 treated; 46 followed at 3–6 months | 34/46 (74%) reported at least slight global improvement; no dose association was detected across 50–200 U in this nonrandomized series<sup>[[21]](#ref21)</sup> |
| Mooney 2021 pilot | n = 21 | Improvement in dyspareunia, sexual function, QoL; 4 previously apareunic patients regained penetrative intercourse<sup>[[26]](#ref26)</sup> |
| Lewis 2023 12-year cohort | n = 182 | Median time to re-intervention 6 months; safety profile across long-term repeat dosing<sup>[[18]](#ref18)</sup> |

### Adverse events

| AE | Incidence | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| Constipation | 6.6% in the 182-patient BTA cohort; 4/30 BTA and 2/29 saline in the Dessie RCT at 2 weeks | Most common at 2 weeks in both RCT arms.<sup>[[17]](#ref17)[[18]](#ref18)[[19]](#ref19)</sup> |
| Urinary incontinence | up to 22% | More common at higher doses<sup>[[17]](#ref17)[[22]](#ref22)</sup> |
| Urinary retention | 3.8% | <sup>[[18]](#ref18)</sup> |
| Urinary tract infection | 2.7% in the BTA cohort; 9% in the Dessie saline arm | The latter is a comparator event, not a BTA rate.<sup>[[18]](#ref18)[[19]](#ref19)</sup> |
| Fecal incontinence | 2.7% | Transient (~2 weeks); more frequent at higher doses<sup>[[18]](#ref18)[[22]](#ref22)</sup> |
| Worsening pelvic pain | 11.5% | <sup>[[18]](#ref18)</sup> |

## IV. Compounded Intravaginal Pharmacotherapy

These are off-label, non-FDA-approved, and rely on compounding-pharmacy formulations whose bioavailability and stability vary between pharmacies. They are adjuncts within a multimodal plan (not stand-alone therapies) and the evidence base is dominated by small studies, case reports, and expert opinion.<sup>[[2]](#ref2)[[27]](#ref27)[[9]](#ref9)</sup>

### Intravaginal diazepam — most studied

- **Published doses:** 5–10 mg compounded suppositories; this is not a validated routine regimen for MFPP.
- **Pharmacokinetics (eight healthy volunteers):** vaginal administration produces a lower peak (Cmax about 31 ng/mL at about 3.1 h) and lower bioavailability (70.5%) than oral, but a prolonged half-life (about 82 h) with active nordiazepam metabolite peaking at about 132 h. Steady-state takes about 1 week; accumulation favors intermittent dosing (e.g., before PT or intercourse) rather than daily use.<sup>[[28]](#ref28)</sup>
- **Evidence is mixed:**
  - In one retrospective series of 26 women receiving vaginal diazepam alongside PFPT and trigger-point injections, 25/26 reported subjective improvement, but the sexual-pain VAS change was not statistically significant (p = 0.14); perineometry measures of tone improved.<sup>[[29]](#ref29)</sup>
  - A triple-blinded RCT (10 mg nightly × 28 days) (21 women) found no improvement in resting EMG, FSFI, or VAS versus placebo as monotherapy.<sup>[[30]](#ref30)</sup>
  - In women with vestibulodynia receiving TENS, vaginal diazepam improved dyspareunia and relaxation outcomes, but not the between-group VAS pain outcome.<sup>[[31]](#ref31)</sup>
  - A systematic review found two small placebo-controlled trials without significant pain benefit and the limited TENS findings above. Benefit as a PFPT adjunct remains uncertain; the evidence does not establish it as a routinely effective treatment.<sup>[[32]](#ref32)</sup>
- **Safety:** meaningful systemic absorption and accumulation remain possible; fatigue occurred in 3/8 volunteers. Apply benzodiazepine precautions for sedation, dependence and concurrent opioids or other sedatives.<sup>[[28]](#ref28)</sup>

### Intravaginal / topical baclofen

- GABA-B receptor agonist with antispastic and analgesic effects.<sup>[[33]](#ref33)</sup>
- **Formulations:** topical creams have been reported with palmitoylethanolamide (PEA) or amitriptyline; no established evidence-based vaginal dose exists.
- **Evidence.** A case report described one 33-year-old woman with vulvodynia and proctodynia whose symptoms decreased by more than 50% and who resumed pain-free intercourse after 3 months of topical baclofen 5% plus oral PEA 400 mg three times daily; the later mechanistic review discusses that same case.<sup>[[34]](#ref34)[[35]](#ref35)</sup> Amitriptyline-baclofen creams have shown pre-to-post improvement in non-RCT vulvodynia studies.<sup>[[36]](#ref36)</sup> No RCTs of intravaginal baclofen alone for pelvic-floor MFPP.
- **Mechanism:** acts at peripheral GABA-B receptors on nociceptor terminals and modulates nociceptor or immunocompetent or epithelial cross-talk in vulvar or vaginal mucosa; centrally reduces spasticity.<sup>[[33]](#ref33)[[35]](#ref35)</sup>
- **Safety:** formal vaginal-baclofen pharmacokinetic data are lacking, so reduced systemic risk cannot be assumed. Oral baclofen causes sedation and dizziness and can cause withdrawal after abrupt cessation.<sup>[[33]](#ref33)</sup>

### Intravaginal / topical ketamine

- NMDA-receptor antagonist; rationale is modulation of peripheral sensitization at nociceptor afferents.<sup>[[37]](#ref37)</sup>
- **Formulation:** compounded topical combinations are described, but neither an effective vaginal dose nor equivalence between cream concentrations and suppository doses is established.
- **Evidence is weak:**
  - A 2025 Cochrane review found very-low-certainty evidence insufficient to determine whether topical ketamine reduces immediate-term chronic pain versus placebo (MD 1.90 on a 0–100 scale, 95% CI −18.73 to 22.53; one study, 47 participants). This was not intravaginal MFPP evidence.<sup>[[38]](#ref38)</sup>
  - Small studies of topical ketamine alone show no statistically significant benefit for neuropathic pain.
  - Retrospective analyses of topical ketamine combinations (including amitriptyline or lidocaine) for predominantly nonpelvic neuropathic pain reported 40–75% responses, with additional case reports; these findings do not establish benefit for vaginal MFPP, and optimal dosing and combinations remain unclear.<sup>[[55]](#ref55)</sup>
  - **No RCTs** of intravaginal ketamine for MFPP or vulvodynia.
- **Safety:** psychomimetic effects, unclear long-term cognitive impact, addiction risk. Chronic systemic ketamine causes ketamine cystitis (bladder inflammation or fibrosis), particularly relevant in the urologic context, and absence of reported cases with vaginal formulations does not establish their long-term safety.<sup>[[37]](#ref37)</sup>

### Summary of compounded intravaginal agents

| Agent | Dose evidence | Mechanism | Evidence | Clinical implication |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Diazepam | 5–10 mg studied | GABA-A modulator | Small RCTs and observational studies | Routine benefit unproven; systemic absorption and accumulation<sup>[[28]](#ref28)[[30]](#ref30)[[31]](#ref31)[[32]](#ref32)</sup> |
| Baclofen | No established vaginal dose | GABA-B agonist | Case reports / series | Vaginal efficacy and pharmacokinetics uncertain<sup>[[34]](#ref34)[[35]](#ref35)</sup> |
| Ketamine | No established vaginal dose | NMDA antagonist | Very low certainty | Topical effect uncertain; no basis for routine vaginal combinations<sup>[[38]](#ref38)</sup> |

## V. Practical Treatment Ladder

1. **Start with individualized PFPT**, relaxation and a home program. Select oral treatments for a defined comorbid pain phenotype; routine gabapentin for unexplained female CPP is not supported by the GaPP2 trial (see the [CPP hub](/docs/clinical-conditions/03h-pelvic-pain/chronic-pelvic-pain)).<sup>[[7]](#ref7)[[2]](#ref2)[[9]](#ref9)</sup>
2. **Persistent focal tenderness or inability to tolerate manual therapy:** consider local-anesthetic TPIs. Immediate PFPT may be feasible, but its added benefit rests on retrospective evidence; steroid addition is not routine.<sup>[[12]](#ref12)[[15]](#ref15)[[16]](#ref16)</sup>
3. **Refractory symptoms:** reassess diagnosis, comorbidities and goals before considering off-label Botox. Explain the negative larger trial and the positive small endometriosis-specific trial; neither warrants automatic escalation.<sup>[[40]](#ref40)[[41]](#ref41)[[42]](#ref42)</sup>
4. **Compounded vaginal drugs:** consider only through shared decisions about uncertain benefit, formulation variability and systemic adverse effects; avoid treating them as a mandatory stage.<sup>[[28]](#ref28)[[32]](#ref32)[[35]](#ref35)[[38]](#ref38)</sup>
5. **Multidisciplinary reassessment** is appropriate for persistent disability. An uncontrolled 186-woman series of nerve blocks, TPIs and PFPT reported improvement, but cannot isolate treatment effects or establish this combination as the standard protocol.<sup>[[7]](#ref7)[[39]](#ref39)</sup>

## Key Principles

- Include a structured pelvic-floor examination when evaluating pelvic pain; prevalence varies widely by case mix.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[2]](#ref2)[[6]](#ref6)</sup>
- Start with relaxation-focused PFPT; avoid unsupervised strengthening during symptomatic hypertonicity.<sup>[[9]](#ref9)[[40]](#ref40)</sup>
- TPIs can be considered for focal refractory tenderness; small studies do not establish injectant equivalence or require immediate post-injection PT.<sup>[[7]](#ref7)[[11]](#ref11)[[16]](#ref16)</sup>
- Botox is off-label, with mixed randomized evidence and no established optimal dose. Observational pudendal-block comparisons do not prove that a block can never help an individual patient.<sup>[[18]](#ref18)[[22]](#ref22)[[25]](#ref25)[[41]](#ref41)[[42]](#ref42)</sup>
- Compounded vaginal diazepam, baclofen and ketamine have uncertain benefit and require attention to systemic exposure and formulation safety.<sup>[[28]](#ref28)[[30]](#ref30)[[32]](#ref32)[[38]](#ref38)</sup>
- Routine opioids are not recommended for MFPP.<sup>[[7]](#ref7)[[2]](#ref2)</sup>
