# Interstitial Cystitis / Bladder Pain Syndrome

**Interstitial cystitis or bladder pain syndrome (IC/BPS)** is chronic bladder and/or pelvic pain, pressure, or discomfort of greater than six weeks' duration accompanied by urinary urgency and frequency, in the absence of infection or other identifiable causes.<sup>[[3]](#ref3)[[29]](#ref29)</sup> The 2022 AUA Guideline emphasizes an individualized, multimodal treatment approach rather than a tiered algorithm; treatments are grouped by modality (behavioral or non-pharmacologic, oral medications, intravesical, procedures, major surgery) and combined based on phenotype and response.<sup>[[3]](#ref3)</sup>

For the procedural and surgical end of the pathway, see [Pelvic Floor Physical Therapy](/docs/surgical-techniques/04f-incontinence-procedures/procedures/pelvic-floor-pt), [Intradetrusor onabotulinumtoxinA](/docs/surgical-techniques/04f-incontinence-procedures/procedures/intradetrusor-botox), [Sacral Neuromodulation](/docs/surgical-techniques/04f-incontinence-procedures/procedures/sacral-neuromodulation), and the major-surgery options ([Bladder Augmentation](/docs/surgical-techniques/04b-bladder-reconstruction/bladder-augmentation), [Supratrigonal Cystectomy](/docs/surgical-techniques/04b-bladder-reconstruction/supratrigonal-cystectomy), [Simple Cystectomy and Urinary Diversion](/docs/surgical-techniques/04c-urinary-diversion/simple-cystectomy)).

## Definition and Classification

### Diagnostic criteria

**ESSIC definition:** chronic pelvic pain, pressure, or discomfort perceived as bladder-related (ESSIC specified >6 months, versus AUA/CUA >6 weeks), with at least one other urinary symptom (persistent urge or frequency), after exclusion of confusable diseases. ESSIC also classifies the syndrome by cystoscopy with hydrodistension and bladder biopsy findings, a research-oriented step that the AUA does not require for diagnosis.<sup>[[32]](#ref32)</sup>

**Terminology (AUGS-IUGA 2025).** A joint AUGS-IUGA report proposes replacing "interstitial cystitis/bladder pain syndrome" in women with female bladder pain syndrome (FBPS): a chronic, intermittent condition of at least 3 months' duration affecting women, with pain or discomfort localized to the bladder, often with bladder filling, that is not attributed to other pathology. Hunner lesion disease is kept as a phenotypic subtype. The report covers women only and does not address men.<sup>[[34]](#ref34)</sup> The same report describes Hunner lesions in 5–10% of women with FBPS, advises cystoscopy for hematuria, suspected Hunner lesions or no relief with conservative care, and recommends against routine urodynamics for diagnosis (detrusor overactivity is found in about 15%, so the 1997 NIDDK urodynamic exclusion criteria do not separate FBPS from OAB).<sup>[[34]](#ref34)</sup> An AUGS commentary clarifies that "IC/BPS" remains appropriate for research grants and ICD-10 billing, because men also develop the condition (often labeled chronic prostatitis/chronic pelvic pain syndrome) and the term is established in payer systems and treatment protocols.<sup>[[35]](#ref35)</sup> This page uses IC/BPS, following the AUA guideline and the literature it cites.

**AUA Guideline basic diagnostic principles:**<sup>[[3]](#ref3)</sup>

- Symptoms present ≥6 weeks with documented negative urine cultures
- Document voiding frequency, urgency, pain location and severity
- Baseline assessment using GUPI, ICSI/ICPI, or VAS
- One-day voiding log to establish low-volume frequency pattern
- Brief neurological exam; evaluate for incomplete bladder emptying
- Cystoscopy and urodynamics not required for uncomplicated presentations; consider them when the diagnosis is in doubt (expert opinion)

### Phenotypic classification — Hunner vs non-Hunner

IC/BPS is increasingly recognized as two distinct clinical entities.<sup>[[2]](#ref2)[[5]](#ref5)[[6]](#ref6)</sup>

| Feature | Hunner-lesion IC/BPS | Non-Hunner IC/BPS |
|---|---|---|
| Age | Older (mean diff +6.7 years) | Younger |
| Pathology | Pancystitis, B-cell abnormalities, epithelial denudation | Minimal histological changes |
| Bladder capacity | Reduced (mean diff −113 mL) | Preserved |
| Urinary frequency | Higher (mean diff +3.2/day) | Lower |
| Urinary biomarkers | Elevated CXCL10, NGF, IL-6, IL-8, MIF, luminal NO | Lower inflammatory markers |
| Pain scores, symptom duration, sex | No significant difference in the meta-analysis | No significant difference |
| Comorbid pain syndromes | Some studies report fewer; results conflicting | Some studies report more (fibromyalgia, IBS); results conflicting |
| Treatment response | Responds to lesion-directed fulguration/triamcinolone; observational cyclosporine A series report higher response than in non-Hunner disease, but comparative evidence is limited | Variable; multimodal |

Beyond the Hunner/non-Hunner split, symptom pattern also predicts which treatment is more likely to help. Pelvic floor muscle tenderness on exam is common (81% of participants with a urologic chronic pelvic pain syndrome diagnosis versus 9% of controls in the MAPP Symptom Patterns Study) and tracks with worse disease severity, supporting a pelvic floor-directed evaluation and referral to myofascial physical therapy when tenderness is prominent.<sup>[[37]](#ref37)</sup> On baseline body maps from 424 MAPP participants (233 women, 191 men) with urologic chronic pelvic pain syndrome, 25% reported pain in the abdomen and pelvis only and 75% reported pain beyond the pelvis, 38% of whom had widespread pain (3 to 7 additional body regions); more pain locations were associated with worse nonpelvic pain, sleep disturbance, depression, anxiety and quality of life, but not with worse pelvic pain or urinary symptom severity.<sup>[[55]](#ref55)</sup> Separately, a MAPP reanalysis of three prior negative IC/BPS treatment trials found that participants with predominantly local, pelvic pain responded better to therapy targeting local (bladder) symptoms, while participants with widespread pain beyond the pelvis responded better to centrally acting treatment; this pain-distribution phenotype is a candidate way to match patients to bladder-directed versus systemic therapy, though it has not yet been tested prospectively as a treatment-selection tool.<sup>[[38]](#ref38)</sup>

## Epidemiology

**Prevalence:**<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[7]](#ref7)[[8]](#ref8)</sup>

- Women: 2.7–6.5% using symptom-survey definitions; 1.08% in a US population estimate derived from chart-validated Veterans Health Administration data and demographic adjustment
- Men: 0.66% in the same chart-validated, demographically adjusted estimate
- US overall: 0.87% (95% CI 0.32–1.42)
- Estimated 3.3–7.9 million US women with IC/BPS symptoms
- **Only 9.7%** of women meeting survey symptom criteria reported an IC/BPS diagnosis; survey-positive symptoms and a clinically established diagnosis are not interchangeable

**Demographics:**<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup>

- Sex ratios vary substantially with case definition and ascertainment; a universal 10:1 ratio is not supported by the chart-validated estimates above
- Peak onset 40–50 years
- Associated with depression, anxiety, sexual dysfunction, reduced QoL

## Pathophysiology

The etiology is incompletely understood; several mechanisms likely co-exist and interact.<sup>[[10]](#ref10)[[11]](#ref11)[[12]](#ref12)</sup>

1. **Urothelial dysfunction or GAG layer deficiency.** Defective glycosaminoglycan barrier allows urinary solutes (especially potassium) to penetrate the bladder wall<sup>[[10]](#ref10)[[11]](#ref11)</sup>
2. **Mast cell activation.** Increased mast cell infiltration with histamine release<sup>[[10]](#ref10)</sup>
3. **Chronic inflammation.** Persistent inflammatory state with elevated cytokines<sup>[[11]](#ref11)[[12]](#ref12)</sup>
4. **Oxidative stress.** Reactive oxygen species impair bladder function through multiple pathways<sup>[[11]](#ref11)[[12]](#ref12)</sup>
5. **Neurogenic inflammation.** C-fiber activation, substance P release, peripheral neural upregulation<sup>[[4]](#ref4)[[10]](#ref10)</sup>
6. **Autoimmune dysregulation.** B-cell abnormalities, particularly in the Hunner-lesion phenotype<sup>[[2]](#ref2)[[6]](#ref6)</sup>
7. **Central sensitization.** Spinal cord changes producing chronic neuropathic pain<sup>[[4]](#ref4)</sup>

## Diagnosis

**Basic assessment** (clinical principle):<sup>[[3]](#ref3)</sup> careful history, physical exam, urinalysis and culture; exclude infections and confusable disorders; document symptoms characteristic of IC/BPS.

**Ketamine use.** Regular recreational ketamine use is associated with severe storage symptoms, pelvic pain and ulcerative cystitis on cystoscopy, and ketamine-associated uropathy belongs in the differential diagnosis of ulcerative cystitis. Ask young adults with these features about ketamine use. See [Ketamine-Associated Uropathy (Ketamine Cystitis)](/docs/clinical-conditions/03h-pelvic-pain/ketamine-cystitis).<sup>[[56]](#ref56)</sup>

**In men,** IC/BPS overlaps substantially with chronic prostatitis/chronic pelvic pain syndrome and is likely under-recognized because of it: more than half of men with CP/CPPS report pain that worsens with bladder filling, a pattern also characteristic of IC/BPS.<sup>[[39]](#ref39)[[40]](#ref40)</sup> AUA 2025 advises considering IC/BPS in men with bladder-related pain, pressure or discomfort and lower urinary tract symptoms lasting more than six weeks, after excluding infection and other identifiable causes (Statement 9; Expert Opinion).<sup>[[53]](#ref53)</sup> Evaluate for bladder outlet obstruction, urethral stricture, stones or malignancy when the presentation suggests them.

**Cystoscopy indications:**<sup>[[3]](#ref3)</sup>

- Should be performed when Hunner lesions are suspected (expert opinion)
- Consider in patients >50 years (higher odds of Hunner lesions)
- Consider when the diagnosis is in doubt or conventional therapies fail (expert opinion; the guideline states cystoscopy and/or urodynamics are not necessary in uncomplicated presentations)
- Not required for routine diagnosis in uncomplicated presentations

**Glomerulations.** Hunner lesions typically involve the bladder dome, posterior wall and lateral walls, sparing the trigone.<sup>[[42]](#ref42)</sup> Glomerulations, submucosal petechiae seen after bladder distension, are a separate and nonspecific finding: a systematic review of 29 studies found no consistent relationship between glomerulations and IC/BPS diagnosis, no correlation with symptom severity, and reports of glomerulations in people without bladder pain, including asymptomatic women undergoing tubal ligation and men undergoing TURP for BPH.<sup>[[43]](#ref43)[[44]](#ref44)[[45]](#ref45)</sup> Glomerulations alone should not be used to confirm or exclude IC/BPS.

**Urine markers.** In 24-hour urine from 36 women with interstitial cystitis and 36 age-matched female controls, antiproliferative factor, epidermal growth factor, IGF binding protein-3 and IL-6 were increased and heparin-binding EGF-like growth factor and cyclic GMP were decreased in interstitial cystitis; only IL-6 correlated with a symptom (nocturia). Antiproliferative factor showed the least overlap between groups and was judged the most likely candidate for a diagnostic test, but this small study did not establish a clinical assay.<sup>[[54]](#ref54)</sup>

**Validated assessment instruments:**

- O'Leary–Sant Interstitial Cystitis Symptom Index (ICSI)
- O'Leary–Sant Interstitial Cystitis Problem Index (ICPI)
- Genitourinary Pain Index (GUPI)
- Visual Analog Scale (VAS) for pain
- Voiding diary (minimum 1 day)

See the [Assessment Tools & Questionnaires](/docs/evaluation/history-physical/assessment-tools) page for detail.

## Behavioral and Non-Pharmacologic Treatment

### Self-care and lifestyle (clinical principle)<sup>[[3]](#ref3)</sup>

- Fluid management (restriction or additional hydration)
- Individualized dietary modification based on reproducible symptom triggers
- Heat or cold application to bladder or perineum
- Stress management, meditation, imagery
- Bladder training with urge suppression
- Avoid tight-fitting clothing; manage constipation

### Dietary triggers and supplements

Commonly reported triggers include coffee, citrus, tomatoes, alcohol, carbonated drinks and spicy foods. Use a symptom diary and elimination and rechallenge to identify personal triggers; blanket exclusion of all these foods or all potassium-rich foods is not required.<sup>[[3]](#ref3)[[14]](#ref14)[[15]](#ref15)</sup>

Evidence for supplements such as calcium glycerophosphate is limited. Sodium bicarbonate adds sodium and alkali exposure and is not a routine treatment for every patient.<sup>[[15]](#ref15)</sup>

A small dietary-intervention study reported improved symptom and pain scores; this does not establish one effective exclusion diet for all IC/BPS phenotypes.<sup>[[14]](#ref14)</sup>

### Pelvic floor physical therapy — Statement 12 (Standard, Grade A)<sup>[[3]](#ref3)[[16]](#ref16)</sup>

**Appropriate manual techniques** should be offered to patients with pelvic floor tenderness when appropriately trained clinicians are available. Kegel-type pelvic floor strengthening should be avoided: the guideline finds no evidence that strengthening improves symptoms and notes it may worsen the condition. Where trained therapists are unavailable, it advises against routine strengthening-based pelvic floor therapy.

Appropriate techniques: myofascial release, trigger point therapy, soft tissue massage, connective tissue manipulation, muscle contracture lengthening.

**RCT evidence:** in 81 women with pelvic floor tenderness and symptoms for no more than three years, 10 sessions of myofascial PT produced 59% versus 26% global response with massage (p = 0.0012). Pain, urgency and frequency improved in both groups without significant between-group differences in those individual secondary outcomes.<sup>[[16]](#ref16)</sup>

## Oral Medications

**AUA Guideline Statement 14 (Option; evidence Grade B for amitriptyline, cimetidine and pentosan polysulfate, C for hydroxyzine):** these may be administered, listed alphabetically with no hierarchy implied. Statement 15 requires counseling about macular risk before PPS.<sup>[[3]](#ref3)[[17]](#ref17)</sup>

**CUA 2025 changes the PPS discussion:** it conditionally recommends against oral PPS, weighing limited benefit against visual harm and cost. It makes no recommendation for or against cyclosporine because evidence is insufficient, including for differential benefit by Hunner status.<sup>[[29]](#ref29)</sup> For current doses, drug monitoring and reassessment, use the [oral IC/BPS pharmacology hub](/docs/foundations/pharmacology/bladder-pain-ic-bps/oral-ic-bps-agents).

| Agent | Mechanism | Dosing | Evidence | Key considerations |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Amitriptyline** | TCA; anticholinergic, antihistamine, analgesic | See pharmacology hub | B | ICSI reduction; anticholinergic side effects |
| **Pentosan polysulfate (PPS)** | Proposed barrier protection | See pharmacology hub | AUA option (B); CUA 2025 conditional against | FDA-approved oral agent; retinal risk can be irreversible |
| **Hydroxyzine** | H1 antihistamine | See pharmacology hub | C | Sedation, anticholinergic burden and QT risk |
| **Cimetidine** | H2 blocker | See pharmacology hub | B | Limited evidence; may reduce pain |
| **Cyclosporine A** | Calcineurin inhibitor | Specialist-directed | AUA option (C), Hunner lesions refractory to fulguration/triamcinolone; off-label | Selected refractory cases; nephrotoxicity and monitoring burden |

Older network meta-analysis rankings do not establish a best oral agent.<sup>[[18]](#ref18)</sup> AUA permits cyclosporine, particularly for Hunner lesions refractory to fulguration and/or triamcinolone; this is an option requiring individualized toxicity assessment, not a general efficacy hierarchy.<sup>[[3]](#ref3)</sup>

### PPS maculopathy — safety alert

PPS is associated with a progressive, vision-threatening maculopathy.<sup>[[3]](#ref3)[[19]](#ref19)[[20]](#ref20)[[21]](#ref21)</sup>

**Clinical features:**<sup>[[19]](#ref19)[[21]](#ref21)[[22]](#ref22)</sup>

- Prolonged dark adaptation; difficulty reading; blurred vision
- Hyperpigmented macular clumps on fundus exam
- Distinctive autofluorescence abnormalities on multimodal imaging
- **May progress even after drug cessation**
- Dose-dependent (cumulative exposure)

**US prescribing-information warning (not boxed) and screening:**<sup>[[30]](#ref30)</sup>

- Most reported cases followed three or more years of use, but shorter use has been associated with pigmentary changes
- Detailed ophthalmologic history before initiation
- Comprehensive retinal examination before starting in patients with preexisting eye disease
- Baseline retinal exam including OCT and fundus autofluorescence is suggested for all patients within 6 months of starting
- Periodic retinal exams during treatment; no single annual interval is specified in the label
- If pigmentary changes develop, reevaluate continuation and maintain retinal follow-up after stopping because changes may progress

## Intravesical Instillations

**AUA 2022 (Option; evidence Grade B or C depending on agent):** DMSO, heparin, and/or lidocaine may be administered.<sup>[[3]](#ref3)</sup>

| Agent | Mechanism | Protocol | Evidence | Notes |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **DMSO 50% w/w (Rimso-50)** | Multimodal local effects | Label: 50 mL, 15-minute dwell, every 2 weeks until maximal relief, then longer intervals | Grade C | FDA-approved intravesical agent; garlic odor<sup>[[31]](#ref31)</sup> |
| **Lidocaine** | Local anesthetic | 1–2% solution, 20–30 min dwell | Grade B | Short-term relief; alkalinization increases penetration |
| **Heparin** | GAG-layer restoration | 10,000–40,000 U | Grade C; off-label | Often combined with lidocaine |
| **Hyaluronic acid** | GAG-layer restoration | 40 mg weekly × 4–6 | Off-label | European use; limited US data |
| **Chondroitin sulfate** | GAG-layer restoration | Variable | Off-label | Often combined with HA |

The Cochrane review of intravesical therapy found a limited, mixed-quality randomized evidence base and very sparse DMSO data.<sup>[[9]](#ref9)</sup> A 2025 DMSO meta-analysis included both randomized and observational evidence.<sup>[[24]](#ref24)</sup> Separately, Moss 2023 compared DMSO plus triamcinolone with BTH (bupivacaine/triamcinolone/heparin), not DMSO alone. Of 83 randomized patients, 70 completed treatment; the primary responder comparison among patients with evaluable paired ICSI data was nonsignificant (63% vs43%, p=0.15), while pain and nocturia changes favored DMSO plus triamcinolone.<sup>[[23]](#ref23)</sup>

**No single intravesical agent or cocktail is preferred by CUA 2025.**<sup>[[29]](#ref29)</sup> Use a pharmacy-validated formulation; see the [intravesical pharmacology hub](/docs/foundations/pharmacology/bladder-pain-ic-bps/intravesical-agents) for the exact Moss study mixtures and evidence limitations.

DMSO enhances absorption of other substances, which creates potential for lidocaine toxicity when both are included in a cocktail.<sup>[[3]](#ref3)</sup>

## Procedures

### Hydrodistension (Statement 18, Option, Grade C)<sup>[[3]](#ref3)</sup>

Cystoscopy under anesthesia with low-pressure (60 to 80 cm H<sub>2</sub>O), short-duration (under 10 minutes) hydrodistension. Allows inspection for Hunner lesions, stones, tumors; mild distension makes Hunner lesions easier to identify. Can provide clinically significant relief. Temporary symptom flares can occur. Observational repeat-treatment studies give mixed results on capacity under anesthesia: a retrospective review of 76 patients undergoing repeated Hunner-lesion electrocautery found no meaningful change (438.6 mL before versus 422.4 mL after treatment, with high patient satisfaction),<sup>[[50]](#ref50)</sup> while a separate retrospective review of 47 patients undergoing repeated fulguration or resection combined with hydrodistension found capacity at the fourth procedure had fallen to 80% of the capacity at the first procedure.<sup>[[51]](#ref51)</sup> These are distinct single-center cohorts, not a pooled estimate, and neither establishes that retreatment itself causes capacity loss. AUA Statement 25 (Recommendation, Grade C) advises against high-pressure, long-duration hydrodistension because of bladder rupture and sepsis risk.

Performed with the patient positioned as for standard operative cystoscopy (dorsal lithotomy; see [Endoscopy](/docs/evaluation/ancillary-tests/endoscopy)). The guideline does not describe routine catheter placement after an uncomplicated distension; its literature review instead describes an isolated case of bladder perforation during hydrodistension managed with an indwelling catheter for one week, a complication-driven drainage decision rather than a routine postprocedure step.<sup>[[3]](#ref3)</sup> No hydrodistension-specific VTE recommendation exists; as a brief anesthesia-based endoscopic procedure, it falls under the general low-risk ambulatory-procedure category in the [VTE Prophylaxis](/docs/foundations/pharmacology/perioperative-eras/vte-prophylaxis) framework rather than needing its own regimen.

### Hunner lesion treatment (Statement 19, Recommendation, Grade C)<sup>[[3]](#ref3)</sup>

When Hunner lesions are identified:

- **Fulguration** with laser or electrocautery
- **Intralesional triamcinolone**, with concentration, aliquots and total dose determined by the specialist protocol; see [intralesional corticosteroids](/docs/foundations/pharmacology/dermatologic-topical-urethral/intralesional-corticosteroids)
- Combination

As chapter-author technique, Hunner lesions are usually visible on initial entry to the bladder before formal distension causes bleeding; location, number and size are recorded first, suspicious lesions and trigonal mucosa are biopsied (Hunner lesions rarely involve the trigone), and overdistension is avoided because it can obscure the field. Fulguration outlines the outer margin of each lesion and proceeds inward, because inflammatory change tends to spread outward from the lesion with electrocautery. One described submucosal triamcinolone technique injects 0.5 mL aliquots to a depth of 2–3 mm; the bladder is then emptied and instilled with 20 mL of 1% lidocaine and 20 mL of 0.25% bupivacaine for postoperative comfort.<sup>[[49]](#ref49)</sup>

Benefit often lasts months and recurrence may require retreatment. Case series report symptom improvement in most Hunner-lesion patients (75–90%) after fulguration and/or triamcinolone injection, with mean time to retreatment of 7–12 months after triamcinolone injection and 11–29 months after fulguration.<sup>[[5]](#ref5)</sup> Follow symptoms and bladder capacity over time; repeated ablation should not be presented as risk-free or guaranteed to preserve capacity.

A single-center randomized trial of 126 patients with Hunner lesions found no significant difference between transurethral resection and transurethral coagulation in recurrence-free time or in secondary symptom outcomes (voiding-diary frequency, nocturia, urgency, ICSI/ICPI, the PUF scale and pain VAS); bladder injury was more frequent after resection (7.9%) than after coagulation (3.4%).<sup>[[46]](#ref46)</sup> In a separate single-center retrospective series of 59 patients (106 fulguration procedures over 1993–2011), 13.1% required repeat fulguration within 1 year and 57.2% by 4 years, when the rate plateaued.<sup>[[47]](#ref47)</sup>

### Intradetrusor botulinum toxin A (Statement 20, Option, Grade C)<sup>[[3]](#ref3)</sup>

An off-label option for IC/BPS after inadequate improvement with other treatments. CUA 2025 gives botulinum toxin A, with or without hydrodistension, a conditional recommendation based on very low-certainty evidence; it is not uniquely supported by strong evidence.<sup>[[29]](#ref29)</sup> Benefit may last several months. Common adverse events: dysuria, straining to void, elevated PVR, CISC requirement. Patients must accept the possibility of intermittent self-catheterization. Injection sites vary (trigonal, lateral, posterior walls). In a small randomized trial (n=47) of women wanting procedural treatment, onabotulinumtoxinA gave lower O'Leary-Sant scores than bladder instillations at 2 months, but the difference did not persist at 6–9 months; 8% needed self-catheterization, and retreatment within 6–9 months was less frequent after toxin.<sup>[[33]](#ref33)</sup>

### Sacral neuromodulation (Statement 21, Option, Grade C)<sup>[[3]](#ref3)[[25]](#ref25)[[26]](#ref26)[[27]](#ref27)</sup>

Not FDA-approved specifically for IC/BPS, but many patients meet frequency or urgency indications and respond. Less effective for pain than for frequency or urgency.

**Evidence:** a meta-analysis of 583 patients reported improved pain, but predominantly uncontrolled, heterogeneous studies limit causal inference. Longer-term cohorts report sustained improvement in selected recipients; these data do not establish a predictable durable response for all refractory patients.<sup>[[25]](#ref25)[[26]](#ref26)[[27]](#ref27)</sup>

### Percutaneous tibial nerve stimulation (PTNS) — not in the AUA guideline

PTNS is not addressed by the AUA IC/BPS guideline. In one trial, weekly 30-minute sessions for 12 weeks, followed by maintenance roughly every 4 weeks, decreased urgency, frequency and non-IC pelvic pain in up to 60% of patients treated for chronic pelvic pain; there is no trial evidence specifically for IC/BPS pain itself.<sup>[[41]](#ref41)</sup>

## Major Surgery

AUA Statement 22 (Option, Grade C) allows major surgery (substitution cystoplasty, or urinary diversion with or without cystectomy) in carefully selected patients with bladder-centric symptoms, or in the rare end-stage small fibrotic bladder, after all other therapies have failed. Evidence consists of small retrospective series with variable selection and outcome reporting. Patient selection matters more than operation type. The best-documented predictors of success are Hunner lesions and small bladder capacity under anesthesia, with capacity under 300 cc most favorable; this profile is uncommon within the overall IC/BPS population. Large capacity, absence of Hunner lesions, no relief from local-anesthetic instillation, pelvic muscle tenderness, pain beyond the pelvis and a polysymptomatic presentation point to an extravesical source and a higher risk of failure. Pain can persist after cystectomy, and the guideline advises against surgery in polysyndromic patients.<sup>[[3]](#ref3)</sup>

| Option | Effect on capacity | Effect on pain |
|---|---|---|
| **Augmentation cystoplasty** | Increases | Not reliably improved |
| **Supratrigonal cystectomy with augmentation** | Reservoir reconstructed; trigone preserved | Variable; in the guideline's pooled cohorts (n=170) 23% failed and 72% of those reporting voiding ability voided spontaneously; pain or ulcers can recur in the trigone |
| **Urinary diversion ± cystectomy** | Diverts | May or may not eliminate pain |

A separate systematic review of 20 studies (450 patients undergoing subtotal cystectomy with cystoplasty, cystectomy with orthotopic neobladder, cystectomy with ileal conduit, or urinary diversion alone) reported a 23% overall risk of failure to improve symptoms across these operations, with higher improvement rates after total cystectomy with orthotopic neobladder than after the other approaches; this is a distinct, broader estimate from the guideline's narrower supratrigonal-cystectomy-with-augmentation cohort above.<sup>[[48]](#ref48)</sup>

Irreversible; shared decision-making and multidisciplinary input (pain, psychiatry, PT) are needed.

## EAU 2026 Recommendations

EAU classifies this condition as primary bladder pain syndrome (PBPS), defined as persistent or recurrent bladder-region pain with at least one other symptom, such as pain worsening with filling or frequency, and no proven infection or other obvious local pathology. EAU reports a prevalence range of 0.06% to 20% across studies, a female predominance of about 10 to 1, and a Hunner lesion proportion of 5% to 50%. It states that pain does not correlate with cystoscopic or histologic findings, and that type 3C disease can be confirmed only by cystoscopy and histology.<sup>[[36]](#ref36)</sup>

Diagnosis and general care (all strong): perform rigid cystoscopy under general anesthesia to subtype the disease and exclude confusable disease, use a validated symptom and quality-of-life instrument, assess associated non-bladder conditions and psychological, sexual and behavioral effects, and offer subtype- and phenotype-oriented therapy. Always consider multimodal behavioral, physical and psychological techniques alongside oral or invasive treatment (strong). Offer dietary advice (weak).<sup>[[36]](#ref36)</sup>

| EAU 2026 recommendation | Strength | Evidence level |
|---|---|---|
| Amitriptyline | Strong | 1b |
| Oral pentosan polysulfate | Strong | 1a |
| Oral pentosan polysulfate plus subcutaneous heparin in low responders to pentosan alone | Weak | 1b |
| Do not use oral corticosteroids long term | Strong | 3 |
| Intravesical hyaluronic acid or chondroitin sulfate, heparin, or lidocaine with sodium bicarbonate before more invasive measures | Weak | 2b, 3 and 1b respectively |
| Do not use bladder distension alone as treatment | Weak | 3 |
| Submucosal bladder wall and trigonal onabotulinumtoxinA plus hydrodistension if instillations have failed | Strong | 1b |
| Neuromodulation before more invasive interventions | Weak | 3 for sacral neuromodulation; pudendal nerve stimulation was superior to sacral neuromodulation (1b) |
| Transurethral resection, coagulation or laser of bladder lesions in type 3C only | Strong | 3 |
| Ablative or reconstructive surgery only as a last resort, by experienced surgeons after multidisciplinary assessment including pain management | Strong | 3 |

EAU rates intravesical BCG as not effective (1b), notes limited data for cimetidine (2b) and calls the outcome of cystectomy variable (3). For pentosan, EAU reports about half of patients responding at 32 weeks, a response related to treatment duration rather than dose, and a better effect in non-lesion disease than in type 3C for frequency. The EAU text does not address pentosan maculopathy, so counsel on retinal risk using the AUA and CUA statements above, which differ from EAU's strong recommendation.<sup>[[36]](#ref36)</sup>

## Treatment Algorithm Summary

The AUA 2022 approach is modality-based; the options below are not a mandatory sequence:<sup>[[3]](#ref3)</sup>

- **All patients.** Education, self-care, behavioral modifications
- **Pelvic floor tenderness.** Manual physical therapy (avoid Kegels)
- **Suspected Hunner lesions.** Cystoscopy, then fulguration and/or triamcinolone if present
- **Oral medications.** Symptom-directed options; PPS requires the updated guideline and retinal-risk discussion above
- **Intravesical.** DMSO, heparin, and/or lidocaine (alone or in combination)
- **Procedures.** Hydrodistension, botulinum toxin A, sacral neuromodulation
- **Major surgery.** [augmentation cystoplasty](/docs/surgical-techniques/04b-bladder-reconstruction/bladder-augmentation), [supratrigonal cystectomy](/docs/surgical-techniques/04b-bladder-reconstruction/supratrigonal-cystectomy), [simple cystectomy and urinary diversion](/docs/surgical-techniques/04c-urinary-diversion/simple-cystectomy) (carefully selected bladder-centric disease or an end-stage small fibrotic bladder after other feasible treatments have failed; Hunner lesions are not required).<sup>[[3]](#ref3)</sup>

**Core principles:** treatment is individualized; shared decision-making is required; multimodal approaches are often required; phenotyping (Hunner versus non-Hunner) guides therapy.

---

## Treatments Not to Offer

The AUA 2022 guideline advises against:<sup>[[3]](#ref3)</sup>

- Long-term oral antibiotics (Statement 23, Standard, Grade B)
- Intravesical BCG outside investigational settings (Statement 24, Standard, Grade B)
- High-pressure, long-duration hydrodistension (Statement 25, Recommendation, Grade C)
- Long-term oral glucocorticoids (Statement 26, Recommendation, Grade C)

As evidence context for the antibiotic recommendation, a 50-patient randomized, placebo-controlled trial of 18 weeks of sequential oral antibiotics (rifampin followed by 3-week courses of doxycycline, erythromycin, metronidazole, clindamycin, amoxicillin and ciprofloxacin) found no significant difference in intention-to-treat overall improvement (12/25 vs 6/25, p=0.14) or pain/urgency improvement (10/25 vs 5/25, p=0.22), with more adverse effects on antibiotics (80% vs 40%, p=0.009).<sup>[[52]](#ref52)</sup>

**Potassium sensitivity testing** should not be used as a diagnostic tool: a positive result does not change management or treatment, false positives occur in other conditions on the differential (bacterial cystitis, radiation cystitis), and the test is painful and can trigger a severe symptom flare.

## Comorbidities

IC/BPS frequently co-occurs with other chronic pain and functional conditions:<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[12]](#ref12)[[15]](#ref15)</sup>

- Irritable bowel syndrome
- Fibromyalgia
- Chronic fatigue syndrome
- Vulvodynia
- Chronic headache / migraine
- Temporomandibular disorder
- Depression and anxiety

This overlap supports common mechanisms (central sensitization, chronic inflammation, oxidative stress) and a multidisciplinary treatment approach.<sup>[[13]](#ref13)</sup>
