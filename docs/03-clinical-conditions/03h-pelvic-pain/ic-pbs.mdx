# Interstitial Cystitis / Bladder Pain Syndrome

**Interstitial cystitis or bladder pain syndrome (IC/BPS)** is chronic bladder and/or pelvic pain, pressure, or discomfort of greater than six weeks' duration accompanied by urinary urgency and frequency, in the absence of infection or other identifiable causes.<sup>[[3]](#ref3)[[29]](#ref29)</sup> The 2022 AUA Guideline emphasizes an individualized, multimodal treatment approach rather than a tiered algorithm; treatments are grouped by modality (behavioral or non-pharmacologic, oral medications, intravesical, procedures, major surgery) and combined based on phenotype and response.<sup>[[3]](#ref3)</sup>

For the procedural and surgical end of the pathway, see [Pelvic Floor Physical Therapy](/docs/surgical-techniques/04f-incontinence-procedures/procedures/pelvic-floor-pt), [Intradetrusor onabotulinumtoxinA](/docs/surgical-techniques/04f-incontinence-procedures/procedures/intradetrusor-botox), [Sacral Neuromodulation](/docs/surgical-techniques/04f-incontinence-procedures/procedures/sacral-neuromodulation), and the major-surgery options ([Bladder Augmentation](/docs/surgical-techniques/04b-bladder-reconstruction/bladder-augmentation), [Supratrigonal Cystectomy](/docs/surgical-techniques/04b-bladder-reconstruction/supratrigonal-cystectomy), [Simple Cystectomy and Urinary Diversion](/docs/surgical-techniques/04c-urinary-diversion/simple-cystectomy)).

## Definition and Classification

### Diagnostic criteria

**ESSIC definition:** chronic pelvic pain, pressure, or discomfort perceived as bladder-related (ESSIC specified >6 months, versus AUA/CUA >6 weeks), with at least one other urinary symptom (persistent urge or frequency), after exclusion of confusable diseases. ESSIC also classifies the syndrome by cystoscopy with hydrodistension and bladder biopsy findings, a research-oriented step that the AUA does not require for diagnosis.<sup>[[32]](#ref32)</sup>

**Terminology (AUGS-IUGA 2025).** A joint AUGS-IUGA report proposes replacing "interstitial cystitis/bladder pain syndrome" in women with female bladder pain syndrome (FBPS): a chronic, intermittent condition of at least 3 months' duration affecting women, with pain or discomfort localized to the bladder, often with bladder filling, that is not attributed to other pathology. Hunner lesion disease is kept as a phenotypic subtype. The report covers women only and does not address men.<sup>[[34]](#ref34)</sup> An AUGS commentary clarifies that "IC/BPS" remains appropriate for research grants and ICD-10 billing, because men also develop the condition (often labeled chronic prostatitis/chronic pelvic pain syndrome) and the term is established in payer systems and treatment protocols.<sup>[[35]](#ref35)</sup> This page uses IC/BPS, following the AUA guideline and the literature it cites.

**AUA Guideline basic diagnostic principles:**<sup>[[3]](#ref3)</sup>

- Symptoms present ≥6 weeks with documented negative urine cultures
- Document voiding frequency, urgency, pain location and severity
- Baseline assessment using GUPI, ICSI/ICPI, or VAS
- One-day voiding log to establish low-volume frequency pattern
- Brief neurological exam; evaluate for incomplete bladder emptying
- Cystoscopy and urodynamics not required for uncomplicated presentations; consider them when the diagnosis is in doubt (expert opinion)

### Phenotypic classification — Hunner vs non-Hunner

IC/BPS is increasingly recognized as two distinct clinical entities.<sup>[[2]](#ref2)[[5]](#ref5)[[6]](#ref6)</sup>

| Feature | Hunner-lesion IC/BPS | Non-Hunner IC/BPS |
|---|---|---|
| Age | Older (mean diff +6.7 years) | Younger |
| Pathology | Pancystitis, B-cell abnormalities, epithelial denudation | Minimal histological changes |
| Bladder capacity | Reduced (mean diff −113 mL) | Preserved |
| Urinary frequency | Higher (mean diff +3.2/day) | Lower |
| Urinary biomarkers | Elevated CXCL10, NGF, IL-6, IL-8, MIF, luminal NO | Lower inflammatory markers |
| Pain scores, symptom duration, sex | No significant difference in the meta-analysis | No significant difference |
| Comorbid pain syndromes | Some studies report fewer; results conflicting | Some studies report more (fibromyalgia, IBS); results conflicting |
| Treatment response | Responds to lesion-directed fulguration/triamcinolone; comparative studies suggest better response to cyclosporine A | Variable; multimodal |

## Epidemiology

**Prevalence:**<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[7]](#ref7)[[8]](#ref8)</sup>

- Women: 2.7–6.5% using symptom-survey definitions; 1.08% in a US population estimate derived from chart-validated Veterans Health Administration data and demographic adjustment
- Men: 0.66% in the same chart-validated, demographically adjusted estimate
- US overall: 0.87% (95% CI 0.32–1.42)
- Estimated 3.3–7.9 million US women with IC/BPS symptoms
- **Only 9.7%** of women meeting survey symptom criteria reported an IC/BPS diagnosis; survey-positive symptoms and a clinically established diagnosis are not interchangeable

**Demographics:**<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup>

- Sex ratios vary substantially with case definition and ascertainment; a universal 10:1 ratio is not supported by the chart-validated estimates above
- Peak onset 40–50 years
- Associated with depression, anxiety, sexual dysfunction, reduced QoL

## Pathophysiology

The etiology is incompletely understood; several mechanisms likely co-exist and interact.<sup>[[10]](#ref10)[[11]](#ref11)[[12]](#ref12)</sup>

1. **Urothelial dysfunction or GAG layer deficiency.** Defective glycosaminoglycan barrier allows urinary solutes (especially potassium) to penetrate the bladder wall<sup>[[10]](#ref10)[[11]](#ref11)</sup>
2. **Mast cell activation.** Increased mast cell infiltration with histamine release<sup>[[10]](#ref10)</sup>
3. **Chronic inflammation.** Persistent inflammatory state with elevated cytokines<sup>[[11]](#ref11)[[12]](#ref12)</sup>
4. **Oxidative stress.** Reactive oxygen species impair bladder function through multiple pathways<sup>[[11]](#ref11)[[12]](#ref12)</sup>
5. **Neurogenic inflammation.** C-fiber activation, substance P release, peripheral neural upregulation<sup>[[4]](#ref4)[[10]](#ref10)</sup>
6. **Autoimmune dysregulation.** B-cell abnormalities, particularly in the Hunner-lesion phenotype<sup>[[2]](#ref2)[[6]](#ref6)</sup>
7. **Central sensitization.** Spinal cord changes producing chronic neuropathic pain<sup>[[4]](#ref4)</sup>

## Diagnosis

**Basic assessment** (clinical principle):<sup>[[3]](#ref3)</sup> careful history, physical exam, urinalysis and culture; exclude infections and confusable disorders; document symptoms characteristic of IC/BPS.

**Cystoscopy indications:**<sup>[[3]](#ref3)</sup>

- Should be performed when Hunner lesions are suspected (expert opinion)
- Consider in patients >50 years (higher odds of Hunner lesions)
- Consider when the diagnosis is in doubt or conventional therapies fail (expert opinion; the guideline states cystoscopy and/or urodynamics are not necessary in uncomplicated presentations)
- Not required for routine diagnosis in uncomplicated presentations

**Validated assessment instruments:**

- O'Leary–Sant Interstitial Cystitis Symptom Index (ICSI)
- O'Leary–Sant Interstitial Cystitis Problem Index (ICPI)
- Genitourinary Pain Index (GUPI)
- Visual Analog Scale (VAS) for pain
- Voiding diary (minimum 1 day)

See the [Assessment Tools & Questionnaires](/docs/evaluation/history-physical/assessment-tools) page for detail.

## Behavioral and Non-Pharmacologic Treatment

### Self-care and lifestyle (clinical principle)<sup>[[3]](#ref3)</sup>

- Fluid management (restriction or additional hydration)
- Individualized dietary modification based on reproducible symptom triggers
- Heat or cold application to bladder or perineum
- Stress management, meditation, imagery
- Bladder training with urge suppression
- Avoid tight-fitting clothing; manage constipation

### Dietary triggers and supplements

Commonly reported triggers include coffee, citrus, tomatoes, alcohol, carbonated drinks and spicy foods. Use a symptom diary and elimination and rechallenge to identify personal triggers; blanket exclusion of all these foods or all potassium-rich foods is not required.<sup>[[3]](#ref3)[[14]](#ref14)[[15]](#ref15)</sup>

Evidence for supplements such as calcium glycerophosphate is limited. Sodium bicarbonate adds sodium and alkali exposure and is not a routine treatment for every patient.<sup>[[15]](#ref15)</sup>

A small dietary-intervention study reported improved symptom and pain scores; this does not establish one effective exclusion diet for all IC/BPS phenotypes.<sup>[[14]](#ref14)</sup>

### Pelvic floor physical therapy — Grade A (Standard)<sup>[[3]](#ref3)[[16]](#ref16)</sup>

**Appropriate manual techniques** should be offered to patients with pelvic floor tenderness. Kegel-type pelvic floor strengthening should be avoided; the muscles are typically hypertonic, not weak.

Appropriate techniques: myofascial release, trigger point therapy, soft tissue massage, connective tissue manipulation, muscle contracture lengthening.

**RCT evidence:** in 81 women with pelvic floor tenderness and symptoms for no more than three years, 10 sessions of myofascial PT produced 59% versus 26% global response with massage (p = 0.0012). Pain, urgency and frequency improved in both groups without significant between-group differences in those individual secondary outcomes.<sup>[[16]](#ref16)</sup>

## Oral Medications

**AUA Guideline Statement 14 (Option; evidence Grade B for amitriptyline, cimetidine and pentosan polysulfate, C for hydroxyzine):** these may be administered, listed alphabetically with no hierarchy implied. Statement 15 requires counseling about macular risk before PPS.<sup>[[3]](#ref3)[[17]](#ref17)</sup>

**CUA 2025 changes the PPS discussion:** it conditionally recommends against oral PPS, weighing limited benefit against visual harm and cost. It makes no recommendation for or against cyclosporine because evidence is insufficient, including for differential benefit by Hunner status.<sup>[[29]](#ref29)</sup> For current doses, drug monitoring and reassessment, use the [oral IC/BPS pharmacology hub](/docs/foundations/pharmacology/bladder-pain-ic-bps/oral-ic-bps-agents).

| Agent | Mechanism | Dosing | Evidence | Key considerations |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Amitriptyline** | TCA; anticholinergic, antihistamine, analgesic | See pharmacology hub | B | ICSI reduction; anticholinergic side effects |
| **Pentosan polysulfate (PPS)** | Proposed barrier protection | See pharmacology hub | AUA option (B); CUA 2025 conditional against | FDA-approved oral agent; retinal risk can be irreversible |
| **Hydroxyzine** | H1 antihistamine | See pharmacology hub | C | Sedation, anticholinergic burden and QT risk |
| **Cimetidine** | H2 blocker | See pharmacology hub | B | Limited evidence; may reduce pain |
| **Cyclosporine A** | Calcineurin inhibitor | Specialist-directed | AUA option (C), Hunner lesions refractory to fulguration/triamcinolone; off-label | Selected refractory cases; nephrotoxicity and monitoring burden |

Older network meta-analysis rankings do not establish a best oral agent.<sup>[[18]](#ref18)</sup> AUA permits cyclosporine, particularly for Hunner lesions refractory to fulguration and/or triamcinolone; this is an option requiring individualized toxicity assessment, not a general efficacy hierarchy.<sup>[[3]](#ref3)</sup>

### PPS maculopathy — safety alert

PPS is associated with a progressive, vision-threatening maculopathy.<sup>[[3]](#ref3)[[19]](#ref19)[[20]](#ref20)[[21]](#ref21)</sup>

**Clinical features:**<sup>[[19]](#ref19)[[21]](#ref21)[[22]](#ref22)</sup>

- Prolonged dark adaptation; difficulty reading; blurred vision
- Hyperpigmented macular clumps on fundus exam
- Distinctive autofluorescence abnormalities on multimodal imaging
- **May progress even after drug cessation**
- Dose-dependent (cumulative exposure)

**US prescribing-information warning (not boxed) and screening:**<sup>[[30]](#ref30)</sup>

- Detailed ophthalmologic history before initiation
- Comprehensive retinal examination before starting in patients with preexisting eye disease
- Baseline retinal exam including OCT and fundus autofluorescence is suggested for all patients within 6 months of starting
- Periodic retinal exams during treatment; no single annual interval is specified in the label
- If pigmentary changes develop, reevaluate continuation and maintain retinal follow-up after stopping because changes may progress

## Intravesical Instillations

**AUA 2022 (Option; evidence Grade B or C depending on agent):** DMSO, heparin, and/or lidocaine may be administered.<sup>[[3]](#ref3)</sup>

| Agent | Mechanism | Protocol | Evidence | Notes |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **DMSO 50% w/w (Rimso-50)** | Multimodal local effects | Label: 50 mL, 15-minute dwell, every 2 weeks until maximal relief, then longer intervals | Grade C | FDA-approved intravesical agent; garlic odor<sup>[[31]](#ref31)</sup> |
| **Lidocaine** | Local anesthetic | 1–2% solution, 20–30 min dwell | Grade B | Short-term relief; alkalinization increases penetration |
| **Heparin** | GAG-layer restoration | 10,000–40,000 U | Grade C; off-label | Often combined with lidocaine |
| **Hyaluronic acid** | GAG-layer restoration | 40 mg weekly × 4–6 | Off-label | European use; limited US data |
| **Chondroitin sulfate** | GAG-layer restoration | Variable | Off-label | Often combined with HA |

The Cochrane review of intravesical therapy found a limited, mixed-quality randomized evidence base and very sparse DMSO data.<sup>[[9]](#ref9)</sup> A 2025 DMSO meta-analysis included both randomized and observational evidence.<sup>[[24]](#ref24)</sup> Separately, Moss 2023 compared DMSO plus triamcinolone with BTH (bupivacaine/triamcinolone/heparin), not DMSO alone. Of 83 randomized patients, 70 completed treatment; the primary responder comparison among patients with evaluable paired ICSI data was nonsignificant (63% vs43%, p=0.15), while pain and nocturia changes favored DMSO plus triamcinolone.<sup>[[23]](#ref23)</sup>

**No single intravesical agent or cocktail is preferred by CUA 2025.**<sup>[[29]](#ref29)</sup> Use a pharmacy-validated formulation; see the [intravesical pharmacology hub](/docs/foundations/pharmacology/bladder-pain-ic-bps/intravesical-agents) for the exact Moss study mixtures and evidence limitations.

DMSO enhances absorption of other substances, which creates potential for lidocaine toxicity when both are included in a cocktail.<sup>[[3]](#ref3)</sup>

## Procedures

### Hydrodistension (Option, Grade C)<sup>[[3]](#ref3)</sup>

Cystoscopy under anesthesia with low-pressure, short-duration hydrodistension. Allows inspection for Hunner lesions, stones, tumors; mild distension makes Hunner lesions easier to identify. Can provide clinically significant relief. Temporary symptom flares can occur. Observational repeat-treatment studies are reassuring about capacity, but do not guarantee absence of harm. AUA advises against high-pressure, long-duration hydrodistension (Recommendation, Grade C) because of bladder rupture and sepsis risk.

### Hunner lesion treatment (Recommendation, Grade C)<sup>[[3]](#ref3)</sup>

When Hunner lesions are identified:

- **Fulguration** with electrocautery
- **Intralesional triamcinolone**, with concentration, aliquots and total dose determined by the specialist protocol; see [intralesional corticosteroids](/docs/foundations/pharmacology/dermatologic-topical-urethral/intralesional-corticosteroids)
- Combination

Benefit often lasts months and recurrence may require retreatment. Follow symptoms and bladder capacity over time; repeated ablation should not be presented as risk-free or guaranteed to preserve capacity.

### Intradetrusor botulinum toxin A (Option, Grade C)<sup>[[3]](#ref3)</sup>

An off-label option for IC/BPS after inadequate improvement with other treatments. CUA 2025 gives botulinum toxin A, with or without hydrodistension, a conditional recommendation based on very low-certainty evidence; it is not uniquely supported by strong evidence.<sup>[[29]](#ref29)</sup> Benefit may last several months. Common adverse events: dysuria, straining to void, elevated PVR, CISC requirement. Patients must accept the possibility of intermittent self-catheterization. Injection sites vary (trigonal, lateral, posterior walls). In a small randomized trial (n=47) of women wanting procedural treatment, onabotulinumtoxinA gave lower O'Leary-Sant scores than bladder instillations at 2 months, but the difference did not persist at 6–9 months; 8% needed self-catheterization, and retreatment within 6–9 months was less frequent after toxin.<sup>[[33]](#ref33)</sup>

### Sacral neuromodulation (Option, Grade C)<sup>[[3]](#ref3)[[25]](#ref25)[[26]](#ref26)[[27]](#ref27)</sup>

Not FDA-approved specifically for IC/BPS, but many patients meet frequency or urgency indications and respond. Less effective for pain than for frequency or urgency.

**Evidence:** a meta-analysis of 583 patients reported improved pain, but predominantly uncontrolled, heterogeneous studies limit causal inference. Longer-term cohorts report sustained improvement in selected recipients; these data do not establish a predictable durable response for all refractory patients.<sup>[[25]](#ref25)[[26]](#ref26)[[27]](#ref27)</sup>

## Major Surgery

Reserved for severe, refractory cases after all other options are exhausted. Requires extensive counseling; pain can persist after cystectomy because of central sensitization.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup>

| Option | Effect on capacity | Effect on pain |
|---|---|---|
| **Augmentation cystoplasty** | Increases | Not reliably improved |
| **Supratrigonal cystectomy with augmentation** | Reservoir reconstructed; trigone preserved | Variable; careful bladder-centric selection |
| **Urinary diversion ± cystectomy** | Diverts | May or may not eliminate pain |

Irreversible; shared decision-making and multidisciplinary input (pain, psychiatry, PT) are needed.

## Treatment Algorithm Summary

The AUA 2022 approach is modality-based; the options below are not a mandatory sequence:<sup>[[3]](#ref3)</sup>

- **All patients.** Education, self-care, behavioral modifications
- **Pelvic floor tenderness.** Manual physical therapy (avoid Kegels)
- **Suspected Hunner lesions.** Cystoscopy, then fulguration and/or triamcinolone if present
- **Oral medications.** Symptom-directed options; PPS requires the updated guideline and retinal-risk discussion above
- **Intravesical.** DMSO, heparin, and/or lidocaine (alone or in combination)
- **Procedures.** Hydrodistension, botulinum toxin A, sacral neuromodulation
- **Major surgery.** [augmentation cystoplasty](/docs/surgical-techniques/04b-bladder-reconstruction/bladder-augmentation), [supratrigonal cystectomy](/docs/surgical-techniques/04b-bladder-reconstruction/supratrigonal-cystectomy), [simple cystectomy and urinary diversion](/docs/surgical-techniques/04c-urinary-diversion/simple-cystectomy) (carefully selected bladder-centric disease or an end-stage small fibrotic bladder after other feasible treatments have failed; Hunner lesions are not required).<sup>[[3]](#ref3)</sup>

**Core principles:** treatment is individualized; shared decision-making is required; multimodal approaches are often required; phenotyping (Hunner versus non-Hunner) guides therapy.

---

## Treatments Not to Offer

The AUA 2022 guideline advises against:<sup>[[3]](#ref3)</sup>

- Long-term oral antibiotics (Standard, Grade B)
- Intravesical BCG outside investigational settings (Standard, Grade B)
- High-pressure, long-duration hydrodistension (Recommendation, Grade C)
- Long-term oral glucocorticoids (Recommendation, Grade C)

---

## Comorbidities

IC/BPS frequently co-occurs with other chronic pain and functional conditions:<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[12]](#ref12)[[15]](#ref15)</sup>

- Irritable bowel syndrome
- Fibromyalgia
- Chronic fatigue syndrome
- Vulvodynia
- Chronic headache / migraine
- Temporomandibular disorder
- Depression and anxiety

This overlap supports common mechanisms (central sensitization, chronic inflammation, oxidative stress) and a multidisciplinary treatment approach.<sup>[[13]](#ref13)</sup>
