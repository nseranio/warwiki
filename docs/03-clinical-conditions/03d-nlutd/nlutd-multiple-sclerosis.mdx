# Multiple Sclerosis

Multiple sclerosis (MS) is the second-largest NLUTD population by volume. Lower urinary tract symptoms become more common with MS duration and are reported by almost all patients 10 years or more after symptom onset. OAB or detrusor overactivity can occur early and coexist with impaired emptying; a minority have detrusor areflexia or DSD as spinal plaques accumulate.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[3]](#ref3)</sup> The reconstructive urologist's role in MS is different from SCI: the lesions are disseminated and evolving, the phenotype can shift, and major reconstruction is uncommon, but botulinum, neuromodulation, and occasionally augmentation or diversion are all in play for the subset with refractory disease.

See [Neurogenic Lower Urinary Tract Dysfunction](/docs/clinical-conditions/03d-nlutd/neurogenic-bladder) for the general framework. For the procedural options that come into play in refractory MS, see [Intradetrusor OnabotulinumtoxinA](/docs/surgical-techniques/04f-incontinence-procedures/procedures/intradetrusor-botox), [Sacral Neuromodulation](/docs/surgical-techniques/04f-incontinence-procedures/procedures/sacral-neuromodulation), [Percutaneous Tibial Nerve Stimulation](/docs/surgical-techniques/04f-incontinence-procedures/procedures/percutaneous-tibial-nerve-stimulation), [Bladder Augmentation](/docs/surgical-techniques/04b-bladder-reconstruction/bladder-augmentation), and the [Ileal Conduit](/docs/surgical-techniques/04c-urinary-diversion/ileal-conduit).

## Epidemiology and Natural History

- **US prevalence** about 1 million; more common in women (about 3:1) and young adults.
- About 10% of patients have voiding dysfunction at onset and about 75% develop it after 10 years of disease (EAU 2026).<sup>[[6]](#ref6)</sup>
- Urodynamic series (EAU 2026 summary) report neurogenic detrusor overactivity in 43–65%, DSD in about 35% and detrusor underactivity in about 25%; overlapping patterns are common, so the categories do not sum to 100%.<sup>[[6]](#ref6)</sup>
- **Urinary symptoms track disability and disease duration.** LUTS correlate with age, clinical course, disease duration, EDSS disability and fatigue.<sup>[[6]](#ref6)</sup> They are rarely isolated and usually coexist with bowel, sexual and mobility impairment. Symptoms alone do not predict the urodynamic pattern, which is why unknown-risk patients need urodynamics.

---

## Pathophysiology

MS plaques disseminate in time and space. Urinary phenotype depends chiefly on the location of lesions, although it also drifts with disease duration and disability:

- **Cortical or subcortical plaques** give OAB or DO (uninhibited detrusor contractions).
- **Cervical or thoracic spinal plaques** give DO and DSD (suprasacral phenotype; upper-tract risk if sustained high pressure).
- **Sacral cord / conus involvement** may impair detrusor contraction. Cauda-equina disease is a peripheral lesion; do not automatically attribute it to MS without evaluating another cause.

Because lesions evolve, a patient's phenotype can shift (for example, from pure OAB to OAB with incomplete emptying), requiring re-evaluation when symptoms change.

## Evaluation

Initial workup is the AUA/SUFU framework.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup> Most MS patients are unknown-risk at entry and should have baseline:

- History and focused neurologic exam (EDSS, mobility, dexterity, cognition)
- Voiding diary and UA and PVR
- Renal ultrasound and eGFR
- Multichannel urodynamics to complete unknown-risk classification

**Additional reasons to reassess with multichannel urodynamics:**
- Refractory symptoms despite empirical therapy
- Elevated PVR suggesting DSD or underactivity
- Upper-tract deterioration
- Before botulinum injection, SNM, or reconstructive surgery
- Recurrent febrile UTI

Video-urodynamics specifically documents DSD and VUR, both of which change management.

In a neurourology clinic series of patients with MS, 66 of 113 referred patients had both urodynamics and screening renal ultrasonography at initial evaluation. Eleven (16.7%) had abnormal ultrasound findings, mostly focal caliectasis, and none had more than mild hydronephrosis. No demographic factor, serum creatinine or urodynamic finding, including DSD and the threshold or amplitude of detrusor overactivity, was associated with an abnormal ultrasound, so the series did not identify a subgroup in whom baseline imaging could be omitted.<sup>[[14]](#ref14)</sup>

Unknown-risk is not low-risk. AUA/SUFU (2021; Moderate Recommendation, Grade C) calls for renal function assessment, upper-tract imaging and multichannel urodynamics to classify unknown-risk NLUTD; mild symptoms alone do not establish safe storage. For stable moderate risk, perform annual focused history, examination, symptom and renal function assessment, with upper-tract imaging every 1–2 years. For stable high risk, perform annual focused history, examination, symptom and renal function assessment plus annual upper-tract imaging, and repeat multichannel urodynamics when clinically indicated (both Moderate Recommendations, Grade C).<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup>

## Management

### Behavioral and pharmacologic first line

- **Fluid management** (avoid late-evening intake for nocturia).
- **Bowel regimen.** MS constipation is common and worsens urinary symptoms.
- **Pelvic-floor muscle training.** Modest benefit in MS; AUA/SUFU allows it in appropriately selected patients, particularly those with MS or cerebrovascular accident (statement 32, conditional recommendation, Grade C).<sup>[[2]](#ref2)</sup>
- **Antimuscarinics.** EAU strongly recommends antimuscarinics or β3 agonists for neurogenic OAB and calls antimuscarinics its first-line choice for urodynamically confirmed NDO.<sup>[[6]](#ref6)</sup> Trospium (minimal CNS penetration) is often preferred in MS given cognitive risk.
- **β3-adrenergic agonists** (mirabegron, vibegron) are an alternative when anticholinergic side effects or cognitive concerns are limiting; EAU 2026 advises switching to a β3 agonist or other therapy if cognition is affected. Short MS/SCI studies of mirabegron report symptom benefits similar to antimuscarinics, without significant improvement in detrusor pressure or cystometric capacity. A retrospective SCI cohort reported improved capacity and compliance with vibegron, but MS urodynamic benefit and upper-tract protection remain unestablished; EAU 2026 strongly recommends against using β3 agonists to reduce urodynamically proven NDO.<sup>[[6]](#ref6)</sup><sup>[[6]](#ref6)</sup>
- **Combination therapy** when monotherapy inadequate.
- **Desmopressin.** Selected nocturnal-polyuria cases after renal, sodium and fluid-intake assessment; older age raises hyponatremia risk. Evidence in MS is heterogeneous and low quality, hyponatremia is frequent, and EAU 2026 strongly recommends screening and monitoring serum sodium.<sup>[[6]](#ref6)[[9]](#ref9)</sup> Use the [drug-specific safety and monitoring guidance](/docs/foundations/pharmacology/storage-oab/desmopressin).
- **α-blockers.** May improve voiding parameters in selected patients who spontaneously void; they are not a substitute for safe drainage.<sup>[[2]](#ref2)</sup>

### Catheterization

- **CIC.** Consider for clinically important incomplete emptying, unsafe voiding or an agreed continence plan. A single PVR &gt;100 mL or DSD label alone is not a universal indication; consider capacity, repeat volumes, symptoms, infections and pressures. Counsel before starting: in one retrospective MS cohort with PVR &gt;100 mL, starting CIC was associated with about seven times the odds of at least one UTI over one year (OR 6.8) and less symptom improvement than medical therapy.<sup>[[6]](#ref6)[[13]](#ref13)</sup> Assess hand dexterity, positioning, vision, cognition and caregiver support before CIC; reassess catheterization ability as MS progresses.<sup>[[5]](#ref5)[[6]](#ref6)</sup>
- **Indwelling SPC.** When dexterity, spasticity, or caregiver support preclude CIC; AUA/SUFU recommends suprapubic over urethral drainage in appropriately selected patients who need a chronic indwelling catheter (statement 36, strong recommendation, Grade C), and CIC over indwelling catheters for emptying (statement 35, strong, Grade C).<sup>[[2]](#ref2)</sup>
- **Intermittent versus indwelling drainage.** Assess ability, caregiver support, complications and treatment burden. CIC may be assisted and does not guarantee freedom from infection; SPC is an option when the intermittent regimen is not feasible.<sup>[[5]](#ref5)</sup>

### Intradetrusor onabotulinumtoxinA

For refractory adult detrusor overactivity associated with MS, the US NDO dose is 200 U; the 100-U idiopathic OAB regimen is a different indication. Assess retention risk and ensure a feasible catheterization plan if needed. Retreat according to response and the label’s minimum interval, not an automatic calendar. AUA/SUFU strongly recommends onabotulinumtoxinA for MS or SCI refractory to oral medication (statement 40, Grade A) and requires discussion of retention risk and possible CIC before treatment in patients who void (statement 42, Clinical Principle). EAU 2026 strongly recommends intradetrusor botulinum toxin A when antimuscarinic therapy fails; effect lasts about nine months and repeat injections remain effective.<sup>[[6]](#ref6)</sup>

| Trial (MS-relevant) | Dose | Result |
|---|---|---|
| Ginsberg 2012 (MS and SCI, n = 416)<sup>[[10]](#ref10)</sup> | 200 U or 300 U vs placebo | At week 6, incontinence fell about 21–23 vs 9 episodes/week; among patients not catheterizing at baseline, 35% (200 U) and 42% (300 U) started CIC, vs 10% on placebo; no added benefit from 300 U |
| Tullman 2018 (MS, non-catheterizing, n = 144)<sup>[[11]](#ref11)</sup> | 100 U vs placebo | At week 6, incontinence fell 3.3 vs 1.1 episodes/day; CIC started in 15.2% vs 2.6% during treatment cycle 1; protocol-defined UTI during the first 12 weeks (culture plus leukocyturia, symptoms not required) 25.8% (17/66) vs 6.4% (5/78); symptomatic UTI 13.6% (9/66) vs 1.3% (1/78) |

The 100-U MS data suggest lower retention rates in patients who do not already catheterize, but 200 U is the labeled NDO dose, so plan CIC either way. See [indications, dosing and follow-up](/docs/surgical-techniques/04f-incontinence-procedures/procedures/intradetrusor-botox#fda-approved-indications-and-dosing).<sup>[[2]](#ref2)</sup>

### Sacral neuromodulation (SNM)

- **Reasonable** in selected MS patients with refractory OAB or non-obstructive retention (AUA/SUFU statement 49, conditional recommendation, Grade C, for urgency, frequency or urgency incontinence; the statement 50 exclusion covers SCI and spina bifida, not MS); EAU 2026 notes MS patients with NDO are often good responders, but disease-specific evidence is limited and urodynamic effects are uncertain.<sup>[[2]](#ref2)[[6]](#ref6)</sup>
- Test phase before permanent implant; MS lesions can complicate response.
- **MRI compatibility.** MS patients require periodic MRI surveillance, which makes compatibility a required consideration. MRI-conditional generator and lead combinations permit surveillance imaging under their specified conditions. Older systems may allow head-only MRI, which does not meet a need for spinal surveillance; verify the generator, lead, field strength and scan region against current local labeling before implantation.<sup>[[16]](#ref16)</sup> See the [SNM page](/docs/surgical-techniques/04f-incontinence-procedures/procedures/sacral-neuromodulation#mri).<sup>[[6]](#ref6)</sup>

### Percutaneous tibial nerve stimulation (PTNS)

An option for selected spontaneously voiding patients with urgency, frequency or urgency incontinence (AUA/SUFU statement 48, conditional recommendation, Grade C).<sup>[[2]](#ref2)</sup> A meta-analysis of eight MS studies reported improved PVR and symptom outcomes, but the studies are small and uncontrolled, and evidence does not establish a durability ranking against SNM in MS.<sup>[[5]](#ref5)[[12]](#ref12)</sup>

### Cannabinoids

Nabiximols (Sativex, oromucosal THC/CBD) is not approved for bladder indications in the US. In the 135-patient RCT in MS-related OAB the primary endpoint (incontinence episodes) did not differ from placebo, though nocturia, voids per day and global impression improved.<sup>[[7]](#ref7)</sup> An exploratory pooled analysis of two randomized trials and one open-label study (426 patients enrolled; a control group was constructed for the open-label study) found a small reduction in incontinence (mean difference −0.35 episodes/24 h) with frequent mild adverse events; EAU 2026 calls the evidence preliminary.<sup>[[6]](#ref6)[[8]](#ref8)</sup>

### Surgical reconstruction

- **Stress incontinence and outlet surgery.** When stress leakage coexists with MS-related storage dysfunction, establish acceptable storage parameters and a feasible emptying plan before sling or AUS surgery; concurrent bladder management may be needed.<sup>[[2]](#ref2)[[6]](#ref6)</sup>
- **Augmentation cystoplasty.** Rare in MS; consider for refractory DO or poor compliance after less-invasive therapy; SNM failure and established upper-tract injury are not mandatory prerequisites.<sup>[[2]](#ref2)</sup>
- **Continent catheterizable channel (Mitrofanoff).** Occasionally constructed when hand dexterity for urethral CIC fails but abdominal-wall access is still feasible.
- **Ileal conduit.** For patients with severe disability, no practical CIC option, and refractory incontinence. Selection depends on the individual’s disease trajectory, goals and ability to sustain bladder care; MS diagnosis alone does not establish a lower surgical threshold.

## Special Considerations in MS

### MRI and device selection

MS patients typically need recurrent brain and spinal-cord MRIs for disease surveillance. Always verify device MRI compatibility before permanent implants (for example, SNM or AUS).

### Disease-modifying therapy and perioperative planning

- Many MS patients are on immunomodulators (interferons, natalizumab, fingolimod, ocrelizumab). Coordinate medication-specific perioperative decisions with the MS neurologist, considering infection, immune status and relapse or rebound risk; do not use a blanket stop-or-continue rule.
- **Relapse steroids.** The French MS Society found no increased UTI incidence after high-dose methylprednisolone in the available MS studies (Level C); assess and treat symptomatic UTI before relapse treatment.<sup>[[15]](#ref15)</sup>

### Progressive disease and advance care planning

Urologic management evolves as disability progresses. Discuss future options before an acute deterioration; botulinum, catheter drainage and reconstruction are individualized choices rather than a mandatory sequence.

### UTI in MS

- Incidence of symptomatic UTI is higher in MS, especially in those on CIC or with high PVR.
- **Pseudo-relapse.** Febrile UTI can transiently worsen existing MS symptoms without new inflammatory activity. Evaluate fever and urinary symptoms alongside neurologic change; treat symptomatic UTI and avoid treating asymptomatic bacteriuria.<sup>[[4]](#ref4)[[6]](#ref6)</sup>

## Sexual Function

- **Sexual dysfunction:** in one 217-person MS questionnaire study, 56% of women and 75% of men reported sexual dysfunction.<sup>[[17]](#ref17)</sup>
- Women: reduced lubrication, anorgasmia, genital sensory changes.
- Men: EAU strongly recommends oral PDE5 inhibitors as first-line medical treatment for neurogenic erectile dysfunction, with intracavernosal injections as second-line and prostheses reserved for selected refractory cases.<sup>[[6]](#ref6)</sup>
- Ejaculatory dysfunction is common; fertility workup as indicated.

## Clinical Correlations for the Reconstructive Urologist

- **Symptoms change with disease.** Reassess any MS patient whose urinary pattern shifts; a previously effective regimen can become inadequate with new spinal plaques.
- **MRI compatibility drives device choice.** SNM MRI-conditional models, AUS, and any implant require verification against the patient's MS-surveillance MRI protocol.
- **Cognitive load of medications matters.** Prefer β3 agonists and trospium over other anticholinergics in MS with cognitive symptoms.
- **Early management.** Behavioral measures or medication may address early urinary symptoms, but unknown-risk MS still requires renal function assessment, upper-tract imaging and multichannel urodynamics; symptom improvement alone does not establish safe storage.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[6]](#ref6)</sup>
- **Multidisciplinary team.** MS neurologist, urologist, pelvic-floor PT, incontinence / continent-catheter nurse educator, mental health support for the sexual-function domain.
