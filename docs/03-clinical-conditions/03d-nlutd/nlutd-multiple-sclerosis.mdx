# Multiple Sclerosis

**Multiple sclerosis (MS)** is the second-largest NLUTD population by volume. Approximately **75% of patients develop urinary symptoms** during their disease course, most commonly **OAB / detrusor overactivity** early, evolving toward **mixed patterns with impaired emptying** and, in a minority, frank detrusor areflexia or **DSD** as spinal plaques accumulate.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[3]](#ref3)</sup> The reconstructive urologist's role in MS is different from SCI: the lesions are disseminated and evolving, the phenotype can shift, and major reconstruction is uncommon — but botulinum, neuromodulation, and occasionally augmentation / diversion are all in play for the subset with refractory disease.

See [Neurogenic Lower Urinary Tract Dysfunction](/docs/clinical-conditions/03d-nlutd/neurogenic-bladder) for the general framework. For the procedural options that come into play in refractory MS, see [Intradetrusor OnabotulinumtoxinA](/docs/surgical-techniques/04f-incontinence-procedures/procedures/intradetrusor-botox), [Sacral Neuromodulation](/docs/surgical-techniques/04f-incontinence-procedures/procedures/sacral-neuromodulation), [Percutaneous Tibial Nerve Stimulation](/docs/surgical-techniques/04f-incontinence-procedures/procedures/percutaneous-tibial-nerve-stimulation), [Bladder Augmentation](/docs/surgical-techniques/04b-bladder-reconstruction/bladder-augmentation), and the [Ileal Conduit](/docs/surgical-techniques/04c-urinary-diversion/ileal-conduit).

---

## Epidemiology and Natural History

- **US prevalence** ~1 million; more common in women (~3:1) and young adults.
- About **10% of patients have voiding dysfunction at onset** and about **75% develop it after 10 years** of disease (EAU 2026).<sup>[[6]](#ref6)</sup>
- Urodynamic series (EAU 2026 summary) report **neurogenic detrusor overactivity in 43–65%**, **DSD in about 35%** and **detrusor underactivity in about 25%**; overlapping patterns are common, so the categories do not sum to 100%.<sup>[[6]](#ref6)</sup>
- **Urinary symptoms track disability and disease duration.** LUTS correlate with age, clinical course, disease duration, EDSS disability and fatigue.<sup>[[6]](#ref6)</sup> They are rarely isolated and usually coexist with bowel, sexual and mobility impairment. Symptoms alone do not predict the urodynamic pattern, which is why unknown-risk patients need urodynamics.

---

## Pathophysiology

MS plaques disseminate in time and space. Urinary phenotype depends chiefly on the **location of lesions**, although it also drifts with disease duration and disability:

- **Cortical / subcortical plaques** → **OAB / DO** (uninhibited detrusor contractions).
- **Cervical / thoracic spinal plaques** → **DO + DSD** (suprasacral phenotype; upper-tract risk if sustained high pressure).
- **Sacral cord / conus involvement** may impair detrusor contraction. Cauda-equina disease is a peripheral lesion; do not automatically attribute it to MS without evaluating another cause.

Because lesions evolve, a patient's phenotype can shift — e.g., from pure OAB to OAB + incomplete emptying — requiring re-evaluation when symptoms change.

## Evaluation

Initial workup is the AUA/SUFU framework.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup> Most MS patients are **unknown-risk** at entry and should have baseline:

- History + focused neurologic exam (EDSS, mobility, dexterity, cognition)
- Voiding diary + UA + PVR
- Renal ultrasound and eGFR
- Multichannel urodynamics to complete unknown-risk classification

**Additional reasons to reassess with multichannel urodynamics:**
- Refractory symptoms despite empirical therapy
- Elevated PVR suggesting DSD or underactivity
- Upper-tract deterioration
- Before botulinum injection, SNM, or reconstructive surgery
- Recurrent febrile UTI

Video-urodynamics specifically documents DSD and VUR, both of which change management.

**Unknown-risk is not low-risk.** Under AUA/SUFU, complete renal assessment, upper-tract imaging and multichannel urodynamics to establish risk in an unknown-risk patient; mild symptoms alone do not establish safe storage.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup>

## Management

### Behavioral and pharmacologic first line

- **Fluid management** (avoid late-evening intake for nocturia).
- **Bowel regimen** — MS constipation is common and worsens urinary symptoms.
- **Pelvic-floor muscle training** — modest benefit in MS.
- **Antimuscarinics** — first-line for OAB. Trospium (minimal CNS penetration) is often preferred in MS given cognitive risk.
- **β3-adrenergic agonists** (mirabegron, vibegron) — an alternative when anticholinergic side effects or cognitive concerns are limiting; EAU 2026 advises switching to a β3 agonist or other therapy if cognition is affected. They improve symptoms about as well as antimuscarinics but have not been shown to lower detrusor pressure or raise capacity in NDO, so do not rely on them to protect the upper tract (EAU Strong: do not use β3 agonists with the intention of reducing urodynamically proven NDO).<sup>[[6]](#ref6)</sup>
- **Combination therapy** when monotherapy inadequate.
- **Desmopressin** — selected nocturnal-polyuria cases after renal, sodium and fluid-intake assessment; older age raises hyponatremia risk. Evidence in MS is heterogeneous and low quality, hyponatremia is frequent, and EAU 2026 strongly recommends screening and monitoring serum sodium.<sup>[[6]](#ref6)[[9]](#ref9)</sup> Use the [drug-specific safety and monitoring guidance](/docs/foundations/pharmacology/storage-oab/desmopressin).
- **α-blockers** — may improve voiding parameters in selected patients who spontaneously void; they are not a substitute for safe drainage.<sup>[[2]](#ref2)</sup>

### Catheterization

- **CIC** — consider for clinically important incomplete emptying, unsafe voiding or an agreed continence plan. A single PVR &gt;100 mL or DSD label alone is not a universal indication; consider capacity, repeat volumes, symptoms, infections and pressures. Counsel before starting: in one retrospective MS cohort with PVR &gt;100 mL, starting CIC was associated with about seven times the odds of at least one UTI over one year (OR 6.8) and less symptom improvement than medical therapy.<sup>[[6]](#ref6)[[13]](#ref13)</sup> Many MS patients retain sufficient hand function for CIC well into the disease; visual and cognitive issues are commoner obstacles than motor.
- **Indwelling SPC** — when dexterity, spasticity, or caregiver support preclude CIC; preferable to urethral catheter in men and in women with progressive disability.
- **Intermittent versus indwelling drainage** — assess ability, caregiver support, complications and treatment burden. CIC may be assisted and does not guarantee freedom from infection; SPC is an option when the intermittent regimen is not feasible.<sup>[[5]](#ref5)</sup>

### Intradetrusor onabotulinumtoxinA

For refractory adult detrusor overactivity associated with MS, the US NDO dose is **200 U**; the 100-U idiopathic OAB regimen is a different indication. Assess retention risk and ensure a feasible catheterization plan if needed. Retreat according to response and the label’s minimum interval, not an automatic calendar. EAU 2026 strongly recommends intradetrusor botulinum toxin A when antimuscarinic therapy fails; effect lasts about nine months and repeat injections remain effective.<sup>[[6]](#ref6)</sup>

| Trial (MS-relevant) | Dose | Result |
|---|---|---|
| Ginsberg 2012 (MS and SCI, n = 416)<sup>[[10]](#ref10)</sup> | 200 U or 300 U vs placebo | Incontinence fell about 21–23 vs 9 episodes/week; among patients not catheterizing at baseline, 35% (200 U) and 42% (300 U) started CIC, vs 10% on placebo; no added benefit from 300 U |
| Tullman 2018 (MS, non-catheterizing, n = 144)<sup>[[11]](#ref11)</sup> | 100 U vs placebo | Incontinence fell 3.3 vs 1.1 episodes/day; CIC started in 15.2% vs 2.6%; UTI 25.8% |

The 100-U MS data suggest lower retention rates in patients who do not already catheterize, but 200 U is the labeled NDO dose, so plan CIC either way. See [indications, dosing and follow-up](/docs/surgical-techniques/04f-incontinence-procedures/procedures/intradetrusor-botox#fda-approved-indications-and-dosing).<sup>[[2]](#ref2)</sup>

### Sacral neuromodulation (SNM)

- **Reasonable** in selected MS patients with refractory OAB or non-obstructive retention; EAU 2026 notes MS patients with NDO are often good responders, but disease-specific evidence is limited and urodynamic effects are uncertain.<sup>[[2]](#ref2)[[6]](#ref6)</sup>
- Test phase before permanent implant; MS lesions can complicate response.
- **MRI compatibility** — critical consideration because MS patients require periodic MRI surveillance. Current MRI-conditional generators and leads remove the old reason to avoid SNM in patients who need MRI follow-up (EAU 2026), but eligibility depends on the exact generator and lead models; older leads are excluded. See the [SNM page](/docs/surgical-techniques/04f-incontinence-procedures/procedures/sacral-neuromodulation#mri).<sup>[[6]](#ref6)</sup>

### Percutaneous tibial nerve stimulation (PTNS)

An option for selected neurogenic storage symptoms. A meta-analysis of eight MS studies reported improved PVR and symptom outcomes, but the studies are small and uncontrolled, and evidence does not establish a durability ranking against SNM in MS.<sup>[[5]](#ref5)[[12]](#ref12)</sup>

### Cannabinoids

Nabiximols (Sativex, oromucosal THC/CBD) is not approved for bladder indications in the US. In the 135-patient RCT in MS-related OAB the primary endpoint (incontinence episodes) did not differ from placebo, though nocturia, voids per day and global impression improved.<sup>[[7]](#ref7)</sup> A pooled analysis of three studies (n = 426) found a small reduction in incontinence (mean difference −0.35 episodes/24 h) with frequent mild adverse events; EAU 2026 calls the evidence preliminary.<sup>[[6]](#ref6)[[8]](#ref8)</sup>

### Surgical reconstruction

- **Augmentation cystoplasty** — rare in MS; consider for refractory DO or poor compliance after less-invasive therapy; SNM failure and established upper-tract injury are not mandatory prerequisites.<sup>[[2]](#ref2)</sup>
- **Continent catheterizable channel (Mitrofanoff)** — occasionally constructed when hand dexterity for urethral CIC fails but abdominal-wall access is still feasible.
- **Ileal conduit** — for patients with severe disability, no practical CIC option, and refractory incontinence. Selection depends on the individual’s disease trajectory, goals and ability to sustain bladder care; MS diagnosis alone does not establish a lower surgical threshold.

## Special Considerations in MS

### MRI and device selection

MS patients typically need recurrent brain and spinal-cord MRIs for disease surveillance. **Always verify device MRI compatibility** before permanent implants (for example, SNM or AUS).

### Disease-modifying therapy and perioperative planning

- Many MS patients are on **immunomodulators** (interferons, natalizumab, fingolimod, ocrelizumab). Coordinate medication-specific perioperative decisions with the MS neurologist, considering infection, immune status and relapse/rebound risk; do not use a blanket stop-or-continue rule.
- Steroid courses for MS relapses increase short-term UTI risk.

### Progressive disease and advance care planning

Urologic management evolves as disability progresses. Discuss future options before an acute deterioration; botulinum, catheter drainage and reconstruction are individualized choices rather than a mandatory sequence.

### UTI in MS

- Incidence of symptomatic UTI is higher in MS, especially in those on CIC or with high PVR.
- **Infection can cause a pseudo-relapse** (temporary worsening without new inflammatory activity). Evaluate infection and neurological change rather than automatically labeling deterioration a true MS relapse; treat symptomatic infection and avoid treating asymptomatic bacteriuria.<sup>[[4]](#ref4)</sup>

## Sexual Function

- **~50% of women** and **~70% of men** with MS report sexual dysfunction.
- Women: reduced lubrication, anorgasmia, genital sensory changes.
- Men: erectile dysfunction → PDE5i first-line; intracavernosal injection or prosthesis in refractory cases.
- Ejaculatory dysfunction is common; fertility workup as indicated.

---

## Clinical Correlations for the Reconstructive Urologist

- **Symptoms change with disease.** Reassess any MS patient whose urinary pattern shifts; a previously effective regimen can become inadequate with new spinal plaques.
- **MRI compatibility drives device choice.** SNM MRI-conditional models, AUS, and any implant require verification against the patient's MS-surveillance MRI protocol.
- **Cognitive load of medications matters.** Prefer β3 agonists and trospium over other anticholinergics in MS with cognitive symptoms.
- **Aggressive early management pays off.** Empiric behavioral + pharmacologic therapy resolves most early MS urinary symptoms; complete risk assessment in unknown-risk disease rather than waiting for treatment failure or renal injury.
- **Multidisciplinary team.** MS neurologist, urologist, pelvic-floor PT, incontinence / continent-catheter nurse educator, mental health support for the sexual-function domain.
