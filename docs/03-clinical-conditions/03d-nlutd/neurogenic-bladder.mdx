# Neurogenic Lower Urinary Tract Dysfunction

**Neurogenic lower urinary tract dysfunction (NLUTD)**, the preferred contemporary term for what is still clinically called neurogenic bladder, is abnormal function of the bladder, bladder neck, and external urethral sphincter resulting from disease of the central or peripheral nervous system.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[2]](#ref2)[[3]](#ref3)</sup> For the reconstructive urologist, NLUTD is a lifetime risk-management problem. The goal is never just continence or quality of life but preservation of renal function through detection and treatment of high-pressure storage, followed by continence restoration in a durable way, for a patient whose neurological condition may be stable, relapsing or progressive. Lesion level suggests possible patterns, but each disease population (SCI, MS, Parkinson's, spina bifida, stroke, cauda equina) has its own natural history and evaluation cadence, covered in dedicated articles linked below.

See also [Autonomic Dysreflexia](/docs/clinical-conditions/03d-nlutd/autonomic-dysreflexia); population-specific articles [Spinal Cord Injury](/docs/clinical-conditions/03d-nlutd/nlutd-spinal-cord-injury), [Multiple Sclerosis](/docs/clinical-conditions/03d-nlutd/nlutd-multiple-sclerosis), [Spina Bifida](/docs/clinical-conditions/03d-nlutd/nlutd-spina-bifida), [Parkinson's Disease](/docs/clinical-conditions/03d-nlutd/nlutd-parkinson), [Stroke and Dementia](/docs/clinical-conditions/03d-nlutd/nlutd-stroke-dementia), and [Cauda Equina Syndrome](/docs/clinical-conditions/03d-nlutd/nlutd-cauda-equina).

## Epidemiology

- **Spinal cord injury (SCI).** Prevalence about 250,000 in the US; about 17,000 new cases per year. Nearly 100% develop NLUTD.
- **Multiple sclerosis (MS).** About 1 million in the US; about 75% develop voiding or storage symptoms over their disease course.<sup>[[3]](#ref3)</sup>
- **Spina bifida (myelomeningocele).** About 1,500 births/year in the US; about 85% have NLUTD at birth.
- **Parkinson disease.** &gt;1 million in the US; about 25–40% have urologic symptoms, typically overactive bladder.
- **Stroke.** About 795,000/year in the US; about 50% have urinary symptoms acutely; about 30% at 6 months.
- **Cauda equina syndrome.** Rare but a surgical emergency; produces lifetime NLUTD in most.
- **Diabetic cystopathy.** A late neuropathic complication in about 25–50% of long-standing diabetes.

The reconstructive workload in NLUTD is dominated in volume by MS and stroke, but dominated in complexity by SCI and spina bifida.

## Pathophysiology — The Lesion-Level Framework

Bladder control depends on an intact loop: bladder wall afferents to the spinal cord, then the pontine micturition center (PMC), then descending spinal efferents to the bladder and sphincter. Lesion level suggests a phenotype, but incomplete, mixed and evolving lesions require clinical and urodynamic confirmation.<sup>[[3]](#ref3)[[4]](#ref4)</sup>

| Lesion level | Examples | Dominant pattern | Storage pressure | Coordination |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Suprapontine** | Stroke, Parkinson, dementia, MS with cortical involvement | **Detrusor overactivity (DO)** without DSD | Usually low | **Preserved** |
| **Suprasacral spinal (above the sacral micturition circuitry)** | SCI above conus, MS with spinal plaques, transverse myelitis | **DO + detrusor–sphincter dyssynergia (DSD)** | May be high — assess upper-tract risk | **Disrupted** |
| **Sacral / infrasacral / peripheral** | Cauda equina, sacral SCI, pelvic-surgery / radical-hysterectomy denervation, diabetic cystopathy, spina bifida (conus level) | **Detrusor underactivity / areflexia** with variable sphincter weakness | Can be high from fibrosis / poor compliance | Depends on residual detrusor and sphincter function |

Diabetic cystopathy's classic description as a purely underactive bladder is an oversimplification. In a retrospective review of urodynamic traces from 182 diabetic patients, detrusor overactivity (55%) was at least as common as impaired detrusor contractility (23%) or detrusor areflexia (10%). A separate urodynamic study of 84 diabetic patients reported urgency in 55%, detrusor overactivity in 42% and increased bladder sensation in 14%, with detrusor-overactivity prevalence independent of diabetes duration. Expect a mixed storage-and-emptying picture rather than a predictable underactive-only phenotype.<sup>[[14]](#ref14)[[15]](#ref15)</sup>

**Pediatric transverse myelitis** often presents with urinary retention requiring catheterization. In the largest reported pediatric series (43 children who required CIC at presentation), intact lower-extremity reflexes and complete neurologic recovery were each associated with a return to volitional voiding, but the cumulative risk of remaining on CIC was 60% at 1 year and still 42% at 10 years — more than half of those who initially catheterized still needed CIC at 1 year. Counsel families that bladder recovery can continue for years and does not track tightly with overall neurologic recovery.<sup>[[23]](#ref23)</sup>

### Spinal shock and its recovery

After acute suprasacral SCI, bladder behavior evolves during spinal shock and recovery. Maintain drainage and reassess as neurological function stabilizes; a fixed 3–6-month waiting period must not delay evaluation of unsafe storage or upper-tract deterioration.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[9]](#ref9)</sup> The bladder is initially areflexic because injury above the lumbosacral cord disrupts the supraspinal reflex pathway carried by myelinated Aδ afferents; a spinal micturition reflex then emerges as unmyelinated, capsaicin-sensitive C-fiber bladder afferents that do not trigger voiding in the intact state become unmasked, converting the bladder from areflexic to reflexic over the recovery period. Voiding through this reconfigured spinal reflex is often inefficient because of the detrusor–sphincter dyssynergia described above.<sup>[[13]](#ref13)</sup>

### Why upper-tract pressure is the endpoint

DLPP &gt;40 cmH₂O is a historical upper-tract risk marker derived from myelodysplasia: in McGuire's 42 patients, reflux was present in 15 of 22 (68%) with leak pressure above 40 cmH₂O and in none of 20 at or below it.<sup>[[6]](#ref6)</sup> A value below 40 does not establish safety in every adult NLUTD phenotype. In a retrospective series of 88 adults with spinal cord pathology, a storage pressure of only ≥15 cmH₂O was associated with upper-tract damage (adjusted RR 3.9), as was compliance &lt;12.5 mL/cmH₂O.<sup>[[10]](#ref10)</sup> Interpret storage pressures, compliance, reflux, imaging, and renal-function trends together. Neither a single DLPP nor compliance &gt;10–12 mL/cmH₂O is a universal treatment endpoint.<sup>[[6]](#ref6)[[10]](#ref10)</sup>

## Classification

Multiple systems exist; three are widely used:

### Madersbacher classification

Describes detrusor and sphincter function together to distinguish storage, emptying and outlet problems; the observed functional pattern guides treatment.<sup>[[3]](#ref3)</sup>

### ICS functional classification

Based on filling-phase detrusor function and voiding-phase emptying performance. Useful for framing clinical decisions but less mechanistic than Madersbacher.

### AUA-SUFU risk stratification (2021)

The contemporary, practical framework used in the AUA/SUFU NLUTD guideline.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[2]](#ref2)</sup>

| Risk tier | Definition | Workup intensity |
|---|---|---|
| **Low-risk** | Normal and stable renal function, low PVR in voiding patients, normal and stable imaging (if done), and synergic voiding on urodynamics (if done) | No routine imaging, renal testing, or urodynamics (statements 5 and 13) |
| **Unknown-risk** | Information insufficient to classify risk (for example suprasacral lesions, which are unknown-risk until evaluated) | Imaging, renal function, multichannel urodynamics (statement 6) |
| **Moderate-risk** | Normal and stable renal function and normal imaging, with an elevated PVR in voiding patients, neurogenic retention, or DO with incomplete emptying | Scheduled surveillance (statement 14) |
| **High-risk** | Abnormal or unstable renal function; hydronephrosis, new renal scarring, loss of renal parenchyma, or staghorn or large stone burden on imaging; poor compliance; VUR on fluoroscopic urodynamics; or high storage pressures with DO and DSD | Closer surveillance and treatment of the risk factor (statement 15) |

A patient is placed in the highest stratum met in any one category (Table 3 of the guideline). **CIC dependence alone does not make a patient high-risk.** Assign risk from the combined clinical, renal, imaging, and urodynamic findings.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup>

## Clinical Presentation

### Symptoms by phase

| Phase | Symptoms |
|---|---|
| **Storage** | Urgency, frequency, nocturia, urge incontinence (OAB-type symptoms) |
| **Voiding** | Hesitancy, intermittency, weak stream, incomplete emptying, retention |
| **Post-void** | Post-micturition dribble, early refilling |

### Red-flag findings

- **Autonomic dysreflexia** in SCI above T6: see [Autonomic Dysreflexia](/docs/clinical-conditions/03d-nlutd/autonomic-dysreflexia).
- **Persistent hydronephrosis** on surveillance imaging.
- **Renal-function decline.** Rising creatinine, falling eGFR.
- **Recurrent febrile UTIs.** Assess for impaired emptying, stones, unsafe pressure and other causes; do not wait for a fixed count when an infection is serious.
- **New leakage in a catheter-dependent patient.** Assess drainage, storage contractions, infection, stones and possible structural injury. In women who catheterize, pelvic organ prolapse has also been described as a mechanical cause, presenting as leakage between catheterizations, a feeling of bladder fullness sooner after catheterization, or the need to catheterize more often; examine for a vaginal bulge.<sup>[[16]](#ref16)</sup>

### When LUTS suggest an undiagnosed neurological disease

Abrupt, severe or otherwise unexplained LUTS, especially with new visual symptoms, limb weakness or sensory change, gait or balance problems, falls, tremor or bradykinesia, cognitive change, altered perineal sensation, or new voiding dysregulation, can be an early feature of an undiagnosed neurological disease. An International Continence Society consensus working group recommends focused urological and neurological examination and early neurology referral when suspicion remains after common causes are excluded, rather than ordering central-nervous-system imaging directly from the LUTS clinic.<sup>[[17]](#ref17)</sup>

## Diagnosis and Evaluation (AUA/SUFU 2021)

### Initial workup for all NLUTD patients<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup>

- **Focused history.** Neurologic diagnosis, prognosis, cognition, mobility, dexterity (needed for CIC), caregiver support, prior urologic interventions.
- **Physical exam.** Sacral reflexes (bulbocavernosus, anal wink, cremasteric), perineal sensation, rectal tone, resting and voluntary sphincter tone.
- **Urinalysis.** Exclude infection, hematuria.
- **Post-void residual (PVR)** for any patient who spontaneously voids.
- **Optional:** bladder / catheterization diary, non-invasive uroflowmetry, pad test.

### Risk-stratified advanced workup

**Unknown-risk** patients get (statement 6, moderate recommendation, Grade C; low-risk patients do not, statement 5):

- **Upper-tract imaging.** Renal ultrasound (initial); CT or MR urography when indicated.
- **Serum creatinine / eGFR.** In nonweight-bearing patients with reduced muscle mass, a creatinine-based eGFR can overstate renal function: a cohort of 102 nonweight-bearing patients (mostly NLUTD) had a substantially higher creatinine-based eGFR than a cystatin-C-based eGFR (107 vs. 80 mL/min/1.73m², P&lt;.001), and 58% of those with imaging or urinalysis evidence of renal dysfunction were reclassified to a lower eGFR category by cystatin C. Consider cystatin C or another muscle-mass-independent marker when renal function is borderline or trending down.<sup>[[18]](#ref18)</sup>
- **Multichannel (video)urodynamics.** The central test. Reports:
  - **Cystometric capacity** and compliance, interpreted with the pressure trace and upper-tract findings
  - **Detrusor leak-point pressure (DLPP)** when measurable; distinguish it from maximum storage pressure
  - **Detrusor overactivity.** Amplitude, coordination with sphincter
  - **DSD.** Simultaneous sphincter contraction on detrusor activity
  - **Post-void residual** and voiding pressure–flow
  - **VUR** (on fluoroscopy during filling)
  - **Temporary urethral occlusion.** In severe neurogenic sphincteric leakage that otherwise limits filling before planned outlet or continence surgery, a penile clamp in men or a Foley-balloon technique in women has been described to permit adequate filling for assessment of capacity, compliance, reflux and detrusor overactivity; this needs specialist judgment and safety monitoring, not routine use.<sup>[[19]](#ref19)</sup>

### Additional studies (selected)

- **Renal scintigraphy (MAG3).** Split function, obstruction assessment.
- **Cystoscopy.** Not routine at initial evaluation (statement 8). AUA/SUFU recommends it for concomitant hematuria, recurrent UTI or a suspected anatomic anomaly such as stricture or false passage (statement 18, moderate recommendation, Grade B), and after bowel-segment reconstruction with gross hematuria or symptomatic recurrent UTI (statement 60).<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[2]](#ref2)</sup>
- **EMG of pelvic floor or sphincter.** When DSD suspected but ambiguous on urodynamics.

## NEUROGED 2025 — shared neurological and urological care

The EAN/EFAS/INUS guideline supports proactively asking about urinary and sexual symptoms, assessing a three-day bladder diary and PVR when relevant, and sharing care with urology for upper-tract risk, suspected structural disease or failed initial treatment. It adapts existing guidelines and combines evidence-based recommendations with consensus; it is not a comparative trial of procedures.<sup>[[11]](#ref11)</sup>

For incomplete emptying, its PVR >150 mL catheterization recommendation requires assessment of repeated measurements, risks, preferences and practical burden; this is not a universal safety threshold. Catheterization may be performed by a trained caregiver. PFMT requires preserved voluntary contraction, and neurogenic OAB symptom improvement with a β3 agonist does not prove pressure safety. These recommendations complement the AUA risk assessment above; patients with unknown risk still need that assessment.<sup>[[11]](#ref11)</sup>

## Management — Stepwise by Goal

For the operative options below, see the Treatment Atlas: [intradetrusor onabotulinumtoxinA](/docs/surgical-techniques/04f-incontinence-procedures/procedures/intradetrusor-botox), [sacral neuromodulation](/docs/surgical-techniques/04f-incontinence-procedures/procedures/sacral-neuromodulation), [augmentation cystoplasty](/docs/surgical-techniques/04b-bladder-reconstruction/bladder-augmentation), [continent catheterizable channels](/docs/surgical-techniques/04b-bladder-reconstruction/catheterizable-channels), [bladder-neck closure](/docs/surgical-techniques/04ab-bladder-neck-reconstruction/outlet-continence/bladder-neck-closure), and [urinary diversion](/docs/surgical-techniques/04c-urinary-diversion/ileal-conduit).

The reconstructive plan combines renal protection with patient goals, dexterity, cognition, caregiver support, and the burden of treatment:<sup>[[2]](#ref2)[[9]](#ref9)</sup>

1. **Protect the upper tracts.** Treat unsafe pressure, poor compliance, and ineffective drainage.
2. **Achieve social continence.** Dry intervals that match social and functional goals.
3. **Minimize complications.** UTI, stones, skin breakdown, catheter-related complications.
4. **Maximize quality of life and independence.** Integrate with caregiver resources, wheelchair transfers, and long-term durability.

### Emptying strategies

| Strategy | Use | Pros | Cons |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Clean intermittent catheterization (CIC)** | Preferred when effective emptying is otherwise impossible and the regimen is feasible | Avoids continuous urethral instrumentation; supports independence | Dexterity, caregiver access, leakage, infection, and treatment burden require counseling |
| **Spontaneous voiding with timed schedule** | Low-risk patients with intact coordination | Most natural | Risk of high pressures if overactive with DSD |
| **Indwelling urethral catheter** | When CIC is not feasible | Simple; caregiver-friendly | UTI / stone risk; urethral erosion in men, bladder-neck damage in women |
| **Suprapubic catheter (SPC)** | Long-term indwelling alternative; NEUROGED prefers it when a long-term catheter is being considered<sup>[[11]](#ref11)</sup> | Reduced urethral trauma; easier to manage than urethral | Still has UTI / stone risk; surgical insertion. EAU also advises avoiding both indwelling routes when possible and preferring silicone catheters (encrustation, latex allergy)<sup>[[9]](#ref9)</sup> |
| **Intraurethral valve-pump catheter (e.g., In-Flow)** | Selected women with hypocontractile or acontractile bladder unable or unwilling to sustain CIC | External activator opens the urethral valve to void, then closes it; completers had PVR comparable to CIC and improved incontinence-specific quality of life | High early discontinuation, chiefly from discomfort or leakage (169 of 273 women withdrew in the pivotal crossover study); confirm current device availability and regulatory status separately<sup>[[20]](#ref20)</sup> |
| **Credé / Valsalva voiding** | Discourage unless urodynamics confirms safe pressures | — | Raises intravesical pressure during attempted emptying and can increase outlet resistance.<sup>[[9]](#ref9)</sup> |
| **Triggered reflex voiding** | Historic, SCI with coordinated DO | — | Rarely used today — high reflux risk |

EAU 2026 strongly recommends shared decision-making and thorough training in catheterization. Typical practice is four to six catheterizations a day with 12–16 Fr catheters, keeping volumes below about 400–500 mL; hydrophilic catheters are associated with fewer UTIs, and UTI rates do not clearly differ between CIC and indwelling drainage. NEUROGED recommends offering CIC first-line when PVR is consistently above 150 mL. In one observational series, 42% of CIC users stopped within 12 months (inconvenience, leakage, infections). Do not promise that CIC eliminates infection or is uniformly better for quality of life; reassess the patient's ability to sustain the regimen.<sup>[[9]](#ref9)</sup>

### Pharmacologic therapy

For storage (DO or OAB):<sup>[[2]](#ref2)[[7]](#ref7)[[8]](#ref8)</sup>

| Class | Examples | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| **Antimuscarinics** | Oxybutynin, tolterodine, solifenacin, darifenacin, trospium | EAU's first-line choice for NDO (LE 1a); agents perform similarly. Intravesical oxybutynin is a strong recommendation when the oral route is poorly tolerated. Assess constipation, emptying and cognitive burden; consider switching to a β3 agonist or other therapy when cognition is affected. Parkinson disease alone is not a class contraindication.<sup>[[9]](#ref9)[[11]](#ref11)</sup> |
| **β3-adrenergic agonists** | Mirabegron, vibegron | May improve neurogenic OAB symptoms comparably to antimuscarinics, but EAU finds no significant effect on detrusor pressure or capacity in MS and SCI (short follow-up), so symptom response does not establish safe storage pressure. See the [β3-agonist hub](/docs/foundations/pharmacology/storage-oab/beta3-agonists).<sup>[[9]](#ref9)</sup> |
| **Combination** | Antimuscarinic + β3 | Evidence for additional benefit when monotherapy inadequate. |
| **Intradetrusor onabotulinumtoxinA** | 200 U for neurogenic DO (100 U for idiopathic OAB) | Adult NDO dosing is distinct from pediatric weight-based dosing; see the [procedure page](/docs/surgical-techniques/04f-incontinence-procedures/procedures/intradetrusor-botox#fda-approved-indications-and-dosing). AUA/SUFU strongly recommends it for SCI or MS patients refractory to oral therapy (statement 40, Grade A) and allows it for other neurologic causes (statement 41, conditional, Grade C), with discussion of retention risk and possible CIC before use in patients who void (statement 42, Clinical Principle); EAU strongly recommends it when antimuscarinics fail. In the phase 3 trial (416 patients with MS or SCI), 300 U gave no clinically relevant gain over 200 U, and among patients not catheterizing at baseline CIC was started in 35% at 200 U, 42% at 300 U and 10% on placebo; UTI and retention were the commonest adverse events.<sup>[[12]](#ref12)</sup> The guideline likewise found no efficacy difference between 200 U and 300 U but a dose-dependent rise in retention and need for CIC; in the pooled MS analysis it cites, CIC was started for retention in 31.4% at 200 U, 47.1% at 300 U and 4.5% on placebo.<sup>[[2]](#ref2)</sup> Effect lasts about nine months and repeat injections keep working; counsel about catheterization and urodynamic reassessment.<sup>[[2]](#ref2)[[5]](#ref5)[[9]](#ref9)</sup> |

For voiding or outlet:

- **α-blockers** may improve voiding parameters in selected patients who spontaneously void (AUA/SUFU conditional recommendation); EAU also reports lower outlet resistance, PVR and dysreflexia. Routine use in CIC-dependent patients is not the AUA recommendation.<sup>[[2]](#ref2)[[9]](#ref9)</sup>
- **External sphincterotomy** or **urethral-stent endoprostheses** are options for refractory DSD in men who can wear a condom catheter. Sphincterotomy (12 o'clock, cold knife or laser) is irreversible and often needs repeating; stents give comparable results with shorter surgery but cost, complications and reintervention limit their use. CIC plus antimuscarinics or botulinum toxin has displaced both in most patients.<sup>[[9]](#ref9)</sup>

### Non-pharmacologic

- **Pelvic-floor muscle training.** Modest benefit in MS, post-stroke, and mild dysfunction.
- **Behavioral measures.** Timed voiding, fluid management, constipation control (always check bowel program).

### Surgical / device therapy

| Intervention | Indication | Comments |
|---|---|---|
| **Sacral neuromodulation (SNM)** | Selected NLUTD with urgency, frequency, or UUI | AUA/SUFU advises **against offering SNM in SCI or spina bifida** (moderate recommendation, Grade C) and allows it for selected other patients with urgency, frequency or urgency incontinence. EAU 2026 is more permissive ("consider SNM in selected neurological patients", strong; MS patients often respond, effect on urodynamic pressures unclear) and notes MRI-conditional systems remove the old imaging barrier.<sup>[[2]](#ref2)[[9]](#ref9)</sup> |
| **Percutaneous tibial nerve stimulation (PTNS)** | Refractory OAB; MS with OAB phenotype | Office-based; less invasive than SNM. EAU rates the evidence as limited (small non-comparative or low-quality trials; home tibial stimulation improved diaries in MS).<sup>[[9]](#ref9)</sup> |
| **Bladder neck incision versus continence reconstruction** | Incision treats documented outlet obstruction; reconstruction treats outlet incompetence | Opposing goals: establish the mechanism and storage safety before choosing surgery. Reported incision patterns include a single 12 o'clock incision, incisions at 5 and 7 o'clock, or a four-quadrant pattern at 3, 6, 9 and 12 o'clock; no pattern is established as standard. Sympathetic fibers to the internal sphincter run near the 4 and 8 o'clock positions and are best avoided.<sup>[[21]](#ref21)</sup> |
| **Augmentation cystoplasty (ileocystoplasty)** | **Refractory poor compliance or high storage pressures** despite medical therapy; hostile bladder | AUA/SUFU: may be offered in selected patients. EAU: strong recommendation when compliance is low or NDO refractory. Long-term series report perforation 1.9%, mucus production 12.5%, metabolic abnormality 3.35%, bowel dysfunction 15% and stones 10%. Lifelong clinical, metabolic and imaging follow-up; investigate cancer warning symptoms rather than automatic screening cystoscopy.<sup>[[2]](#ref2)[[9]](#ref9)</sup> |
| **Continent catheterizable channel** (Mitrofanoff / Monti) | CIC required but urethra inaccessible (wheelchair, prior urethral reconstruction, obese, pediatric) | May be isolated or combined with augmentation according to storage needs, regardless of neurological diagnosis. In an EAU-cited review, 85 of 213 patients needed reoperation and stomal stenosis occurred in 4–32%.<sup>[[2]](#ref2)[[9]](#ref9)</sup> |
| **Bladder-neck closure** | Incompetent outlet with catheter leakage; radiation bladder-neck erosion | Irreversible outlet sacrifice — arrange reliable drainage through a CCC, suprapubic catheter or selected diversion.<sup>[[2]](#ref2)</sup> |
| **Urinary diversion (ileal conduit or continent)** | Refractory disease where reconstruction is not feasible (radiation, extensive fibrosis, severe dementia, prior failed reconstructions) | Exit pathway; lifelong surveillance. |
| **Continence surgery for neurogenic SUI (sling, AUS)** | Sphincter incompetence once storage pressure is controlled | EAU: autologous sling first-line in women able to self-catheterize (strong); AUS for men (strong) and selected women (weak, experienced centers). Neurogenic AUS complication and reoperation rates run up to 60%, and only 7.1% were revision-free at 20 years in one long series.<sup>[[9]](#ref9)</sup> |

### Autonomic dysreflexia prevention

In SCI at or above T6, any urologic manipulation (cystoscopy, catheterization, urodynamics) risks autonomic dysreflexia. Prophylaxis: topical lidocaine, gentle technique, BP monitoring, ready access to short-acting antihypertensives. See [Autonomic Dysreflexia](/docs/clinical-conditions/03d-nlutd/autonomic-dysreflexia).

## Complications and Surveillance

### Upper tract

- **Hydronephrosis.** Silent until advanced; detected on surveillance imaging.
- **VUR.** From high storage pressure, may require reimplantation in children; in adults, usually managed by pressure reduction.
- **Renal scarring and CKD.** The historical leading cause of mortality in SCI before modern NLUTD care.
- **Urolithiasis.** Bladder and upper-tract stones from stasis, infection, and prolonged catheter colonization.

### Lower tract

- **Recurrent UTI.** No single numerical definition is validated across all NLUTD populations; document symptomatic, culture-supported episodes. Asymptomatic bacteriuria (present in 23–89% of clean-CIC users) should not be screened for or treated (AUA/SUFU statements 23 and 24, moderate recommendations, Grade C; EAU, strong). NLUTD infections are complicated by definition; use culture-directed therapy for about 5–7 days, up to 14 for upper-tract or prostatic involvement.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[9]](#ref9)</sup>
- **Prevention.** First fix the cause: treat NDO, empty effectively, remove stones and indwelling catheters. Then consider hydrophilic catheters and, in selected SCI patients on CIC with recurrent UTI, weekly cycling of antibiotic prophylaxis; drinking-water bladder irrigation may cut catheter-associated UTI (weak). EAU does not support cranberry, probiotics or methenamine in this population, and advises against continuous low-dose antibiotics. AUA/SUFU also recommends against daily antibiotic prophylaxis in indwelling-catheter patients (statement 30, strong, Grade B) and in CIC users without recurrent UTI (statement 31, moderate, Grade B), and finds cranberry extract not shown to reduce UTI (statement 39). Do not use dipstick screening.<sup>[[2]](#ref2)[[9]](#ref9)</sup> Bacterial interference — deliberate bladder colonization with a nonpathogenic *E. coli* strain — has been studied as an investigational alternative: in a multicenter randomized trial of 65 SCI patients with recurrent UTI (3:1 randomization to colonization vs. saline; 27 of 59 inoculated patients remained evaluable), UTI episodes per patient-year were fewer among colonized patients than controls (0.50 vs. 1.68). This is not a standard prevention measure.<sup>[[22]](#ref22)</sup>
- **Urethral trauma or stricture.** Especially from long-term indwelling urethral catheter in men.
- **Bladder cancer risk.** Investigate hematuria or other concerning changes. AUA/SUFU strongly recommends against routine screening or surveillance cystoscopy, including in patients with chronic indwelling catheters (Grade B).<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup> EAU 2026 likewise gives no validated cystoscopy interval and asks for prompt investigation of standard red flags.<sup>[[9]](#ref9)</sup>

### Systemic

- **Autonomic dysreflexia** (SCI ≥T6).
- **Sexual dysfunction and infertility.** Major quality-of-life issue in SCI and MS; addressed with PDE5i, electroejaculation, IVF or ICSI as indicated.
- **Social and functional burden.** Skin, caregiver, employment, transfer-equipment impact.

### Surveillance cadence (2021 AUA/SUFU)<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[2]](#ref2)</sup>

| Risk tier | History / exam / symptoms | Upper-tract imaging | Renal function | Urodynamics |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Low-risk, stable** | Review clinical changes | Not routine | Not routine | Not routine |
| **Unknown-risk** | Complete initial assessment | Baseline | Baseline | Baseline, then assign risk |
| **Moderate-risk, stable** | Annual | Every 1–2 years | Annual | Clinical change |
| **High-risk, stable** | Annual | Annual | Annual | Repeat as clinically indicated |

EAU 2026 supports individualized follow-up and discusses approximately six-monthly ultrasound for high-risk patients. Escalate sooner for new symptoms, infection, hydronephrosis, or renal decline; the AUA annual schedule is not a reason to defer investigation.<sup>[[9]](#ref9)</sup> Do not obtain screening urine cultures in asymptomatic NLUTD patients.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup>

**Triggers for urologic assessment / escalation:**<sup>[[4]](#ref4)</sup>
- Recurrent symptomatic UTIs or a serious or febrile episode requiring assessment; do not wait for three events
- New or worsening hydronephrosis
- Renal function decline
- Stones
- Gross hematuria
- Urethral erosion / trauma
- Ineffective bladder management regimen

## Population-Specific Considerations (at a Glance)

| Population | Dominant pattern | First-line management | Reconstructive peaks |
|---|---|---|---|
| **SCI — suprasacral** | DO + DSD, high pressure | CIC + antimuscarinic ± botulinum | Augmentation, Mitrofanoff, bladder-neck reconstruction |
| **SCI — conus / cauda equina** | Areflexia, areflexic sphincter | CIC; bladder-neck support or AUS | Rare augmentation; AUS for ISD |
| **Multiple sclerosis** | OAB, incomplete emptying; evolves with progression | Antimuscarinic / β3, CIC if retention; botulinum when refractory | Rare augmentation; SNM when OAB-predominant |
| **Parkinson disease** | OAB with preserved coordination | β3 preferred when cognition is a concern; antimuscarinic with cognitive monitoring; botulinum | Rare reconstruction |
| **Spina bifida** | Variable by level; ~85% have NLUTD at birth | CIC from infancy, antimuscarinic, bowel program | Augmentation + Mitrofanoff + BN procedure most commonly |
| **Stroke / dementia** | OAB ± functional incontinence | Caregiver-directed toileting, β3, incontinence pads | Rarely operative — focus on caregiver burden |
| **Adult cerebral palsy** | Variable — DO, DSD, or a large flaccid bladder; neurologic deficits may not otherwise be prominent | Full urologic evaluation of any symptomatic adult; treat per the urodynamic pattern found | Rare — individualized |

Adults with cerebral palsy and voiding dysfunction can have important storage or emptying abnormalities that are easy to miss without a full workup. In a urodynamic cohort of 49 symptomatic adults with moderate-to-severe CP, detrusor overactivity was found in 30%, detrusor-sphincter dyssynergia in 12%, and DLPP above 40 cmH₂O in 51% (median compliance 18 mL/cmH₂O, range 0.78–365).<sup>[[24]](#ref24)</sup> A systematic review across children and adults with CP links upper-tract deterioration particularly to the combination of urinary symptoms and pelvic-floor spasticity.<sup>[[25]](#ref25)</sup> Keep these figures tied to this selected cohort; do not treat 40 cmH₂O as a universal adult threshold — see the DLPP discussion above.

See population-specific articles (linked above) for full detail.

## Clinical Correlations for the Reconstructive Urologist

- **Hostile bladder in SCI or spina bifida: augmentation cystoplasty.** Objective workup before: urodynamics documenting poor compliance or sustained high pressures, upper-tract imaging and failure or intolerance of appropriate less-invasive therapy. Established hydronephrosis is not required before treating refractory unsafe storage. Plan lifelong emptying support, stone surveillance, metabolic testing and prompt investigation of hematuria, unexplained recurrent UTI or new pain; asymptomatic screening cystoscopy is not routine.<sup>[[2]](#ref2)</sup>
- **Mitrofanoff or Monti channel.** Not a consolation prize. For wheelchair-bound patients, obese patients, post-radical-pelvic-surgery patients, and many spina bifida patients, a catheterizable umbilical channel is the difference between CIC independence and caregiver dependence.
- **Bladder-neck closure** is irreversible. Confirm safe storage and reliable alternative drainage; selected patients use a catheterizable channel, permanent suprapubic catheter or diversion. An existing functioning CCC is not a universal prerequisite.<sup>[[2]](#ref2)</sup>
- **Urinary diversion as salvage.** In patients with failed reconstructions, severe cognitive impairment, refractory radiation injury, or where independence is no longer the goal, an ileal conduit can dramatically simplify care.
- **Pregnancy in NLUTD.** Requires a multidisciplinary plan: catheter mechanics change with the gravid uterus, UTI risk rises, surveillance tightens, and mode of delivery is coordinated with obstetrics given augmentation or Mitrofanoff anatomy.
- **Neuro-urologic care is longitudinal.** Unlike episodic urologic conditions, NLUTD patients need lifelong surveillance; the commonest error is loss to follow-up. Primary-care referral triggers should be explicit (see above); a named neuro-urology program improves outcomes.
