# Spinal Cord Injury

Spinal cord injury (SCI) is the archetypal reconstructive neuro-urology population. Nearly 100% of patients with SCI above the conus develop NLUTD, and without structured urologic surveillance CKD or ESRD was historically the leading cause of mortality. The level of injury, completeness, and interval since injury all drive the NLUTD phenotype. Sean Elliott and others have emphasized that the urologist's role in SCI is longitudinal, from the acute spinal-shock catheter drainage through decades of risk-stratified surveillance and, when necessary, reconstructive surgery.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[2]](#ref2)[[3]](#ref3)</sup>

This article focuses on SCI-specific points. For general NLUTD principles see [Neurogenic Lower Urinary Tract Dysfunction](/docs/clinical-conditions/03d-nlutd/neurogenic-bladder); for a life-threatening SCI-specific emergency see [Autonomic Dysreflexia](/docs/clinical-conditions/03d-nlutd/autonomic-dysreflexia). For the reconstructive procedures referenced throughout, see [Intradetrusor OnabotulinumtoxinA](/docs/surgical-techniques/04f-incontinence-procedures/procedures/intradetrusor-botox), [Bladder Augmentation](/docs/surgical-techniques/04b-bladder-reconstruction/bladder-augmentation), [Catheterizable Channels](/docs/surgical-techniques/04b-bladder-reconstruction/catheterizable-channels), and the [Ileal Conduit](/docs/surgical-techniques/04c-urinary-diversion/ileal-conduit) salvage pathway.

## Epidemiology

- **US prevalence** about 250,000–300,000; incidence about 17,000/year.
- Leading etiologies: motor-vehicle trauma, falls, violence, sports, non-traumatic (transverse myelitis, tumor, ischemia).
- Average age at injury is rising (currently about 43 years); aging of the SCI population brings coexisting BPH, prostate cancer, and obesity into reconstructive planning.
- Life expectancy after traumatic SCI remains shorter than in the general population, with higher or complete injuries associated with poorer survival. Urologic complications and pressure injuries still warrant prevention and longitudinal care.<sup>[[25]](#ref25)[[1]](#ref1)</sup>

## Pathophysiology by Level

| Injury level | Phase after injury | Dominant urodynamic phenotype |
|---|---|---|
| **Any level** | **Acute / spinal-shock (days–~6 weeks, range up to months)** | Retention is typical, but the detrusor is acontractile in only about 37% within the first 40 days; DO, DSD or high pressures appear early in many<sup>[[7]](#ref7)</sup> |
| **Above T6 (high cervical / upper thoracic)** | After spinal shock | **Detrusor overactivity + DSD** + risk of **autonomic dysreflexia** |
| **T6–T12 / suprasacral** | After spinal shock | DO + DSD; lower AD risk |
| **Conus (T12–L1 region)** | Stable from onset | Variable — can resemble suprasacral DSD or sacral areflexia |
| **Cauda equina / sacral / infrasacral** | Stable from onset | **Detrusor areflexia + denervated EUS**; retention, overflow, stress incontinence |

In a urodynamic series of traumatic SCI, 81.5% of suprasacral injuries showed detrusor hyperreflexia with or without DSD, 9.2% areflexia and 9.2% normal bladders; of five sacral injuries, four had areflexia. Low compliance and detrusor leak-point pressure above 40 cmH<sub>2</sub>O were common in both groups, and neurologic level predicted the urodynamic pattern imperfectly, so clinical level should not replace urodynamics.<sup>[[8]](#ref8)</sup>

### Spinal shock and early bladder management

- Place an indwelling catheter for the acute phase to keep the bladder decompressed and monitor fluids; do not leave it in place by default once the patient is stable.
- Transition to CIC as soon as fluid intake is controlled and the patient is stable. Voluntary anal contraction plus post-void residuals is a practical marker of recovering function before a trial of void.<sup>[[11]](#ref11)</sup>
- Because dangerous pressures can appear during the "areflexic" phase, obtain urodynamics within weeks to months rather than waiting for spinal shock to end.<sup>[[7]](#ref7)</sup>

## Initial Evaluation and Surveillance Cadence

Per AUA/SUFU 2021 risk-stratified framework.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[2]](#ref2)</sup> SCI generally requires assessment as unknown-risk, followed by classification using renal function, imaging, and urodynamics. Reassess when neurological function stabilizes, with earlier investigation for concerning changes. See the [NLUTD risk and surveillance framework](/docs/clinical-conditions/03d-nlutd/neurogenic-bladder#classification).

**Baseline workup (timed to clinical stability and risk):**
- History and exam (including AD triggers, skin, bowel program)
- Urinalysis, serum Cr / eGFR
- Renal ultrasound
- **Video-urodynamics.** Documents DO, DSD, DLPP, compliance, VUR
- PVR and bladder diary

**Longitudinal surveillance (high-risk):**
- Annual clinical review; obtain urine testing for symptoms rather than routine infection screening
- Renal ultrasound annually for high-risk disease; the 1–2-year interval belongs to stable moderate-risk disease
- Assess renal function annually; in SCI with reduced muscle mass, creatinine-based eGFR can overestimate measured GFR, so consider cystatin C-based eGFR when available.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[26]](#ref26)</sup>
- Urodynamics when clinical change (new symptoms, hydronephrosis, declining function, new AD pattern)
- Cystoscopy for an indication such as hematuria, recurrent UTI, or suspected structural pathology; not annual screening solely because a catheter is present

AUA/SUFU (statements 13–15) sets the intervals: low-risk stable patients need no surveillance imaging or urodynamics; moderate-risk patients need annual history/exam and renal function with upper-tract imaging every 1–2 years; high-risk patients need annual history, exam, renal function and upper-tract imaging, with urodynamics repeated when clinically indicated. EAU 2026 is more intensive, suggesting ultrasound about every six months in high-risk patients and a urodynamic baseline repeated during follow-up.<sup>[[2]](#ref2)[[5]](#ref5)</sup>

Long-term outcome data support continuing this surveillance for life rather than treating a stable patient as low risk indefinitely. In a retrospective cohort of 43 SCI patients followed for a median of 45 years (range 40–50), recurrent UTI occurred in all patients, and vesicoureteral reflux (33%), bladder stones (49%), and hydronephrosis (47%) continued to appear throughout follow-up; most complications first occurred in the first 25 years after injury, but new complications still arose later, and male sex, cervical injury, and condom-catheter use tracked with a higher complication burden.<sup>[[17]](#ref17)</sup>

## Management

### Emptying strategies<sup>[[2]](#ref2)[[3]](#ref3)[[4]](#ref4)</sup>

The numbered approaches below are not a universal preference order. Select according to storage safety, hand function, caregiver support, anatomy, and patient goals; when catheter drainage is indicated, AUA/SUFU 2021 recommends CIC over indwelling catheters and suprapubic over urethral drainage for appropriately selected patients requiring a chronic catheter (both Strong Recommendations, Grade C).<sup>[[2]](#ref2)</sup>

1. **Clean intermittent catheterization (CIC).** AUA/SUFU recommends it over indwelling catheters for bladder emptying (statement 35, strong recommendation, Grade C). Typical practice is 4–6×/day; hydrophilic catheters are associated with fewer UTIs, and bladder volumes should stay below about 400–500 mL. Among SCI registry respondents now using an indwelling catheter or urinary conduit who had stopped CIC, about 42% reported stopping within 12 months of starting it; review dexterity, leakage and infection burden early.<sup>[[5]](#ref5)</sup> In a retrospective cohort of 308 SCI patients evaluated a mean 18.7 years after injury, upper-tract abnormalities occurred in 56 chronic Foley users (18.2% of the entire cohort), 20 CIC users (6.5%), and 24 spontaneous voiders (7.8%); these are not within-group risks. Foley use independently predicted low creatinine clearance and was associated with proteinuria, whereas vesicoureteral reflux independently predicted upper-tract imaging abnormalities.<sup>[[14]](#ref14)</sup> A Canadian cost-effectiveness model likewise found CIC the dominant strategy (more QALYs at lower cost) versus suprapubic or indwelling urethral catheters from a public-payer perspective.<sup>[[14]](#ref14)[[15]](#ref15)</sup>
2. **Indwelling SPC.** When dexterity or caregiver support or body habitus preclude CIC. AUA/SUFU recommends it over an indwelling urethral catheter in appropriately selected patients who need a chronic indwelling catheter (statement 36, strong recommendation, Grade C).<sup>[[2]](#ref2)</sup>
3. **Indwelling urethral catheter.** Last-line; high urethral-erosion risk in men, bladder-neck and sphincter damage in women.
4. **Reflex voiding with external condom drainage (men).** Historically common; requires confirmed safe detrusor pressures and low outlet resistance, often achieved by sphincterotomy or botulinum toxin for DSD. Triggering can provoke autonomic dysreflexia at or above T6. Not practical in women, who have no condom-drainage equivalent.<sup>[[5]](#ref5)</sup>
5. **Credé or Valsalva.** Discourage because they raise intravesical pressure during attempted emptying and may provoke sphincter contraction; use only when urodynamics show safe voiding pressure and effective emptying. A sacral lesion alone does not establish safety.<sup>[[5]](#ref5)</sup>

In the US National Spinal Cord Injury Database (24,762 patients at discharge from rehabilitation, 1972 to 2005), CIC at discharge rose from 12.6% in 1972 to a peak of 56.2% in 1991. Among 12,984 patients with follow-up data, only 20% of those discharged on CIC remained on it, whereas 71.1% of those discharged with an indwelling catheter still used one at 30 years.<sup>[[23]](#ref23)</sup> In a 1,479-person observational cohort from the United States and Canada, 51% used CIC, 18% an indwelling catheter, 18% voiding and 13% had had bladder surgery. Compared with CIC, an indwelling catheter or prior surgery was associated with better patient-reported bladder function and voiding with worse function, in both paraplegia and tetraplegia; the design shows associations only, and people with worse bladder problems may have been more likely to enroll.<sup>[[24]](#ref24)</sup>

### Pharmacologic

- **Antimuscarinics** are first-line for neurogenic detrusor overactivity. AUA/SUFU 2021 conditionally permits a β3 agonist alone or with an antimuscarinic for NLUTD storage parameters (Grade C), and combination therapy is an option when monotherapy inadequately controls symptoms. EAU 2026 strongly advises against using β3 agonists to reduce urodynamically proven NDO; symptom improvement alone does not establish safe storage pressure.<sup>[[2]](#ref2)[[5]](#ref5)</sup> See [Neurogenic Lower Urinary Tract Dysfunction](/docs/clinical-conditions/03d-nlutd/neurogenic-bladder) for the agent table.
- **Intradetrusor onabotulinumtoxinA 200 U** (the labeled neurogenic dose) is highly effective for neurogenic DO in SCI; repeat about every 8–9 months (median time to re-treatment 256 days); patients who void spontaneously must be counseled about the risk of retention and possible need for CIC before treatment (AUA/SUFU statement 42, Clinical Principle). AUA/SUFU found no efficacy difference between 200 U and 300 U, with a dose-dependent rise in retention and CIC. In the phase 3 trial (MS and SCI), patients not catheterizing at baseline started CIC for retention in 35% at 200 U and 42% at 300 U versus 10% with placebo, and 300 U added no efficacy or duration benefit.<sup>[[4]](#ref4)[[6]](#ref6)</sup>
- **α-blockers** may improve voiding parameters in selected spontaneous voiders; routine use in CIC-dependent patients is not the AUA recommendation.<sup>[[2]](#ref2)</sup>

### Surgical

- **Augmentation cystoplasty.** Hostile bladder refractory to medical therapy and botulinum. Typically ileum; ileocecal in selected cases.
- **Mitrofanoff or Monti catheterizable channel.** Wheelchair-dependent patients, obese patients with urethral inaccessibility, patients with prior urethral reconstruction.
- **Bladder-neck closure and continent catheterizable channel.** In selected patients with refractory stress incontinence, closure is irreversible and requires reliable CIC through an existing or planned channel. When outlet closure could worsen compliance, assess storage pressure and compliance by urodynamics before surgery to protect the upper tracts (AUA/SUFU 2021 statement 47, Expert Opinion).<sup>[[2]](#ref2)</sup>
- **Ileal conduit or continent diversion.** Salvage when reconstruction has failed or is not feasible.
- **Sacral neuromodulation.** Guidelines differ. AUA/SUFU 2021 (statement 50, moderate recommendation, Grade C) says not to offer SNM to patients with SCI or spina bifida. EAU 2026 says to consider SNM in selected neurological patients, noting MRI-compatible devices now remove the imaging objection, while acknowledging that disease-specific evidence is sparse and that responders are mainly reported in MS. In SCI, treat SNM as an exception requiring explicit counseling.<sup>[[2]](#ref2)[[5]](#ref5)</sup>
- **Sacral anterior root stimulation (Brindley) with posterior rhizotomy.** For selected patients with complete suprasacral lesions; stimulation is above the pain threshold and post-stimulus voiding results from faster sphincter relaxation. Rhizotomy abolishes reflex DO but also reflexes, sensation and erections, so it is now used mainly as an adjunct to SARS.<sup>[[5]](#ref5)</sup> In an uncontrolled retrospective series of 104 patients (95 men, 9 women) undergoing extradural implantation and rhizotomy, urinary incontinence was reported in 100% before surgery versus 14% afterward, and urinary infection in 95/104 (91%) before versus 16/104 (15%) afterward.<sup>[[19]](#ref19)</sup> Published Brindley-procedure series overlap across single- and multicenter reports, so this cohort's results should not be pooled with other published series into a single rate.<sup>[[19]](#ref19)</sup> Patient selection is restricted to a stable, complete spinal cord injury with intact sacral efferent pathways and a detrusor that contracts on stimulation; sacral anterior-root stimulation can also support defecation and erections through the same stimulated roots.<sup>[[20]](#ref20)</sup> The rhizotomy is irreversible and sacrifices sacral sensation and reflexes, which is why it is reserved for complete lesions; incomplete deafferentation of an incomplete lesion has been described as a source of pain rather than relief.<sup>[[21]](#ref21)</sup>
- **External sphincterotomy.** Largely replaced by CIC plus medical therapy, but still effective in men who can wear a condom catheter for DSD, hydronephrosis, AD or recurrent UTI. It is irreversible, is best done at 12 o'clock with cold knife or laser, and often needs repeating.<sup>[[5]](#ref5)</sup>

### SCI-specific perioperative caveats

- **Autonomic dysreflexia.** For SCI at or above T6, assess AD risk before cystoscopy, urodynamics, and surgery, and monitor BP during instrumentation. Have a rapid-onset, short-acting antihypertensive (such as nitroglycerin paste or nifedipine) available. Urethral lidocaine can be considered before cystoscopy but may alter diagnostic urodynamics; do not give prophylactic antihypertensives before urodynamics. If AD develops during testing, stop the procedure, drain the bladder, and continue BP monitoring. Check recent PDE5 inhibitor use before giving a nitrate. See [Autonomic Dysreflexia](/docs/clinical-conditions/03d-nlutd/autonomic-dysreflexia).<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[27]](#ref27)</sup>
- **Positioning and pressure injury.** Lithotomy or prone positioning with insensate skin; meticulous padding, frequent repositioning, and postop skin checks.
- **Spasticity.** Baclofen pumps and botulinum-for-limb-spasticity regimens may interact with bladder care timing.
- **Heterotopic ossification.** At hips; affects access and positioning.

## Specific Scenarios

### Indwelling catheter exit — transitioning to CIC

Assess whether CIC is feasible with the patient's dexterity, transfers, caregiver support, and goals. If a chronic indwelling catheter is required, AUA/SUFU favors suprapubic over urethral drainage to limit urethral injury. Neither approach removes infection or stone risk.<sup>[[2]](#ref2)</sup>

### UTI prevention

- Do not routinely screen for or treat asymptomatic bacteriuria in NLUTD (AUA/SUFU 2021 statements 23–24, Moderate Recommendations, Grade C; EAU 2026, Strong). Treatment exceptions are pregnancy and urologic procedures with anticipated urothelial disruption or upper-tract manipulation. With suspected UTI, obtain urinalysis and urine culture (AUA/SUFU statement 25, Moderate Recommendation, Grade C); optimize emptying, remove stones and other foreign bodies, and use hydrophilic catheters first.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[5]](#ref5)</sup>
- Cranberry and probiotics have not shown benefit in RCTs, and methenamine hippurate is not effective in neurological patients (EAU 2026).
- For select SCI patients on CIC with recurrent UTI, EAU 2026 offers weekly cycling of antibiotic prophylaxis; avoid continuous long-term antibiotics because of resistance.<sup>[[5]](#ref5)</sup>

### Bladder cancer surveillance

- Long-term indwelling catheterization is associated with bladder cancer, with SCC overrepresented. A meta-analysis found an incidence of about 6 per 1,000 SCI patients, squamous histology in roughly 37%, and diagnosis a mean of 24 years after injury.<sup>[[9]](#ref9)</sup> A systematic review of 15 neuro-urological studies identified 332 bladder cancers (0.3% of patients); 98.8% of cancer cases had SCI. Transitional cell carcinoma was the most frequent histology (53.1%), and cancer was diagnosed a mean of 24.9 years after onset of neurologic disease.<sup>[[16]](#ref16)</sup>
- AUA/SUFU recommends against routine screening or surveillance cystoscopy, including in chronic indwelling-catheter users (statements 19 and 20, strong recommendations, Grade B).<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup>
- **Any gross hematuria, persistent irritative symptoms, or pelvic pain** warrants cystoscopy and clinical evaluation rather than waiting for a scheduled visit.

### Fertility and sexual health

- **Erection physiology by injury level.** Reflexogenic erection requires an intact sacral reflex arc (S2-S4) and pudendal nerve; psychogenic erection requires cortical input reaching an intact sympathetic erection center (T11-L2). An injury at S2 or below spares psychogenic erections but abolishes reflexogenic ones; an injury at T10 or above spares reflexogenic erections but blocks cortical input to T11-L2, so most men with a complete T11-L2 injury lose psychogenic erections as well. An injury between L2 and S2 gives a mixed, individually variable picture, sometimes retaining both mechanisms; a psychogenic erection in this setting tends to be engorged rather than rigid and short-lived, and is harder to treat. Acutely, spinal shock (flaccid paralysis and depressed reflexes, hours to weeks, occasionally with vasodilation-driven tumescence in a complete injury) precedes the return of spasticity and reflexes and then the chronic pattern set by level and completeness.<sup>[[12]](#ref12)[[13]](#ref13)</sup>
- **Erectile dysfunction treatment.** PDE5i first-line (sildenafil improved erections and intercourse success, 53% versus 12%, including in complete SCI); intracavernosal injection or penile prosthesis in refractory cases. Many SCI patients keep nitrates for autonomic dysreflexia, and PDE5 inhibitors are contraindicated with nitrates, so agree an alternative rescue agent first (see [Autonomic Dysreflexia](/docs/clinical-conditions/03d-nlutd/autonomic-dysreflexia)).<sup>[[5]](#ref5)[[10]](#ref10)</sup>
- **Ejaculatory dysfunction.** Penile vibratory stimulation (PVS) first-line; electroejaculation (EEJ) if PVS fails; retrieved sperm used with IUI or IVF or ICSI; testicular sperm extraction if both fail. Clean IC may preserve semen quality better than indwelling catheters or reflex voiding.<sup>[[5]](#ref5)</sup> After a dry climax, sperm in post-ejaculatory bladder urine can confirm retrograde ejaculation; neurologic and surgical history helps distinguish it from failure of emission (AUA/SMSNA Statement 19 discussion). Absence of sperm alone does not establish failure of emission.<sup>[[18]](#ref18)[[22]](#ref22)</sup>
- **Female SCI.** Fertility preserved; pregnancy-specific bladder management plan required; delivery planning with OB/GYN.

### Aging SCI population

- **BPH.** Increasingly common; treating in CIC patients requires balancing outlet resistance (needed for dry intervals between CIC) versus ease of catheter passage.
- **Prostate cancer.** Screening with PSA in SCI follows general guidelines; transrectal biopsy requires AD prophylaxis.
- **Falls, cognitive decline, caregiver changes.** Trigger reassessment of feasible bladder management.

## Clinical Correlations for the Reconstructive Urologist

- **Begin safe drainage and arrange reassessment early.** Time video-urodynamics to clinical stability and risk; do not use a fixed post-injury waiting period to defer investigation of deterioration.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup>
- **Hostile bladder in SCI is reversible if caught early.** Escalate treatment according to measured pressure and compliance; β3-related symptom improvement alone does not prove safe storage. Do not wait for eGFR decline.
- **Care is multidisciplinary.** SCI medicine physiatrist, urologist, neuro-urology nurse / educator, physical therapy, and social work are the standard team.
- **Transition from pediatric SCI or spina bifida.** Aging-out patients need deliberate handoff to adult reconstructive urology; otherwise they fall out of surveillance and present years later with advanced disease.
