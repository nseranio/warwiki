# Spina Bifida

**Spina bifida** includes open and closed spinal dysraphism; **myelomeningocele** is the open defect discussed predominantly here.<sup>[[5]](#ref5)</sup><sup>[[11]](#ref11)</sup> Virtually every major reconstructive technique in use today, including clean intermittent catheterization as a care standard, augmentation cystoplasty, the Mitrofanoff principle, bladder-neck reconstruction, MACE for bowel management, and multidisciplinary transitional care, was refined in this population. Most children with myelomeningocele have NLUTD (the EAU Neuro-Urology guideline cites bladder impairment in up to 96% of registry patients),<sup>[[5]](#ref5)</sup> and the lesion level only imperfectly predicts the urodynamic phenotype, so every infant with spina bifida needs urodynamic evaluation.<sup>[[2]](#ref2)</sup> The reconstructive urologist inherits these patients in one of two ways: in a lifelong pediatric-to-adult continuum at a spina-bifida center, or as transitioned young adults presenting with hostile bladders, failed prior reconstructions, fertility or pregnancy concerns, and pressure-injury or stone complications.

See [Neurogenic Lower Urinary Tract Dysfunction](/docs/clinical-conditions/03d-nlutd/neurogenic-bladder) for the general framework. The reconstructive ladder discussed below is detailed in the Treatment Atlas: [Intradetrusor OnabotulinumtoxinA](/docs/surgical-techniques/04f-incontinence-procedures/procedures/intradetrusor-botox), [Bladder Augmentation](/docs/surgical-techniques/04b-bladder-reconstruction/bladder-augmentation), [Catheterizable Channels](/docs/surgical-techniques/04b-bladder-reconstruction/catheterizable-channels) (Mitrofanoff / Monti), and the [Artificial Urinary Sphincter](/docs/surgical-techniques/04f-incontinence-procedures/procedures/artificial-urinary-sphincter) for outlet incompetence.

## Epidemiology

- **Birth prevalence** is about 3–4 per 10,000 in the EAU epidemiology summary, and lumbar and lumbosacral lesions are the most common (about 60%).<sup>[[5]](#ref5)</sup> Periconceptional folate supplementation lowers the risk of neural tube defects.
- **Bladder function is impaired in most patients** (up to about 96% in registry data), and more than half report incontinence; open and closed defects can have equally severe NLUTD.<sup>[[5]](#ref5)</sup>
- **Renal risk persists for life:** in a 1,029-patient myelomeningocele cohort followed by urology (median 17 years), end-stage kidney disease risk was 0.3% at 20 years and 2.1% at 30 years, about 100 times the general population. Patients who needed early surgery for a hostile bladder had higher risk (6% versus 1.5% at 30 years).<sup>[[6]](#ref6)</sup>
- **Fetal MMC closure** (MOMS trial) reduced shunt placement by 12 months (40% versus 82%) and improved motor outcomes.<sup>[[11]](#ref11)</sup> At school age, fewer prenatal-repair children needed CIC (62% versus 87%) and more voided volitionally (24% versus 4%), but most still had bladder dysfunction and augmentation, vesicostomy and urethral dilation rates did not differ, so urodynamic follow-up remains mandatory.<sup>[[12]](#ref12)</sup>
- Associated anomalies: Chiari II malformation, hydrocephalus (most patients; 82% of the postnatal-repair arm in MOMS were shunted by 12 months)<sup>[[11]](#ref11)</sup>, tethered spinal cord, neurogenic bowel, orthopedic deformities, sexual dysfunction, fertility issues.

## Pathophysiology

Unlike traumatic SCI, spina bifida NLUTD reflects a developmental, non-uniform cord lesion. The lesion level on imaging correlates only loosely with urodynamic phenotype; every infant with MMC requires urodynamic testing.<sup>[[2]](#ref2)</sup>

Typical urodynamic patterns:

| Pattern | Frequency | Implications |
|---|---|---|
| **DO + DSD** | Common | High-pressure storage → hostile bladder; requires aggressive early pressure reduction |
| **DO + incompetent sphincter** | Common | Leakage does not establish safe pressures or protect against all upper-tract risk |
| **Areflexic detrusor + incompetent sphincter** | Frequent | Retention + stress leakage; overflow pattern |
| **Areflexic + coordinated sphincter** | Less common | Retention dominant |

Tethered cord can change urodynamics over time; new or worsening urinary or bowel symptoms in a growing spina-bifida child warrant re-imaging and neurosurgical consultation.

## Initial Evaluation

Standard at modern spina-bifida centers:

Age-specific surveillance: SBA recommends renal and bladder ultrasound, urodynamics and serum creatinine within the first three months. In stable children, ultrasound is generally every six months until age two, then yearly; urodynamics is generally yearly through age three and thereafter for clinical indications or continence planning. Recurrent symptomatic UTI, hydronephrosis or suspected bladder hostility prompts earlier investigation. In adolescence and adulthood, maintain annual renal and bladder surveillance and individualize urodynamics. Low muscle mass can make creatinine misleading.<sup>[[3]](#ref3)</sup>

## Management — The Spina-Bifida Reconstructive Ladder

### Early childhood (CIC-based)

- **CIC** is initiated in infancy when bladder hostility or inadequate emptying warrants it, within a program of urodynamic surveillance and pressure management.<sup>[[3]](#ref3)</sup> Parents and then patients are taught the skill. In a Swedish national-program cohort of 41 children with neurogenic bladder-sphincter dysfunction followed a median of 11 years (most managed from birth), no child required renal-protective surgery; one child underwent surgery for incontinence and six received botulinum-toxin injection.<sup>[[29]](#ref29)</sup>
- **Antimuscarinic** (oxybutynin) added at low doses in infancy for DO or poor compliance; use in infants is off-label. Labeled pediatric use begins at age 5 (immediate-release syrup or tablet, usual 5 mg twice daily, maximum 5 mg three times daily) or age 6 (extended-release, start 5 mg daily, maximum 20 mg daily) for detrusor overactivity with a neurologic condition such as spina bifida.<sup>[[8]](#ref8)</sup> Watch for constipation, which compounds neurogenic bowel, and for reduced sweating and heat intolerance.
- **Mirabegron** is FDA-approved for pediatric NDO from age 3 (granules for children under 35 kg; ER tablets at 35 kg or more); phase 3 data show increased cystometric capacity and reduced detrusor pressure.<sup>[[9]](#ref9)</sup><sup>[[10]](#ref10)</sup> Vibegron has no pediatric NDO approval; pediatric reports are limited to case-level data.
- **Bowel program.** Needed and parallel to bladder care; neurogenic bowel management drives quality of life and continence (see [Neurogenic Bowel](/docs/clinical-conditions/03i-defecatory-disorders/neurogenic-bowel) for the program escalation from transanal irrigation through MACE to colostomy). In the National Spina Bifida Patient Registry (3,670 patients with MMC and neurogenic bowel, age 5 or older), only 45% were fecally continent (fewer than one accident a month); antegrade enemas were the most common treatment and had the highest associated continence rate, though this is observational and confounded by severity.<sup>[[16]](#ref16)</sup>

### Escalation when CIC + antimuscarinic fails

1. **Intradetrusor onabotulinumtoxinA.** FDA-approved for pediatric NDO from age 5 (200 U for patients 34 kg or more; 6 U/kg for those under 34 kg); effective for neurogenic DO with hostile bladder.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup>
2. **Augmentation cystoplasty.** The reconstructive foundation. Ileum standard; ileocecum when continent cutaneous reservoir is desired.
3. **Continent catheterizable channel (Mitrofanoff).** concomitant with augmentation in most spina-bifida patients. Umbilical stoma allows CIC independence even in wheelchair-bound patients. Appendix (classic Mitrofanoff) or reconfigured ileum (Monti). In a systematic review of 213 adult neurogenic patients (retrospective, high risk of bias), stomal stenosis occurred in 4–32% (median 14%), 85 postoperative events required reoperation, and catheterization ability was at least 84% in 10 studies but 58.3% in one study at last follow-up.<sup>[[15]](#ref15)</sup>
4. **Bladder-neck reconstruction, sling, AUS, or closure.** For intrinsic sphincter deficiency, options include a pediatric bladder-neck sling, AUS in selected older children or adults, or closure with dependable concurrent drainage (for example, an established CIC channel) when outlet leakage is refractory. Assess storage pressures with urodynamics when outlet surgery could worsen compliance and threaten the upper tracts. For adults, AUA/SUFU grades sling Moderate Recommendation/Grade C, AUS Conditional Recommendation/Grade C, and closure Expert Opinion.<sup>[[22]](#ref22)</sup>
5. **MACE (antegrade continence enema) channel.** Appendix or cecostomy for antegrade large-volume bowel irrigation. Often performed concurrently with urologic reconstruction.
6. **Ileal conduit or continent diversion.** Options include salvage after failed reconstruction or diversion when other bladder-management approaches are inappropriate; AUA/SUFU recommends offering diversion in either setting (Moderate Recommendation, Grade C). An ileal conduit is appropriate when dependable catheterization is not feasible, whereas continent diversion requires a reliable catheterization and reservoir-care plan.<sup>[[5]](#ref5)</sup><sup>[[22]](#ref22)</sup>

For adults, AUA/SUFU 2021 states that augmentation cystoplasty and continent catheterizable channels may be offered to selected patients (statements 51 and 52, conditional recommendations, Grade C), bladder-neck closure with concomitant drainage may be offered after counseling (statement 47, Expert Opinion), urinary diversion should be offered when other options have failed or are inappropriate (statement 54, moderate recommendation, Grade C), and sacral neuromodulation should not be offered to patients with spina bifida or SCI (statement 50, moderate recommendation, Grade C).<sup>[[22]](#ref22)</sup>

### Augmentation-cystoplasty specifics in spina bifida

- **Ileum** is the commonest segment; patch detubularized and anastomosed to bivalved bladder.
- **Reoperation is common.** In a 413-patient spina bifida augmentation cohort (median follow-up 11 years), 10-year risk of any additional surgery was 44%: bladder stones 28% (recurrence in about half), perforation 9.6% with detubularized reconfigured ileocystoplasty versus 23.7% without, reaugmentation 5.3% versus 15.2%, bowel obstruction 4.5% and conversion to diversion 2.7%.<sup>[[14]](#ref14)</sup> Pooled adult neurogenic-bladder data are lower and less certain: perforation 1.9%, mucus problems 12.5%, metabolic abnormalities 3.35%, bowel dysfunction 15%, stones 10%.<sup>[[5]](#ref5)</sup> Perforation in adults tracks with missed catheterization and substance use, so screen adherence before offering augmentation.
- **Need for concurrent Mitrofanoff.** Very common, especially in wheelchair-using patients.
- **Metabolic surveillance.** Acidosis, B12 deficiency, electrolyte derangement (annual labs).
- **Stone surveillance.** Bladder stones are the most common complication in augmented patients; high-volume daily irrigation (240 mL or more) reduced stones and symptomatic UTI in one transitional-clinic series, while correcting metabolic abnormalities or adding mucolytics did not help.<sup>[[17]](#ref17)</sup> Silent upper-tract deterioration occurred in one third of those with renal scarring, so imaging surveillance continues even when the patient is well.
- **Malignancy surveillance.** Bladder cancer affects an estimated 2–4% of people with spina bifida and is usually advanced at diagnosis (median age 41; 71% stage III–IV in a systematic review of cases), so counsel every patient. Among 52 published myelomeningocele bladder-cancer cases, overall survival differed by augmentation status in unadjusted analysis (P = .009); after eight gastric-augmentation cases were excluded, the comparison of eight augmented and 36 nonaugmented cases was not statistically significant (P = .112).<sup>[[7]](#ref7)</sup> SBA does not recommend a fixed screening cystoscopy schedule; evaluate concerning symptoms.<sup>[[3]](#ref3)</sup><sup>[[7]](#ref7)</sup> AUA/SUFU likewise recommends against screening or surveillance cystoscopy in adults with NLUTD (statement 19, strong recommendation, Grade B) and advises cystoscopy for hematuria, recurrent UTI or suspected anatomic anomaly (statement 18) and after bowel-segment reconstruction with gross hematuria or symptomatic recurrent UTI (statement 60).<sup>[[22]](#ref22)</sup>

### Tethered cord

- Any new urologic or bowel symptom change in a growing spina-bifida child should trigger MRI for tethered cord and neurosurgical consultation.
- **Untethering** can stabilize or improve NLUTD but does not always restore function. In a Japanese MMC series of 55 children, about half (27) needed tethered cord release, usually prompted by worsening video-urodynamics (high pressure, reduced capacity, more DO, reflux); bladder findings improved in 18 of 27 (67%) after release, and routine urodynamics was the main way deterioration was detected.<sup>[[13]](#ref13)</sup>
- **Who develops symptomatic tethering.** Essentially all myelomeningocele patients have some post-closure tethering, but in a National Spina Bifida Patient Registry cohort of 6,339 patients, only 21.5% underwent tethered cord release, most (75.8%) just once. The rate did not correlate with functional lesion level; it was lower among non-ambulators than community ambulators and among patients with treated hydrocephalus (HR 0.30), and higher after prior Chiari decompression (HR 1.71). This is a pediatric/adolescent registry population, but the ambulatory, prior-Chiari-decompression phenotype is a reasonable prompt for a lower threshold to re-image an adult transition-clinic patient with new symptoms.<sup>[[24]](#ref24)</sup>
- **Adult-onset tethering, general population.** A separate adult neurosurgical series of 24 newly diagnosed tethered-cord patients (age 20–77; mostly taut filum, conus lipoma, or diastematomyelia rather than a prior myelomeningocele closure) found bladder dysfunction in 21, and among the 20 who had surgery, bladder function normalized in 12 and improved in 3; recovery across pain, motor, sensory, and bladder measures was more complete when symptom duration before surgery was under 6 to 8 months.<sup>[[25]](#ref25)</sup> This is a mixed-etiology adult cohort, not a myelomeningocele-specific series, but it illustrates that detethering can still meaningfully improve bladder function in adulthood, particularly when addressed soon after new symptoms appear.

## Transition to Adult Care

The most vulnerable moment in a spina-bifida patient's life is the pediatric-to-adult care transition. Attrition at transition is the leading cause of late upper-tract deterioration and late diagnosis of reconstruction complications.

Best-practice transition elements:
- Dedicated spina-bifida multidisciplinary clinics spanning both pediatric and adult populations.
- Structured transition readiness assessment starting in adolescence.
- Warm hand-off from pediatric urology to adult neuro-urology.
- Continued surveillance protocol with no gaps.

The SBA transition guideline (updated 2023) reports that structured transition programs increase the likelihood of establishing with an adult clinic and reduce acute-care use in young adults.<sup>[[18]](#ref18)</sup>

Sexual and reproductive health is a specific gap in transition counseling that clinicians can close simply by raising it first rather than waiting for the patient to ask. In one questionnaire study of adolescents and young adults with myelomeningocele and their parents, 95% of patients and 59% of parents felt their sexual and reproductive-health knowledge was inadequate; only 39% of patients had discussed these topics with a physician, while 93% of adolescents and all surveyed parents said they would discuss them if the physician initiated the conversation.<sup>[[26]](#ref26)</sup> Sex counseling has been recommended as part of routine care for adults with spina bifida.<sup>[[27]](#ref27)</sup> Proactively addressing sexuality, contraception, fertility, heredity, and pregnancy is itself a transition-readiness intervention.

## Special Adult Reconstructive Issues

### Pregnancy

- Most women with spina bifida can carry to term; pregnancy requires a coordinated urologic-obstetric plan.
- As the uterus grows, augmentation or Mitrofanoff anatomy may make catheterization difficult; a urethral catheter may be impossible after bladder-neck closure. In a small retrospective series of reconstructed women with spinal dysraphism, 8/15 delivered pregnancies involved catheterization difficulty and 13/15 new or worsening hydronephrosis. Urgently involve urology for drainage difficulty, assess the upper tracts, and tailor temporary bladder drainage to the reconstructed outlet and channel.<sup>[[4]](#ref4)</sup><sup>[[22]](#ref22)</sup><sup>[[31]](#ref31)</sup>
- Mode of delivery: individual multidisciplinary planning; vaginal birth remains possible. SBA specifically recommends cesarean to protect continence after bladder-neck reconstruction or AUS, and notes that previous urinary reconstruction can make cesarean technically complex.<sup>[[4]](#ref4)</sup>
- Higher UTI risk; lower threshold for treatment. In a Quebec cohort of 397 deliveries to women with spina bifida, maternal ICU admission (PR 3.4) and respiratory morbidity (PR 9.5) were more frequent than in women without spina bifida, so plan delivery at a center able to provide obstetric and critical-care support.<sup>[[19]](#ref19)</sup>

### Sexual function and fertility

- **Men.** Erectile dysfunction common; the EAU recommends PDE5i first-line, intracavernous injection second-line and prosthesis only after conservative treatment fails. Ejaculatory dysfunction can be addressed with sympathomimetics for retrograde ejaculation or semen harvest from urine, then vibrostimulation, electroejaculation or surgical sperm retrieval (PESA/TESE) when needed.<sup>[[5]](#ref5)</sup> In a small 1995 cohort of 22 men with myelomeningocele, erection was achieved by visual stimulation in 95% and by tactile stimulation in 86%, yet only 27% of those who achieved erections were satisfied with penile rigidity, and ejaculation with orgasm occurred in 67%; the gap between achieving an erection and being satisfied with it is a reason to ask specifically about rigidity, not only erectile capability, when counseling.<sup>[[28]](#ref28)</sup> The Spina Bifida Association men's health guideline lists abnormalities of testicular descent, development and function, fertility, penile sensation, and erectile, ejaculatory and orgasmic function as common in men with spina bifida; prostate cancer has been diagnosed in this population, but few data guide screening, diagnosis or treatment.<sup>[[30]](#ref30)</sup>
- **Women.** In limited studies summarized by the EAU, sexual dysfunction is common among women with spina bifida; pelvic sensation may be reduced. The EAU strongly recommends against medical therapy for neurogenic sexual dysfunction in women; its strong recommendation for individualized multidisciplinary care concerns fertility, pregnancy, and delivery.<sup>[[4]](#ref4)</sup><sup>[[5]](#ref5)</sup>
- **Latex allergy.** Patients with spina bifida are at high risk of latex sensitization from repeated early exposure, and the SBA guidelines call for lifelong evidence-based latex allergy management.<sup>[[20]](#ref20)</sup> Latex avoidance from birth lowered sensitization from 27% (historic controls) to 4.5% in one series, so use latex-free gloves, catheters and equipment from the first procedure and always confirm status preoperatively.<sup>[[21]](#ref21)</sup> The EAU also prefers silicone catheters for this reason.<sup>[[5]](#ref5)</sup>

### Bladder cancer in augmented spina bifida

- Bladder cancer in spina bifida presents at a median age of 41 years, often at stage III–IV, with or without augmentation.<sup>[[7]](#ref7)</sup>
- Obtain cystoscopy and upper-tract evaluation for relevant changes, including gross hematuria, recurrent symptomatic UTI, increasing incontinence or pelvic pain; do not substitute a routine screening timetable for symptom assessment.<sup>[[3]](#ref3)</sup>

### Pelvic organ and rectal prolapse

- A retrospective comparison in adults with spina bifida and an acontractile detrusor found an association between Valsalva voiding and rectal prolapse or intussusception.<sup>[[23]](#ref23)</sup> A retrospective comparison of 55 patients (28 voiding by Valsalva, 27 on CIC) found de novo or worsened rectal prolapse/intussusception in 32.1% of Valsalva voiders versus 3.7% of CIC users over a median follow-up of 80.6 and 65.6 months, respectively (P = .01); de novo or worsened vaginal prolapse was also more frequent with Valsalva voiding (33.3% vs 11.1%) but did not reach significance.<sup>[[23]](#ref23)</sup>
- Examine women for pelvic organ prolapse and both sexes for rectal prolapse or intussusception at follow-up visits, with a lower threshold in patients who empty by Valsalva rather than CIC; this is an argument for CIC over Valsalva voiding wherever hand function and access allow it.

### Obesity and reconstruction access

- Many spina-bifida adults have wheelchair-associated weight gain, which complicates urethral CIC access and makes a Mitrofanoff umbilical stoma highly valuable.

## Clinical Correlations for the Reconstructive Urologist

- **Urodynamics drive NLUTD decisions, not lesion level.** Two infants with the "same" MMC level can have opposite phenotypes; treat the bladder you find, not the one you expected.
- **Hostile bladder: early CIC and antimuscarinic.** Early pressure management and reliable surveillance reduce preventable upper-tract injury.
- **The augmentation and Mitrofanoff and MACE stack is the modern reconstructive foundation.** Often performed in the same OR in adolescence to establish durable bladder, bowel, and continence programs before adulthood.
- **Transition care is a life-or-death issue.** Patients who fall out of surveillance between pediatric and adult care present with advanced disease.
- **Tethered cord is the most important hidden driver of late decline.** Re-image anyone with new symptoms.
- **Latex allergy is a surgical hazard.** Always flagged preoperatively.
- **A named spina-bifida program** coordinating urology, neurosurgery, orthopedics, PM&R, GI, and gynecology improves every outcome domain. Align with the nearest one if these patients are seen sporadically.
