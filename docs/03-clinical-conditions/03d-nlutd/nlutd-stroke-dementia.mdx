# Stroke and Dementia

**Stroke and dementia** together account for the largest absolute NLUTD volume by prevalence, though individually each patient's urologic workload is lighter than SCI or spina bifida. Both are **suprapontine** processes: the dominant pattern is **detrusor overactivity with preserved coordination and low storage pressures**, with very low upper-tract risk. The reconstructive urologist's focus in these populations is different — **functional / situational incontinence, caregiver burden, fall risk, and cognitive-side-effect avoidance** dominate decisions far more than any reconstructive anatomy.

See [Neurogenic Lower Urinary Tract Dysfunction](/docs/clinical-conditions/03d-nlutd/neurogenic-bladder) for the general framework. When refractory OAB justifies procedural therapy in cognitively intact patients, see [Intradetrusor OnabotulinumtoxinA](/docs/surgical-techniques/04f-incontinence-procedures/procedures/intradetrusor-botox) and [Sacral Neuromodulation](/docs/surgical-techniques/04f-incontinence-procedures/procedures/sacral-neuromodulation); the [Ileal Conduit](/docs/surgical-techniques/04c-urinary-diversion/ileal-conduit) is an occasional palliative option for intractable incontinence-related skin breakdown.

---

## Epidemiology

- **Stroke** — ~795,000 new events / year in the US. Urinary incontinence affects roughly **40–60%** of patients admitted after stroke, about 25% at discharge and ~15% at one year.<sup>[[4]](#ref4)</sup> EAU cites LUTS in 57–83% at one month, with **71–80% spontaneous recovery by six months**; bladder emptying usually improves within ~75 days. Persistent incontinence correlates with a poor prognosis, and post-stroke incontinence relates to cognitive dysfunction and stroke severity.<sup>[[5]](#ref5)</sup> A 2024 meta-analysis of 21 observational studies (n = 7,327) pooled an incidence of 38%.<sup>[[6]](#ref6)</sup>
- **Early pattern** — urgency incontinence, frequency and nocturia (detrusor overactivity) predominate; acute retention occurs mainly after large or severe strokes and usually resolves.<sup>[[5]](#ref5)</sup>
- **Dementia (Alzheimer, vascular, mixed)** — Alzheimer disease is ~80% of cases. Urinary incontinence is about three times more frequent than in age-matched patients without dementia and rises with severity; OAB / urgency incontinence with detrusor overactivity is the usual urodynamic finding.<sup>[[5]](#ref5)</sup>
- Combined stroke + dementia + age-related LUTS (BPH in men, GSM in women) is the clinical reality in most patients.

---

## Pathophysiology

- **Suprapontine lesion (cortex, subcortex, basal ganglia)** → disinhibition of the pontine micturition center → **detrusor overactivity with preserved coordination**.
- Storage pressures are often low, and upper-tract risk is much lower than in spinal cord injury or spina bifida, but risk classification still depends on emptying, complications and any coexisting disease.<sup>[[3]](#ref3)</sup>
- **Cognitive and motor impairment** add a **functional incontinence** layer — recognition of the urge, transfer speed, clothing management, caregiver response time all affect continence as much as detrusor behavior.

---

## Evaluation

The AUA/SUFU guideline risk-stratifies NLUTD patients (low, moderate, high, unknown) and re-stratifies when symptoms change.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup> The typical suprapontine picture (storage symptoms, insignificant PVR, detrusor overactivity)<sup>[[5]](#ref5)</sup> is usually low risk, so urodynamics is generally reserved for the exceptions below. EAU recommends video-urodynamics when invasive testing is done in neurological patients.<sup>[[5]](#ref5)</sup>

**Baseline:**
- History, with explicit attention to **functional status**, cognition, caregiver support, mobility, fall risk, and current medications (especially those with anticholinergic burden).
- Bladder / incontinence diary — distinguishes urge vs functional vs mixed.
- UA, PVR.
- Renal US and urodynamics **only if** elevated PVR, hematuria, recurrent febrile UTI, or coexisting suspected obstruction (e.g., man with BPH + stroke).

## Management

### Behavioral and caregiver-directed

- **Scheduled toileting (prompted voiding)** — the mainstay for cognitively impaired patients. Every 2–3 hours, before and after meals. Cochrane evidence shows short-term increases in self-initiated voiding and fewer incontinent episodes, with no long-term data;<sup>[[7]](#ref7)</sup> after stroke, Cochrane found too few small trials to rank interventions.<sup>[[4]](#ref4)</sup>
- **Environmental modifications** — bedside commode, elevated toilet seats, clothing with velcro / elastic, clear nighttime path to the bathroom, night lights.
- **Fluid and caffeine timing** — most fluid intake before mid-afternoon to reduce nocturia.
- **Bowel regimen** — constipation worsens urinary symptoms in older patients.
- **Pelvic-floor PT** — may help LUTS and incontinence after stroke in patients who can participate; EAU rates the evidence as limited.<sup>[[5]](#ref5)</sup>

### Pharmacologic

- **β3-adrenergic agonists (mirabegron, vibegron)** — often useful when avoiding anticholinergic burden; AUA permits antimuscarinics, beta-3 agonists or combination after individual assessment, rather than a mandatory class order.<sup>[[2]](#ref2)</sup>
- **Antimuscarinics** — the 2023 AGS Beers Criteria list antimuscarinic bladder drugs among strongly anticholinergic agents to avoid in dementia or cognitive impairment (and in delirium).<sup>[[8]](#ref8)</sup> Lower-CNS-penetrating agents (e.g., trospium) are sometimes chosen when one is unavoidable, but no antimuscarinic is established as cognitively risk-free; **avoid oxybutynin IR** in particular. EAU advises considering a switch to a beta-3 agonist if cognition worsens.<sup>[[5]](#ref5)</sup> Add each agent to the patient's **anticholinergic burden score** — urologic drugs plus antihistamines, TCAs and bowel agents compound cognitive decline.
- **Cholinesterase inhibitors** — donepezil and related drugs raise detrusor tone and are associated with new OAB diagnoses and antimuscarinic prescriptions (donepezil more than rivastigmine); the resulting prescribing cascade opposes the dementia drug and is linked to delirium and falls.<sup>[[9]](#ref9)</sup><sup>[[10]](#ref10)</sup> Before adding an antimuscarinic, review whether a recently started cholinesterase inhibitor is the cause.
- **Mirabegron / vibegron cautions** — check blood pressure (mirabegron can raise it) and PVR before and after starting. In neurological patients the evidence is still limited, though a standard 50 mg dose showed no cognitive worsening in Parkinson disease.<sup>[[5]](#ref5)</sup>
- **Alpha-blockers** (men with coexisting BPH) — orthostatic hypotension and falls are the concern after stroke; Beers advises against nonselective peripheral alpha-1 blockers in patients with syncope.<sup>[[8]](#ref8)</sup>
- **Duloxetine** (for mixed stress / urge in stroke with pelvic-floor weakness) — limited data in neurologic populations; not first-line.
- **Desmopressin** — nocturia indication; avoid in frail older patients due to hyponatremia risk (Beers 2023 advises against its use for nocturia or nocturnal polyuria).<sup>[[8]](#ref8)</sup>

### Catheter-based management

- **Indwelling catheters** are common in post-acute stroke units but carry a high UTI burden; aim to remove as soon as functionally feasible and return to toileting / pads.
- **CIC** is rarely feasible in moderate dementia; appropriate in selected stroke patients with intact cognition and caregiver assistance.
- **[Condom catheter](/docs/surgical-techniques/04f-incontinence-procedures/procedures/condom-catheters)** (men) — practical for overnight management in selected cases. Bacteriuria rate ~12%/month with continuous use; cognitive impairment requires extra vigilance for unrecognized strangulation or skin breakdown.

### Intradetrusor onabotulinumtoxinA

- Reasonable in stroke patients with refractory OAB and preserved cognition.<sup>[[2]](#ref2)</sup>
- **Caution** in patients who cannot perform CIC — post-injection retention may require indwelling catheter.
- Generally **avoided in moderate-to-severe dementia** for the same reason.

### Sacral neuromodulation (SNM)

- Reasonable in **post-stroke** patients with refractory OAB and preserved cognition.<sup>[[2]](#ref2)</sup>
- Not typically used in dementia given the programming and MRI-compatibility considerations.

### Reconstructive surgery

- **Very uncommon** in stroke / dementia. Upper-tract risk is low; reconstruction is rarely justified by QoL alone in a cognitively impaired patient with a short or uncertain trajectory.
- **Urinary diversion / ileal conduit** is an occasional option in the setting of devastating skin breakdown from incontinence and inability to toilet — particularly in bed-bound patients. Usually a palliative / caregiver-oriented decision.

## Common Scenarios

### Acute stroke with urinary retention

- Acute retention can occur immediately after stroke. Insert a catheter, trial removal as mobility and cognition recover.
- Persistent retention beyond the acute rehab phase warrants PVR check, imaging, and — in men — BPH evaluation.

### Post-stroke OAB in a patient with coexisting BPH

- Pragmatic path: **α-blocker + β3 agonist** first; urodynamics if refractory or equivocal; outlet surgery only for documented obstruction.
- Avoid aggressive antimuscarinic use in patients at elevated fall / delirium risk.

### Dementia with functional incontinence

- **Functional interventions dominate.** Scheduled toileting, clothing modifications, caregiver education, pads / briefs. Medication review to reduce anticholinergic burden.
- **Treat the reversible** — symptomatic UTI, constipation, diuretic dosing, sleep apnea. Bacteriuria without new genitourinary symptoms or systemic signs (fever, hemodynamic instability) warrants observation and reassessment rather than antibiotics, including in women who cannot report symptoms.<sup>[[11]](#ref11)[[12]](#ref12)</sup>

### Nursing-home patient with urinary incontinence

- Avoid "placement decision driven by incontinence" where possible — pads + scheduled toileting often restore manageable care at home.
- Reasonable pharmacologic trial: β3 agonist; avoid chronic anticholinergics.

### Post-stroke retention in an older man

Commonly a "BPH + stroke" presentation. **Urodynamics is valuable** before committing to outlet surgery — a weak stream may reflect detrusor underactivity, obstruction or both; symptom improvement after outlet surgery cannot be predicted from the stream alone.

## Clinical Correlations for the Reconstructive Urologist

- **Upper-tract risk is low — QoL and caregiver burden dominate.** Resist escalating to reconstructive procedures in cognitively impaired patients without durable benefit.
- **Individualize medication choice** and minimize anticholinergic burden; discuss possible cognitive adverse effects.
- **Functional incontinence is often the dominant driver.** Toileting schedules, clothing, mobility aids, and caregiver education deliver more continence than any medication.
- **Acute-phase retention after stroke** is common and usually resolves — don't commit to chronic catheterization in the first 1–2 months.
- **Mixed BPH + stroke OAB** needs urodynamics before outlet surgery.
- **Quality-of-life goals are paramount.** What does the patient (and caregiver) value? Continuing independence? Avoiding falls? Skin preservation? These drive the plan more than the urodynamic phenotype.
- **Trajectory matters.** Reconstruction with a long maintenance burden (catheterization, metabolic surveillance and symptom-triggered investigations) rarely serves a patient with progressing dementia.
