# Autonomic Dysreflexia

**Autonomic dysreflexia (AD)** is a potentially life-threatening syndrome of **uncontrolled sympathetic discharge** triggered by a noxious stimulus below the level of a spinal-cord injury (SCI), most commonly arising **at or above the T6 level**. It manifests as **acute, severe hypertension** with reflex compensatory parasympathetic activation above the lesion (headache, flushing, sweating; heart rate may fall or, more often, rise). The dominant trigger across all SCI series is **urinary** — bladder distension, catheter blockage, urinary tract infection, or urologic procedures — making AD recognition and prevention central to the urologic care of any SCI patient with a lesion T6 or above.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[2]](#ref2)[[3]](#ref3)</sup>

For the broader NLUTD framework see the [Neurogenic Lower Urinary Tract Dysfunction](/docs/clinical-conditions/03d-nlutd) section. For urodynamics in this population see [Urodynamics](/docs/evaluation/ancillary-tests/urodynamics). For the surgical interventions that depend on AD-aware perioperative management see [Sacral Neuromodulation](/docs/surgical-techniques/04f-incontinence-procedures/procedures/sacral-neuromodulation), [Intradetrusor OnabotulinumtoxinA](/docs/surgical-techniques/04f-incontinence-procedures/procedures/intradetrusor-botox), and [Augmentation Cystoplasty](/docs/surgical-techniques/04b-bladder-reconstruction/bladder-augmentation).

## Definition and Epidemiology

The **International Standards on AD** (Krassioukov 2012; reaffirmed by ASIA / ISCoS) define AD as **a rise in systolic blood pressure of more than 20 mmHg above baseline** in a patient with SCI at or above T6 in response to a noxious stimulus. The Consortium for Spinal Cord Medicine/PVA guideline (2.1) and the EAU Neuro-Urology guideline (2026) use the same threshold. Resting systolic pressure after high SCI is often only 90–110 mmHg, so a reading that looks normal in an able-bodied patient (for example 130 mmHg) can already be AD. Know the patient's baseline.<sup>[[4]](#ref4)[[6]](#ref6)[[14]](#ref14)</sup>

| Parameter | Detail |
|---|---|
| **Prevalence in T6 and above SCI** | Common. Signs of AD appeared during urodynamics in 13/22 tetraplegic patients (59%; 91% of motor-complete, 27% of incomplete) and in none of the paraplegic patients in one series;<sup>[[5]](#ref5)</sup> 63% of a T6-and-above outpatient cohort had a systolic rise &gt; 20 mmHg during urodynamics;<sup>[[9]](#ref9)</sup> 51% of children with ≥ T6 SCI had documented AD<sup>[[12]](#ref12)</sup> |
| **Onset after injury** | First episodes typically appear **1–6 months** after injury; incomplete injuries are also at risk<sup>[[2]](#ref2)</sup> |
| **Lesion-level threshold** | **T6 and above** — splanchnic sympathetic outflow (T5–L2) split off from supraspinal control |
| **Below T6** | AD is rare and generally less severe; isolated reports in T7–T10 lesions |
| **Trigger distribution** | **Bladder** &gt; **bowel** &gt; cutaneous / pressure ulcer / fracture / DVT / labor (in one pediatric-onset series, urologic 75%, bowel impaction 18%)<sup>[[12]](#ref12)</sup> |

## Pathophysiology

The pathophysiologic substrate is a **disconnection of supraspinal inhibitory control** from the sympathetic outflow that supplies the splanchnic vasculature (T5–L2). When a noxious stimulus enters the cord below the lesion, it triggers a massive, unopposed sympathetic discharge. Above the lesion, baroreceptor activation drives a parasympathetic counter-response — but the parasympathetic limb cannot cross the lesion to inhibit the runaway sympathetic outflow below.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[3]](#ref3)</sup>

| Below the lesion | Above the lesion |
|---|---|
| Massive sympathetic discharge → **vasoconstriction, severe hypertension** | Compensatory **parasympathetic activation** |
| Pale, cool skin; piloerection | Baroreflex bradycardia is the classic finding, but in a 445-episode series relative tachycardia (68%) was far more common than bradycardia (0.3%)<sup>[[8]](#ref8)</sup> |
| Splanchnic vasoconstriction | Flushing, sweating, blurred vision, nasal congestion |
| | **Pounding headache** (the cardinal symptom) |

The **gut and bladder are over-represented as triggers** because their afferent fibers ascend densely through the splanchnic / hypogastric / pelvic nerve trunks — each is essentially a tripwire for the unopposed sympathetic loop.

## Clinical Presentation

The classic syndrome combines **acute hypertension** (often 20–40 mmHg above the SCI baseline of ~ 90–110 mmHg systolic) with **a pounding headache** and a **constellation of vasomotor and autonomic symptoms** above and below the lesion.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[6]](#ref6)</sup>

### Above the lesion

- **Pounding / throbbing headache** — most common and most reliable symptom
- **Flushing, profuse sweating** (especially face, neck, upper torso)
- **Nasal congestion**
- **Blurred vision, mydriasis**
- **Anxiety, sense of impending doom**
- **Heart-rate change**: tachycardia is more common than bradycardia in practice; neither a slow pulse nor headache is required for AD.<sup>[[6]](#ref6)[[8]](#ref8)</sup>

### Below the lesion

- **Pale, cool, dry skin** with piloerection ("goosebumps")
- **Spasticity exacerbation**

### Severity spectrum

- **Asymptomatic AD ("silent AD")** — measurable BP elevation without symptoms; most common during routine urologic procedures (urodynamics, cystoscopy)<sup>[[7]](#ref7)</sup>
- **Symptomatic AD** — the classic syndrome above
- **Severe AD** — systolic BP &gt; 180 mmHg; risk of intracerebral hemorrhage, hypertensive encephalopathy, seizures, retinal hemorrhage, myocardial ischemia, pulmonary edema, and death<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[6]](#ref6)</sup>

## Common Triggers

The dominant trigger categories, most common first (bladder and bowel account for most episodes):<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[2]](#ref2)[[3]](#ref3)</sup>

| Category | Specific triggers |
|---|---|
| **Urinary** | Bladder distension (kinked catheter, blocked supra-pubic tube, retention from CIC overdue), UTI, bladder stones, urodynamics, cystoscopy, intradetrusor botulinum-toxin injection, ESWL, urethral catheterization, prostate stimulation / vibrostimulation for sperm retrieval |
| **Bowel** | Fecal impaction, flatulence, anal fissure, hemorrhoids, digital rectal exam, enema, anorectal procedure |
| **Cutaneous** | Pressure ulcer, ingrown toenail, burns, tight clothing |
| **Skeletal / orthopedic** | Long-bone fracture, heterotopic ossification |
| **Vascular** | DVT, thrombophlebitis |
| **Reproductive** | Labor / delivery, menstrual cramps, ejaculation, vibrostimulation, electroejaculation |
| **Pharmacologic** | Misoprostol; sympathomimetics; abrupt withdrawal of clonidine or baclofen |

## Acute Management

The current **Consortium for Spinal Cord Medicine/PVA** guideline guides acute care:<sup>[[6]](#ref6)[[8]](#ref8)</sup>

### 1. Position upright

- **Sit the patient up immediately and lower the legs** — blood pools below the lesion and BP falls. Take BP every 2–5 minutes while the trigger is sought.
- **Loosen tight clothing or constrictive devices** (binders, abdominal binders, support stockings).

### 2. Identify and remove the trigger — bladder first

Because **urinary triggers dominate**, the bladder is checked first:

| Catheter status | Action |
|---|---|
| **No catheter, can't void** | Insert urethral catheter using **2% lidocaine jelly** (instill at least 3–5 minutes before instrumentation when time allows); monitor BP during drainage |
| **Indwelling catheter present** | Check for kinks, clots, sediment occlusion; **irrigate gently** with 10–15 mL warm saline; stop if fluid does not drain |
| **Suprapubic tube** | Same — confirm patency; replace if blocked |
| **CIC schedule overdue** | Catheterize immediately |

Persistent hypertension requires assessment for bowel and other triggers. Before rectal examination/disimpaction, consider antihypertensive treatment if systolic BP remains ≥150 mmHg and use topical anesthetic; stop if AD worsens.<sup>[[6]](#ref6)</sup>

### 3. Pharmacologic BP reduction

At **≥150 mmHg systolic in adults**, consider rapid-onset, short-duration medication **before catheter manipulation**; do not wait for all triggers to be removed:<sup>[[6]](#ref6)</sup>

| Agent | Dose | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| **Nitroglycerin 2% ointment (nitropaste)** | 0.5–2 inches (1.25–5 cm), thin layer on clean hairless skin of the chest or back | First-line in the CPG appendix; **wipe it off** if BP overshoots, so the effect is controllable. Not available in some countries (nitroglycerin spray 400 mcg, one spray every 5–10 minutes to a maximum of three, is the CPG's example alternative) |
| **Nifedipine immediate-release** | 10 mg orally, capsule swallowed (or punctured and swallowed) | The CPG advises against sublingual use: absorption is variable and little faster than swallowing. Can cause a significant BP drop. Avoid in older adults and in coronary disease |
| **Captopril** | 25 mg sublingual | Alternative when nitrates are unavailable or contraindicated; onset about 15 minutes, may last longer in renal impairment |
| **Hydralazine** | 10–20 mg orally (CPG appendix) | Onset 20–30 minutes; a parenteral form is used in monitored hospital settings |
| **Clonidine** | 0.1–0.2 mg orally (sublingual clonidine is also named in Rec 2.13) | Slower onset (30–60 minutes) |

The CPG (Rec 2.13) names nitropaste, nifedipine, hydralazine or sublingual clonidine as rapid-onset, short-duration options. Beta-blockers such as labetalol are not among its listed agents. Expect the drug to keep acting after the trigger is cleared, so watch for hypotension.<sup>[[6]](#ref6)</sup>

**Critical caveat:** ask about recent **PDE5 inhibitor** use before any nitrate. The CPG appendix advises no nitroglycerin within 24 hours of sildenafil or vardenafil or 48 hours of tadalafil, because of profound hypotension. Use captopril or another non-nitrate agent instead. Headache and flushing from a PDE5 inhibitor can mimic AD, so check the BP before treating. The EAU guideline likewise counsels that patients using on-demand nitrates for AD must not take PDE5 inhibitors.<sup>[[6]](#ref6)[[14]](#ref14)</sup>

### 4. Continuous BP and HR monitoring

- BP and HR every **1–2 minutes** during the acute episode, then every 2–5 minutes for symptomatic hypotension until BP is stable (Rec 2.30).
- Once resolved, continue monitoring for at least **2 hours** in a clinic or inpatient setting to detect recurrent AD or hypotension (Rec 2.31).
- If AD does not resolve after the bladder and bowel are cleared, look for other triggers (skin, fracture, DVT) and send the patient to an emergency department.<sup>[[6]](#ref6)</sup>

## Prevention in Urologic Practice

Because **the bladder is the dominant trigger**, urologic-procedure planning is the single most important AD-prevention domain.

### Routine outpatient procedures

| Procedure | AD-prevention strategy |
|---|---|
| **CIC** | Patient education on schedule adherence; technique; lidocaine jelly |
| **Indwelling catheter exchanges** | Pre-procedure lidocaine; monitor during drainage and exchange |
| **Cystoscopy** | Instill 2% lidocaine jelly at least 3–5 minutes beforehand (CPG 4.4); consider pharmacologic prophylaxis (4.5); BP at least every 2 minutes; treat UTI and ensure a recent bowel program first (4.1)<sup>[[6]](#ref6)[[7]](#ref7)</sup> |
| **Urodynamics** | BP at least every 2 minutes, more often if AD develops (CPG 4.7). If draining the bladder and drugs fail, stop and sit the patient up (4.9). Higher AD risk with cervical injury, DSD, poor compliance and &gt; 2 years since injury. Document AD susceptibility on the report<sup>[[6]](#ref6)[[9]](#ref9)</sup> |
| **Intradetrusor BoNT-A** | Pre-procedure NTG paste in known AD-susceptible patients; continuous BP monitoring; have nifedipine immediately available |
| **ESWL / cystolitholapaxy** | Anesthesia / sedation with continuous BP monitoring; postprocedural hypertension surveillance |
| **Vibrostimulation / electroejaculation for fertility** | BP every minute (CPG 4.6). Nifedipine 10 mg bite-and-chew is suggested only for men familiar with its effects or monitored in clinic; nifedipine reduced BP peaks and aborted trials in a small electroejaculation series. The EAU guideline strongly recommends counselling men with SCI at or above T6 and fertility clinics about AD<sup>[[6]](#ref6)[[10]](#ref10)[[14]](#ref14)</sup> |

### Surgical procedures

| Setting | Considerations |
|---|---|
| **Sacral neuromodulation** | Discuss with anesthesia beforehand; consider intraoperative arterial-line BP monitoring in T6-and-above patients (clinical practice; no series specific to SNM was found) |
| **Augmentation cystoplasty** | Significant AD risk during bladder mobilization and distension; arterial-line monitoring; **regional vs general anesthesia** decision driven by AD susceptibility and respiratory function |
| **Bladder neck reconstruction / closure** | High AD risk; arterial-line standard of care |
| **Renal stone surgery** | PCNL / ureteroscopy positioning effects; intraoperative AD reported during ureteric manipulation |

### Long-term prevention

- **Optimize bladder management** — convert PVR-positive patients to CIC; treat NDO (anticholinergics, β3-agonists, intradetrusor BoNT-A); augmentation cystoplasty for refractory NDO
- **Evaluate and treat symptomatic UTI**; bacteriuria alone does not establish a UTI — UTI is the second-most-common urinary AD trigger after distension
- **Bowel program** — prevent fecal impaction with scheduled bowel care
- **Skin surveillance** — pressure-ulcer prevention
- **Patient and caregiver education** — every SCI patient and family should know the AD-recognition triad (headache + hypertension + autonomic symptoms) and the home algorithm

## Special Populations

### Pregnancy and labor

AD can complicate labor in SCI women and is a recognized cause of intracerebral hemorrhage in this population. The CPG recommends anesthesia even when labor is painless: **spinal or epidural anesthesia is the most reliable way to prevent AD** in women with SCI at T6 and above (Rec 5.5), planned with an anesthesiologist before labor begins (5.2). Regional anesthesia blocks the afferent limb of the reflex. AD can occur antepartum, intrapartum and postpartum and can reduce uteroplacental flow, so follow these women with a multidisciplinary team. Hypertension in pregnancy needs differentiation from preeclampsia.<sup>[[6]](#ref6)[[11]](#ref11)</sup>

### Pediatric SCI

AD presents similarly in children but baseline BP is lower; the PVA recognition threshold is a systolic rise **greater than 15 mmHg above baseline**. Pharmacologic treatment thresholds are age-specific: about 120 mmHg in young children, 130 mmHg at 6–12 years and 140 mmHg in adolescents.<sup>[[6]](#ref6)</sup> Infants and preschool children may show only subtle signs; headache and piloerection are uncommon under 5 years.<sup>[[12]](#ref12)</sup> Children with myelomeningocele rarely develop AD because lesion levels are usually below T6.

### Athletes — "boosting"

A doping-related concern: some Paralympic athletes have intentionally induced AD ("boosting") to improve performance through the secondary BP / cardiac-output effect. The IPC prohibits the practice given the cerebrovascular risk, and detected attempts lead to disqualification. Urologists should be aware as a counseling topic.<sup>[[13]](#ref13)</sup>

## Differential Diagnosis

Acute hypertension in an SCI patient is AD until proven otherwise, but clinically differentiate:

- **Pre-existing essential hypertension** — chronic, not triggered, no headache
- **Hypertensive emergency** — usually no clear noxious trigger
- **Pulmonary embolism** — hypoxia and tachycardia; tachycardia does not exclude AD
- **Acute coronary syndrome** — chest discomfort if sensation preserved
- **Sepsis** — fever, tachycardia, hypotension trajectory
- **Substance / sympathomimetic ingestion**

## Complications and Outcomes

Untreated severe AD carries serious morbidity:

| Complication | Mechanism |
|---|---|
| **Intracerebral hemorrhage** | Acute hypertensive cerebrovascular event |
| **Hypertensive encephalopathy / seizures** | Cerebral autoregulation failure |
| **Retinal hemorrhage** | Microvascular rupture |
| **Pulmonary edema** | Acute LV afterload mismatch |
| **Myocardial ischemia / infarction** | Coronary supply-demand mismatch |
| **Death** | Reported at all severity tiers but rare with prompt management |

With recognition and the position-bladder-then-pharmacology algorithm, **most AD episodes resolve within minutes**. The cumulative cardiovascular toll of recurrent AD over a lifetime is the more chronic concern — driving the long-term-prevention emphasis on bladder optimization.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[3]](#ref3)</sup>

---

## Key Takeaways

1. **AD is a urologic disease** in functional terms — the bladder is the most common trigger. Check it first in any AD episode.
2. **Recognition triad** — sudden severe hypertension + pounding headache + a noxious trigger below a T6-or-above SCI lesion.
3. **Sit upright, monitor, and check the bladder promptly; consider medication before instrumentation when BP is high.**
4. **Lidocaine jelly** before urethral instrumentation is simple, low-cost prophylaxis recommended by the CPG. Evidence for prevention strategies is mostly observational.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup>
5. **Nitrates are contraindicated shortly after PDE5 inhibitors** (24 hours for sildenafil or vardenafil, 48 hours for tadalafil) — use an alternative agent.
6. **Document AD susceptibility** on every urodynamics, cystoscopy, and operative report; the next provider needs to know.
7. **Long-term prevention is bladder management** — CIC adherence, NDO control, UTI treatment, augmentation when refractory.
