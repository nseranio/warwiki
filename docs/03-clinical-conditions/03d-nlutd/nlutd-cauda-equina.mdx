# Cauda Equina Syndrome

Suspected cauda equina compression is a spinal emergency. New bladder or bowel dysfunction, altered perineal sensation or sexual dysfunction with back or leg symptoms warrants urgent assessment and emergency MRI. Do not wait for complete retention or every feature of the classic syndrome, and do not delay imaging for urodynamics. Confirmed compressive disease requires prompt specialist decisions about decompression as soon as safely feasible.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup>

Drain clinically important retention while arranging spinal care. Record symptoms, neurological findings and bladder volume. Subsequent neuro-urological care depends on recovery and measured lower urinary tract function, not the label “cauda equina” alone.

## Recognition and timing

CES is most often caused by a large central lumbar disc herniation, less often by tumor, trauma, epidural abscess or hematoma. Typical features are low back pain with bilateral sciatica, saddle (perineal) numbness, new urinary retention or incontinence, bowel incontinence or reduced anal tone, and sexual dysfunction. Only a minority of patients with suspected CES have compression on imaging (19% had clinical and radiological CES in a pooled analysis), and published definitions vary widely, so incidence and outcome figures are not directly comparable.<sup>[[5]](#ref5)[[6]](#ref6)</sup>

Two working categories guide counseling:

- **CES-I (incomplete):** altered urinary sensation, reduced desire to void, poor stream or straining, but still voluntary voiding.
- **CES-R (retention):** painless retention with overflow, and usually loss of bladder sensation.

Timing evidence is observational. A 2026 meta-analysis of 15 comparative studies found better early urinary and neurological outcomes when decompression occurred within about 48 hours of onset (pooled OR about 2.3 for urinary recovery), with the effect concentrated in incomplete CES. Retention-type CES showed no significant timing difference, surgery inside 24 hours was not consistently better than 24–48 hours, and at 12 months or more baseline bladder status was the dominant prognostic factor.<sup>[[6]](#ref6)</sup> Practical consequence: do not delay in a patient who still voids, since that patient has the most to lose, and counsel patients presenting in retention that bladder recovery is uncertain even after prompt surgery.

Urology's role in the acute phase:

- Catheterize for retention and record the volume, PVR and perineal or anal findings.
- Do not delay MRI or neurosurgical referral for urological testing.
- Where imaging and examination are equivocal, multichannel urodynamics can show whether the bladder is neurologically involved; in one retrospective referral series, only 57.6% of patients with suspected CES and MRI compression had neurovesical involvement.<sup>[[7]](#ref7)</sup> This is an adjunct for equivocal cases, not a gate to imaging or decompression.

## Bladder phenotype after the acute event

Root-level (sacral or infrasacral) injury classically gives an underactive or acontractile detrusor, a denervated or low-tone external sphincter, reduced sensation and weak outlet resistance. Expect these patterns, but they are not universal:

| Feature | Typical pattern | Caveat |
|---|---|---|
| Detrusor | Underactive or acontractile | Detrusor overactivity in 20 of 61 patients (32.8%) in a urodynamic series, most with electrophysiological injury at L2 or above (possible conus involvement)<sup>[[2]](#ref2)</sup> |
| External sphincter | Denervated, EMG shows denervation | Sphincter EMG and pelvic neurophysiology help when sacral root involvement is in doubt<sup>[[4]](#ref4)</sup> |
| Storage pressure | Usually low | Poor compliance can develop later; surveillance is still needed |
| Outlet | Weak, stress leakage and overflow | Variable; some bladder necks remain competent |
| Sensation | Reduced or absent | Bladder fullness may go unrecognized |

Assess storage pressure and compliance, emptying and outlet competence separately. Symptoms and MRI level do not reliably predict bladder type, so use urodynamics before planning irreversible treatment.<sup>[[2]](#ref2)</sup>

## Outcomes

Recovery is often incomplete. In a 2026 meta-analysis of 16 studies (987 patients), persistent bladder dysfunction at 12 months or later occurred in 32.6% (95% CI 23.4–41.9%; marked heterogeneity), and in about 47% of those with bladder dysfunction before surgery in crude paired data. Retention at presentation predicted worse recovery.<sup>[[8]](#ref8)</sup> There is no fixed recovery deadline; reassess over the first year or longer before committing to irreversible reconstruction.

## Evaluation and follow-up

- **After neurological stabilization:** renal function, upper-tract ultrasound, PVR and symptoms; multichannel (ideally video) urodynamics to document detrusor function, compliance, outlet competence and reflux. Reassess earlier for retention, infection or renal findings.<sup>[[3]](#ref3)</sup>
- **Surveillance:** follow the NLUTD risk category, with earlier review for clinical change. Do not obtain routine urine cultures or treat asymptomatic bacteriuria outside a recognized exception.<sup>[[3]](#ref3)</sup>

## Management

Emptying. Intermittent catheterization is the mainstay when hand function and anatomy allow. Avoid habitual Credé or Valsalva unless urodynamics show safe intravesical pressures and effective emptying; both can raise outlet resistance and worsen stress leakage.<sup>[[3]](#ref3)[[4]](#ref4)</sup> Indwelling catheters are a short-term bridge or last resort. A small 2025 series of 40 patients with CES-related bladder dysfunction reported better urodynamic and quality-of-life results with intermittent catheterization plus TENS than with indwelling catheterization plus TENS; the design does not test TENS itself.<sup>[[9]](#ref9)</sup>

**Storage and continence.** Treat the dysfunction that is demonstrated.

| Option | Role in CES |
|---|---|
| Pelvic-floor training, alpha-agonists | Limited benefit with denervation |
| Antimuscarinic | Only if overactivity or falling compliance is shown |
| Bulking agent | Occasional adjunct |
| Sling | Consider for persistent stress incontinence when the patient can catheterize (autologous sling is first-line for women who self-catheterize, EAU 2026) |
| Artificial urinary sphincter | Established option for neurogenic SUI (EAU 2026: strong recommendation in men, weak and referral to an experienced center in selected women), with higher complication and reoperation rates in neurological patients; requires safe storage and a catheterization plan<sup>[[4]](#ref4)</sup> |
| Sacral neuromodulation | EAU 2026 supports it in selected neurological patients, but the evidence is not specific to CES and it relies on intact sacral pathways, so it is rarely suited to a denervated bladder<sup>[[4]](#ref4)</sup> |
| Augmentation, catheterizable channel, bladder-neck closure, diversion | Late options for poor compliance, inaccessible urethra or failed reconstruction; augmentation is uncommon because capacity is usually adequate |

Choose an outlet procedure only for established SUI with safe bladder storage and a workable catheterization plan. AUS is one selected option, not a default operation for CES.

**Bowel and sexual function.** Sacral denervation usually affects all three systems, so ask about them together.

- **Bowel:** fecal incontinence and constipation are common. Use a scheduled regimen (fiber, softeners, timed evacuation), transanal irrigation for refractory cases and an antegrade enema (MACE) where needed.
- **Sexual:** a 2026 systematic review (5 studies) found long-term sexual dysfunction in 48–67% (pooled 58.4%) of men and women, including arousal, orgasm and satisfaction problems as well as erectile dysfunction. Outcomes track severity of neural compromise more than timing. Manage erectile dysfunction stepwise (PDE5 inhibitor, intracavernosal injection, prosthesis); refer for sperm retrieval when fertility is desired.<sup>[[10]](#ref10)</sup>
