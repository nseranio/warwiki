# Cauda Equina Syndrome

Suspected cauda equina compression is a spinal emergency. New sacral symptoms (altered bladder or bowel function, perineal sensation, or sexual function) warrant urgent assessment for cauda equina compression. Back or leg pain can be absent; obtain emergency MRI when CES is suspected.<sup>[[11]](#ref11)</sup> Do not wait for complete retention or every feature of the classic syndrome, and do not delay imaging for urodynamics. Confirmed compressive disease requires prompt specialist decisions about decompression as soon as safely feasible.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup>

Drain clinically important retention while arranging spinal care. Record symptoms, neurological findings and bladder volume. Subsequent neuro-urological care depends on recovery and measured lower urinary tract function, not the label “cauda equina” alone.

## Recognition and timing

CES is most often caused by a large central lumbar disc herniation, less often by tumor, trauma, epidural abscess or hematoma. Typical features are low back pain with bilateral sciatica, saddle (perineal) numbness, new urinary retention or incontinence, bowel incontinence or reduced anal tone, and sexual dysfunction. Across 18 studies of adults with suspected CES, 19% met both clinical and radiological criteria for CES; published definitions vary widely, so incidence and outcome figures are not directly comparable.<sup>[[5]](#ref5)[[6]](#ref6)</sup>

Two working categories guide counseling:

- **CES-I (incomplete):** altered urinary sensation, reduced desire to void, poor stream or straining, but still voluntary voiding.
- **CES-R (retention):** painless retention with overflow, and usually loss of bladder sensation.

Timing evidence is observational. A 2026 meta-analysis of 15 comparative studies involving 26,627 adults decompressed for degenerative CES found that, in clinical cohorts, surgery within 48 hours of symptom onset was associated with better early urinary recovery (pooled OR about 2.3) and neurological outcomes than later surgery; the timing signal was strongest in incomplete CES. Retention-type CES showed no significant timing difference, surgery inside 24 hours was not consistently better than 24–48 hours, and at 12 months or more baseline bladder status was the dominant prognostic factor.<sup>[[6]](#ref6)</sup> Expedite MRI for both CES-I and CES-R, and decompress confirmed compressive CES as soon as safely feasible. For retention-type CES, counsel that bladder recovery remains uncertain even after prompt decompression.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[6]](#ref6)</sup>

Urology's role in the acute phase:

- Catheterize for retention and record the drained volume and perineal or anal findings. In patients who void, document PVR; a PVR &lt;200 mL does not exclude CES and must not delay emergency MRI when suspicion persists.<sup>[[12]](#ref12)</sup>
- Do not delay MRI or neurosurgical referral for urological testing.
- After emergency MRI shows compression, multichannel urodynamics may clarify neurovesical involvement in selected patients with equivocal clinical findings; in one retrospective referral series with equivocal findings and positive MRI, 141/245 evaluable patients (57.6%) had neurovesical involvement attributed to cauda equina compression after a protocol of uroflowmetry/PVR screening and selective invasive urodynamics (performed in 211 patients).<sup>[[7]](#ref7)</sup> This is an adjunct for equivocal cases, not a gate to imaging or decompression.

## Bladder phenotype after the acute event

Root-level (sacral or infrasacral) injury classically gives an underactive or acontractile detrusor, a denervated or low-tone external sphincter, reduced sensation and weak outlet resistance. Typical patterns include:

| Feature | Typical pattern | Caveat |
|---|---|---|
| Detrusor | Underactive or acontractile | Detrusor overactivity in 20 of 61 patients with CES and neurogenic bladder (32.8%) in a urodynamic series, most with electrophysiological injury at L2 or above (possible conus involvement)<sup>[[2]](#ref2)</sup> |
| External sphincter | Denervated, EMG shows denervation | Sphincter EMG and pelvic neurophysiology help when sacral root involvement is in doubt<sup>[[4]](#ref4)</sup> |
| Storage pressure | Usually low | Poor compliance can develop later; surveillance is still needed |
| Outlet | Weak, stress leakage and overflow | Variable; some bladder necks remain competent |
| Sensation | Reduced or absent | Bladder fullness may go unrecognized |

Assess storage pressure and compliance, emptying and outlet competence separately. Symptoms and MRI level do not reliably predict bladder type, so use urodynamics before planning irreversible treatment.<sup>[[2]](#ref2)</sup>

## Outcomes

Recovery is often incomplete. In a 2026 meta-analysis of 16 observational studies of surgically decompressed degenerative CES, pooled persistent bladder dysfunction at 12 months or later was 32.6% (95% CI 23.4–41.9%; marked heterogeneity) among 987 patients across study-defined urinary outcomes. Among those with preoperative dysfunction and paired data, 130/278 (46.8%) had persistent dysfunction. Retention at presentation predicted worse recovery.<sup>[[8]](#ref8)</sup> There is no fixed recovery deadline; reassess over the first year or longer before committing to irreversible reconstruction.

## Evaluation and follow-up

- **After neurological stabilization:** assess urinary symptoms and PVR in patients who void. If NLUTD risk remains unknown, obtain renal function assessment, upper-tract ultrasound and multichannel (ideally video) urodynamics to document detrusor function, compliance, outlet competence and reflux. Reassess earlier for retention, infection or renal findings.<sup>[[3]](#ref3)</sup>
- **Surveillance:** follow the NLUTD risk category, with earlier review for clinical change. Do not obtain routine urine cultures or treat asymptomatic bacteriuria outside a recognized exception.<sup>[[3]](#ref3)</sup>

## Management

Emptying. Intermittent catheterization is the mainstay when hand function and anatomy allow; AUA/SUFU recommends it over indwelling catheters (statement 35, strong recommendation, Grade C).<sup>[[3]](#ref3)</sup> Avoid habitual Credé or Valsalva unless urodynamics show safe intravesical pressures and effective emptying; both can raise outlet resistance and worsen stress leakage.<sup>[[3]](#ref3)[[4]](#ref4)</sup> The NHS England GIRFT pathway (updated March 2026) advises a trial without catheter as soon as possible after surgery, with pre- and post-void bladder scans. Following a failed trial, it calls for recatheterization, prompt teaching of intermittent self-catheterization when feasible, and follow-up with spinal cord injury or urology services.<sup>[[13]](#ref13)</sup> Indwelling catheters are a short-term bridge or last resort. A small 2025 series of 40 patients with CES-related bladder dysfunction reported better urodynamic and quality-of-life results with intermittent catheterization plus TENS than with indwelling catheterization plus TENS; the design does not test TENS itself.<sup>[[9]](#ref9)</sup>

**Storage and continence.** Treat the dysfunction that is demonstrated.

| Option | Role in CES |
|---|---|
| Pelvic-floor training, alpha-agonists | Limited benefit with denervation |
| Antimuscarinic | Only if overactivity or falling compliance is shown |
| Bulking agent | Occasional adjunct |
| Sling | Consider for persistent stress incontinence when the patient can catheterize; AUA/SUFU recommends slings in selected NLUTD patients with SUI and acceptable storage parameters (statement 45, moderate recommendation, Grade C)<sup>[[3]](#ref3)</sup> (autologous sling is EAU 2026 first-line treatment for women with neurogenic SUI who can self-catheterize, strong recommendation)<sup>[[4]](#ref4)</sup> |
| Artificial urinary sphincter | Option for neurogenic SUI (AUA/SUFU statement 46, conditional recommendation, Grade C, for selected patients with acceptable storage parameters<sup>[[3]](#ref3)</sup>; EAU 2026: strong recommendation in men, weak and referral to an experienced center in selected women), with higher complication and reoperation rates in neurological patients; requires safe storage and a catheterization plan<sup>[[4]](#ref4)</sup> |
| Sacral neuromodulation | EAU 2026 supports it in selected neurological patients, but the evidence is not specific to CES and it relies on intact sacral pathways, so it is rarely suited to a denervated bladder<sup>[[4]](#ref4)</sup> |
| Augmentation, catheterizable channel, bladder-neck closure, diversion | Late options for refractory poor compliance, inaccessible urethra or failed reconstruction; consider augmentation according to measured storage pressure and compliance after less invasive treatment fails<sup>[[4]](#ref4)</sup> |

Choose an outlet procedure only for established SUI with safe bladder storage and a workable catheterization plan. AUS is one selected option, not a default operation for CES.

**Bowel and sexual function.** Sacral denervation usually affects all three systems, so ask about them together.

- **Bowel:** fecal incontinence and constipation are common. Use a scheduled regimen (fiber, softeners, timed evacuation), transanal irrigation for refractory cases and an antegrade enema (MACE) where needed.
- **Sexual:** a 2026 systematic review (5 studies) found long-term sexual dysfunction in 48–67% (pooled 58.4%) of men and women, including arousal, orgasm and satisfaction problems as well as erectile dysfunction. Outcomes track severity of neural compromise more than timing. Manage erectile dysfunction stepwise (PDE5 inhibitor, intracavernosal injection, prosthesis); refer for sperm retrieval when fertility is desired.<sup>[[10]](#ref10)</sup>
