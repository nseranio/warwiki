# Parkinson's Disease

Parkinson disease (PD) and related atypical parkinsonism syndromes, most importantly multiple system atrophy (MSA), are a high-volume but generally non-reconstructive NLUTD population. Symptoms are dominantly storage-phase OAB with preserved coordination, and management is largely pharmacologic and behavioral. The reconstructive urologist's role is mostly diagnostic (distinguishing PD from MSA, from coexisting BPH) and risk-avoidance (anticholinergics in a cognitively vulnerable population, functional incontinence in a mobility-impaired population).

See [Neurogenic Lower Urinary Tract Dysfunction](/docs/clinical-conditions/03d-nlutd/neurogenic-bladder) for the general framework. For the procedural therapies referenced below, see [Intradetrusor OnabotulinumtoxinA](/docs/surgical-techniques/04f-incontinence-procedures/procedures/intradetrusor-botox), [Sacral Neuromodulation](/docs/surgical-techniques/04f-incontinence-procedures/procedures/sacral-neuromodulation), and [Percutaneous Tibial Nerve Stimulation](/docs/surgical-techniques/04f-incontinence-procedures/procedures/percutaneous-tibial-nerve-stimulation); for refractory MSA, the [Ileal Conduit](/docs/surgical-techniques/04c-urinary-diversion/ileal-conduit) is the salvage option.

---

## Epidemiology

- **US prevalence** of PD &gt;1 million; incidence rises with age.
- **Urinary symptoms are common in PD:** published estimates range widely (roughly 27–80%, depending on age, stage and how symptoms are defined), and about half of patients report LUTS at some point.<sup>[[3]](#ref3)</sup><sup>[[4]](#ref4)</sup>
- **Urgency and nocturia** are the most common symptoms; OAB or detrusor overactivity is the dominant phenotype.<sup>[[3]](#ref3)</sup><sup>[[4]](#ref4)</sup>
- **Urinary symptoms correlate with motor severity and autonomic dysfunction.**
- **MSA** is much rarer but has a far more aggressive autonomic phenotype; urinary symptoms often precede the motor diagnosis and are severe from onset.

---

## Pathophysiology

- **PD** is a suprapontine disease. Loss of basal ganglia dopaminergic input disinhibits the pontine micturition center, producing detrusor overactivity usually with preserved coordination (true DSD is atypical and merits reassessment). Storage pressures are typically low; upper-tract risk is rare.<sup>[[2]](#ref2)</sup>
- **MSA** has both suprapontine and sacral involvement. Patients develop early and severe OAB with later retention, DSD-like patterns, and profound sphincter denervation. Urinary retention with elevated PVR is common and an important clue.
- **Coexisting BPH.** Most PD patients are older men, so mixed PD-OAB and BPH outlet obstruction is common.

---

## Evaluation

**Initial:**
- History: timing of storage symptoms and retention relative to motor features (early severe retention with orthostatic symptoms raises concern for MSA), fall risk, medications, cognition, and caregiver status.<sup>[[4]](#ref4)</sup>
- UA, PVR, voiding diary.

**Adjunctive (as indicated):**
- **Urodynamics.** Recommended before prostate surgery in men with PD to distinguish outlet obstruction from PD OAB (changes postoperative expectations), and whenever MSA is suspected.
- **Renal US and eGFR** in patients with elevated PVR or MSA phenotype.

### PD vs MSA — the urologic clue

| Feature | PD | MSA |
|---|---|---|
| Onset of urinary symptoms | Can occur early; timing alone is nonspecific<sup>[[23]](#ref23)</sup> | Early severe retention is more concerning for MSA<sup>[[4]](#ref4)</sup> |
| OAB | Yes | Yes, often severe |
| PVR | Usually low | **Elevated — early retention** |
| Orthostatic hypotension | Modest | **Severe** |
| Response to dopaminergic therapy | Good (motor) | Poor |
| EMG of external anal sphincter | Usually normal | **Denervation pattern in many** (less discriminating than PVR in one large series)<sup>[[5]](#ref5)</sup> |

Early urinary retention and severe orthostatic hypotension and poor dopamine response should raise concern for MSA and change counseling about prognosis and procedure selection. In a retrospective series of 273 patients with MSA or PD, free-flow PVR &gt;150 mL was associated with MSA (OR 8.7), whereas weak detrusor contraction was not statistically discriminatory.<sup>[[5]](#ref5)</sup> This research cutoff does not exclude MSA at lower residuals: the 2022 MDS criteria include unexplained voiding difficulty with PVR ≥100 mL as one possible autonomic feature of clinically established MSA and allow any residual for clinically probable MSA, with other required features and exclusions.<sup>[[4]](#ref4)</sup><sup>[[18]](#ref18)</sup>

Sphincter EMG can add information when the diagnosis is uncertain, but timing and nonspecificity limit it. In a cohort of 84 probable-MSA patients, a neurogenic change on external-sphincter EMG was present in 62% overall, rising from 52% in the first year of disease to 83% by the fifth year; a negative result early in the disease cannot exclude MSA.<sup>[[11]](#ref11)</sup> A pathology-confirmed series of 30 MSA cases with a disease duration over five years found an abnormal result in 24, a borderline result in 5, and a normal result in only 1.<sup>[[12]](#ref12)</sup> The same reinnervation pattern is not specific to MSA: it has also been described in longstanding Parkinson disease, progressive supranuclear palsy, dementia with Lewy bodies, spinocerebellar ataxia type 3, obstetric anal-sphincter injury, and cauda equina lesions, so an abnormal EMG supports but does not establish the diagnosis without the other clues in the table above.<sup>[[12]](#ref12)[[13]](#ref13)[[14]](#ref14)[[15]](#ref15)[[16]](#ref16)</sup>

MSA can also cause syncope specifically during voiding. Compared with age-matched controls, MSA patients show a smaller systolic blood-pressure rise during bladder filling and a further, sometimes prolonged, blood-pressure fall during and after voiding (mean systolic reduction about 15 mmHg from baseline, and about 29 mmHg below controls, in one series of 25 MSA patients and 16 controls); the fall was greater in patients who strain to void and in those who reported micturition syncope or presyncope.<sup>[[17]](#ref17)</sup> Ask about presyncope or syncope during voiding, particularly in patients who strain, and counsel on sitting to void.

## Management

### Behavioral and pharmacologic first line

- **Timed voiding, bowel program, fluid management.**
- **Pelvic-floor training and urge suppression.** Offer a structured behavioral program to patients who can participate. A 12-week randomized noninferiority trial (77 people with PD; 84% men) found nurse-led behavioral treatment noninferior to solifenacin for ICIQ-OAB symptoms (mean score 5.5 versus 5.8 at 12 weeks; prespecified 15% margin). Six falls were reported in the solifenacin arm versus none in the behavioral arm, an exploratory safety observation; patients with MoCA &lt;18 or PVR ≥150 mL were excluded.<sup>[[24]](#ref24)</sup>
- **β3-adrenergic agonists (mirabegron, vibegron).** Often attractive when avoiding anticholinergic burden. AUA/SUFU permits antimuscarinics, beta-3 agonists or both (statement 33, conditional recommendation, Grade C); it does not require a beta-3-first sequence for all PD patients. Disease-specific evidence is stronger for mirabegron than for assuming a class-wide effect: the PaDoMi RCT (117 randomized patients with parkinsonism) improved OAB symptoms at weeks 4 and 8, with PVR increasing to about 64 mL at visit 4 and one acute urinary-retention event, and a Medicare cohort found fewer acute-care encounters with beta-3 agonists than antimuscarinics (HR 1.23 for antimuscarinics).<sup>[[8]](#ref8)</sup><sup>[[9]](#ref9)</sup><sup>[[3]](#ref3)</sup><sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup> Check blood pressure and PVR at baseline; mirabegron can raise blood pressure, and PD patients often have orthostatic hypotension, so review the full antihypertensive and dopaminergic regimen. Vibegron has limited PD-specific data.<sup>[[4]](#ref4)</sup>
- **Antimuscarinics.** Individualize benefit versus cognition, constipation, retention and total anticholinergic burden; lower CNS penetration does not establish absence of cognitive risk. Avoid oxybutynin IR in cognitively impaired patients. The AGS Beers Criteria advise avoiding strongly anticholinergic drugs in patients with dementia or cognitive impairment, which covers most antimuscarinics for PD dementia or Lewy body disease; if cognition declines on treatment, switch to a beta-3 agonist or non-drug measures.<sup>[[10]](#ref10)</sup><sup>[[4]](#ref4)</sup> Dopaminergic drugs can improve or worsen LUTS, so review timing of dose changes against symptoms.<sup>[[3]](#ref3)</sup>
- **Alpha-blockers.** Helpful for documented obstruction; counsel about orthostatic hypotension and falls, particularly in MSA and autonomic failure. Follow each agent's product-specific dosing instructions; check supine and standing blood pressure when starting or increasing treatment, and discuss nighttime falls if an evening dose is chosen.<sup>[[19]](#ref19)</sup><sup>[[20]](#ref20)</sup>
- **Desmopressin.** Consider selectively for documented nocturnal polyuria after addressing reversible causes. The EAU guideline strongly recommends screening serum sodium before treatment and monitoring it during use; hyponatremia was frequent in heterogeneous neurologic studies. Weigh age, frailty, and fall risk in selection.<sup>[[4]](#ref4)</sup>

### Intradetrusor onabotulinumtoxinA

- - Four small uncontrolled PD series (n=4, 20, 9, and 10) describe improvement in storage symptoms after intradetrusor onabotulinumtoxinA (100–200 U; follow-up 12 weeks to six months); PD-specific randomized efficacy has not been established.<sup>[[21]](#ref21)</sup> In the United States, BOTOX is indicated for eligible adults with OAB and for urinary incontinence due to detrusor overactivity associated with a neurologic condition; follow the applicable indication and dose, and counsel that post-injection retention may require CIC.<sup>[[22]](#ref22)</sup><sup>[[4]](#ref4)</sup> AUA/SUFU states that clinicians may offer onabotulinumtoxinA to oral-refractory NLUTD patients other than those with SCI or MS (statement 41, conditional recommendation, Grade C) and must discuss retention risk and possible CIC beforehand in patients who void (statement 42, Clinical Principle).<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup>
- **Caution:** post-injection urinary retention may tip a borderline patient into full retention requiring CIC, and many PD patients lack the dexterity for CIC. Discuss explicitly before proceeding.

### Prostate / outlet surgery in men with PD

- For urodynamically confirmed prostatic obstruction in men with PD, consider outlet surgery after assessing for MSA and counseling about uncertain PD-specific outcomes. The cited review reports PD-focused outcomes for TURP and mixed-cohort data for transurethral incision; it does not report PD-specific outcomes for HoLEP or simple prostatectomy.<sup>[[3]](#ref3)</sup><sup>[[6]](#ref6)</sup> Evidence is small and biased: a 2024 review counted study-defined success in 19/70 (27.1%) men with PD data from two PD-focused studies and one mixed neurologic cohort (reported median follow-ups 9.2 months, two years, and three years). De novo urge incontinence occurred in 6/30 (20%) previously continent men in one older cohort; this was not a pooled PD estimate.<sup>[[6]](#ref6)</sup>
- **Counsel about persistent OAB symptoms post-op.** Outlet surgery does not fix the neurogenic component.
- **In MSA, avoid routine outlet surgery.** One high-bias TURP cohort reported de novo incontinence in 12/20 men (60%); if surgery is considered for verified obstruction, assess sphincter function and counsel about this risk.<sup>[[4]](#ref4)</sup><sup>[[6]](#ref6)</sup>

### Catheter-based management

- **Indwelling urethral catheter.** Sometimes the pragmatic choice in late PD or MSA with severe mobility limitation and caregiver support.
- **CIC.** Feasible in early PD with intact dexterity; becomes harder as tremor progresses.
- **Suprapubic catheter.** AUA/SUFU recommends suprapubic over urethral drainage in appropriately selected patients who need a chronic indwelling catheter (statement 36, strong recommendation, Grade C).<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup>

### Sacral neuromodulation and PTNS

- **SNM** is a reasonable option in PD with refractory OAB; AUA/SUFU allows it for selected NLUTD patients with urgency, frequency or urgency incontinence (statement 49, conditional recommendation, Grade C).<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup>
- **Tibial nerve stimulation.** AUA/SUFU permits PTNS in selected spontaneously voiding NLUTD patients with urgency, frequency or urgency incontinence (statement 48, conditional recommendation, Grade C). In the PD-specific STRIPE trial (148 randomized), Geko transcutaneous stimulation did not outperform sham on ICIQ-OAB at 12 weeks; this device-specific result does not establish the effect of percutaneous PTNS.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup><sup>[[25]](#ref25)</sup>
- **MRI compatibility.** PD patients often have co-indications for brain imaging (e.g., if DBS surgery is planned); verify device MRI-conditional status.
- **DBS interaction.** Subthalamic DBS does not impair bladder function and may increase cystometric capacity (mean +124 mL in meta-analysis); coordinate with the movement-disorders team.<sup>[[7]](#ref7)</sup>
- **Usually not used in MSA** because of the aggressive autonomic phenotype.

### Reconstructive surgery

- **Rare** in PD. Augmentation is almost never indicated because upper-tract risk is low.
- **MSA.** Occasionally needs urinary diversion for refractory combined retention and incontinence, but advanced care planning and prognosis often drive the decision.

## Common Scenarios

### PD with OAB and mixed BPH

Pragmatic pathway: (1) behavioral measures and an individualized β3 agonist; consider an α-blocker for coexisting obstructive symptoms after assessing orthostasis; (2) urodynamics before outlet surgery to confirm obstruction and assess bladder function; (3) outlet surgery for documented obstruction, with continued treatment of residual OAB; (4) intradetrusor onabotulinumtoxinA after discussing retention risk and confirming a feasible CIC plan.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup><sup>[[3]](#ref3)</sup><sup>[[4]](#ref4)</sup><sup>[[6]](#ref6)</sup>

### PD with nocturia

Very common and multifactorial (fluid intake, peripheral edema redistribution, sleep fragmentation, depression). Treat the modifiable factors first (elevation, compression, evening fluid restriction) before adding desmopressin.

### PD with dementia / functional incontinence

Shifts emphasis to caregiver-directed toileting schedules, incontinence products, and careful medication review (anticholinergic burden, diuretics). Avoid aggressive pharmacologic management that risks cognitive or fall side effects.

### MSA with retention

Teach CIC early while dexterity remains; plan for SPC as progression limits function.

---

## Clinical Correlations for the Reconstructive Urologist

- **Differentiate PD from MSA.** Early urinary retention with severe orthostasis and anal-sphincter EMG denervation suggests MSA. This matters for prognosis and for avoiding futile outlet surgery.
- **Choose storage medication individually.** Avoid unnecessary anticholinergic burden in cognitively vulnerable patients; no universal sequence applies.
- **Urodynamics before outlet surgery in men with PD.** OAB and BPH mix is common; operating without documenting obstruction leaves an unhappy patient.
- **CIC is often impractical in late PD.** Plan drainage around that reality (SPC rather than ongoing CIC failure).
- **Functional incontinence is often bigger than neurogenic incontinence in late PD.** Caregiver strategies, toilet transfer aids, clothing modifications, and scheduled voiding matter more than drugs.
- **MRI-compatibility and DBS coexistence** drive implant selection.
