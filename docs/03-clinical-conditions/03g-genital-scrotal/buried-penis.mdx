# Buried Penis

**Buried penis (BP)** is a condition in which the penile shaft — anatomically normal — is concealed beneath surrounding skin, prepubic fat, or cicatricial scar, which may impair voiding, hygiene, and sexual activity. It exists in two operationally distinct forms: a **congenital (pediatric) variant** caused by abnormal dartos / fascial attachments, and an **adult acquired buried penis (AABP)** driven by morbid obesity, prior circumcision with cicatricial scarring, lichen sclerosus, and scrotal lymphedema.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[2]](#ref2)[[3]](#ref3)</sup> The modern AABP operation — **escutcheonectomy + diseased-skin excision + split-thickness skin grafting (STSG)** ± panniculectomy and scrotoplasty — has reported durable unburying in roughly **78–100% of selected surgical cohorts** (about 90% in most series, lower with longer follow-up) with improvements in urinary, sexual, and psychological quality of life, but complications are frequent (~30–50%, mostly low-grade wound problems). The evidence is retrospective, single-center and at high risk of bias.<sup>[[4]](#ref4)[[23]](#ref23)[[29]](#ref29)[[36]](#ref36)</sup>

The single most important conceptual point: **buried penis is not micropenis** — the corporeal bodies are normal in size; the penis is hidden, not small. Treatment of these is entirely different.<sup>[[2]](#ref2)[[6]](#ref6)</sup>

This page covers the condition, classification, and decision framework. For operative technique, see [Buried Penis Repair](/docs/surgical-techniques/04e-genital-reconstruction/buried-penis-repair) and its component procedures — [Escutcheonectomy](/docs/surgical-techniques/04e-genital-reconstruction/escutcheonectomy), [Panniculectomy](/docs/surgical-techniques/04e-genital-reconstruction/panniculectomy), and [Penile Skin Grafting](/docs/surgical-techniques/04e-genital-reconstruction/penile-skin-grafting).

---

## Terminology

- **Buried penis** — penile shaft of normal size hidden within the prepubic fat pad, scrotal tissue, or cicatricial skin
- **Concealed / hidden penis** — used interchangeably with buried penis
- **Trapped penis** — the iatrogenic variant in which a circumcision scar contracts and traps the shaft beneath the skin
- **Webbed penis** — penoscrotal webbing obscures the ventral shaft
- **Micropenis** — true reduction in corporeal length (&gt; 2.5 SD below mean stretched length); a distinct entity that must be excluded

---

## Epidemiology

- **Congenital BP** — rare; presents in childhood; exact prevalence unknown<sup>[[8]](#ref8)[[9]](#ref9)</sup>
- **AABP** — described as increasingly common with rising obesity rates; no population incidence estimates exist, and published series are from referral centers<sup>[[7]](#ref7)[[36]](#ref36)</sup>
- **Typical AABP demographics:** mean or median age **44–56 years** and mean or median BMI **43–55 kg/m²** across series<sup>[[3]](#ref3)[[4]](#ref4)[[10]](#ref10)[[11]](#ref11)</sup>

---

## Etiology and Pathophysiology

### Congenital (pediatric) BP

The pediatric form reflects abnormal dartos and fascial mechanics:<sup>[[6]](#ref6)[[8]](#ref8)[[9]](#ref9)</sup>

- **Abnormal dartos tissue** — 78% of congenital BP have hypotrophic or randomly distributed smooth muscle fibers (vs the normal parallel pattern), suggesting shared pathophysiology with hypospadias<sup>[[9]](#ref9)</sup>
- **Abnormal fundiform / suspensory ligaments** — fail to anchor the penis<sup>[[8]](#ref8)</sup>
- **Long inner prepuce (LIP)** — a feature of the cited congenital BP series; may produce ballooning during voiding (congenital megaprepuce is a Grade I variant)<sup>[[8]](#ref8)</sup>
- **Hypermobility of ventral skin and dartos** — corporeal bodies telescope proximally into the scrotum and pubis<sup>[[6]](#ref6)</sup>
- **Excess suprapubic fat** — contributes in higher grades

**Hadidi classification of congenital BP (single-series operative framework, not a universal treatment algorithm):**<sup>[[8]](#ref8)</sup>

| Grade | Features | Frequency |
|---|---|---|
| **I** | Long inner prepuce only | 48% |
| **II** | LIP + indrawn penis requiring fundiform/suspensory ligament division | 33% |
| **III** | Grade II + excess suprapubic fat | 20% |

### Adult acquired buried penis (AABP)

| Driver | Mechanism |
|---|---|
| **Morbid obesity** | Massive prepubic fat pad engulfs the shaft; pannus covers the genitalia<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[2]](#ref2)[[3]](#ref3)</sup> |
| **Massive weight loss (post-bariatric)** | Redundant skin apron covers a normal-position penis ("pseudo-BP") — apronectomy often sufficient<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup> |
| **Prior circumcision with cicatricial scarring** | Circumcision scar contracts over the shaft → "trapped penis"; circumcision often **worsens** unrecognized BP<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[2]](#ref2)</sup> |
| **Lichen sclerosus (BXO)** | Chronic inflammation → phimosis → skin contracture; **23%** prevalence in AABP cohorts; LS-positive patients are 2.3× more likely to need stricture surgery<sup>[[14]](#ref14)</sup> |
| **Scrotal lymphedema** | Massive scrotal edema engulfs the shaft<sup>[[3]](#ref3)[[10]](#ref10)</sup> |
| **Hidradenitis suppurativa, diabetes** | Chronic perineal inflammation; tissue-quality and infection risk; hidradenitis and scrotal lymphedema were common in a small plastic-surgery series<sup>[[2]](#ref2)[[10]](#ref10)</sup> |

**The vicious cycle:** in morbid obesity, the prepubic fat pad creates a warm, moist, occluded environment around the retracted penis. Chronic urine exposure produces maceration, dermatitis, and secondary lichen sclerosus — even in previously circumcised men. The result is obesity → buried penis → urine trapping → LS → further skin contracture → worsening concealment.<sup>[[13]](#ref13)[[14]](#ref14)[[15]](#ref15)</sup>

**Weight loss may improve fat-related concealment and operative risk, but does not reliably reverse established scarring or redundant skin.** In a mixed-methods interview study (n = 20), 4 patients reported that weight loss made their condition worse, and the mons pannus and skin changes (LS, scarring) are often irreversible.<sup>[[16]](#ref16)[[17]](#ref17)</sup> In a survey of men after bariatric surgery, those with AABP (11 of 64) reported improved voiding, anatomy and sexual function, but the study was small and cross-sectional.<sup>[[38]](#ref38)</sup>

## Classification of AABP

Several systems are in use, each oriented to a different decision. Maizels (1986) proposed an early classification of penile concealment in children that guided choice of procedure.<sup>[[39]](#ref39)</sup>

### Mirastschijski (2018) — surgical phenotype<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup>

| Type | Phenotype | Operation |
|---|---|---|
| **1 — Pseudo-BP** | Normal-position penis under lax skin (post-bariatric) | Apronectomy + prepubic lift with tissue fixation |
| **2 — Intermediate** | Partial penile invagination | Prepubic apronectomy + anchoring sutures |
| **3 — Classical** | Complete retraction into prepubic fat ± cicatricial circumcision scarring | Scar excision + prepubic fat reduction + shaft extraction + anchoring + skin reconstruction |

### Hesse (2019) — preoperative anatomy<sup>[[11]](#ref11)</sup>

I (skin deficiency / iatrogenic scarring), II (excess abdominal skin and fat), III (combined; most common — 59%), IV (III + severe scrotal edema).

### Pariser–Santucci (2018) — surgical complexity<sup>[[18]](#ref18)</sup>

I (local flap), II (skin graft), III (scrotal surgery), IV (escutcheonectomy), V (panniculectomy). **Categories III–V are "complex" repairs** (~69% of that series); they had higher BMI and higher rates of high-grade complications. Success was defined as an unburied penis without additional unburying surgery (91%; all failures were complex repairs).<sup>[[18]](#ref18)</sup>

### Flynn–Erickson (2022)<sup>[[7]](#ref7)</sup>

Classifies by status of four anatomic components — abdominal pannus, escutcheon, penile skin, scrotal skin — with their respective fascial attachments, using standardized photographs. The resulting penis-abdomen-scrotum (PAS) system had excellent interrater reliability (κ = 0.95) in a 101-patient, 10-center cohort, and its subtypes tracked with the operation performed; outcome correlation has not yet been shown.<sup>[[37]](#ref37)</sup>

## Clinical Presentation

AABP affects multiple functional domains.<sup>[[14]](#ref14)[[16]](#ref16)[[19]](#ref19)</sup>

- **Urinary dysfunction** in ~95% — inability to direct stream, sitting to void, urine pooling in skin folds, chronic wetness and maceration
- **Sexual dysfunction** in ~95% of interviewed patients; 10 of 11 patients in a small surgical series reported significant preoperative ED
- **Recurrent infection** — balanitis, cellulitis, candidal dermatitis from the warm, moist environment
- **Lichen sclerosus** — present on pathology in **23%**
- **Concurrent urethral stricture** — common in referral cohorts; in the 180-patient Daly cohort, **54.8%** with LS versus **23.8%** without LS required stricture intervention
- **Psychological distress** — depression and/or anxiety in **55%**; relationship avoidance, social isolation
- **Care barriers** — 70% report difficulty obtaining treatment

---

## Penile Cancer Association

AABP combines multiple risk factors for penile SCC (chronic inflammation, phimosis, LS, poor hygiene, HPV exposure):<sup>[[14]](#ref14)[[20]](#ref20)[[21]](#ref21)[[22]](#ref22)</sup>

- **Penile SCC was found in 7% in a selected referral cohort**; this is not a population incidence estimate
- **Other potentially relevant skin findings: 35%** in that cohort (including condyloma, LS and carcinoma in situ); these are not all equivalent to histologically confirmed penile intraepithelial neoplasia
- **5%** of patients have penile cancer on final pathology at the time of BP repair
- **Implication:** thorough penile examination (which often requires surgical unburying), biopsy of suspicious tissue, pathologic exam of all excised specimens, and **ongoing surveillance** in patients with adverse skin changes

---

## Diagnostic Evaluation

1. **History** — duration, weight history, prior circumcision, voiding and sexual function, infections, care-access barriers
2. **Physical exam** — confirm normal corporeal length (distinguish from micropenis), assess skin quality, LS changes, escutcheon size, pannus, scrotal pathology
3. **Standardized preoperative photographs** for classification and surgical planning<sup>[[7]](#ref7)</sup>
4. **Urethral evaluation** — given the association with stricture, consider cystoscopy or RUG, particularly with obstructive symptoms or LS<sup>[[14]](#ref14)[[18]](#ref18)</sup>
5. **Skin biopsy** if LS or malignancy suspected
6. **Preoperative optimization assessment** — BMI, glycemic control, smoking, nutrition, frailty index<sup>[[4]](#ref4)[[23]](#ref23)</sup>

## Management

### Conservative measures

EAU guidance recommends weight and other risk-factor optimization plus individualized counseling about surgery.<sup>[[35]](#ref35)</sup>

- **Weight management is part of treatment**; residual redundant skin or fixed scarring may still require reconstruction<sup>[[16]](#ref16)[[17]](#ref17)</sup>
- **Topical steroids** for LS provide symptom relief but do not address the anatomic problem<sup>[[15]](#ref15)</sup>
- **Hygiene optimization** to reduce infection
- **Preoperative weight optimization** is encouraged where feasible. Reported BMI associations below come from a retrospective surgical cohort and are not universal eligibility cutoffs<sup>[[23]](#ref23)</sup>

### Surgical management — AABP

The modern operation is multi-component reconstruction.<sup>[[3]](#ref3)[[4]](#ref4)[[24]](#ref24)[[25]](#ref25)</sup>

**Core surgical steps:**

1. **Penile degloving and unburying** — release of all cicatricial attachments; excision of diseased shaft skin
2. **Escutcheonectomy** — en-bloc excision of the suprapubic fat pad (escutcheon) to prevent re-burying
3. **Panniculectomy** when indicated (~28% of cases) — removal of the abdominal pannus; adding it did not significantly raise 30-day complications versus isolated repair in an NSQIP analysis, although those patients have higher BMI<sup>[[4]](#ref4)[[26]](#ref26)</sup>
4. **Scrotoplasty** when indicated — reduction of redundant or lymphedematous scrotal tissue<sup>[[3]](#ref3)[[10]](#ref10)</sup>
5. **Penile skin reconstruction with split-thickness skin graft (STSG)** to the denuded shaft<sup>[[3]](#ref3)[[25]](#ref25)[[27]](#ref27)</sup>
6. **Anchoring sutures** — fixation of penopubic and penoscrotal skin to deep fascia (Buck's, rectus) to prevent retraction<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[6]](#ref6)</sup>
7. **Concurrent urethral surgery** — meatotomy, urethroplasty, or perineal urethrostomy for stricture disease<sup>[[14]](#ref14)[[18]](#ref18)</sup>

#### Specific technique variants

| Approach | Notes |
|---|---|
| **Escutcheonectomy + STSG (Tang/Santucci, Fuller/Rusilko)**<sup>[[3]](#ref3)[[24]](#ref24)</sup> | En-bloc escutcheon excision; STSG harvested from thigh or **from the excised escutcheon specimen itself** (eliminates donor-site morbidity); bolster for 5–7 days; STSG take 80–100% (mean ~92%) |
| **Outpatient panniculectomy + STSG (Figler)**<sup>[[27]](#ref27)</sup> | Modified trapezoid pannus mobilization; STSG harvested from pannus; discharge on postoperative day 0 or 1 in 19 patients (median age 70), graft take ≥ 95% in all, cellulitis 26%, minor dehiscence 16% |
| **Limited panniculectomy + STSG (Hampson/Voelzke)**<sup>[[28]](#ref28)</sup> | 42 patients; 85% long-term success at mean 39 mo; 85% would have surgery again; 74% positive life change |
| **Penile degloving + ventral anchoring sutures (Alter–Ehrlich)**<sup>[[6]](#ref6)</sup> | For patients with adequate shaft skin (no STSG needed); ventral tacking of penoscrotal subdermis to tunica albuginea prevents proximal telescoping |

### Surgical management — congenital (pediatric) BP

Pediatric repair is generally simpler and more durable than adult repair.<sup>[[6]](#ref6)[[8]](#ref8)[[30]](#ref30)[[31]](#ref31)[[32]](#ref32)[[33]](#ref33)[[34]](#ref34)</sup>

**Principles:**

1. Penile **degloving**
2. **Excision of abnormal dartos / fibrous tethering**
3. **Release of tethering where needed**; the Hadidi series describes ligament division in Grades II–III, not a requirement for every buried-penis repair
4. **Anchoring** — penile-base skin to Buck's fascia or tunica albuginea
5. **Skin coverage** — circumcision with redistribution, Z-plasty, scrotal advancement flap, or dorsal dartos flap
6. **Individualized management of suprapubic adiposity**; the cited Grade III approach should not be generalized to routine lipectomy in children

**Selected pediatric outcomes:**

| Technique | Success | Follow-up |
|---|---|---|
| **Dorsal dartos flap**<sup>[[32]](#ref32)</sup> | 100% (no reoperations) | 96 mo |
| **External phallopexy ("3 stitches")**<sup>[[30]](#ref30)</sup> | 100% (no recurrence) | 11 mo |
| **Modified penoplasty**<sup>[[33]](#ref33)</sup> | 95% (10/201 with retraction) | 6 mo |
| **Midline incision rotation flaps**<sup>[[31]](#ref31)</sup> | 89% good, 11% satisfactory | 56 mo |
| **Simplified Frenkl technique**<sup>[[34]](#ref34)</sup> | 88.5% in the circumcision group (3/26 needed repeat repair); mild recurrence in 14% of the revision-of-circumcision group | 4.4 yr |

## Outcomes — AABP

The following estimates come from heterogeneous surgical referral series with different definitions and follow-up; they are not pooled probabilities for an individual patient.

| Outcome | Result |
|---|---|
| Durable unburying | **~78–91%** in larger series: 91% (Pariser, median 209 d), 1-year recurrence-free survival 88.7% (Falcone) and 91.5% (Plamadeala, 204 patients; 83.7% at 24 months); 78% at mean 436 d (Chestnut). Selected small series report 100%<sup>[[3]](#ref3)[[18]](#ref18)[[23]](#ref23)[[29]](#ref29)[[40]](#ref40)</sup> |
| Recurrence (re-burying) | 5–22% across series, depending on definition and follow-up (22% at mean 436 d in Chestnut)<sup>[[23]](#ref23)[[36]](#ref36)[[40]](#ref40)</sup> |
| Overall complication rate | **27–50%** (30% Chestnut, 32% Falcone, 33% Hampson, 27% Plamadeala, 50% Staniorski)<sup>[[4]](#ref4)[[23]](#ref23)[[28]](#ref28)[[29]](#ref29)[[40]](#ref40)</sup> |
| Wound dehiscence | 16–31%<sup>[[4]](#ref4)[[27]](#ref27)</sup> |
| Wound infection / cellulitis | 26–30% in the two largest surgical series (4.7–33% across a systematic review)<sup>[[4]](#ref4)[[27]](#ref27)[[36]](#ref36)</sup> |
| High-grade complications (Clavien ≥ 3) | 7% overall (Falcone); 23% in Pariser high-complexity repairs<sup>[[18]](#ref18)[[29]](#ref29)</sup> |
| STSG take rate | 80–100%; partial or total graft loss 1.5–21% across a systematic review<sup>[[3]](#ref3)[[27]](#ref27)[[36]](#ref36)</sup> |
| Urinary function improvement | 82–91% (small series, n = 11–20)<sup>[[5]](#ref5)[[12]](#ref12)[[16]](#ref16)[[19]](#ref19)</sup> |
| Sexual function improvement | 41–87.5% (12–16 patients per study)<sup>[[5]](#ref5)[[12]](#ref12)[[16]](#ref16)</sup> |
| Would undergo surgery again | 85–92%<sup>[[12]](#ref12)[[28]](#ref28)</sup> |
| Positive life change | 74–83%<sup>[[12]](#ref12)[[28]](#ref28)</sup> |
| Depression (CES-D) | 7 of 11 preoperatively → 2 of 11 postoperatively<sup>[[19]](#ref19)</sup> |

### Risk factors for complications and recurrence

| Factor | Effect |
|---|---|
| **BMI &gt; 40** | **12.7-fold higher odds of recurrence** (76 patients, wide uncertainty)<sup>[[23]](#ref23)</sup> |
| **BMI &gt; 38** | **6.7-fold higher odds of any complication**<sup>[[23]](#ref23)</sup> |
| Each 1-point BMI increase | +12% recurrence; +11% complication<sup>[[23]](#ref23)</sup> |
| **Frailty (MFI ≥ 2)** | Complication 71% vs 41%; adjusted OR 6.4 (95% CI 1.8–23)<sup>[[4]](#ref4)</sup>; mFI-5 also predicted complications in a separate 55-patient series (adjusted OR 5.3)<sup>[[41]](#ref41)</sup> |
| Complex repair (Pariser III–V) | High-grade complications 23% vs 0% (p = 0.02)<sup>[[18]](#ref18)</sup> |

## Concurrent Urethral Stricture

Urethral assessment is important, particularly with obstructive symptoms or LS. In Daly’s 180-patient surgical cohort, 23% had LS on pathology and stricture intervention was required in 54.8% with LS versus 23.8% without LS; these are selected-cohort estimates, not a universal 47% prevalence. Concurrent meatotomy, urethroplasty, or perineal urethrostomy may be performed at the time of BP repair.<sup>[[14]](#ref14)[[18]](#ref18)</sup>

For the broader operative framework, see [Lichen Sclerosus](/docs/clinical-conditions/03g-genital-scrotal/lichen-sclerosus) and [Urethrocutaneous Fistula](/docs/clinical-conditions/03f-fistulas/in-males/urethrocutaneous).

## Special Considerations

### Circumcision and BP

Circumcision performed on a child or adult with an unrecognized buried penis is a well-known cause of **trapped penis** — the scar contracts over the retracted shaft, worsens concealment, and creates cicatricial phimosis. Circumcision should be avoided or performed only as part of a proper buried-penis repair.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[2]](#ref2)[[8]](#ref8)</sup>

### Multidisciplinary planning

Complex AABP repair often benefits from collaboration between **urology and plastic surgery** for panniculectomy, complex closure, and skin grafting; dermatology input is valuable for LS management.<sup>[[2]](#ref2)[[7]](#ref7)[[25]](#ref25)</sup>

### Perioperative optimization

- **Weight loss** before surgery is encouraged (without indefinite delay — the condition causes ongoing harm)<sup>[[23]](#ref23)</sup>
- **Glycemic control** — wound healing and infection risk
- **Smoking cessation** — graft survival
- **Frailty assessment** — frail patients benefit from prehabilitation<sup>[[4]](#ref4)</sup>
- **VTE prophylaxis** — long operative times (~312 min for complex repairs) and patient comorbidities<sup>[[18]](#ref18)</sup>

## Key Principles

- **Buried penis is not micropenis** — corporeal bodies are normal; the penis is concealed, not small<sup>[[2]](#ref2)[[6]](#ref6)</sup>
- **Weight management improves risk factors**; reconstruction may be needed for persistent functional concealment with scarring or redundant skin<sup>[[16]](#ref16)[[17]](#ref17)</sup>
- The modern AABP operation (escutcheonectomy + diseased-skin excision + STSG ± panniculectomy ± scrotoplasty) reports durable unburying in **~78–91% of larger series**, with recurrence rising with BMI and follow-up<sup>[[18]](#ref18)[[23]](#ref23)[[40]](#ref40)</sup>
- Wound complications are common (27–50% in selected series) but mostly low-grade and manageable<sup>[[4]](#ref4)[[12]](#ref12)</sup>
- **BMI is an important reported predictor** of both recurrence and complications<sup>[[23]](#ref23)</sup>
- **Screen for penile cancer** — selected cohorts contain occult SCC and other skin disease; biopsy suspicious tissue<sup>[[14]](#ref14)[[20]](#ref20)</sup>
- **Screen for urethral stricture** — evaluate according to symptoms, examination and LS; concurrent management may be needed<sup>[[14]](#ref14)[[18]](#ref18)</sup>
- **Lichen sclerosus** was present on pathology in 23% of the Daly surgical cohort and is a major driver of stricture disease<sup>[[14]](#ref14)</sup>
- The psychological burden is substantial and improved after surgery in small series<sup>[[16]](#ref16)[[19]](#ref19)</sup>
- **Congenital BP** is a distinct entity (abnormal dartos and fascial attachments) with simpler, durable anchoring repairs<sup>[[8]](#ref8)[[9]](#ref9)[[32]](#ref32)</sup>
