# Clitoral Phimosis and Clitoral Adhesions

**Clitoral phimosis** is the inability to retract the clitoral prepuce (clitoral hood) to expose the entire glans clitoris. It is a distinct entity from clitoral adhesions, in which the prepuce is directly tethered to the glans by epithelial bridging. Either may be asymptomatic or associated with trapped keratin / smegma, periclitoral pseudocysts or pain. Sexual symptoms require a broader assessment; phimosis alone does not establish the cause of impaired arousal or orgasm. Scarring may be an architectural sequela of **vulvar lichen sclerosus (LS)**.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[2]](#ref2)[[3]](#ref3)</sup> 

For the underlying dermatologic disease, see [Lichen Sclerosus](/docs/clinical-conditions/03g-genital-scrotal/lichen-sclerosus). For aesthetic clitoral-hood reduction in the absence of pathologic phimosis, see [Inverted-Y Clitoral Hoodoplasty](/docs/surgical-techniques/04l-cosmetic-genital-surgery/inverted-y-clitoral-hoodoplasty) and [Liu Classification Hoodoplasty](/docs/surgical-techniques/04l-cosmetic-genital-surgery/liu-classification-hoodoplasty); those are cosmetic procedures and are not interchangeable with phimosis release. 

---

## Definition and Classification

A 2025 international Delphi consensus (Krapf et al.) standardized terminology:<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup> 

- **Clitoral phimosis.** Inability to retract the prepuce to expose the entire glans clitoris. 
- **Clitoral adhesions.** Direct tethering of the prepuce to the glans clitoris. 
- The degree of phimosis should be classified as mild, moderate, or severe, and described as partially retractable or completely non-retractable. 
- Both prepuce characteristics and glans size should be documented.

The two entities frequently coexist (phimotic prepuce with bridging adhesions to the glans beneath) and treatment depends on symptoms and underlying disease. Selected symptomatic adhesions may be lysed in the office; persistent symptomatic scar-related phimosis may warrant specialist surgical release.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[4]](#ref4)</sup>

## Prevalence

| Population | Rate |
|---|---|
| 3,650 sexually active heterosexual women aged 20–45 (Czech cohort) | **1.3%** (46 women) had some form of clitoral phimosis; severe in 9<sup>[[5]](#ref5)</sup> |
| Sexual-dysfunction clinic referrals | **22%** in the cited referral cohort; not a population prevalence<sup>[[2]](#ref2)</sup> |
| Sexual medicine practice, retrospective vulvoscopy review (614 evaluable patients) | **23%** had clitoral adhesions (mild 44%, moderate 34%, severe 22%); 14% of the adhesion group presented with clitorodynia; a specialty-clinic cohort, not a population prevalence<sup>[[20]](#ref20)</sup> |

## Etiology

| Cause | Notes |
|---|---|
| **Lichen sclerosus (LS)** | An important scarring cause — chronic inflammation, epithelial thinning, and progressive scarring fuse the labia minora, phimose the clitoral hood, and create the classic "figure of eight" architectural distortion of the vulva.<sup>[[3]](#ref3)[[6]](#ref6)</sup> |
| **Lichen planus (LP)** | Similar scarring mechanism with mucosal involvement<sup>[[7]](#ref7)</sup> |
| **Recurrent vulvar dermal infections / blunt trauma** | Proposed by the authors of the referral-clinic study to alter preputial elasticity; not demonstrated<sup>[[2]](#ref2)</sup> |
| **Other associated factors** | A retrospective vulvoscopy cohort found clitoral adhesions associated with a history of sexual pain, yeast infection, urinary tract infection, blunt perineal or genital trauma, lichen sclerosus, low calculated free testosterone, and other sexual dysfunctions including persistent genital arousal disorder; an association, not established causation<sup>[[20]](#ref20)</sup> |
| **Congenital fusion** | Rare; can present as a periclitoral mass from trapped sebaceous material<sup>[[8]](#ref8)</sup> |
| **Anatomic predisposition** | The glandopreputial sulcus of the clitoris is often **incompletely formed** (unlike the penile sulcus), predisposing to phimosis, cyst formation, and pilonidal sinus<sup>[[9]](#ref9)</sup> |

## Clinical Presentation

- **Sexual dysfunction.** Decreased clitoral sensation, impaired arousal, anorgasmia, and dyspareunia. Most consistent in severe LS-related phimosis.<sup>[[5]](#ref5)[[10]](#ref10)</sup> A retrospective vulvoscopy cohort found clitorodynia in 14% of women with clitoral adhesions.<sup>[[20]](#ref20)</sup>
- **Sexual pain.** The only symptom the referral-clinic study linked to phimosis; it found no correlation with desire, arousal or orgasm disorders.<sup>[[2]](#ref2)</sup> 
- **Smegma or keratin pearl accumulation** beneath the fused prepuce: palpable mass that can mimic vulvar malignancy.<sup>[[8]](#ref8)[[11]](#ref11)</sup> 
- **Pseudocyst formation** from trapped eccrine secretions.<sup>[[9]](#ref9)</sup>
- **Pediatric or adolescent LS.** Atrophic change can include clitoral phimosis, labial resorption, or adhesions; in a 15-patient series, separate adolescents required surgery for urinary retention and for dyspareunia caused by clitoral entrapment.<sup>[[12]](#ref12)</sup>

---

## Diagnosis

- **Exam-based.** Cephalad displacement of the labia minora to retract the prepuce. Inability to fully expose the glans confirms the diagnosis.<sup>[[2]](#ref2)</sup>
- **Document phimosis versus adhesion.** Phimosis is preputial-aperture stenosis; adhesions are epithelial bridges that may be lysed at the bedside.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup> 
- **Biopsy** when the diagnosis is uncertain, treatment fails or a lesion is suspicious for neoplasia; persistent focal hyperkeratosis, ulceration or a mass warrants assessment. LS-associated cancer risk supports surveillance, not routine biopsy of every clitoral adhesion.<sup>[[6]](#ref6)</sup>
- The clitoris should be routinely examined in any patient presenting with sexual dysfunction, vulvar symptoms, or known vulvar dermatoses.<sup>[[4]](#ref4)</sup>

## Treatment Ladder

Treatment is guided by severity, symptom burden, and underlying etiology; asymptomatic adhesions do not automatically require intervention. For LS-related vulvar surgery, a 2026 German S3 guideline strongly recommends agreement on topical treatment before and after surgery and interdisciplinary counselling that includes pelvic-floor physiotherapy and sex therapy; it suggests against surgery in girls except selected functional impairment. Its positive recommendation for adult vulvar surgery addresses persistent introital stenosis after guideline-conform steroid treatment, not clitoral phimosis specifically.<sup>[[22]](#ref22)</sup> BAD guidance supports treatment of inflammation and selective surgery for troublesome scarring.<sup>[[14]](#ref14)</sup> Most patients require ongoing dermatologic maintenance even after a successful procedure because the underlying LS / LP is chronic.<sup>[[7]](#ref7)[[10]](#ref10)</sup>

### 1. Medical (first-line for the underlying dermatosis)

- **High-potency topical corticosteroids.** An individualized induction and maintenance regimen (see the linked LS page). Prevents progression but does not reverse established architectural changes.<sup>[[3]](#ref3)[[6]](#ref6)[[12]](#ref12)</sup> 
- **Maintenance for LS or LP.** Continue individualized anti-inflammatory treatment after release when an inflammatory dermatosis is present; routine steroid use solely to prevent readhesion without LS or LP has not been established.<sup>[[7]](#ref7)[[3]](#ref3)[[4]](#ref4)</sup>
- **Topical testosterone is not a substitute.** In the Cochrane review of genital LS, four trials found no significant benefit from topical testosterone, dihydrotestosterone or progesterone, and testosterone used as maintenance after clobetasol worsened symptoms; a retrospective series found clobetasol superior to 2% testosterone for remission (91.7% versus 77.5%) and recurrence (6.7% versus 20%).<sup>[[17]](#ref17)[[19]](#ref19)</sup> 

### 2. Office lysis of symptomatic adhesions

For selected symptomatic clitoral adhesions, after consent and adequate analgesia, office-based lysis with fine Jacobsen mosquito forceps to separate prepuce from glans, with removal of trapped smegma and keratin pearls. In the Myers 2022 retrospective series, 41 of 61 eligible patients responded (67%); the following are respondent-reported outcomes without a control group or validated pre-post instrument:<sup>[[4]](#ref4)</sup> 

- **76% (19/25 pain-item respondents)** reported pain improvement
- **63% (25/40)** reported improved arousal
- **64% (25/39)** reported improved orgasmic function
- **93%** would recommend the procedure

A later pre-post study of office lysis with keratin-pearl excision (32 of 74 eligible patients responded) found mean clitoral pain falling from 6.9 to 2.5 on an 11-point scale and FSFI rising from 12.1 to 17.7; 28% recurred, with a median of 2 repeat procedures.<sup>[[15]](#ref15)</sup> A 2026 meta-analysis of these interventions was retracted after an error in the Krapf sample size propagated through its analyses, so no valid pooled estimates are available.<sup>[[16]](#ref16)</sup> Counsel that recurrence is common and that repeat lysis is often needed.

Fixed scar-related preputial narrowing needs a separate specialist assessment; neither finding by itself mandates a procedure.

### 3. Surgical management (persistent symptomatic phimosis)

#### Clitoral circumcision / prepucectomy

Excision of the phimotic preputial ring to expose the glans clitoris. Small uncontrolled reports include Goldstein / Burrows (8 women with LS; 88% very satisfied, and all 4 with reduced preoperative clitoral sensation regained sensation and orgasm)<sup>[[10]](#ref10)</sup> and the Chmel study (9 women with severe phimosis, 12-month follow-up); these do not establish comparative superiority:<sup>[[5]](#ref5)</sup>

- FSFI improved from 17.9 to 26.6 at 12 months (p < 0.001) 
- Significant improvement in arousal, orgasm, and dyspareunia domains
- Concurrent perineoplasty may be required when introital stenosis coexists (2 of 9).<sup>[[5]](#ref5)</sup> 

In a 2026 cohort of mixed LS surgery for introital stenosis or clitoral phimosis, 86 of 148 identified patients responded and 71 had paired FSFI scores rising from 15.0 to 22.7. Among the 86 respondents, 80 (93%) were satisfied, 29 (33.7%) reported recurrent scarring, and 9 (10.5%) underwent revision a mean 3.9 years after initial surgery; the mean surgery-to-survey interval was 4.35 years. Clitoral-only outcomes were not reported. It had no control group.<sup>[[18]](#ref18)</sup>

For severe fixed phimosis, one described technique examines the opening under magnification and, when an opening is present, uses a lacrimal probe to identify and orient the clitoris beneath the fused hood. A skin incision is placed lateral and parallel to the clitoral body, stopping at Colles' fascia, and sharp lateral-to-medial dissection separates the body from the scar and mobilizes it toward the glans; the fused hood and frenulum are removed while the clitoral body is preserved, the vascular frenular junction is secured, and the subcutaneous layer is closed over the repositioned clitoris. This sequence has been described by Baggish; no comparative sensory, pain or sexual-function outcomes are reported for it.<sup>[[21]](#ref21)</sup>

#### CO₂ laser preputial release

Used for clitoral phimosis secondary to LS or LP with individualized pre- and postoperative topical therapy. Kroft and Shier series of 23 women:<sup>[[7]](#ref7)</sup> 

- Mild reagglutination occurred in 5 of 20 LS patients. 
- 5 of 23 patients required reoperation (3 with LS, 2 with LP).
- Overall satisfaction was high.

#### Surgicel application after lysis

In a three-adolescent LS case report, oxidized regenerated cellulose (Surgicel) was sutured to the exposed clitoral-hood and labial surfaces after sharp lysis; none had recurrence at one year, without a comparator to show that the material prevented it.<sup>[[11]](#ref11)</sup>

## Postoperative Considerations

- **Reagglutination is a reported failure mode.** Mild reagglutination occurred in 5 of 20 women with LS after CO₂ laser release; recurrent adhesions were reported by 19 of 41 (46%) after office lysis in Myers and by 28% of 32 respondents after office lysis with keratin-pearl excision. The 33.7% recurrent-scarring rate after mixed LS-related vulvar surgery is not a clitoral-specific rate.<sup>[[7]](#ref7)[[4]](#ref4)[[15]](#ref15)[[18]](#ref18)</sup>
- **Continued topical treatment for LS or LP** is part of postoperative care for the underlying dermatosis.<sup>[[10]](#ref10)[[7]](#ref7)</sup> In one mixed vulvar-adhesion surgery cohort, patients applied clobetasol 0.05% to surgical sites daily until healing and then twice weekly; they were instructed to retract the clitoral prepuce and introital surgical sites four to six times daily during healing. This is a reported regimen, not a proven universal recurrence-prevention schedule.<sup>[[18]](#ref18)</sup>
- **Counsel about perioperative harms.** In a mixed LS-surgery cohort, reported events included anesthesia or positioning problems, urinary symptoms or infection, and bleeding requiring a suture; these are not clitoral-only complication rates.<sup>[[18]](#ref18)</sup>
- **Periodic surveillance examination** for both reagglutination and the lifelong vulvar SCC risk in patients with LS.<sup>[[6]](#ref6)</sup>

## Key Principles

- Clitoral phimosis is distinct from clitoral adhesions; the Krapf 2025 Delphi consensus standardized this distinction.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup> 
- **LS is an important cause**; treat the underlying dermatosis with maintenance topical steroids regardless of whether a procedure is offered.<sup>[[3]](#ref3)[[6]](#ref6)</sup>
- Symptomatic adhesions may be treated with office lysis; persistent symptomatic scar-related phimosis may require surgical release after control of inflammation.<sup>[[4]](#ref4)[[1]](#ref1)</sup> 
- Aesthetic clitoral hoodoplasty is not interchangeable with phimosis release (different anatomy, different goals, different outcome instruments).<sup>[[13]](#ref13)</sup> 
- **Reagglutination** occurs after laser release and office lysis, with reported recurrence varying across cohorts and procedures. Continue disease-directed anti-inflammatory treatment when LS or LP is present.<sup>[[7]](#ref7)[[4]](#ref4)[[15]](#ref15)</sup>
- **Biopsy** suspicious lesions in any LS patient; continue surveillance for LS-associated vulvar SCC.<sup>[[6]](#ref6)</sup>
- **Pediatric LS demands early diagnosis.** About half (7 of 15) of children in one series presented with already irreversible atrophic changes from diagnostic delay (median 7 months).<sup>[[12]](#ref12)</sup> 
- **Offer a consented clitoral examination** when assessing relevant sexual symptoms or vulvar disease. Referral-cohort prevalence does not prove that every sexual symptom arises from phimosis.<sup>[[5]](#ref5)[[2]](#ref2)</sup>
