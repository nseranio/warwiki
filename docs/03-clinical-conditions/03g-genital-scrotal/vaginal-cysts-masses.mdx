# Vaginal Cysts and Masses

**Vaginal cysts and masses** span benign cystic, benign solid, premalignant, and malignant lesions of the vaginal wall and paravaginal tissues. The vast majority are benign and asymptomatic, with anterior-vaginal-wall cysts reported in roughly 0.5–1% of women. The most common cystic lesions are Müllerian, epidermal inclusion and Gartner duct cysts; their proportions vary by sampled population. In a 126-patient referral or treatment series, urethral diverticula accounted for 39.7% and Skene cysts or abscesses for 30.2% of periurethral or anterior-wall masses.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[2]](#ref2)[[3]](#ref3)[[4]](#ref4)[[5]](#ref5)</sup> Special attention belongs to masses in women over 40 or those that are fixed, painful, or bleeding; malignancy is rare but must be excluded.

For periurethral cystic masses and urethral meatal lesions, see also [Female Urethral & Periurethral Masses](/docs/clinical-conditions/03b-voiding-outlet/female-urethral-masses) and [Urethral Diverticulum](/docs/clinical-conditions/03b-voiding-outlet/urethral-diverticula). For lichen-sclerosus-related vulvovaginal lesions, see [Lichen Sclerosus](/docs/clinical-conditions/03g-genital-scrotal/lichen-sclerosus). For the operative management of the most common anterior-wall mass, see [Urethral Diverticulum Repair](/docs/surgical-techniques/04a-urethral-reconstruction/urethral-diverticulum-repair); for excision-defect coverage, see [Vulvar Reconstruction](/docs/surgical-techniques/04e-genital-reconstruction/vulvar).

## Organizational Framework

| Category | Lesions |
|---|---|
| **Embryologic cysts** | Müllerian, Gartner duct (mesonephric), Skene gland (paraurethral) |
| **Acquired cysts** | Epidermal inclusion, endometriotic, Bartholin duct |
| **Urologic** | Urethral diverticulum, ectopic ureterocele, ectopic ureter |
| **Benign solid tumors** | Leiomyoma, fibroepithelial stromal polyp, angiomyofibroblastoma, cellular angiofibroma, superficial myofibroblastoma |
| **Locally aggressive** | Aggressive angiomyxoma |
| **Premalignant** | Vaginal intraepithelial neoplasia (VAIN / vaginal HSIL) |
| **Malignant** | Squamous cell carcinoma (85–90%), adenocarcinoma, melanoma, sarcoma, metastatic |
| **Iatrogenic / other** | Mesh-related, pseudocysts, abscesses |

## Part I — Benign Cystic Lesions

### Müllerian cysts

- **Most common type in the largest mucin-typed pathology series** (44% of 43 cysts in Pradhan 1986; about one-third in Deppisch 1975, where inclusion cysts were the most frequent type). Rankings vary with the sampled population<sup>[[3]](#ref3)[[4]](#ref4)</sup>
- **Embryology.** Paramesonephric (Müllerian) duct remnants; lined by mucus-secreting endocervical-type or ciliated tubal-type epithelium<sup>[[3]](#ref3)[[4]](#ref4)</sup>
- **Location.** Typically anterolateral vaginal wall
- **Presentation.** Often larger and more symptomatic than other vaginal cysts; pelvic pressure, dyspareunia, palpable mass
- **Diagnosis.** Examination and MRI for anatomy; histology shows mucicarmine-positive columnar lining<sup>[[2]](#ref2)[[3]](#ref3)[[15]](#ref15)</sup>
- **Management.** Observation if asymptomatic; excision for symptomatic cysts<sup>[[2]](#ref2)[[15]](#ref15)</sup>

### Gartner duct (mesonephric) cysts

- **Embryology.** Mesonephric (Wolffian) duct remnants; non-mucus-secreting cuboidal or low columnar epithelium<sup>[[3]](#ref3)</sup>
- **Prevalence.** 5–11% of vaginal cysts (most lesions historically called Gartner duct cysts have mucin and are actually Müllerian)<sup>[[3]](#ref3)[[4]](#ref4)</sup>
- **Location.** Anterolateral vaginal wall along the mesonephric remnant<sup>[[16]](#ref16)[[17]](#ref17)</sup>
- **Presentation in a surgical series.** 38% were asymptomatic; symptoms included dyspareunia (38%), pelvic pain or pressure (24%), palpable bulge (17%), urinary symptoms (7%); mean size 3.5 cm<sup>[[18]](#ref18)</sup>
- **Diagnostic pitfall:** large Gartner duct cysts can mimic anterior compartment prolapse (cystocele); consider the diagnosis when prolapse compartments don't add up<sup>[[5]](#ref5)[[16]](#ref16)</sup>
- **Associated GU anomalies** in about 10% of a 29-patient surgical series and 19 of 92 (21%) surgically managed patients in a literature review: ipsilateral renal agenesis, uterine didelphys, septate vagina, urethral diverticulum, ectopic ureter; reflects shared mesonephric origin<sup>[[9]](#ref9)[[17]](#ref17)[[18]](#ref18)</sup>
- **Renal agenesis, HWW and OHVIRA link.** A paravaginal cystic mass with an absent or dysplastic ipsilateral kidney may be a Gartner duct cyst, an ectopic ureter or blind hemibladder, or an obstructed hemivagina (Herlyn-Werner-Wunderlich syndrome: uterus didelphys, obstructed hemivaginal septum, ipsilateral renal agenesis), and the two can coexist. In 26 girls with renal agenesis or dysplasia and pelvic cystic lesions, 9 had Gartner duct cysts, 3 HWW and 3 both. Image the uterus, vagina and kidneys (MRI or 3D ultrasound), especially for cyclic pain or continuous wetting at puberty; excising a septum relieves an obstructed hemivagina. Evidence is case reports and small series<sup>[[49]](#ref49)[[50]](#ref50)[[51]](#ref51)</sup>
- **Workup.** Exam and transvaginal US (most common); MRI for complex or large lesions or to exclude urologic involvement<sup>[[16]](#ref16)[[18]](#ref18)</sup>

**Management options:** Published counts are from small, heterogeneous series and do not establish a best technique.<sup>[[17]](#ref17)[[18]](#ref18)</sup>

| Approach | Notes |
|---|---|
| **Observation** | Asymptomatic |
| **Excision** | Most commonly reported; 4/50 recurred in a review |
| **Marsupialization** | Alternative; 0/14 recurred in that review |
| **Tetracycline sclerotherapy after aspiration** | 1/15 recurred in that review |
| **Unroofing / partial excision** | 0/9 recurred in that review |
| **Fluorescein dye-assisted excision** | Single-case adjunct to delineate walls and assess communication; not a definitive exclusion test<sup>[[17]](#ref17)</sup> |

In the 29-patient series (excision in 26, marsupialization in 3; median follow-up 82 mo), 1 possible recurrence (3.4%) and no intraoperative complications. Two of 11 asymptomatic patients were operated on for interval growth.<sup>[[18]](#ref18)</sup>

### Epidermal inclusion (squamous inclusion) cysts

- **Second most common** in Pradhan (23%); the most frequent type in Deppisch, usually clinically insignificant<sup>[[3]](#ref3)[[4]](#ref4)</sup>
- **Etiology.** Acquired; squamous epithelium implanted at episiotomy, vaginal laceration, or prior surgery<sup>[[3]](#ref3)[[4]](#ref4)</sup>
- **Location.** Posterior or lateral vaginal wall, often at episiotomy scars
- **Histology.** Keratinizing stratified squamous epithelium with keratinous debris
- **Management.** Observation; simple excision if symptomatic; usually benign; atypical or changing lesions warrant reassessment<sup>[[2]](#ref2)[[3]](#ref3)</sup>

### Bartholin duct cysts and abscesses

- **Anatomy.** Bartholin glands at posterolateral introitus (4 and 8 o'clock)<sup>[[19]](#ref19)</sup>
- **Lifetime risk.** About 2% of women develop a cyst or abscess<sup>[[19]](#ref19)</sup>
- **Pathophysiology.** Ductal obstruction leads to cyst; secondary infection leads to abscess

**Management.** No single technique is proven superior. In a 161-woman RCT, 1-year recurrence was 12% with Word catheter versus 10% with marsupialization (RR 1.1, 95% CI 0.64–1.91); a 2024 meta-analysis of 735 patients (2 RCTs, 2 observational) found about 7.6% versus 9.4% (OR 0.99, wide CI). The catheter was placed faster and needed less analgesia. Trials of marsupialization versus silver nitrate or cavity closure were too small to conclude.<sup>[[19]](#ref19)[[20]](#ref20)[[52]](#ref52)[[53]](#ref53)</sup>

| Technique | Notes |
|---|---|
| **Word catheter** | Office-based; 4–6 weeks for fistulization; recurrence about 8–12% in the trial and meta-analysis above |
| **Marsupialization** | Permanent drainage tract; recurrence about 9–10% in the same data; more analgesia and OR time |
| **Silver nitrate sclerotherapy** | Alternative fistulization |
| **I&D / aspiration alone** | Higher recurrence — not first-line |
| **Gland excision** | Selected recurrent disease; a new Bartholin-region mass at age ≥40 warrants tissue assessment to exclude carcinoma, not automatic gland excision<sup>[[47]](#ref47)</sup><sup>[[2]](#ref2)[[19]](#ref19)</sup> |

### Skene gland (paraurethral) cysts

- **Embryology.** Skene glands are homologs of the male prostate; lined by transitional epithelium<sup>[[21]](#ref21)[[22]](#ref22)[[23]](#ref23)</sup>
- **Location.** Periurethral, adjacent to the meatus<sup>[[22]](#ref22)[[24]](#ref24)[[25]](#ref25)</sup>
- **Prevalence.** about 30% of periurethral or anterior-wall masses<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup>
- **Presentation.** Dyspareunia (40%), recurrent UTI (40%), vaginal mass, voiding dysfunction, urinary spraying<sup>[[23]](#ref23)[[25]](#ref25)</sup>
- **Diagnostic pitfall.** Frequently mislabeled as Bartholin cyst; the discriminator is location (periurethral versus posterolateral introitus). MRI is useful when examination or ultrasound cannot establish anatomy or before complex surgery.<sup>[[24]](#ref24)[[25]](#ref25)</sup>
- **Management.** Observe asymptomatic benign-appearing lesions; excision or marsupialization for symptoms, with urethral evaluation when indicated. No recurrences occurred in one 10-patient excision series at mean 3.5 yr<sup>[[25]](#ref25)</sup>

### Endometriotic cysts

- about 7% of vaginal cysts<sup>[[4]](#ref4)</sup>
- **Location.** Posterior fornix (most common vaginal endometriosis site) or episiotomy scar<sup>[[8]](#ref8)[[4]](#ref4)</sup>
- **Presentation.** Cyclical pain, dyspareunia, dysmenorrhea, tender nodule that enlarges with menses
- **Diagnosis.** Exam, transvaginal US, MRI for deep infiltrating disease<sup>[[8]](#ref8)</sup>
- **Management.** Excision for symptomatic lesions; hormonal therapy as adjunct

### Ectopic ureter / megaureter presenting as a "vaginal cyst"

A rare but important pitfall: an ectopic ureter or congenital megaureter can present as a vaginal-wall cyst with continuous incontinence. MRI is needed. Definitive management may require nephroureterectomy with marsupialization of the distal ureter.<sup>[[9]](#ref9)</sup>

## Part II — Benign Solid Vaginal Masses

Benign perivaginal masses account for about 1.6% of urogynecologic surgeries.<sup>[[6]](#ref6)</sup>

### Vaginal leiomyoma

- Classically the most frequent benign solid vaginal tumor, though uncommon in practice; smooth muscle origin<sup>[[6]](#ref6)[[8]](#ref8)</sup>
- Painless mass, dyspareunia, urinary symptoms; well-circumscribed, homogeneous on MRI
- Excision for symptomatic lesions

### Fibroepithelial stromal polyp

- Benign polypoid lesion of vaginal or vulvar stroma; pedunculated or sessile<sup>[[10]](#ref10)[[11]](#ref11)</sup>
- Can be large and edematous, mimicking aggressive angiomyxoma; important diagnostic pitfall<sup>[[26]](#ref26)</sup>
- Simple excision is curative

### Angiomyofibroblastoma (AMFB)

- **Benign** mesenchymal tumor of vulvovaginal region; clinically often mistaken for Bartholin cyst<sup>[[27]](#ref27)</sup>
- Well-circumscribed; alternating hypercellular / hypocellular zones with abundant capillary-type vessels; vimentin+ diffusely; desmin and smooth-muscle actin positive in only a minority in some series (negative for actin in the original description)<sup>[[27]](#ref27)[[28]](#ref28)</sup>
- **Low recurrence after complete excision.** Distinguish from infiltrative aggressive angiomyxoma<sup>[[27]](#ref27)[[29]](#ref29)</sup>

### Aggressive angiomyxoma (AAM)

- **Locally aggressive** mesenchymal neoplasm of pelvis or perineum in reproductive-age women (mean about 38 yr)<sup>[[12]](#ref12)[[30]](#ref30)[[31]](#ref31)</sup>
- **Infiltrative** without a true capsule (distinguishes it from AMFB)<sup>[[12]](#ref12)[[27]](#ref27)[[29]](#ref29)</sup>
- ER+, PR+, desmin+, vimentin+, variable SMA<sup>[[30]](#ref30)[[31]](#ref31)</sup>
- **Recurrence 30–40%**, sometimes years to decades after excision<sup>[[12]](#ref12)</sup>
- Metastasis is exceptionally rare; local recurrence drives management<sup>[[12]](#ref12)[[29]](#ref29)</sup>
- **Management:** wide surgical excision (often difficult given infiltrative nature) plus GnRH agonists (or SERMs or aromatase inhibitors) as adjuvant hormonal therapy given ER/PR positivity; long-term surveillance is mandatory<sup>[[12]](#ref12)[[30]](#ref30)[[31]](#ref31)</sup>

### Other rare benign solid tumors

Cellular angiofibroma, superficial (cervicovaginal) myofibroblastoma, mammary-type myofibroblastoma: the genital stromal-tumor family; all benign with low recurrence after excision.<sup>[[10]](#ref10)[[11]](#ref11)</sup> Vaginal angioma and solitary fibrous tumor are rare and curable by excision.<sup>[[6]](#ref6)</sup>

## Part III — Vaginal Intraepithelial Neoplasia (VAIN)

### Definition and epidemiology

- VAIN is the HPV-related premalignant condition of vaginal squamous epithelium, analogous to CIN and VIN<sup>[[13]](#ref13)[[32]](#ref32)[[14]](#ref14)</sup>
- **Rare.** &lt; 1% of female genital intraepithelial neoplasias<sup>[[32]](#ref32)</sup>
- **Terminology:** VAIN 1 (LSIL) versus VAIN 2/3 (HSIL)<sup>[[14]](#ref14)</sup>
- **Progression to invasive carcinoma** about 5% for HSIL (8% in some long-term series)<sup>[[14]](#ref14)[[33]](#ref33)</sup>
- **Mean age** at diagnosis about 55 years<sup>[[34]](#ref34)</sup>

### Risk factors

HPV (especially HPV 16 and 31), prior CIN or cervical cancer, prior hysterectomy (especially for CIN or cancer; 20–55% of VAIN patients have had prior hysterectomy), immunosuppression (HIV), prior pelvic radiation, smoking.<sup>[[13]](#ref13)[[14]](#ref14)[[33]](#ref33)[[34]](#ref34)[[35]](#ref35)</sup>

**Location:** &gt; 80% involve the vaginal vault (post-hysterectomy) or upper vagina.<sup>[[33]](#ref33)</sup>

### Management<sup>[[13]](#ref13)[[32]](#ref32)[[33]](#ref33)[[36]](#ref36)[[37]](#ref37)[[38]](#ref38)[[39]](#ref39)</sup>

| Grade / option | Notes |
|---|---|
| **VAIN 1 (LSIL)** | **Observation preferred** — spontaneous regression up to 81% in pooled expectant-management data; 7 of 8 (88%) in Rome's small series |
| **VAIN 2/3 (HSIL)** | Active treatment indicated |
| **CO₂ laser ablation** | Cure 69–90%; recurrence 6–23%; in a 116-woman high-grade series regression fell with repeated treatments (75% → 53% → 27% after the first, second and third-or-later laser), so consider another modality after two failures; rule out invasion before ablation. Less suitable for vault disease after hysterectomy for CIN 3, where epithelium can be buried in the vaginal scar<sup>[[48]](#ref48)</sup> |
| **Surgical excision (local; upper vaginectomy)** | Mainstay if invasion cannot be excluded; cure ~69%; margins do not reliably predict residual disease |
| **Topical imiquimod 5%** | Topical agents overall (imiquimod, 5-FU) cure 25–98% across series; imiquimod had the lowest recurrence and highest HPV clearance in the ESGO/ISSVD/ECSVD/EFC consensus<sup>[[48]](#ref48)</sup>; in the only RCT (n = 30 pilot, 16 weeks) HPV clearance 63% vs 11% laser and 17% observation, and histologic regression 80% vs 100% laser vs 67% observation; useful for multifocal disease |
| **Topical 5-fluorouracil** | Historical option; ESGO/ISSVD/ECSVD/EFC consensus discourages routine use<sup>[[48]](#ref48)</sup> |
| **Brachytherapy** | Cure 71–90%; refractory/multifocal disease or poor surgical candidates; reserved due to toxicity |

**Recurrence and progression:** about 22% of high-grade VAIN recurs after treatment (independent risk factors in one 323-woman series: prior hysterectomy and excisional treatment; VAIN 3 was significant only on univariate analysis); about 1.5% progress to invasive carcinoma at median 87 months; long-term surveillance is mandatory.<sup>[[34]](#ref34)[[39]](#ref39)</sup>

## Part IV — Primary Vaginal Cancer

### Overview

- **Extremely rare** (about 1–2% of gynecologic malignancies)<sup>[[40]](#ref40)[[41]](#ref41)[[42]](#ref42)[[43]](#ref43)[[14]](#ref14)</sup>
- **Histology.** Squamous cell carcinoma 85–90%; adenocarcinoma; melanoma; sarcoma<sup>[[41]](#ref41)[[14]](#ref14)</sup>
- **HPV-associated** (predominantly HPV 16) in most squamous cell carcinomas<sup>[[14]](#ref14)</sup>
- **Mean age** sixth decade; rising incidence in younger women with high HIV prevalence<sup>[[41]](#ref41)[[42]](#ref42)</sup>
- **Presentation.** Vaginal bleeding (most common), discharge, palpable mass, dyspareunia, urinary symptoms<sup>[[42]](#ref42)[[44]](#ref44)</sup>

### Treatment (NCCN Guidelines)<sup>[[14]](#ref14)[[40]](#ref40)</sup>

FIGO (2009) staging is clinical: I confined to the vaginal wall; II into subvaginal tissue, not the pelvic wall; III to the pelvic wall; IVA bladder or rectal mucosa or beyond the true pelvis; IVB distant metastasis.<sup>[[42]](#ref42)</sup> MRI is the preferred imaging for local extent and PET/CT for nodal and distant disease.<sup>[[43]](#ref43)</sup>

| Stage | Approach |
|---|---|
| **I** | Definitive radiation is the usual organ-sparing approach — EBRT + brachytherapy ± concurrent platinum-based chemotherapy; surgical resection only when complete excision with negative margins is feasible without excessive morbidity |
| **II–IVA** | Concurrent chemoradiation (platinum-based) + brachytherapy |
| **Surgery** | Reserved for cases where complete resection with clear margins avoids the need for adjuvant RT; definitive surgical management is uncommon |

**Brachytherapy** is a component of definitive radiation; retrospective associations support its use, without proving the size of a causal survival benefit.<sup>[[44]](#ref44)</sup>

**Outcome denominators matter:** In the 124-patient Mayo retrospective series, 5-year disease-specific survival was 84.3%, 73.7%, 78.7% and 26.5% for stages I–IV; the corresponding progression-free survival was 58.7%, 59.4%, 67.3% and 31.8%. These are separate endpoints, not survival ranges, and small stage groups limit generalization. Older radiation cohorts reflect different treatment eras.<sup>[[44]](#ref44)[[45]](#ref45)</sup>

### Other rare vaginal malignancies

HPV-associated adenocarcinoma, clear cell carcinoma (historically DES-associated), endometrioid carcinoma, mesonephric adenocarcinoma, carcinosarcoma, neuroendocrine carcinoma, adenocarcinoma of Skene-gland origin, adenosarcoma, germ cell tumors.<sup>[[14]](#ref14)</sup>

## Part V — Diagnostic Approach

The single most useful diagnostic clue is anatomic location.<sup>[[2]](#ref2)[[6]](#ref6)[[7]](#ref7)[[8]](#ref8)[[22]](#ref22)</sup>

| Location | Top differentials |
|---|---|
| **Anterolateral wall** | Müllerian cyst, Gartner duct cyst |
| **Periurethral / suburethral** | Urethral diverticulum, Skene gland cyst |
| **Posterolateral introitus (4–8 o'clock)** | Bartholin duct cyst |
| **Posterior fornix** | Endometriotic cyst |
| **Episiotomy scar** | Epidermal inclusion cyst |

**Workup:**

1. **History.** Duration, mass sensation, dyspareunia, discharge, voiding dysfunction, incontinence, obstetric history (episiotomy, lacerations), prior pelvic surgery, menstrual cyclicity, age
2. **Examination.** Location, mobility, tenderness, consistency (cystic versus solid), fixation
3. **Transvaginal or transperineal ultrasound.** First-line when imaging is needed; not required for every incidental benign cyst<sup>[[6]](#ref6)[[8]](#ref8)</sup>
4. **MRI pelvis.** Characterizes complex anatomy and local disease extent; it cannot exclude malignancy without tissue assessment when clinically suspicious<sup>[[8]](#ref8)[[15]](#ref15)[[16]](#ref16)</sup>
5. **VCUG.** If urethral diverticulum suspected<sup>[[7]](#ref7)</sup>
6. **Cystourethroscopy.** Selected periurethral lesions or operative planning; a negative examination does not exclude every diverticular communication<sup>[[6]](#ref6)[[25]](#ref25)</sup>
7. **Biopsy.** Suspicious, fixed, bleeding, growing or otherwise atypical lesions; age ≥40 specifically lowers the threshold for a new Bartholin-region mass<sup>[[2]](#ref2)</sup>

## Management Principles

Imaging and intervention should follow symptoms, examination and diagnostic uncertainty rather than a universal MRI, cystoscopy or biopsy protocol.<sup>[[46]](#ref46)</sup>

- **Asymptomatic benign cysts.** Observation with periodic follow-up<sup>[[2]](#ref2)[[18]](#ref18)</sup>
- **Symptomatic benign cysts.** Surgical excision; marsupialization is an alternative for large Gartner duct or Bartholin cysts<sup>[[17]](#ref17)[[18]](#ref18)[[19]](#ref19)</sup>
- **Periurethral masses.** Establish urethral relationships before surgery using examination and selected imaging or endoscopy. In a 126-patient referral series, de novo SUI was 5.6% and existing SUI resolved in 67.6%; those are cohort outcomes<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup>
- **Solid benign tumors.** Distinguish AMFB (usually benign, low recurrence) from AAM (locally aggressive, 30–40% recurrence)<sup>[[12]](#ref12)[[27]](#ref27)</sup>
- **VAIN.** Low-grade observation; high-grade laser, excision, or topical therapy; long-term surveillance<sup>[[13]](#ref13)[[32]](#ref32)[[39]](#ref39)</sup>
- **Malignancy.** Biopsy any suspicious lesion; refer to gynecologic oncology; vaginal cancer is treated predominantly with chemoradiation and brachytherapy<sup>[[14]](#ref14)[[40]](#ref40)</sup>

## Key Principles

- Most vaginal cysts are benign and need no intervention unless symptomatic<sup>[[2]](#ref2)[[7]](#ref7)</sup>
- **Location is the most important diagnostic clue**<sup>[[3]](#ref3)[[4]](#ref4)</sup>
- **Gartner duct cysts can mimic cystocele.** Consider when prolapse compartments are inconsistent<sup>[[5]](#ref5)[[16]](#ref16)</sup>
- **Associated GU anomalies in 10–21%** of Gartner duct cysts; image the upper tract<sup>[[17]](#ref17)[[18]](#ref18)</sup>
- **Rule out malignancy.** Biopsy suspicious lesions and assess a new Bartholin-region mass at age ≥40; 1.6% malignancy was observed in one selected periurethral or anterior-wall mass series<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[2]](#ref2)</sup>
- **MRI is the primary imaging study** for complex vaginal masses<sup>[[8]](#ref8)[[15]](#ref15)[[16]](#ref16)</sup>
- **Aggressive angiomyxoma** is the one benign-appearing vaginal mass with major recurrence risk (30–40%); wide excision and long-term surveillance<sup>[[12]](#ref12)[[31]](#ref31)</sup>
