# Vaginal Cysts and Masses

**Vaginal cysts and masses** span benign cystic, benign solid, premalignant, and malignant lesions of the vaginal wall and paravaginal tissues. The vast majority are benign and asymptomatic, with vaginal cysts found in roughly **0.5–1%** of women. The most common cystic lesions are **Müllerian cysts (34–44%)**, **epidermal inclusion cysts (~23%)**, and **Gartner duct cysts (5–11%)**; the most common periurethral / anterior-wall masses are **urethral diverticula (~40%)** and **Skene gland cysts (~30%)**.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[2]](#ref2)[[3]](#ref3)[[4]](#ref4)[[5]](#ref5)</sup> Special attention belongs to masses in women **over 40** or those that are **fixed, painful, or bleeding** — malignancy is rare but must be excluded.

For periurethral cystic masses and urethral meatal lesions, see also [Female Urethral & Periurethral Masses](/docs/clinical-conditions/03b-voiding-outlet/female-urethral-masses) and [Urethral Diverticulum](/docs/clinical-conditions/03b-voiding-outlet/urethral-diverticula). For lichen-sclerosus-related vulvovaginal lesions, see [Lichen Sclerosus](/docs/clinical-conditions/03g-genital-scrotal/lichen-sclerosus). For the operative management of the most common anterior-wall mass, see [Urethral Diverticulum Repair](/docs/surgical-techniques/04a-urethral-reconstruction/urethral-diverticulum-repair); for excision-defect coverage, see [Vulvar Reconstruction](/docs/surgical-techniques/04e-genital-reconstruction/vulvar).

## Organizational Framework

| Category | Lesions |
|---|---|
| **Embryologic cysts** | Müllerian, Gartner duct (mesonephric), Skene gland (paraurethral) |
| **Acquired cysts** | Epidermal inclusion, endometriotic, Bartholin duct |
| **Urologic** | Urethral diverticulum, ectopic ureterocele, ectopic ureter |
| **Benign solid tumors** | Leiomyoma, fibroepithelial stromal polyp, angiomyofibroblastoma, cellular angiofibroma, superficial myofibroblastoma |
| **Locally aggressive** | Aggressive angiomyxoma |
| **Premalignant** | Vaginal intraepithelial neoplasia (VAIN / vaginal HSIL) |
| **Malignant** | Squamous cell carcinoma (85–90%), adenocarcinoma, melanoma, sarcoma, metastatic |
| **Iatrogenic / other** | Mesh-related, pseudocysts, abscesses |

## Part I — Benign Cystic Lesions

### Müllerian cysts

- **Most common vaginal cyst** in modern series (34–44%)<sup>[[3]](#ref3)[[4]](#ref4)</sup>
- **Embryology** — paramesonephric (Müllerian) duct remnants; lined by mucus-secreting endocervical-type or ciliated tubal-type epithelium<sup>[[3]](#ref3)[[4]](#ref4)</sup>
- **Location** — typically anterolateral vaginal wall
- **Presentation** — often larger and more symptomatic than other vaginal cysts; pelvic pressure, dyspareunia, palpable mass
- **Diagnosis** — examination + MRI for anatomy; histology shows mucicarmine-positive columnar lining<sup>[[2]](#ref2)[[3]](#ref3)[[15]](#ref15)</sup>
- **Management** — observation if asymptomatic; excision for symptomatic cysts<sup>[[2]](#ref2)[[15]](#ref15)</sup>

### Gartner duct (mesonephric) cysts

- **Embryology** — mesonephric (Wolffian) duct remnants; non-mucus-secreting cuboidal/low columnar epithelium<sup>[[3]](#ref3)</sup>
- **Prevalence** — 5–11% of vaginal cysts (most lesions historically called Gartner duct cysts have mucin and are actually Müllerian)<sup>[[3]](#ref3)[[4]](#ref4)</sup>
- **Location** — anterolateral vaginal wall along the mesonephric remnant<sup>[[16]](#ref16)[[17]](#ref17)</sup>
- **Presentation** — most are small and asymptomatic (38%); when symptomatic: dyspareunia (38%), pelvic pain/pressure (24%), palpable bulge (17%), urinary symptoms (7%); mean size 3.5 cm<sup>[[18]](#ref18)</sup>
- **Diagnostic pitfall:** large Gartner duct cysts can **mimic anterior compartment prolapse (cystocele)** — consider the diagnosis when prolapse compartments don't add up<sup>[[5]](#ref5)[[16]](#ref16)</sup>
- **Associated GU anomalies** in 10–21% — ipsilateral renal agenesis, uterine didelphys, septate vagina, urethral diverticulum, ectopic ureter; reflects shared mesonephric origin<sup>[[9]](#ref9)[[17]](#ref17)[[18]](#ref18)</sup>
- **Workup** — exam + transvaginal US (most common); MRI for complex / large lesions or to exclude urologic involvement<sup>[[16]](#ref16)[[18]](#ref18)</sup>

**Management options:**<sup>[[17]](#ref17)[[18]](#ref18)</sup>

| Approach | Notes |
|---|---|
| **Observation** | Asymptomatic |
| **Excision** | Most common (54%); lowest recurrence (~8%) |
| **Marsupialization** | Alternative for large cysts; no recurrence in one review |
| **Tetracycline sclerotherapy after aspiration** | 7% recurrence |
| **Unroofing / partial excision** | No recurrence reported |
| **Fluorescein dye-assisted excision** | Intracystic fluorescein delineates walls and confirms absence of urologic communication<sup>[[17]](#ref17)</sup> |

Median follow-up 82 mo: only 1 possible recurrence (3.4%) after excision; no intraoperative complications.<sup>[[18]](#ref18)</sup>

### Epidermal inclusion (squamous inclusion) cysts

- **Second most common** vaginal cyst (~23%)<sup>[[4]](#ref4)</sup>
- **Etiology** — acquired; squamous epithelium implanted at episiotomy, vaginal laceration, or prior surgery<sup>[[3]](#ref3)[[4]](#ref4)</sup>
- **Location** — posterior or lateral vaginal wall, often at episiotomy scars
- **Histology** — keratinizing stratified squamous epithelium with keratinous debris
- **Management** — observation; simple excision if symptomatic; no malignant potential<sup>[[2]](#ref2)[[3]](#ref3)</sup>

### Bartholin duct cysts and abscesses

- **Anatomy** — Bartholin glands at posterolateral introitus (4 and 8 o'clock)<sup>[[19]](#ref19)</sup>
- **Lifetime prevalence** ~2% of women<sup>[[19]](#ref19)</sup>
- **Pathophysiology** — ductal obstruction → cyst; secondary infection → abscess

**Management** — no single technique is clearly superior; healing and recurrence are similar across fistulization, marsupialization, and sclerotherapy.<sup>[[19]](#ref19)[[20]](#ref20)</sup>

| Technique | Notes |
|---|---|
| **Word catheter** | Office-based; 4–6 weeks for fistulization |
| **Marsupialization** | Permanent drainage tract |
| **Silver nitrate sclerotherapy** | Alternative fistulization |
| **I&D / aspiration alone** | Higher recurrence — not first-line |
| **Gland excision** | Recurrent disease; **mandatory consideration in women &gt; 40** to exclude Bartholin gland carcinoma<sup>[[2]](#ref2)[[19]](#ref19)</sup> |

### Skene gland (paraurethral) cysts

- **Embryology** — Skene glands are homologs of the male prostate; lined by transitional epithelium<sup>[[21]](#ref21)[[22]](#ref22)[[23]](#ref23)</sup>
- **Location** — periurethral, adjacent to the meatus<sup>[[22]](#ref22)[[24]](#ref24)[[25]](#ref25)</sup>
- **Prevalence** — ~30% of periurethral / anterior-wall masses<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup>
- **Presentation** — dyspareunia (40%), recurrent UTI (40%), vaginal mass, voiding dysfunction, urinary spraying<sup>[[23]](#ref23)[[25]](#ref25)</sup>
- **Diagnostic pitfall** — frequently mislabeled as Bartholin cyst; the discriminator is **location (periurethral vs posterolateral introitus)**. **MRI is essential to differentiate from urethral diverticulum.**<sup>[[24]](#ref24)[[25]](#ref25)</sup>
- **Management** — surgical excision; cystoscopy to exclude urethral communication; no recurrences in a 10-patient series at mean 3.5 yr<sup>[[25]](#ref25)</sup>

### Endometriotic cysts

- ~7% of vaginal cysts<sup>[[4]](#ref4)</sup>
- **Location** — posterior fornix (most common vaginal endometriosis site) or episiotomy scar<sup>[[8]](#ref8)[[4]](#ref4)</sup>
- **Presentation** — cyclical pain, dyspareunia, dysmenorrhea, tender nodule that enlarges with menses
- **Diagnosis** — exam, transvaginal US, MRI for deep infiltrating disease<sup>[[8]](#ref8)</sup>
- **Management** — excision for symptomatic lesions; hormonal therapy as adjunct

### Ectopic ureter / megaureter presenting as a "vaginal cyst"

A rare but important pitfall — an ectopic ureter or congenital megaureter can present as a vaginal-wall cyst with continuous incontinence. MRI is essential. Definitive management may require nephroureterectomy with marsupialization of the distal ureter.<sup>[[9]](#ref9)</sup>

## Part II — Benign Solid Vaginal Masses

Benign perivaginal masses account for ~1.6% of urogynecologic surgeries.<sup>[[6]](#ref6)</sup>

### Vaginal leiomyoma

- Most common benign solid vaginal tumor; smooth muscle origin<sup>[[8]](#ref8)</sup>
- Painless mass, dyspareunia, urinary symptoms; well-circumscribed, homogeneous on MRI
- Excision for symptomatic lesions

### Fibroepithelial stromal polyp

- Benign polypoid lesion of vaginal/vulvar stroma; pedunculated or sessile<sup>[[10]](#ref10)[[11]](#ref11)</sup>
- Can be large and edematous, **mimicking aggressive angiomyxoma** — important diagnostic pitfall<sup>[[26]](#ref26)</sup>
- Simple excision is curative

### Angiomyofibroblastoma (AMFB)

- **Benign** mesenchymal tumor of vulvovaginal region; clinically often mistaken for Bartholin cyst<sup>[[27]](#ref27)</sup>
- Well-circumscribed; alternating hypercellular / hypocellular zones with abundant capillary-type vessels; vimentin+, desmin+, variable actin<sup>[[27]](#ref27)[[28]](#ref28)</sup>
- **No recurrence after complete excision** — the key distinction from aggressive angiomyxoma<sup>[[27]](#ref27)[[29]](#ref29)</sup>

### Aggressive angiomyxoma (AAM)

- **Locally aggressive** mesenchymal neoplasm of pelvis/perineum in reproductive-age women (mean ~38 yr)<sup>[[12]](#ref12)[[30]](#ref30)[[31]](#ref31)</sup>
- **Infiltrative** without a true capsule — distinguishes it from AMFB<sup>[[12]](#ref12)[[27]](#ref27)[[29]](#ref29)</sup>
- ER+, PR+, desmin+, vimentin+, variable SMA<sup>[[30]](#ref30)[[31]](#ref31)</sup>
- **Recurrence 30–40%**, sometimes years to decades after excision<sup>[[12]](#ref12)</sup>
- Essentially no metastatic potential<sup>[[12]](#ref12)[[29]](#ref29)</sup>
- **Management:** wide surgical excision (often difficult given infiltrative nature) + **GnRH agonists** (or SERMs / aromatase inhibitors) as adjuvant hormonal therapy given ER/PR positivity; **long-term surveillance is mandatory**<sup>[[12]](#ref12)[[30]](#ref30)[[31]](#ref31)</sup>

### Other rare benign solid tumors

Cellular angiofibroma, superficial (cervicovaginal) myofibroblastoma, mammary-type myofibroblastoma — the genital stromal-tumor family; all benign with low recurrence after excision.<sup>[[10]](#ref10)[[11]](#ref11)</sup> Vaginal angioma and solitary fibrous tumor are rare and curable by excision.<sup>[[6]](#ref6)</sup>

## Part III — Vaginal Intraepithelial Neoplasia (VAIN)

### Definition and epidemiology

- VAIN is the HPV-related premalignant condition of vaginal squamous epithelium, analogous to CIN and VIN<sup>[[13]](#ref13)[[32]](#ref32)[[14]](#ref14)</sup>
- **Rare** — &lt; 1% of female genital intraepithelial neoplasias<sup>[[32]](#ref32)</sup>
- **Terminology:** VAIN 1 (LSIL) vs VAIN 2/3 (HSIL)<sup>[[14]](#ref14)</sup>
- **Progression to invasive carcinoma** ~5% for HSIL (8% in some long-term series)<sup>[[14]](#ref14)[[33]](#ref33)</sup>
- **Mean age** at diagnosis ~55 years<sup>[[34]](#ref34)</sup>

### Risk factors

HPV (especially HPV 16 and 31), prior CIN/cervical cancer, prior hysterectomy (especially for CIN/cancer — 20–55% of VAIN patients have had prior hysterectomy), immunosuppression (HIV), prior pelvic radiation, smoking.<sup>[[13]](#ref13)[[14]](#ref14)[[33]](#ref33)[[34]](#ref34)[[35]](#ref35)</sup>

**Location:** &gt; 80% involve the vaginal vault (post-hysterectomy) or upper vagina.<sup>[[33]](#ref33)</sup>

### Management<sup>[[13]](#ref13)[[32]](#ref32)[[33]](#ref33)[[36]](#ref36)[[37]](#ref37)[[38]](#ref38)[[39]](#ref39)</sup>

| Grade / option | Notes |
|---|---|
| **VAIN 1 (LSIL)** | **Observation preferred** — spontaneous regression in 81–88% |
| **VAIN 2/3 (HSIL)** | Active treatment indicated |
| **CO₂ laser ablation** | Cure 69–90%; recurrence 6–23%; regression declines with repeated treatments (75% → 53% → 27%); rule out invasion before ablation |
| **Surgical excision (local; upper vaginectomy)** | Mainstay if invasion cannot be excluded; cure ~69%; margins do not reliably predict residual disease |
| **Topical imiquimod 5%** | Cure 25–98%; HPV clearance 63% (vs 11% laser, 17% observation); useful for multifocal disease |
| **Topical 5-fluorouracil** | Cure ~46%; multifocal/recurrent and post-radiation patients |
| **Brachytherapy** | Cure 71–90%; refractory/multifocal disease or poor surgical candidates; reserved due to toxicity |

**Recurrence and progression:** ~22% of high-grade VAIN recurs after treatment (risk factors: VAIN 3, prior hysterectomy, excisional treatment); ~1.5% progress to invasive carcinoma at median 87 months — **long-term surveillance is mandatory**.<sup>[[34]](#ref34)[[39]](#ref39)</sup>

## Part IV — Primary Vaginal Cancer

### Overview

- **Extremely rare** (&lt; 1% of female genital cancers)<sup>[[40]](#ref40)[[41]](#ref41)[[42]](#ref42)[[43]](#ref43)[[14]](#ref14)</sup>
- **Histology** — squamous cell carcinoma 85–90%; adenocarcinoma; melanoma; sarcoma<sup>[[41]](#ref41)[[14]](#ref14)</sup>
- **HPV-associated** (predominantly HPV 16) in most squamous cell carcinomas<sup>[[14]](#ref14)</sup>
- **Mean age** sixth decade; rising incidence in younger women with high HIV prevalence<sup>[[41]](#ref41)[[42]](#ref42)</sup>
- **Presentation** — vaginal bleeding (most common), discharge, palpable mass, dyspareunia, urinary symptoms<sup>[[42]](#ref42)[[44]](#ref44)</sup>

### Treatment (NCCN Guidelines)<sup>[[14]](#ref14)[[40]](#ref40)</sup>

| Stage | Approach |
|---|---|
| **I** | Definitive radiation (preferred) — EBRT + brachytherapy ± concurrent platinum-based chemotherapy; surgical resection only when complete excision with negative margins is feasible without excessive morbidity |
| **II–IVA** | Concurrent chemoradiation (platinum-based) + brachytherapy |
| **Surgery** | Reserved for cases where complete resection with clear margins avoids the need for adjuvant RT; definitive surgical management is uncommon |

**Brachytherapy** is a critical component — its addition to EBRT improves survival in stages III–IV.<sup>[[44]](#ref44)</sup>

**5-year survival:** Stage I 58–84%; Stage II 59–74%; Stage III 67–79%; Stage IV 27–32%.<sup>[[44]](#ref44)[[45]](#ref45)</sup>

### Other rare vaginal malignancies

HPV-associated adenocarcinoma, clear cell carcinoma (historically DES-associated), endometrioid carcinoma, mesonephric adenocarcinoma, carcinosarcoma, neuroendocrine carcinoma, adenocarcinoma of Skene-gland origin, adenosarcoma, germ cell tumors.<sup>[[14]](#ref14)</sup>

## Part V — Diagnostic Approach

The single most useful diagnostic clue is **anatomic location**.<sup>[[2]](#ref2)[[6]](#ref6)[[7]](#ref7)[[8]](#ref8)[[22]](#ref22)</sup>

| Location | Top differentials |
|---|---|
| **Anterolateral wall** | Müllerian cyst, Gartner duct cyst |
| **Periurethral / suburethral** | Urethral diverticulum, Skene gland cyst |
| **Posterolateral introitus (4–8 o'clock)** | Bartholin duct cyst |
| **Posterior fornix** | Endometriotic cyst |
| **Episiotomy scar** | Epidermal inclusion cyst |

**Workup:**

1. **History** — duration, mass sensation, dyspareunia, discharge, voiding dysfunction, incontinence, obstetric history (episiotomy, lacerations), prior pelvic surgery, menstrual cyclicity, age
2. **Examination** — location, mobility, tenderness, consistency (cystic vs solid), fixation
3. **Transvaginal / transperineal ultrasound** — first-line imaging<sup>[[6]](#ref6)[[8]](#ref8)</sup>
4. **MRI pelvis** — best for complex masses, urethra/bladder/rectum relationships, additional cysts, malignancy exclusion<sup>[[8]](#ref8)[[15]](#ref15)[[16]](#ref16)</sup>
5. **VCUG** — if urethral diverticulum suspected<sup>[[7]](#ref7)</sup>
6. **Cystourethroscopy** — mandatory for periurethral masses to exclude urethral communication<sup>[[6]](#ref6)[[25]](#ref25)</sup>
7. **Biopsy** — solid masses, fixed lesions, women &gt; 40, or any suspicion of malignancy<sup>[[2]](#ref2)</sup>

## Management Principles

- **Asymptomatic benign cysts** — observation with periodic follow-up<sup>[[2]](#ref2)[[18]](#ref18)</sup>
- **Symptomatic benign cysts** — surgical excision; marsupialization is an alternative for large Gartner duct or Bartholin cysts<sup>[[17]](#ref17)[[18]](#ref18)[[19]](#ref19)</sup>
- **Periurethral masses** — preoperative MRI + cystoscopy mandatory to differentiate urethral diverticulum from Skene gland cyst (different operations); de novo SUI in 5.6% after excision; preexisting SUI resolves in ~68%<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup>
- **Solid benign tumors** — distinguish AMFB (benign, no recurrence) from AAM (locally aggressive, 30–40% recurrence)<sup>[[12]](#ref12)[[27]](#ref27)</sup>
- **VAIN** — low-grade observation; high-grade laser, excision, or topical therapy; long-term surveillance<sup>[[13]](#ref13)[[32]](#ref32)[[39]](#ref39)</sup>
- **Malignancy** — biopsy any suspicious lesion; refer to gynecologic oncology; vaginal cancer is treated predominantly with chemoradiation + brachytherapy<sup>[[14]](#ref14)[[40]](#ref40)</sup>

---

## Key Principles

- Most vaginal cysts are benign and need no intervention unless symptomatic<sup>[[2]](#ref2)[[7]](#ref7)</sup>
- **Location is the most important diagnostic clue**<sup>[[3]](#ref3)[[4]](#ref4)</sup>
- **Gartner duct cysts can mimic cystocele** — consider when prolapse compartments are inconsistent<sup>[[5]](#ref5)[[16]](#ref16)</sup>
- **Associated GU anomalies in 10–21%** of Gartner duct cysts — image the upper tract<sup>[[17]](#ref17)[[18]](#ref18)</sup>
- **Rule out malignancy** — biopsy in women &gt; 40, fixed masses, or those with pain or bleeding; periurethral / anterior-wall mass malignancy rate ~1.6%<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[2]](#ref2)</sup>
- **MRI is the imaging workhorse** for complex vaginal masses<sup>[[8]](#ref8)[[15]](#ref15)[[16]](#ref16)</sup>
- **Aggressive angiomyxoma** is the one benign-appearing vaginal mass with major recurrence risk (30–40%) — wide excision and long-term surveillance<sup>[[12]](#ref12)[[31]](#ref31)</sup>
