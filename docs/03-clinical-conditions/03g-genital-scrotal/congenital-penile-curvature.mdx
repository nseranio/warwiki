# Congenital Penile Curvature

**Congenital penile curvature (CPC)** is bending of the erect penis present from birth, without an antecedent episode of trauma or inflammation, distinguishing it from the acquired fibrotic curvature of [Peyronie's disease](/docs/clinical-conditions/03g-genital-scrotal/peyronies-disease).<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup> A closely related entity, **chordee without hypospadias**, describes ventral curvature associated with an abnormal distribution of ventral tissue (dysgenetic corpus spongiosum, dartos, or Buck's fascia) in a boy with an otherwise normally positioned urethral meatus; some classification schemes group chordee without hypospadias together with congenital curvature as points on the same developmental spectrum.<sup>[[2]](#ref2)[[3]](#ref3)</sup>

The EAU paediatric guideline strongly recommends an artificial erection test at the beginning of surgery.<sup>[[11]](#ref11)</sup> For chordee without hypospadias, deglove and release abnormal skin or fascial tethering, assess urethral involvement, then correct residual corporal curvature; in Kramer's 20-child series, two underwent urethral mobilization and two urethral division with staged urethroplasty.<sup>[[3]](#ref3)[[11]](#ref11)</sup> Confirm straightening with a repeat artificial erection.<sup>[[11]](#ref11)</sup> For operative technique and outcomes, see [Tunica Plication](/docs/surgical-techniques/04j-sexual-dysfunction/peyronies-disease/tunica-plication), which covers plication and corporoplasty for both congenital curvature and Peyronie's disease.

## Epidemiology

Reported incidence is difficult to establish because mild curvature often goes unrecognized at birth and is not reliably detected without an erection. A single-institution examination of 500 consecutive male neonates found 3 cases of congenital penile curvature, an incidence of 0.6% (3 in 500); this is a small, single-center sample and should be read as an estimate rather than a population-representative rate.<sup>[[4]](#ref4)</sup> Most patients come to attention later, in adolescence or adulthood, when erections make the curvature apparent or functionally significant, rather than in infancy.

## Pathophysiology and Classification

Isolated congenital penile curvature is attributed to disproportionate development of the tunica albuginea of the corporal bodies without urethral malformation.<sup>[[6]](#ref6)</sup> In chordee without hypospadias, deficient ventral spongiosal or fascial tissue can tether the erect penis; the EAU paediatric guideline also describes ventral skin deficiency in minor curvature, corporal asymmetry in more severe curvature, and a short urethra among the described causes.<sup>[[2]](#ref2)[[11]](#ref11)</sup> These embryologic mechanisms are proposed rather than definitively established.<sup>[[2]](#ref2)[[5]](#ref5)</sup>

Devine and Horton (1973) described three patterns of chordee without hypospadias: type I, deficient ventral spongiosum, Buck's fascia, and dartos; type II, intact spongiosum with abnormal Buck's fascia and dartos; and type III, an isolated dartos abnormality.<sup>[[2]](#ref2)</sup> Later classifications added corporal disproportion as type IV and congenital short urethra as type V.<sup>[[3]](#ref3)[[12]](#ref12)</sup> This scheme is of historical interest and is not a validated staging system for current practice; direction, severity, and tissue cause are better described directly than forced into these five categories.

## Clinical Features and Evaluation

- **Direction.** Most congenital curvature is ventral; dorsal and lateral curvature also occur.
- **Timing of presentation.** Mild curvature may be unnoticed until puberty or sexual activity begins; some patients present only in adulthood when a partner or the patient notices the deformity or when it causes penetrative difficulty.
- **Associated findings.** A normal urethral meatus distinguishes isolated congenital curvature and chordee without hypospadias from hypospadias-associated curvature; see [Hypospadias & Epispadias](/docs/special-populations/05f-lifelong-care/hypospadias-epispadias) when the meatus is ectopic. Curvature recurrence years after childhood hypospadias repair is a distinct, separately documented problem; see [Redo Hypospadias Repair in Adolescents and Adults](/docs/surgical-techniques/04a-urethral-reconstruction/redo-hypospadias-adults).
- **History and examination.** Document curvature severity and direction with erect-penis photographs from several angles, using a pharmacologically induced erection when needed; record penile length, girth symmetry, and any palpable corporal disproportion.<sup>[[6]](#ref6)[[11]](#ref11)</sup> Ask directly about penetrative difficulty, pain, partner impact, and psychosocial distress; functional and psychological impact guide the decision to operate.
- **Differential diagnosis.** Peyronie's disease is acquired and may present with a plaque, pain on erection, or changing deformity; a recalled injury is not required.<sup>[[10]](#ref10)</sup> Distinguish it and hypospadias-associated curvature from isolated congenital curvature before counseling on management.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[2]](#ref2)</sup>

## Psychosocial Impact

Curvature sufficient to impair penetrative intercourse or cause the patient visible distress can affect self-image, relationships, and sexual confidence, independent of the measured angle. Evaluation should ask directly about functional and psychological impact rather than relying on curvature severity alone to judge how much the condition is bothering the patient.

## Management

Observation is reasonable for mild curvature that does not impair intercourse or bother the patient. The EAU sexual and reproductive health guideline states that surgery for congenital curvature can be deferred until after puberty because penile growth is incomplete.<sup>[[6]](#ref6)</sup> The EAU paediatric guideline strongly recommends surgery when curvature has functional implications and generally considers postponement until the patient can decide reasonable.<sup>[[11]](#ref11)</sup> For congenital curvature requiring correction, the sexual and reproductive health guideline strongly recommends Nesbit or Yachia repair or plication, with or without neurovascular bundle dissection.<sup>[[6]](#ref6)</sup> In a single-center randomized study of 42 men with 60°–90° ventral congenital curvature, girth asymmetry was reported in 1/21 after corporal rotation IV versus 15/21 after rotation III, while dorsal length decreased by 3% versus 0.5%.<sup>[[13]](#ref13)</sup> The EAU guideline describes corporal derotation for ventral congenital curvature and discusses extratunical grafting for hourglass or hinge deformity in its Peyronie's disease section.<sup>[[6]](#ref6)</sup> In a 55-patient modified Nesbit series for congenital curvature, 51/55 met the authors' overall success criterion (no curvature or residual curvature &lt;15° without major long-term complications), median erect penile length loss was 1.7 cm, and IIEF-EF scores worsened in 3.6% at mean follow-up of 36.7 months; transient glans sensory change and bothersome palpable suture knots were also reported.<sup>[[9]](#ref9)</sup>

A 2022 systematic review of surgical correction of adult congenital penile curvature identified 55 studies and 2,956 patients treated by plication (n = 1,375) or corporoplasty (n = 1,580). Reported study-defined treatment-success rates (patient-reported satisfaction or residual curvature &lt;20°) ranged from 75% to 100% after plication and 73% to 100% after corporoplasty. The review found both approaches safe and effective, but could not establish comparative superiority because of wide heterogeneity in outcome definitions and reporting across the included studies, and it did not pool a single complication or erectile-dysfunction rate across all included series.<sup>[[7]](#ref7)</sup> Operative technique, patient selection, suture material, and outcome detail for plication and corporoplasty are covered on [Tunica Plication](/docs/surgical-techniques/04j-sexual-dysfunction/peyronies-disease/tunica-plication), including dedicated congenital-curvature outcome series by technique.

## Recurrence After Childhood Repair

After childhood hypospadias repair with curvature correction, penile curvature may recur in adolescence or young adulthood, sometimes many years later as the penis grows through puberty.<sup>[[8]](#ref8)</sup> In a series of 59 adolescents and young adults (ages 14-21) who presented with recurrent curvature after childhood hypospadias repair (TIP with dorsal plication in 28, Thiersch-Duplay with Nesbit repair in 9, preputial island onlay with Nesbit repair in 6, and two-stage repair with ventral dermal graft in 3; 13 were repaired elsewhere with records unavailable), surgical correction (one-stage dorsal plication and skin detethering in 32, one-stage urethral mobilization with corporal/dermal grafts in 12, and staged corporal/dermal graft followed by tubularization in 15) healed well in 53 of 55 patients with available follow-up (median 30 months); 28 of the 59 total patients reported satisfactory sexual activity, and no patient who received a corporal or dermal graft reported a new erectile abnormality. The authors attributed recurrence to peri-urethral and skin fibrosis, or to disproportionate growth of a hypoplastic ventral corporal wall or reconstructed urethra, and recommended following boys with proximal hypospadias repair through puberty rather than assuming an initially good childhood result will persist.<sup>[[8]](#ref8)</sup> This recurrence pattern, and its operative management, is discussed further on [Redo Hypospadias Repair in Adolescents and Adults](/docs/surgical-techniques/04a-urethral-reconstruction/redo-hypospadias-adults).
