# Erectile Dysfunction (Condition)

## At a glance

- **Assess.** Clarify erection, libido, ejaculation and relationship goals; use a validated symptom measure, focused examination, medication review and metabolic/morning-testosterone assessment. Review cardiovascular risk and suitability for sexual activity.<sup>[[22]](#ref22)</sup>
- **Options:** address reversible factors and offer psychosexual support alongside PDE5 inhibitors, vacuum device, intraurethral or intracavernosal therapy, or prosthesis according to informed preference. Review technique and tolerability before labelling a medication ineffective.<sup>[[22]](#ref22)</sup>
- **Reconsider the pathway.** Nitrate use contraindicates PDE5 inhibitors; assess unstable or high-risk cardiovascular disease before sexual activity. Testosterone requires a confirmed deficiency assessment. Implant surgery requires absence of active infection.<sup>[[22]](#ref22)</sup><sup>[[12]](#ref12)</sup>
- **Follow-up.** Reassess goal attainment, correct use, adverse effects and partner concerns. After nerve-sparing prostatectomy, distinguish assisted erections during treatment from unassisted recovery; REACTT did not show improved recovery after washout.<sup>[[22]](#ref22)</sup><sup>[[24]](#ref24)</sup>

[Compare pivotal on-treatment efficacy and postoperative recovery evidence](#selected-evidence). Prescribing details remain in the pharmacology hubs.

**Erectile dysfunction (ED)** is the consistent or recurrent inability to attain or maintain a penile erection sufficient for satisfactory sexual performance.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup> It is one of the most common male sexual dysfunctions, affecting approximately 40% of men aged 40–49 and rising to 50–100% of men over 70.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup> A 1999 modelling projection estimated 322 million cases worldwide by 2025; this is not a measured contemporary global count.<sup>[[3]](#ref3)</sup>

For the operative treatment atlas (penile prosthesis, revascularization, Peyronie's surgery as comorbid disease) see [Sexual Dysfunction Treatment Atlas](/docs/surgical-techniques/04j-sexual-dysfunction). For the pharmacology hubs (PDE5 inhibitors, intracavernosal injection agents, intraurethral alprostadil, testosterone replacement, androgen adjuncts, priapism management) see [Sexual Medicine & Andrology](/docs/foundations/pharmacology/sexual-medicine-andrology).

## Pathophysiology

Erection is a neurovascular event requiring coordination of psychological, endocrine, vascular, and neurological systems. Parasympathetic stimulation triggers release of **nitric oxide (NO)** from endothelial cells and nonadrenergic noncholinergic neurons, which activates guanylyl cyclase to produce **cyclic GMP (cGMP)**. cGMP causes smooth-muscle relaxation in the corpora cavernosa, increasing arterial inflow and compressing subtunical venules to trap blood and produce rigidity. PDE5 inhibitors prolong this signal by blocking cGMP degradation.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[4]](#ref4)</sup>

ED is classified as psychogenic, organic, or mixed, with mixed being most common:<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup>

| Class | Mechanism | Examples |
|---|---|---|
| **Vasculogenic** | Arterial insufficiency, corporal veno-occlusive dysfunction (venous leak), sinusoidal fibrosis | Atherosclerosis, post-radiation, post-traumatic |
| **Neurogenic** | Central or peripheral neural disruption | Stroke, MS, spinal-cord injury, diabetic neuropathy, post–radical pelvic surgery |
| **Endocrinological** | Testosterone, prolactin, thyroid axis | Hypogonadism, hyperprolactinemia, diabetes mellitus |
| **Drug-induced** | Pharmacologic interference with NO / sympathetic balance | Antihypertensives, SSRIs, antiandrogens, major tranquilizers |
| **Psychogenic** | Performance anxiety, depression, relationship dynamics | ~ 20% of presentations<sup>[[5]](#ref5)</sup> |

## Risk Factors

Age is the dominant risk factor; type 2 diabetes mellitus is the second, with ED in 50–75% of diabetic men.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup> Other contributors:<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[5]](#ref5)[[4]](#ref4)</sup>

- **Hypertension.** ED prevalence up to 68%
- **Dyslipidemia**
- **Obesity and metabolic syndrome**
- **Sedentary lifestyle**
- **Cigarette smoking.** Doubles ED prevalence versus nonsmokers
- **Alcohol and recreational drug use**
- **Lower urinary tract symptoms / BPH**
- **Depression**
- **Prostate-cancer treatment.** Radical prostatectomy, radiation, brachytherapy

---

## ED as a Cardiovascular Risk Marker

ED and cardiovascular disease (CVD) share the same pathophysiology: endothelial dysfunction and atherosclerosis.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[6]](#ref6)</sup> Because penile arteries are smaller-caliber than coronary arteries, the same degree of endothelial dysfunction manifests earlier in erectile tissues.

The Mostafaei 2021 umbrella review of meta-analyses confirmed that ED independently predicts:<sup>[[7]](#ref7)</sup>

| Outcome | Relative risk |
|---|---|
| Cardiovascular disease | **1.45** |
| Coronary heart disease | **1.50** |
| Myocardial infarction | **1.55** |
| Stroke | **1.36** |
| All-cause mortality | **1.25** |

ED can precede symptomatic coronary disease, but the interval and proportion depend on the population studied; the Dong meta-analysis evaluated future cardiovascular risk, not a universal two-thirds presentation rule.<sup>[[8]](#ref8)[[4]](#ref4)</sup> The current EAU framework incorporates Princeton IV cardiovascular assessment. ED is a risk marker; urgency and referral depend on cardiovascular symptoms and the individual risk assessment.<sup>[[22]](#ref22)</sup>

## Diagnostic Evaluation

Evaluation begins with a thorough sexual, medical, and psychosocial history and a focused physical examination of the genitourinary, endocrine, vascular, and neurological systems.<sup>[[10]](#ref10)[[12]](#ref12)</sup>

### Validated questionnaire — IIEF-5 (Sexual Health Inventory for Men)

| Score | Severity |
|---|---|
| **5–7** | Severe ED |
| **8–11** | Moderate ED |
| **12–16** | Mild-to-moderate ED |
| **17–21** | Mild ED |
| **22–25** | None |

The IIEF-5 is the standard screening and outcomes-tracking instrument across ED trials and contemporary practice.<sup>[[12]](#ref12)</sup>

### Laboratory testing

- **Fasting glucose or HbA1c.** Diabetes screen
- **Lipid panel.** Atherosclerotic risk
- **Morning total testosterone.** Repeat if abnormal; if low, add free testosterone and LH to differentiate primary versus secondary hypogonadism
- Selected: prolactin, TSH when clinically indicated<sup>[[10]](#ref10)</sup>

### Specialized tests (selected cases)

- **Nocturnal penile tumescence (NPT).** Psychogenic versus organic differentiation
- **Intracavernosal injection (ICI) test.** Assesses vasculogenic component
- **Penile duplex Doppler ultrasound.** Peak systolic velocity (arteriogenic), end-diastolic velocity (veno-occlusive)
- **Cavernosometry or cavernosography.** When surgical revascularization is being considered
- **Selective pudendal arteriography.** Pre-revascularization in young men with focal post-traumatic disease<sup>[[10]](#ref10)</sup>

### Cardiovascular risk assessment

Assess cardiovascular risk and the safety of sexual activity. The EAU 2026 framework incorporates Princeton IV, including risk assessment for predominantly vasculogenic ED and further evaluation when risk is uncertain or high.<sup>[[22]](#ref22)</sup>

## Treatment

Treatment uses shared decisions across the available options. The EAU 2026 framework replaces a rigid three-tier ladder with choices based on efficacy, invasiveness, safety and patient preference; the AUA 2018 and ICSM guidance provide additional context.<sup>[[22]](#ref22)[[12]](#ref12)[[13]](#ref13)</sup>

### 1. Lifestyle modification — the foundation

The AUA guideline recommends counseling men with ED and comorbidities that dietary change and increased physical activity improve overall health and may improve erectile function (Moderate Recommendation, Grade C); it also advises smoking-cessation counseling. Weight loss, exercise and better control of diabetes, hypertension and dyslipidemia benefit cardiovascular health. The effect on erections is small on average, with clinically important gains in a subset of men.<sup>[[12]](#ref12)[[5]](#ref5)</sup>

### 2. First-line — oral PDE5 inhibitors

PDE5 inhibitors are an established first-line pharmacotherapy; response depends on cause, correct use and the outcome definition.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[12]](#ref12)[[13]](#ref13)</sup> See the [PDE5 inhibitors pharmacology hub](/docs/foundations/pharmacology/sexual-medicine-andrology/pde5-inhibitors) for the deep dive on mechanisms, dosing, contraindications, and post-RP rehab evidence.

Use the prescribing hub for agent selection, starting doses, adjustment and contraindications. Avoid interpreting an intercourse-attempt success rate as a patient-level cure rate.

**Key prescribing principles:**

- **Try at least 4–6 times** before deeming ineffective; dose titration to the maximum tolerated dose and correct use rescue a substantial share of apparent non-responders.<sup>[[13]](#ref13)[[16]](#ref16)</sup>
- Review stimulation, timing, food effects, adherence and safe dose adjustment; patients may select another modality without exhausting every oral regimen.<sup>[[12]](#ref12)[[22]](#ref22)</sup>
- **Absolute contraindication:** any organic nitrate or nitric-oxide donor, including recreational amyl nitrite (poppers), and riociguat (soluble guanylate cyclase stimulator), with risk of severe hypotension. Nitrate-free intervals and label details are in the [PDE5 inhibitors hub](/docs/foundations/pharmacology/sexual-medicine-andrology/pde5-inhibitors).<sup>[[22]](#ref22)[[11]](#ref11)</sup>
- With α-blockers, both drugs vasodilate; establish stability on the α-blocker and start the PDE5 inhibitor at its lowest dose. Use caution in patients with autonomic dysfunction.<sup>[[22]](#ref22)[[15]](#ref15)</sup>
- Common side effects: headache, flushing, dyspepsia, nasal congestion, visual disturbances.
- **No clinically significant efficacy difference** among the four agents; choice is driven by patient preference (onset, duration, daily-vs-on-demand).<sup>[[13]](#ref13)</sup>

### 3. Injection, intraurethral and device options

For inadequate oral response, contraindications, or informed preference:<sup>[[12]](#ref12)[[16]](#ref16)[[17]](#ref17)</sup>

- **Intracavernosal injection (ICI).** Alprostadil monotherapy or bimix / trimix / quadmix combinations. ICI is the most reliably effective non-surgical option in PDE5-resistant patients. Risks: penile pain (about half of patients), priapism (about 1% with alprostadil) and penile fibrosis (about 2%); combinations containing papaverine carry more priapism and fibrosis risk.<sup>[[22]](#ref22)</sup> See the [ICI agents pharmacology hub](/docs/foundations/pharmacology/sexual-medicine-andrology/intracavernosal-injection-agents).
- **Intraurethral alprostadil (MUSE).** Less invasive than ICI but typically less effective; useful when ICI is not tolerated. See [intraurethral alprostadil](/docs/foundations/pharmacology/sexual-medicine-andrology/intraurethral-alprostadil).
- **Vacuum erection device (VED).** Non-invasive, mechanical option particularly suitable for patients who cannot use pharmacotherapy or for post-RP penile-rehabilitation programs.

### 4. Penile prosthesis

Surgically implanted inflatable or malleable penile prostheses may be selected after discussion of all options, including when other treatment is ineffective or unacceptable. Multicomponent inflatable prostheses (IPPs) report about 92–100% patient and 91–95% partner satisfaction in contemporary series, among the highest satisfaction rates of any urologic prosthetic surgery.<sup>[[22]](#ref22)[[10]](#ref10)</sup> AUA advises counseling on postoperative expectations and not operating in the presence of systemic, cutaneous or urinary tract infection.<sup>[[12]](#ref12)</sup>

For the operative pathway — preoperative evaluation (CURSED), intraoperative setup (no-touch), approaches (PS / IP / SC), reservoir placement (SoR / HSM), models database, infection prevention, revision, and complications — see [Penile Implants](/docs/surgical-techniques/04j-sexual-dysfunction/penile-implants).

### 5. Testosterone replacement therapy

The AUA recommends measuring morning total testosterone in every man with ED (Moderate Recommendation, Grade C).<sup>[[12]](#ref12)</sup> Testosterone deficiency is defined by the AUA as total testosterone below 300 ng/dL on two separate early-morning measurements together with symptoms or signs.<sup>[[26]](#ref26)</sup> In men with confirmed hypogonadism, testosterone replacement improves IIEF erectile-function scores (pooled mean gain about 2.3 IIEF-EF points, below the 4-point minimal clinically important difference).<sup>[[19]](#ref19)[[20]](#ref20)</sup> Benefit is greater in men with more severe hypogonadism (total T &lt; 8 nmol/L, about 230 ng/dL) and smaller with diabetes or obesity; in men without deficiency, testosterone has little or no effect on erectile function.<sup>[[20]](#ref20)[[9]](#ref9)</sup> TRT may be combined with PDE5 inhibitors for enhanced effect in hypogonadal men with ED: the AUA advises that a PDE5 inhibitor may be more effective combined with testosterone (Moderate Recommendation, Grade C; small, mostly low-quality trials), and that testosterone is not an effective monotherapy for ED.<sup>[[20]](#ref20)[[16]](#ref16)[[12]](#ref12)</sup>

The Endocrine Society 2018 guideline recommends baseline prostate-cancer risk assessment before initiation and monitoring for thromboembolism on therapy.<sup>[[19]](#ref19)</sup> The TRAVERSE 2023 trial and the Bhasin & Snyder 2025 NEJM review clarified the contemporary cardiovascular safety profile (non-inferiority for MACE in symptomatic men aged 45–80 with total T &lt; 300 ng/dL and existing or high risk of cardiovascular disease).<sup>[[25]](#ref25)[[21]](#ref21)</sup> See the [testosterone replacement pharmacology hub](/docs/foundations/pharmacology/sexual-medicine-andrology/testosterone-replacement) for the full framework.

### 6. Emerging therapies

- **Low-intensity extracorporeal shockwave therapy (Li-ESWT).** Proposed mechanism: neovascularization and tissue regeneration. The 2025 Cochrane review (21 trials, 1,357 men) found low-certainty evidence: the average short-term erectile-function benefit may be too small to be clinically important; longer-term benefit remains uncertain and heterogeneous. EAU offers a weak recommendation for mild vasculogenic ED, PDE5 inhibitor poor responders and patients who decline oral therapy, whereas AUA considers treatment investigational (Conditional Recommendation, Grade C). This is not established restorative therapy.<sup>[[2]](#ref2)[[18]](#ref18)[[12]](#ref12)</sup>
- **Stem-cell and platelet-rich-plasma (PRP) therapy.** The AUA classes intracavernosal stem-cell therapy as investigational and PRP as experimental; EAU limits intracavernosal PRP to clinical trials. Small positive studies do not establish routine efficacy.<sup>[[18]](#ref18)[[12]](#ref12)</sup>

### 7. Psychosexual therapy

Counseling is recommended for men with psychogenic ED, either as monotherapy or in combination with pharmacotherapy. The AUA advises considering referral to a mental health professional for any man treated for ED, to support adherence, reduce performance anxiety and integrate treatment into the sexual relationship (Moderate Recommendation, Grade C).<sup>[[12]](#ref12)</sup> Couples-based therapy is particularly valuable when relationship dynamics contribute. The 2007 Cochrane review found group therapy plus sildenafil superior to sildenafil alone (RR 0.46 for persistence of ED; two trials, 71 men), and one small trial (20 men) favored group therapy over sildenafil on IIEF.<sup>[[14]](#ref14)</sup> See the canonical [Psychosexual Therapy](/docs/surgical-techniques/04j-sexual-dysfunction/psychosexual-therapy) page for the full sensate focus / CBT / MBI / PLISSIT framework.

## Special Populations

| Population | Key considerations |
|---|---|
| **Post-radical prostatectomy** | Offer erectile-function treatment; the AUA advises counseling that early PDE5 inhibitor use may not improve spontaneous, unassisted function.<sup>[[12]](#ref12)</sup> REACTT improved assisted function during treatment but did not establish unassisted recovery after washout. Discuss PDE5i, MUSE, ICI, VED and prosthesis according to goals.<sup>[[24]](#ref24)</sup> |
| **Post-pelvic radiation** | Vasculogenic + neurogenic mechanism; PDE5i first-line; ICI / IPP for non-response |
| **Diabetes mellitus** | High prevalence (50–75%); poorer PDE5i response; lower threshold for second-line<sup>[[17]](#ref17)</sup> |
| **Spinal-cord injury / MS** | Neurogenic mechanism; PDE5i first-line; **autonomic-dysreflexia precaution** in lesions T6 and above (see [Autonomic Dysreflexia](/docs/clinical-conditions/03d-nlutd/autonomic-dysreflexia))<sup>[[15]](#ref15)</sup> |
| **Cardiovascular disease** | Princeton IV-informed risk stratification; **nitrate use is an absolute contraindication** to PDE5i<sup>[[11]](#ref11)</sup> |
| **Young men with focal post-traumatic disease** | Selective candidates for **penile revascularization** (AUA: conditional recommendation for young men with focal arterial occlusion and no generalized vascular or veno-occlusive disease; penile venous surgery is not recommended)<sup>[[12]](#ref12)</sup> — see [saphenous vein graft](/docs/foundations/surgical-principles/grafts/saphenous-vein) |
| **Peyronie's disease overlap** | Treat ED with PDE5i first; surgical correction of curvature combined with IPP if both present — see [Peyronie's disease](/docs/clinical-conditions/03g-genital-scrotal/peyronies-disease) |

## Prognosis and Key Takeaways

ED is a treatable condition at all stages. The diagnosis should prompt cardiovascular risk assessment, as it may precede symptomatic CVD by several years (the interval varies by population).<sup>[[7]](#ref7)[[1]](#ref1)[[6]](#ref6)[[8]](#ref8)</sup>

Discuss lifestyle and psychosexual care alongside medication, injection/device therapy and prosthesis. Avoid promising the same success rate across different causes or presenting surgery as a mandatory last step.<sup>[[12]](#ref12)[[22]](#ref22)</sup>

For the reconstructive urologist, ED management runs in parallel with [penile-implant](/docs/surgical-techniques/04j-sexual-dysfunction/penile-implants) and [Peyronie's-disease](/docs/clinical-conditions/03g-genital-scrotal/peyronies-disease) practice. New ED warrants cardiovascular assessment; cardiology referral depends on symptoms, risk and uncertainty rather than being automatic for every patient.
