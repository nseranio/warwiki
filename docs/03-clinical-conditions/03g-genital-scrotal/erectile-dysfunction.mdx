# Erectile Dysfunction (Condition)

**Erectile dysfunction (ED)** is the consistent or recurrent inability to attain or maintain a penile erection sufficient for satisfactory sexual performance.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup> It is one of the most common male sexual dysfunctions, with prevalence estimates of 2–9% at ages 40–49 in one review, while the Massachusetts Male Aging Study found some degree of ED in 39% by age 40; estimates beyond age 70 range from 50–100%.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup> A 1999 modelling projection estimated 322 million cases worldwide by 2025; this is not a measured contemporary global count.<sup>[[3]](#ref3)</sup>

For the operative treatment atlas (penile prosthesis, revascularization, Peyronie's surgery as comorbid disease) see [Sexual Dysfunction Treatment Atlas](/docs/surgical-techniques/04j-sexual-dysfunction). For the pharmacology hubs (PDE5 inhibitors, intracavernosal injection agents, intraurethral alprostadil, testosterone replacement, androgen adjuncts, priapism management) see [Sexual Medicine & Andrology](/docs/foundations/pharmacology/sexual-medicine-andrology).

## Pathophysiology

Erection is a neurovascular event requiring coordination of psychological, endocrine, vascular, and neurological systems. Parasympathetic stimulation triggers release of **nitric oxide (NO)** from endothelial cells and nonadrenergic noncholinergic neurons, which activates guanylyl cyclase to produce **cyclic GMP (cGMP)**. cGMP causes smooth-muscle relaxation in the corpora cavernosa, increasing arterial inflow and compressing subtunical venules to trap blood and produce rigidity. PDE5 inhibitors prolong this signal by blocking cGMP degradation.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[4]](#ref4)</sup> Burnett and colleagues (1992) localized NO synthase in the rat to penile neurons innervating the corpora cavernosa and to neuronal plexuses in the adventitia of penile arteries; small doses of NO synthase inhibitors abolished electrically stimulated erections.<sup>[[41]](#ref41)</sup>

ED is classified as psychogenic, organic, or mixed, with mixed being most common:<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup>

| Class | Mechanism | Examples |
|---|---|---|
| **Vasculogenic** | Arterial insufficiency, corporal veno-occlusive dysfunction (venous leak), sinusoidal fibrosis | Atherosclerosis, post-radiation, post-traumatic |
| **Neurogenic** | Central or peripheral neural disruption | Stroke, MS, spinal-cord injury, diabetic neuropathy, post–radical pelvic surgery |
| **Endocrinological** | Testosterone, prolactin, thyroid axis | Hypogonadism, hyperprolactinemia, diabetes mellitus |
| **Drug-induced** | Pharmacologic interference with NO / sympathetic balance | Antihypertensives, SSRIs, antiandrogens, major tranquilizers |
| **Psychogenic** | Performance anxiety, depression, relationship dynamics | Psychological factors may occur alone or with organic causes<sup>[[5]](#ref5)</sup> |

## Risk Factors

Age is the dominant risk factor; type 2 diabetes mellitus is the second, with ED in 50–75% of diabetic men.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup> Other contributors:<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[5]](#ref5)[[4]](#ref4)</sup>

- **Hypertension.** In an outpatient hypertension-center questionnaire study, 68.3% of 104 respondents reported ED; the questionnaire was mailed to 476 men.<sup>[[43]](#ref43)</sup>
- **Dyslipidemia**
- **Obesity and metabolic syndrome**
- **Sedentary lifestyle**
- **Cigarette smoking.** Doubles ED prevalence versus nonsmokers
- **Alcohol and recreational drug use**
- **Lower urinary tract symptoms / BPH**
- **Depression**
- **Prostate-cancer treatment.** ED can follow radical prostatectomy, radiation, brachytherapy or focal ablation with high-intensity focused ultrasound (HIFU). AUA/ASTRO requires counseling about sexual risks (Statement 9, Clinical Principle); its discussion recommends documenting baseline function.<sup>[[40]](#ref40)</sup> In a selected 92-man focal-HIFU cohort without severe ED before treatment, 36% had severe ED (IIEF-5 ≤7) at 12 months.<sup>[[27]](#ref27)</sup>
- **COVID-19.** Proposed vasculogenic mechanism (endothelial nitric-oxide synthase reduction near penile vascular endothelium); one survey found higher ED prevalence in COVID-19-positive than -negative men (28% vs 9%). An emerging, not yet established, association.<sup>[[28]](#ref28)[[29]](#ref29)</sup>

## ED as a Cardiovascular Risk Marker

ED and cardiovascular disease (CVD) share the same pathophysiology: endothelial dysfunction and atherosclerosis.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[6]](#ref6)</sup> Because penile arteries are smaller-caliber than coronary arteries, the same degree of endothelial dysfunction manifests earlier in erectile tissues.

The Mostafaei 2021 umbrella review of meta-analyses confirmed that ED independently predicts:<sup>[[7]](#ref7)</sup>

| Outcome | Relative risk |
|---|---|
| Cardiovascular disease | **1.45** |
| Coronary heart disease | **1.50** |
| Myocardial infarction | **1.55** |
| Stroke | **1.36** |
| All-cause mortality | **1.25** |

ED can precede symptomatic coronary disease, but the interval and proportion depend on the population studied; the Dong meta-analysis evaluated future cardiovascular risk, not a universal two-thirds presentation rule.<sup>[[8]](#ref8)[[4]](#ref4)</sup> The current EAU framework incorporates Princeton IV cardiovascular assessment. ED is a risk marker; urgency and referral depend on cardiovascular symptoms and the individual risk assessment.<sup>[[22]](#ref22)</sup>

## Diagnostic Evaluation

Evaluation begins with a thorough sexual, medical, and psychosocial history and a focused physical examination of the genitourinary, endocrine, vascular, and neurological systems.<sup>[[10]](#ref10)[[12]](#ref12)</sup>

### Validated questionnaire — IIEF-5 (Sexual Health Inventory for Men)

| Score | Severity |
|---|---|
| **5–7** | Severe ED |
| **8–11** | Moderate ED |
| **12–16** | Mild-to-moderate ED |
| **17–21** | Mild ED |
| **22–25** | None |

The IIEF-5/SHIM is a validated ED screening tool; the separate six-item IIEF-EF domain described below is used to measure treatment efficacy in many trials.<sup>[[9]](#ref9)</sup><sup>[[12]](#ref12)</sup>

A separate instrument, the 6-item Erectile Function domain of the full 15-item IIEF, is scored 6–30 and uses different cutoffs: 26–30 no ED, 22–25 mild, 17–21 mild-to-moderate, 11–16 moderate, 6–10 severe. It is a distinct scale from the 5-item, 25-point IIEF-5/SHIM above, and its cutoffs should not be applied to a SHIM score.<sup>[[30]](#ref30)</sup>

The **Erection Hardness Score (EHS)** is a simple 4-point patient-reported scale used to establish baseline rigidity and track treatment response in the clinical encounter: 1, penis larger but not hard; 2, hard but not hard enough for penetration; 3, hard enough for penetration but not completely hard; 4, completely hard and fully rigid.<sup>[[31]](#ref31)</sup>

### Laboratory testing

- **Fasting glucose or HbA1c.** Diabetes screen
- **Lipid panel.** Atherosclerotic risk
- **Morning total testosterone.** Confirm a low value on a second early-morning draw; obtain LH to classify confirmed deficiency and free testosterone when total testosterone is borderline or SHBG is altered.<sup>[[19]](#ref19)[[26]](#ref26)</sup>
- Selected: prolactin, TSH when clinically indicated<sup>[[10]](#ref10)</sup>

### Specialized tests (selected cases)

- **Nocturnal penile tumescence (NPT).** Psychogenic versus organic differentiation. Recorded over 2–3 consecutive nights with penile strain gauges (RigiScan); sleep disruptors (irregular schedule, sleep apnea, restless leg syndrome, PTSD, depression) impair REM sleep independent of erectile capacity, giving a high false-negative rate. A study is generally reliable only when completely normal, with at least one erectile episode of tip rigidity above 60% lasting more than 10 minutes; most abnormal studies fall short of normal criteria without being fully absent, which limits clinical utility, while absent nocturnal erections are nonspecific and cannot establish an organic cause.<sup>[[32]](#ref32)[[22]](#ref22)</sup>
- **Intracavernosal injection (ICI) test.** Gives limited information about vascular status; use dynamic penile duplex when a diagnostic vascular assessment is warranted.<sup>[[22]](#ref22)</sup>
- **Penile duplex Doppler ultrasound.** Peak systolic velocity (arteriogenic), end-diastolic velocity (veno-occlusive)
- **Shear wave elastography.** Investigational ultrasound measure of corporal stiffness using induced shear waves. In one feasibility study, post-injection measurements distinguished 26 men with arterial ED from 30 with nonvascular ED (AUC 0.81); venogenic classification, cardiovascular-risk prediction and replacement of duplex Doppler were not tested.<sup>[[33]](#ref33)</sup>
- **Cavernosometry or cavernosography.** When surgical revascularization is being considered
- **Selective pudendal arteriography.** Pre-revascularization in young men with focal post-traumatic disease<sup>[[10]](#ref10)</sup>

### Cardiovascular risk assessment

Assess cardiovascular risk and the safety of sexual activity. The EAU 2026 framework adopts the Princeton IV algorithms, which added coronary artery calcium scoring for cardiovascular risk stratification:<sup>[[42]](#ref42)</sup> for men with predominantly vasculogenic ED and no overt disease, calculate the 2019 ACC/AHA 10-year ASCVD risk (low below 5%, borderline 5% to below 7.5%, intermediate 7.5% to below 20%, high 20% or more) and obtain a coronary artery calcium score in the borderline-to-intermediate group, because the risk score alone can underestimate risk in men aged 40 to 60. Men with overt cardiovascular symptoms or disease are assessed by exercise capacity, with a stress test when risk is intermediate or indeterminate and cardiology referral when risk is high. The EAU asks that these flowcharts be adapted to local expertise, test availability and cost, and separately keeps the 3rd Princeton recommendations for prescribing PDE5 inhibitors in men with cardiovascular disease.<sup>[[22]](#ref22)</sup> Princeton IV offers a rough functional screen for usual-partner sexual activity: walking one mile on flat ground in 20 minutes or briskly climbing two flights of stairs in about 10 seconds without cardiac symptoms corresponds to about 2–3 METs (coital activity in established couples has been estimated at about 2.5–3.3 METs). More vigorous sexual activity may require 5–6 METs, and indeterminate cardiovascular risk calls for formal assessment rather than reliance on this proxy.<sup>[[34]](#ref34)[[35]](#ref35)[[42]](#ref42)</sup>

## Treatment

Treatment uses shared decisions across the available options. The EAU 2026 framework replaces a rigid three-tier ladder with choices based on efficacy, invasiveness, safety and patient preference; the AUA 2018 and ICSM guidance provide additional context.<sup>[[22]](#ref22)[[12]](#ref12)[[13]](#ref13)</sup>

### 1. Lifestyle modification — the foundation

The AUA guideline recommends counseling men with ED and comorbidities that dietary change and increased physical activity improve overall health and may improve erectile function (Moderate Recommendation, Grade C); it also advises smoking-cessation counseling. Weight loss, exercise and better control of diabetes, hypertension and dyslipidemia benefit cardiovascular health. The effect on erections is small on average, with clinically important gains in a subset of men.<sup>[[12]](#ref12)[[5]](#ref5)</sup> A meta-analysis of randomized trials found increased physical activity produced a 3.85-point improvement in IIEF erectile-function scores (95% CI 2.33–5.37).<sup>[[36]](#ref36)</sup> Exercise may also potentiate a PDE5 inhibitor: in a small randomized study, adding a prescribed aerobic exercise regimen (mean 3.4 hours/week, about 22.8 MET-hours/week) to PDE5i therapy produced a higher erectile-function domain score than PDE5i alone at three months (26.8 vs 24.7, P = 0.003), with larger gains also seen in confidence, desire and satisfaction subscales.<sup>[[37]](#ref37)</sup>

### 2. First-line — oral PDE5 inhibitors

PDE5 inhibitors are an established first-line pharmacotherapy (EAU 2026: strong); response depends on cause, correct use and the outcome definition.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[12]](#ref12)[[13]](#ref13)[[22]](#ref22)</sup> See the [PDE5 inhibitors pharmacology hub](/docs/foundations/pharmacology/sexual-medicine-andrology/pde5-inhibitors) for the deep dive on mechanisms, dosing, contraindications, and post-RP rehab evidence.

Use the prescribing hub for agent selection, starting doses, adjustment and contraindications. Avoid interpreting an intercourse-attempt success rate as a patient-level cure rate.

**Key prescribing principles:**

- **Try at least 4–6 times** before deeming ineffective; dose titration to the maximum tolerated dose and correct use rescue a substantial share of apparent non-responders.<sup>[[13]](#ref13)[[16]](#ref16)</sup>
- Review stimulation, timing, food effects, adherence and safe dose adjustment; patients may select another modality without exhausting every oral regimen.<sup>[[12]](#ref12)[[22]](#ref22)</sup>
- **Absolute contraindication:** any organic nitrate or nitric-oxide donor, including recreational amyl nitrite (poppers), nicorandil, and riociguat (soluble guanylate cyclase stimulator), with risk of severe hypotension. Nitrate-free intervals and label details are in the [PDE5 inhibitors hub](/docs/foundations/pharmacology/sexual-medicine-andrology/pde5-inhibitors).<sup>[[22]](#ref22)[[11]](#ref11)</sup>
- With α-blockers, both drugs vasodilate; establish stability on the α-blocker and start the PDE5 inhibitor at its lowest dose. Use caution in patients with autonomic dysfunction.<sup>[[22]](#ref22)[[15]](#ref15)</sup>
- Common side effects: headache, flushing, dyspepsia, nasal congestion, visual disturbances.
- **No clinically significant efficacy difference** among the four agents; choice is driven by patient preference (onset, duration, daily-vs-on-demand).<sup>[[13]](#ref13)</sup>

### 3. Injection, intraurethral and device options

For inadequate oral response, contraindications, or informed preference (EAU 2026 rates injections an alternative first-line or second-line option, strong, and topical or intraurethral alprostadil and vacuum devices weak):<sup>[[12]](#ref12)[[16]](#ref16)[[17]](#ref17)[[22]](#ref22)</sup>

- **Intracavernosal injection (ICI).** Alprostadil monotherapy or bimix / trimix / quadmix combinations. ICI is the most reliably effective non-surgical option in PDE5-resistant patients. Risks include penile pain (about half of patients), priapism (about 1% with alprostadil) and penile fibrosis (about 2%). Before home ICI, perform an in-office injection test (AUA Clinical Principle) and instruct patients to return to the office or emergency department for an erection lasting more than four hours (AUA/SMSNA Expert Opinion).<sup>[[22]](#ref22)[[12]](#ref12)[[39]](#ref39)</sup> Combinations containing papaverine carry more priapism risk than alprostadil alone.<sup>[[22]](#ref22)[[39]](#ref39)</sup> EAU 2026 reports fibrosis in 5–10% with papaverine-containing mixtures versus about 2% with alprostadil alone, but these series do not establish a higher risk from a particular mixture.<sup>[[22]](#ref22)</sup> See the [ICI agents pharmacology hub](/docs/foundations/pharmacology/sexual-medicine-andrology/intracavernosal-injection-agents).
- **Intraurethral alprostadil (MUSE).** Less invasive than ICI but typically less effective; useful when ICI is not tolerated. FDA now lists every MUSE strength as discontinued, so US availability cannot be assumed. See [intraurethral alprostadil](/docs/foundations/pharmacology/sexual-medicine-andrology/intraurethral-alprostadil).
- **Vacuum erection device (VED).** Non-invasive, mechanical option particularly suitable for patients who cannot use pharmacotherapy or for post-RP penile-rehabilitation programs. EAU 2026 offers it to well-informed patients requesting drug-free treatment (weak): erections satisfactory for intercourse in up to 90% and satisfaction 27–94%, falling to 50–64% continued use at two years; the constriction ring should come off within 30 minutes to avoid skin necrosis, and EAU 2026 calls VEDs contraindicated with bleeding disorders or anticoagulation; AUA 2018 instead advises caution and use of a vacuum limiter, so assess individual bleeding risk.<sup>[[22]](#ref22)[[12]](#ref12)</sup>
- **Topical MED3000 (Eroxon).** An FDA-cleared, non-prescription topical device (2023) applied to the glans with no active pharmacologic ingredient: a combination of volatile solvents whose evaporation produces a cooling-then-warming sensation; nitric-oxide release and smooth-muscle relaxation are proposed, unproven downstream effects. In FM71, a 96-man, 24-week randomized open-label study, 59% of MED3000 users versus 83% of tadalafil 5 mg users met the study’s severity-adjusted IIEF-EF response criterion, while 44.9% versus 30.0% of intercourse attempts had a noticeable erection within 10 minutes; these exploratory arm comparisons do not establish comparative superiority. The 2.8% MED3000 headache rate came from a separate trial, whereas 19.1% was the tadalafil rate in FM71, so these rates should not be compared head to head. Because it is sold without a prescription, men may bypass the clinician visit that would otherwise screen for the cardiovascular and endocrine risk factors ED often signals.<sup>[[38]](#ref38)</sup>

### 4. Penile prosthesis

Surgically implanted inflatable or malleable penile prostheses may be selected after discussion of all options, including when other treatment is ineffective or unacceptable; EAU 2026 recommends implantation if other treatments fail or according to patient preference, with full counseling on benefits and harms (strong). Penile prosthesis implantation has reported about 92–100% patient and 91–95% partner satisfaction across implant classes; multicomponent inflatable prostheses (IPPs) are also associated with high satisfaction.<sup>[[22]](#ref22)[[10]](#ref10)</sup> AUA advises counseling on postoperative expectations and not operating in the presence of systemic, cutaneous or urinary tract infection.<sup>[[12]](#ref12)</sup> EAU 2026 finds no head-to-head evidence favoring one implant class or approach (level 3) and reports mechanical failure below 5% at five years for modified three-piece devices, survival of 87.2% at five and 76.8% at ten years in a meta-analysis, and infection of 2–3% after primary implantation in low-risk men at high-volume centers (1–2% with coated devices).<sup>[[22]](#ref22)</sup>

For the operative pathway — preoperative evaluation (CURSED), intraoperative setup (no-touch), approaches (PS / IP / SC), reservoir placement (SoR / HSM), models database, infection prevention, revision, and complications — see [Penile Implants](/docs/surgical-techniques/04j-sexual-dysfunction/penile-implants).

### 5. Testosterone replacement therapy

The AUA recommends measuring morning total testosterone in every man with ED (Moderate Recommendation, Grade C).<sup>[[12]](#ref12)</sup> Testosterone deficiency is defined by the AUA as total testosterone below 300 ng/dL on two separate early-morning measurements together with symptoms or signs.<sup>[[26]](#ref26)</sup> In men with confirmed hypogonadism, testosterone replacement improves IIEF erectile-function scores (pooled mean gain about 2.3 IIEF-EF points, below the 4-point threshold assumed by the Cochrane review for its IIEF-EF analysis).<sup>[[19]](#ref19)[[20]](#ref20)[[9]](#ref9)</sup> Benefit is greater in men with more severe hypogonadism (total T &lt; 8 nmol/L, about 230 ng/dL) and smaller with diabetes or obesity; in men without deficiency, testosterone has little or no effect on erectile function.<sup>[[20]](#ref20)[[9]](#ref9)</sup> TRT may be combined with PDE5 inhibitors for enhanced effect in hypogonadal men with ED: the AUA advises that a PDE5 inhibitor may be more effective combined with testosterone (Moderate Recommendation, Grade C; small, mostly low-quality trials), and that testosterone is not an effective monotherapy for ED.<sup>[[20]](#ref20)[[16]](#ref16)[[12]](#ref12)</sup>

The Endocrine Society 2018 guideline recommends against starting testosterone in men planning near-term fertility or with breast or prostate cancer, thrombophilia, untreated severe obstructive sleep apnea, elevated hematocrit, severe LUTS, uncontrolled heart failure, myocardial infarction or stroke within the last six months, or a palpable prostate nodule or PSA above 4 ng/mL (above 3 ng/mL at increased risk) without urologic evaluation. For hypogonadal men being considered for testosterone therapy who are aged 55 to 69 years with a life expectancy over 10 years, or aged 40 to 69 years at increased prostate-cancer risk, it suggests discussing prostate-cancer monitoring, with risk assessment before treatment and 3 to 12 months after starting for those who choose it (recommendation 2.3).<sup>[[19]](#ref19)</sup> TRAVERSE randomized 5,246 symptomatic men aged 45–80 with two testosterone values below 300 ng/dL and existing or high cardiovascular risk (5,204 in the full analysis). Transdermal testosterone was noninferior to placebo for major adverse cardiovascular events; atrial fibrillation, acute kidney injury and pulmonary embolism occurred more often.<sup>[[25]](#ref25)[[21]](#ref21)</sup> See the [testosterone replacement pharmacology hub](/docs/foundations/pharmacology/sexual-medicine-andrology/testosterone-replacement) for the full framework.

### 6. Emerging therapies

- **Low-intensity extracorporeal shockwave therapy (Li-ESWT).** Proposed mechanism: neovascularization and tissue regeneration. The 2025 Cochrane review (21 trials, 1,357 men) found low-certainty evidence: the average short-term erectile-function benefit may be too small to be clinically important; longer-term benefit remains uncertain and heterogeneous. EAU offers a weak recommendation for mild vasculogenic ED, PDE5 inhibitor poor responders and patients who decline oral therapy, whereas AUA considers treatment investigational (Conditional Recommendation, Grade C). This is not established restorative therapy.<sup>[[2]](#ref2)[[18]](#ref18)[[12]](#ref12)</sup>
- **Stem-cell, platelet-rich-plasma (PRP) and botulinum toxin therapy.** The AUA classes intracavernosal stem-cell therapy as investigational and PRP as experimental; EAU limits intracavernosal PRP to clinical trials (level 1b for mild improvement, but no recommendation), finds stem-cell data insufficient for a recommendation, and offers no recommendation for botulinum toxin, whose two randomized trials in non-responders were small. Small positive studies do not establish routine efficacy.<sup>[[18]](#ref18)[[12]](#ref12)</sup>

### 7. Psychosexual therapy

Counseling is recommended for men with psychogenic ED, either as monotherapy or in combination with pharmacotherapy. The AUA advises considering referral to a mental health professional for any man treated for ED, to support adherence, reduce performance anxiety and integrate treatment into the sexual relationship (Moderate Recommendation, Grade C).<sup>[[12]](#ref12)</sup> Couples-based therapy is particularly valuable when relationship dynamics contribute. The 2007 Cochrane review found group therapy plus sildenafil superior to sildenafil alone (RR 0.46 for persistence of ED; two trials, 71 men), and one small trial (20 men) favored group therapy over sildenafil on IIEF.<sup>[[14]](#ref14)</sup> See the canonical [Psychosexual Therapy](/docs/surgical-techniques/04j-sexual-dysfunction/psychosexual-therapy) page for the full sensate focus / CBT / MBI / PLISSIT framework.

## Special Populations

| Population | Key considerations |
|---|---|
| **Post-radical prostatectomy** | Offer erectile-function treatment; the AUA advises counseling that early PDE5 inhibitor use may not improve spontaneous, unassisted function.<sup>[[12]](#ref12)</sup> EAU 2026 concurs (penile rehabilitation with PDE5 inhibitors does not increase spontaneous recovery, level 1a), advises that data are inadequate to support any specific rehabilitation regimen (weak), and still suggests starting pro-erectile treatment at the earliest opportunity after prostatectomy or other curative prostate-cancer treatment (weak).<sup>[[22]](#ref22)</sup> REACTT improved assisted function during treatment but did not establish unassisted recovery after washout. Discuss PDE5i, MUSE, ICI, VED and prosthesis according to goals.<sup>[[24]](#ref24)</sup> |
| **Post-pelvic radiation** | Vasculogenic + neurogenic mechanism; PDE5i first-line; ICI / IPP for non-response |
| **Diabetes mellitus** | High prevalence (50–75%); poorer PDE5i response; lower threshold for second-line<sup>[[17]](#ref17)</sup> |
| **Spinal-cord injury / MS** | Neurogenic mechanism; PDE5i first-line; **autonomic-dysreflexia precaution** in lesions T6 and above (see [Autonomic Dysreflexia](/docs/clinical-conditions/03d-nlutd/autonomic-dysreflexia))<sup>[[15]](#ref15)</sup> |
| **Cardiovascular disease** | Princeton IV-informed risk stratification; **nitrate use is an absolute contraindication** to PDE5i<sup>[[11]](#ref11)</sup> |
| **Young men with focal post-traumatic disease** | Selective candidates for **penile revascularization** (AUA: conditional recommendation for young men with focal arterial occlusion and no generalized vascular or veno-occlusive disease; penile venous surgery is not recommended; EAU 2026 reports 60–70% long-term success after pelvic or perineal trauma, requires confirmation by pharmaco-arteriography, treats veno-occlusive dysfunction as a contraindication and no longer recommends venous ligation)<sup>[[12]](#ref12)[[22]](#ref22)</sup> — see [saphenous vein graft](/docs/foundations/surgical-principles/grafts/saphenous-vein) |
| **Peyronie's disease overlap** | Treat ED with PDE5i first; surgical correction of curvature combined with IPP if both present — see [Peyronie's disease](/docs/clinical-conditions/03g-genital-scrotal/peyronies-disease) |

## Prognosis and Key Takeaways

ED is a treatable condition at all stages. The diagnosis should prompt cardiovascular risk assessment, as it may precede symptomatic CVD by several years (the interval varies by population).<sup>[[7]](#ref7)[[1]](#ref1)[[6]](#ref6)[[8]](#ref8)</sup>

Discuss lifestyle and psychosexual care alongside medication, injection/device therapy and prosthesis. Avoid promising the same success rate across different causes or presenting surgery as a mandatory last step.<sup>[[12]](#ref12)[[22]](#ref22)</sup>

For the reconstructive urologist, ED management runs in parallel with [penile-implant](/docs/surgical-techniques/04j-sexual-dysfunction/penile-implants) and [Peyronie's-disease](/docs/clinical-conditions/03g-genital-scrotal/peyronies-disease) practice. New ED warrants cardiovascular assessment; cardiology referral depends on symptoms, risk and uncertainty rather than being automatic for every patient.
