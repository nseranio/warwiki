# Genital Lymphedema

**Genital lymphedema (GL)** is the accumulation of protein-rich lymphatic fluid in the interstitial tissues of the external genitalia (penis, scrotum, vulva, and/or perineum) from impaired lymphatic drainage. Genital swelling may initially be reversible, while persistent lymphedema can progress and produce discomfort, cosmetic disfigurement, functional disturbances (urinary, sexual, ambulatory), and profound psychosocial impact.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[2]](#ref2)</sup> No standardized treatment algorithm exists; diagnosis and management remain frequently delayed.<sup>[[3]](#ref3)</sup>

For surgical reconstruction technique see [Scrotal Reconstruction](/docs/surgical-techniques/04e-genital-reconstruction/scrotal-reconstruction) and [Scrotal Flap Reconstruction](/docs/surgical-techniques/04e-genital-reconstruction/scrotal-flaps). For the inflammatory dermatologic condition that may coexist or contribute, see [Hidradenitis Suppurativa (GU)](/docs/clinical-conditions/03g-genital-scrotal/hidradenitis-suppurativa).

---

## Epidemiology

- **Lymphatic filariasis** (most common cause globally): *Wuchereria bancrofti* in approximately 90% of infections; older estimates cited approximately 120 million LF cases; approximately 15 million live with chronic filarial lymphedema; scrotal elephantiasis and hydrocele are hallmarks.<sup>[[4]](#ref4)[[5]](#ref5)</sup> Surveys in lymphatic-filariasis-endemic communities reported 1.3% prevalence of lower-limb lymphedema and 7.8% prevalence of filarial lymphedema (98.6% lower limb); neither estimates genital-lymphedema prevalence.<sup>[[4]](#ref4)[[5]](#ref5)[[6]](#ref6)[[7]](#ref7)</sup>
- **Prostate cancer treatment.** After RP, scrotal edema was reported in 1/211 men after extended PLND in one cohort and in 1/202 after extended versus 3/204 after limited PLND in another; older pelvic-irradiation cohorts that included staging PLND reported genital edema or lymphedema in 2–22% of patients.<sup>[[8]](#ref8)</sup>
- **Gynecologic cancer treatment.** GL can accompany lower-limb lymphedema after gynecologic cancer treatment involving pelvic lymph-node excision or radiotherapy.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup>
- **Inguinal / ilioinguinal lymphadenectomy** for melanoma, anal, penile cancer. See [Groin Defect Reconstruction After Inguinal Lymphadenectomy](/docs/surgical-techniques/04e-genital-reconstruction/groin-defect-reconstruction) for groin-wound coverage.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup> After penile-cancer inguinal or pelvic nodal treatment, discuss lymphedema risk, assess for it at follow-up, and refer early to a lymphedema therapist (EAU–ASCO 2026, strong recommendation).<sup>[[60]](#ref60)</sup>
- **Pediatric GL.** 0.6% (25/3889) at a vascular-anomalies center; 92% primary or idiopathic; among 23 primary cases, mean onset was 4.5 yr and 14/23 (60.9%) presented in infancy; among all 25 cases, 18/25 (72%) were penoscrotal and 19/25 (76%) had lower-extremity lymphedema.<sup>[[10]](#ref10)</sup>
- **Obesity-induced lymphedema (OIL).** Risk rises with severe obesity. Selected limb-lymphedema cohorts report BMI thresholds and MLL associations; genital disease risk and a cutoff remain undefined.<sup>[[11]](#ref11)[[12]](#ref12)[[13]](#ref13)</sup>

---

## Etiology and Classification

**Primary GL.** Developmental lymphatic anomalies; congenital, praecox, and tarda; syndromic forms (FOXC2 lymphedema-distichiasis, GATA2 Emberger, Meige, GJC2). In Torio-Padron's 51-man series, 43/51 (84%) had primary lymphedema.<sup>[[16]](#ref16)</sup>

**Secondary GL.** Filariasis; cancer treatment; obesity (OIL or MLL); recurrent cellulitis, STIs, TB; trauma; non-oncologic surgery; Crohn's or ano-genital granulomatosis (in a selected series of 25 men with ano-genital granulomatosis, 10/25 [40%] had intestinal Crohn's and 20/25 [80%] had combined penile and scrotal edema); HS; idiopathic acquired.<sup>[[4]](#ref4)[[8]](#ref8)[[11]](#ref11)[[14]](#ref14)[[18]](#ref18)[[19]](#ref19)</sup>

McDougal 2003: practical division into self-limited (conservative) versus chronic with pathological skin or tissue changes (surgical).<sup>[[15]](#ref15)</sup>

---

## Pathophysiology

Lymphatic obstruction, protein-rich interstitial fluid, and chronic lymph stasis are followed by inflammation, fibrosis, adipose expansion, dysfunctional lymphangiogenesis, and local immunosuppression ("locus minoris resistentiae" predisposing to infection and neoplasia).<sup>[[20]](#ref20)[[21]](#ref21)</sup> Recurrent cellulitis further damages lymphatics in a vicious cycle.<sup>[[22]](#ref22)</sup> In genital MLL specifically: dartos hyperplasia or hypertrophy with microvascular proliferation at the smooth-muscle–stroma interface.<sup>[[23]](#ref23)</sup>

## Staging and Severity

| System | Detail |
|---|---|
| **ISL Stage 0–3** | ISL peripheral-limb staging, applied to genital disease by extension: 0 = latent (subclinical); I = swelling that subsides with elevation (pitting may occur); II = elevation rarely reduces swelling; pitting is present early and may diminish later as fat and fibrosis develop; III = lymphostatic elephantiasis with trophic skin changes.<sup>[[54]](#ref54)</sup> |
| **Genital Lymphedema Score (GLS, Yamamoto)** | 0–9 symptom-based score (urinary trouble, edema, lymphorrhea); correlates with Genital Dermal Backflow (GDB) stage on ICG lymphography. Derived in 32 women with secondary leg lymphedema; later used as an outcome measure in male series.<sup>[[26]](#ref26)</sup> |
| **Ehrl 2023 treatment-oriented classification** | In a nine-man retrospective series, stages I–III distinguished isolated scrotal swelling, penoscrotal swelling, and penoscrotal swelling with buried penis. Stage guided resection and reconstruction extent.<sup>[[28]](#ref28)</sup> |
| **MR-lymphography classification (Lu 2016)** | In a retrospective 36-man cohort, inguinal-node function and lymphatic morphology informed CDP, microsurgery or excision. Patients with moderate dysfunction received different treatments; the study did not validate a stage-to-procedure rule.<sup>[[29]](#ref29)</sup> |

## Clinical Features and Complications

Progressive genital swelling, heaviness, difficulty with ambulation, hygiene, and voiding (buried penis), sexual dysfunction, lymphorrhea, cosmetic disfigurement.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[16]](#ref16)[[30]](#ref30)</sup>

| Complication | Detail |
|---|---|
| **Cellulitis** | The most common complication in a pediatric male series (6/25, 24%). Lymphedema predisposes to cellulitis, and recurrent episodes can worsen lymphatic injury.<sup>[[10]](#ref10)[[21]](#ref21)[[31]](#ref31)</sup> Severe pain out of proportion, rapid spread, crepitus, skin necrosis or systemic toxicity raises concern for necrotizing infection, including Fournier gangrene, and warrants prompt surgical assessment (IDSA strong recommendation, low-quality evidence).<sup>[[56]](#ref56)</sup> |
| Papillomatosis / warty overgrowth | Verrucous chronic skin changes. |
| Lymph vesicles / lymphocutaneous reflux | Superficial lymphatic cysts that rupture; retrograde lymph flow.<sup>[[32]](#ref32)</sup> |
| Recurrent fungal infection | From impaired local immunity. |
| Malignant transformation | **Stewart-Treves lymphangiosarcoma** — rare but devastating; immunosuppression also predisposes to non-melanoma skin cancer.<sup>[[21]](#ref21)[[33]](#ref33)</sup> |

QoL improvement after surgery, mean 1.3 to 7.7 on a 1–10 scale in the MLL series.<sup>[[30]](#ref30)</sup>

## Diagnosis

Clinical examination documents genital edema, peau d'orange and fibrosis; an associated limb can be assessed for a Stemmer sign.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[2]](#ref2)[[34]](#ref34)</sup> In men with genital edema and bowel symptoms, evaluation includes ano-genital granulomatosis and intestinal Crohn disease.<sup>[[18]](#ref18)</sup> In men, scrotal ultrasound can distinguish skin thickening from a coexisting or mimicking hydrocele. In adults without known cancer, new unexplained genital lymphedema warrants abdominal/pelvic CT or MRI to assess for a compressive lesion before a primary diagnosis is made.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup> After cancer treatment, sudden, painful or rapidly progressive genital swelling warrants abdominal/pelvic CT or MRI for recurrence or pelvic obstruction.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup>

| Modality | Role |
|---|---|
| **Lymphoscintigraphy** | Traditional gold standard; ⁹⁹ᵐTc-colloid; delayed / asymmetric drainage, dermal backflow, collateral channels.<sup>[[35]](#ref35)[[36]](#ref36)</sup> |
| **ICG lymphography** | In 169 extremities with secondary lymphedema after lymph-node dissection, ICG sensitivity/specificity versus lymphoscintigraphy were 0.972/0.548; classifying each modality’s mildest pattern as negative yielded 0.978/0.925.<sup>[[36]](#ref36)</sup> Less invasive ICG mapping describes linear → splash → stardust → diffuse patterns and, in genital acquired lymphangiectasia, leg-to-genital (type 1) versus anus-to-genital (type 2) flow.<sup>[[27]](#ref27)[[37]](#ref37)[[38]](#ref38)[[39]](#ref39)</sup> |
| **MR lymphangiography** | Functional + morphological; in 36 men, grading of inguinal-node dysfunction was used to assign treatment (retrospective).<sup>[[29]](#ref29)</sup> |
| **Ultrasound** | Venous duplex evaluates suspected DVT; high-frequency imaging may map superficial lymphatics / perforators for surgery.<sup>[[38]](#ref38)</sup> |
| **Biopsy** | For diagnostic uncertainty / malignancy concern; in the 25-man ano-genital granulomatosis series, 50% of cutaneous biopsies had non-caseating granulomas and 14% had intralymphatic granulomas.<sup>[[18]](#ref18)[[23]](#ref23)</sup> |

## Conservative Management

### Complex Decongestive Therapy (CDT)

The cornerstone; poorly adapted to genital anatomy versus extremities.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[2]](#ref2)[[3]](#ref3)</sup>

| Component | Detail |
|---|---|
| **Manual lymphatic drainage (MLD)** | Genital-anatomy-adapted; partner / self-drainage education.<sup>[[3]](#ref3)[[40]](#ref40)</sup> |
| **Compression** | Scrotal supports, hip spica bandage, panty girdle, individually fitted compression garments; pressure and fit require specialist assessment.<sup>[[2]](#ref2)[[3]](#ref3)</sup> In a 32-person prospective single-arm garment study, 22/28 respondents (78.6%) reported improvement on the patient global impression of change at 12 weeks; three participants stopped use because of swelling or discomfort.<sup>[[63]](#ref63)</sup> |
| **Exercise** | Muscle and joint pumps are part of CDT; encourage gradual aerobic and resistance activity as tolerated. Limb evidence supports progressive training without worsening limb lymphedema, but genital-specific effects have not been clearly evaluated.<sup>[[24]](#ref24)[[1]](#ref1)[[54]](#ref54)</sup> |
| **Skin care** | Meticulous hygiene; treat fungi; moisturize.<sup>[[3]](#ref3)</sup> |

Integrated approach (Torio-Padron n = 51): perioperative CDP with excision and primary closure; 3 of 51 (6%) needed revision surgery (2 hematoma, 1 dehiscence).<sup>[[16]](#ref16)</sup> Yaman 2024 reported reduced scrotal measurements after six CDT sessions over 14 days in one HS-related GL case treated concurrently with ixekizumab.<sup>[[19]](#ref19)</sup>

### Pneumatic compression devices

A possible adjunct to CDT. Evidence and expert consensus largely concern extremity disease; limb-only pumps can displace fluid into the genitalia, so monitor proximal swelling and select coverage carefully.<sup>[[54]](#ref54)</sup><sup>[[22]](#ref22)[[42]](#ref42)</sup>

### Pharmacotherapy

- **Ketoprofen.** Exploratory extremity-lymphedema trials improved skin thickness and histology but not limb volume or bioimpedance. Genital benefit was not assessed; ketoprofen is not routine therapy.<sup>[[53]](#ref53)</sup>
- **Low-dose systemic corticosteroids.** Fujimoto 2014 reported arrest of rapid progression in one patient with acquired idiopathic penile lymphedema and histologic lymphocytic inflammation; this single case does not support routine therapy.<sup>[[14]](#ref14)</sup>
- **Immunomodulators (Crohn's or ano-genital granulomatosis).** In a retrospective 25-man ano-genital granulomatosis series, 13/18 (72%) given oral steroids, usually with antibiotics, reported initial improvement in swelling, often followed by relapse on taper; 9/15 (60%) given azathioprine reported partial or complete improvement; and 3/6 men with concurrent Crohn disease given anti-TNF agents reported improvement.<sup>[[18]](#ref18)</sup>
- **Antifilarials.** Treat confirmed active infection with specialist guidance; chronic lymphedema alone is not an indication for DEC.<sup>[[41]](#ref41)[[55]](#ref55)</sup> DEC is contraindicated when onchocerciasis is possible.<sup>[[55]](#ref55)</sup> With possible *Loa loa* coinfection, quantify blood microfilariae before DEC; 8,000 or more microfilariae/mL calls for specialist-directed load reduction before treatment because of severe neurologic and renal risk.<sup>[[55]](#ref55)[[62]](#ref62)</sup>
- **Recurrent cellulitis.** Address edema, skin breaks, tinea and other predisposing factors first. IDSA 2014 (weak recommendation, moderate evidence) considers penicillin or erythromycin prophylaxis after 3–4 episodes/year despite risk-factor treatment.<sup>[[56]](#ref56)</sup> BLS/LSN 2025 lymphedema guidance considers prophylaxis after two or more bacterial cellulitis episodes/year with specialist review and identifies penicillin V as first-line prophylaxis for recurrent anogenital cellulitis.<sup>[[59]](#ref59)</sup> Leg-cellulitis trials support the principle: penicillin V 250 mg twice daily for 12 months reduced recurrence during treatment (30/136 [22%] versus 51/138 [37%], HR 0.55; PATCH I)<sup>[[58]](#ref58)</sup>, and pooled data show moderate-certainty benefit during treatment that appears to diminish after stopping<sup>[[57]](#ref57)</sup>; BLS/LSN recommends specialist review and consideration of discontinuation after one successful year, with continuation considered if substantial risk factors persist.<sup>[[59]](#ref59)</sup> These trials enrolled leg cellulitis (lymphedema was a subgroup), not genital disease, and prophylaxis is not indicated for every genital-lymphedema patient.<sup>[[56]](#ref56)[[57]](#ref57)[[58]](#ref58)</sup>

### Weight management — fundamental for obesity-induced GL

Weight management is important, though established lymphatic injury / fibrosis may persist after weight loss; animal reversibility may not translate to humans. Continue a structured weight-management plan after excision.<sup>[[13]](#ref13)[[30]](#ref30)</sup>

## Surgical Management

Reported outcomes below come mainly from small, heterogeneous retrospective series without direct comparisons of efficacy or complication risk. After excision, patients continue skin care and individualized compression, with follow-up for recurrent swelling or lymphorrhea.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[54]](#ref54)</sup>

Two complementary categories: excisional or debulking, and physiologic or reconstructive; frequently combined in modern algorithms.<sup>[[43]](#ref43)[[44]](#ref44)</sup>

For symptomatic vulvar lymphedema with hypertrophic labial tissue, described resections remove affected labia minora or majora while sparing the clitoris. For focal symptomatic vulvar or scrotal lymph vesicles that does not require skin resection, electrocautery, cryosurgery and CO₂ laser ablation have been reported.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup>

### Excisional / debulking

| Procedure | Outcomes |
|---|---|
| **Modified Charles procedure and related excisional reconstruction** | Modolin 2006 n = 17 (modified Charles with penile STSG) — symptom regression, 1 recurrence (RT + PLND patient); Salako 2018 n = 11 (modified Charles) — hematoma 27.3%, SSI 18.2%, recurrence 9.1%; Singh 2011 n = 48 filarial (14 isolated penile excision with grafting; 34 penoscrotal scrotoplasty) — all reported satisfactory cosmesis and functional improvement; groin infection 12/48 (25%).<sup>[[17]](#ref17)[[45]](#ref45)[[46]](#ref46)</sup> |
| **Excision + primary closure** | Torio-Padron n = 51 (43/51 primary etiology): all defects were closed with adjacent skin in a perioperative CDP series; 22/51 (43%) also underwent hydrocelectomy, 3/51 (6%) required revision for hematoma or dehiscence, and 5/51 (10%) underwent surgery for scrotal recurrence. Wisenbaugh n = 11 MLL: 10 primary closures, 1 graft; mean 21 kg resected, QoL 1.3 → 7.7, but most gained weight after surgery (mean follow-up 26 months).<sup>[[16]](#ref16)[[30]](#ref30)</sup> |
| **Excision + flap reconstruction** | Guiotto SR: **54.2% reported complications** in the review’s flap subgroup; severity and selection differences prevent a causal comparison.<sup>[[43]](#ref43)</sup> |
| **MLL excision in obesity** | Machol n = 4 — lateral scrotal flaps ± mid-raphe Z-plasty; 2/4 patients had recurrence during follow-up.<sup>[[47]](#ref47)</sup> |
| **Liposuction / SAPL** | Addresses the solid (adipose / fibrotic) component of late ISL stage II–III; most evidence concerns limb disease and ongoing compression; those results cannot be used as a genital-specific volume-reduction prediction.<sup>[[54]](#ref54)</sup> |

### Physiologic / reconstructive

| Procedure | Outcomes |
|---|---|
| **Lymphaticovenous anastomosis (LVA)** | Guiotto SR: **9% reported complications** in the review’s LVA subgroup; not a randomized comparison.<sup>[[43]](#ref43)</sup> After genital acquired lymphangiectasia resection, Hara and Mihara retrospectively studied 25 women: lymphatic-flow mapping guided leg LVA alone in 17 and genital LVA in 8; recurrence occurred in 7/17 versus 3/8 over mean 285-day follow-up.<sup>[[39]](#ref39)</sup> Mukenge described spermatic-funiculus lymphatic-to-pampiniform-vein anastomosis in five of 11 men with secondary genital lymphedema, with patency assessed through 12 months.<sup>[[61]](#ref61)</sup> Genital comparative evidence remains limited.<sup>[[25]](#ref25)[[33]](#ref33)[[35]](#ref35)[[38]](#ref38)</sup> |
| **Vascularized lymph node transfer (VLNT)** | Ehrl 2023: all 9 underwent excision/reconstruction; 5 also had VLNT (3 groin, 2 scrotal). The 2 scrotal recipients showed improved scrotal lymphatic transport on imaging at 3–4 months; their clinical follow-up was 16 and 18 months. No recurrence occurred among all 9 at median 49 months (range 16–67), with or without VLNT.<sup>[[28]](#ref28)</sup> Counsel about donor-site lymphedema; reverse lymphatic mapping may reduce this risk.<sup>[[54]](#ref54)</sup> |
| **SCIP Lymphatic Flap Transfer — "3R" (Yamamoto 2022)** | Radical reduction + reconstruction with a pedicled full-thickness SCIP lymphatic flap in all 7 patients and an added SCIP pure-skin-perforator flap for penile reconstruction in 3. n = 7 (4 scrotal, 3 penoscrotal); mean resected 1,511 g; **no postoperative genital complications or genital recurrence** during mean 22.7 mo follow-up; GLS 6.7 → 0.3.<sup>[[48]](#ref48)</sup> |
| **Complete Functional Lymphatic System Pedicled Transfer (Abdelfattah 2023)** | In 26 men with advanced scrotal or penoscrotal lymphedema treated with excision and pedicled lymphatic transfer, all flaps survived; recorded cellulitis rates declined (p &lt; 0.001) over mean 44.9 months of follow-up.<sup>[[49]](#ref49)</sup> |
| **CHASCIP — Combined Charles + Lymphatic SCIP flap (Ciudad 2025)** | n = 8 ISL Stage III; bilateral pedicled lymphatic SCIP flaps + STSG; mean resected 1,772.7 g; 25% complications; no recurrence in 8/8 during mean 34-month follow-up; sexual dysfunction 87.5% → 0%.<sup>[[50]](#ref50)</sup> |
| **Lymphatic System Transfer (LYST)** | SCIP flap with both lymph nodes and afferent lymphatic vessels intact; Yoshimatsu 2025 n = 8 unilateral lower-extremity lymphedema (not genital) — mean excess-volume improvement 11.2% at last follow-up (mean follow-up 39 mo), cellulitis significantly decreased (p = 0.025); Xu 2026 — pedicled SCIP LYST for lower-limb lymphedema with chronic venous disease; the pedicled flap avoids a vascular microanastomosis, but five of six patients also underwent distal lymphovenous bypass.<sup>[[51]](#ref51)[[52]](#ref52)</sup> |

## Individualized Treatment Framework

The following are clinical patterns, not validated genital-specific stage-to-procedure rules. GLS symptom scores and ISL tissue stages are not interchangeable treatment thresholds.

| Severity | Approach |
|---|---|
| **Early or predominantly fluid swelling** | CDT; weight management when obesity contributes; antifilarials for confirmed active filarial infection; disease-specific immunomodulators for diagnosed ano-genital granulomatosis or Crohn's disease under specialist care. No routine disease-modifying drug therapy for chronic genital lymphedema is established.<sup>[[13]](#ref13)[[18]](#ref18)[[54]](#ref54)[[55]](#ref55)</sup> |
| **Persistent swelling with tissue change** | Specialist CDT; assess pneumatic compression for proximal fluid displacement. Consider prophylactic antibiotics only for recurrent cellulitis despite risk-factor treatment. Imaging, tissue quality and expertise inform selected LVA, VLNT or excisional surgery.<sup>[[16]](#ref16)[[25]](#ref25)[[42]](#ref42)</sup> |
| **Advanced fibrosis / elephantiasis** | Excision with primary closure, graft or flap reconstruction according to tissue quality; selected centers may add **SCIP lymphatic flap transfer or VLNT**. Perioperative CDP.<sup>[[26]](#ref26)[[40]](#ref40)[[47]](#ref47)[[48]](#ref48)[[50]](#ref50)</sup> |
| **Obesity-induced GL / MLL** | Weight loss is fundamental; comprehensive weight-loss plan must accompany excision (Wisenbaugh).<sup>[[11]](#ref11)[[24]](#ref24)[[28]](#ref28)[[47]](#ref47)</sup> |
| **Crohn's / ano-genital granulomatosis** | Coordinate disease-specific treatment with gastroenterology / dermatology; no universal steroid–azathioprine–anti-TNF ladder is established for genital disease. Selected refractory lesions may need reconstruction.<sup>[[18]](#ref18)</sup> |
