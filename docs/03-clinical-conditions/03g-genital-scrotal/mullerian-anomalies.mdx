# Müllerian Anomalies & Vaginal Agenesis

**Müllerian anomalies** encompass a spectrum of congenital developmental defects of the female reproductive tract arising from abnormal formation, fusion, or resorption of the paramesonephric (Müllerian) ducts, affecting about 5% of the general population (5.5% in the pooled estimate), 8% of infertile women, 13.3% of those with pregnancy loss, and 24.5% of those with both pregnancy loss and infertility.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[38]](#ref38)</sup> **Vaginal agenesis**, most commonly seen in Mayer-Rokitansky-Küster-Hauser (MRKH) syndrome, is managed first-line with nonsurgical vaginal dilation (90–96% success), with surgical neovagina creation (McIndoe, Davydov, Vecchietti, bowel vaginoplasty) considered when dilation is unsuccessful or declined after informed counseling.<sup>[[2]](#ref2)</sup>

This article is the cisgender-congenital counterpart to [Feminizing Genital Reconstruction](/docs/special-populations/05c-gender-affirming/feminizing-procedures); the same operative techniques appear in both contexts but with different anatomic starting points and lifelong-care considerations. For operative technique, the Treatment Atlas carries the relevant neovagina-creation pages: McIndoe maps to [Skin Graft Vaginoplasty](/docs/surgical-techniques/04k-gender-affirming-surgery/skin-graft-vaginoplasty), Davydov to [Peritoneal Pull-Through Vaginoplasty](/docs/surgical-techniques/04k-gender-affirming-surgery/peritoneal-pull-through-vaginoplasty), and bowel or sigmoid colpoplasty to [Intestinal Vaginoplasty](/docs/surgical-techniques/04k-gender-affirming-surgery/intestinal-vaginoplasty). For the geometry of vaginal-septum repair, see [Z-plasty](/docs/foundations/surgical-principles/flaps/z-plasty); for sigmoid-segment selection in bowel vaginoplasty, see [Bowel Anatomy](/docs/foundations/anatomy-physiology/pelvis-support/bowel-anatomy).

## Embryology of Müllerian Duct Developmen

The Müllerian (paramesonephric) ducts form the fallopian tubes, uterus, cervix, and upper vagina. Normal development involves three sequential phases:<sup>[[3]](#ref3)[[4]](#ref4)</sup>

1. **Organogenesis** (approximately 6 weeks). Müllerian ducts form as invaginations of the coelomic epithelium lateral to the Wolffian (mesonephric) ducts, regulated by BMP/Pax2 and FGF/Lim1 signaling cascades.<sup>[[5]](#ref5)</sup>
2. **Fusion** (approximately 8–12 weeks). The paired ducts migrate medially and fuse in the midline to form the uterovaginal canal.
3. **Septal resorption.** The fused midline septum undergoes apoptosis, creating a single uterine cavity.

Disruption at any stage produces the spectrum of Müllerian anomalies. Genes implicated include WNT4, PAX2, LHX1, HNF1B, and HOXA gene clusters.<sup>[[3]](#ref3)[[6]](#ref6)[[7]](#ref7)</sup> In males, anti-Müllerian hormone (AMH) from Sertoli cells causes Müllerian duct regression; mutations in AMH or AMHR2 cause persistent Müllerian duct syndrome.<sup>[[8]](#ref8)</sup> See also the broader [GU Embryology](/docs/foundations/anatomy-physiology/pelvis-support/gu-embryology) reference.<sup>[[9]](#ref9)</sup>

## Classification of Müllerian Anomalies

The **ASRM Müllerian Anomalies Classification 2021 (MAC2021)** updated the 1988 AFS system, using descriptive terminology rather than numbered categories and incorporating vaginal and cervical anomalies. It added three groups to the AFS categories (longitudinal vaginal septum, transverse vaginal septum and complex anomalies), and some anomalies appear in more than one category, especially the vaginal ones.<sup>[[10]](#ref10)</sup>

| Category | Description | Key Features |
|---|---|---|
| **Müllerian agenesis** | Absent uterus and cervix with variable hypoplasia of the upper vagina | MRKH syndrome; atrophic uterine remnants may be present, with or without functional endometrium |
| **Cervical agenesis** | Absent or severely underdeveloped cervix | May cause outflow obstruction despite a functional uterine body |
| **Unicornuate uterus** | One Müllerian duct fails to develop | ± rudimentary horn (communicating or non-communicating) |
| **Uterus didelphys** | Complete non-fusion | Two separate uteri, two cervices; ± longitudinal vaginal septum |
| **Bicornuate uterus** | Partial non-fusion | Two uterine horns; external fundal indentation &gt; 1 cm; cervical anatomy varies |
| **Septate uterus** | Failed septal resorption | Most common anomaly in high-risk (infertility / pregnancy-loss) groups; arcuate is commonest in unselected women;<sup>[[38]](#ref38)</sup> endometrial septum &gt; 1 cm from the bicornual line, leading angle &lt; 90°; assess the external contour. MAC2021 allows any imaging technique for the measurement and treats an arcuate uterus as clinically insignificant |
| **Longitudinal vaginal septum** | Failure of vaginal fusion / resorption | May be obstructing or non-obstructing |
| **Transverse vaginal septum** | Failure of canalization | Obstructing → hematocolpos at menarche |
| **Complex anomalies** | Combined elements | Cross-referenced between categories |

Other classification systems include the **ESHRE/ESGE system** (uterine classes U0–U6: normal, dysmorphic, septate, bicorporeal, hemi-uterus, aplastic, unclassified; cervical and vaginal anomalies classified separately)<sup>[[39]](#ref39)</sup> and the **VCUAM system** (Vagina, Cervix, Uterus, Adnexa, associated Malformations).<sup>[[10]](#ref10)</sup>

U0 normal uterus; U1 dysmorphic uterus (a, T-shaped; b, infantilis; c, others); U2 septate uterus (a, partial; b, complete), defined by an internal indentation more than 50% of the uterine wall thickness with a straight external contour or an external indentation under 50%; U3 bicorporeal uterus (a, partial; b, complete; c, bicorporeal septate), defined by an external indentation more than 50% of the wall thickness; U4 hemi-uterus (a, with and b, without rudimentary cavity); U5 aplastic uterus (a, with and b, without rudimentary cavity); U6 unclassified cases. For uterine-shape assessment, the 2024 ASRM guideline recommends three-dimensional transvaginal ultrasound, with or without saline infusion, as first-line noninvasive imaging (Moderate recommendation, evidence B). It recommends offering hysteroscopic septum incision after recurrent miscarriage through shared decision-making (Moderate, B); for infertility alone, incision may be offered, but live-birth benefit remains uncertain.<sup>[[51]](#ref51)</sup> The 2025 Cochrane review found low-certainty evidence of little or no live-birth difference versus expectant management in one randomized trial of 79 women (OR 0.83, 95% CI 0.32–2.11).<sup>[[11]](#ref11)</sup>

## Mayer-Rokitansky-Küster-Hauser (MRKH) Syndrome

### Epidemiology and Presentation

MRKH syndrome is the most common cause of vaginal agenesis, with a prevalence of 1 in 4,500–5,000 live female births and the second most common cause of primary amenorrhea (10–15% of cases).<sup>[[2]](#ref2)[[12]](#ref12)[[13]](#ref13)[[14]](#ref14)</sup> Patients have a 46,XX karyotype, normally functioning ovaries, and normal secondary sexual characteristics. The diagnosis is typically made during adolescence when menstruation fails to begin.<sup>[[2]](#ref2)[[13]](#ref13)</sup>

### Classification<sup>[[13]](#ref13)[[14]](#ref14)</sup>

- **Type I** (isolated). Uterovaginal aplasia without extragenital anomalies; 78.9% in one South Indian tertiary-center cohort, with proportions varying across cohorts.<sup>[[12]](#ref12)[[14]](#ref14)</sup>
- **Type II** (associated). Extragenital anomalies present, including:
  - **Renal anomalies** (15–31%). Unilateral renal agenesis, ectopic or pelvic kidney, horseshoe kidney.<sup>[[12]](#ref12)[[14]](#ref14)[[15]](#ref15)</sup>
  - **Skeletal anomalies.** One MRKH cohort found abnormalities in 59/184 patients who underwent skeletal imaging (32%); reported defects include Klippel-Feil anomaly and scoliosis.<sup>[[14]](#ref14)</sup>
  - **MURCS association.** Müllerian duct aplasia, renal agenesis, and cervicothoracic somite dysplasia; reported in 16.5% of one South Indian tertiary-center cohort.<sup>[[12]](#ref12)</sup>
  - Hearing loss, cardiac defects.<sup>[[13]](#ref13)</sup>

### Diagnostic Workup<sup>[[2]](#ref2)</sup>

- **Laboratory.** Testosterone, FSH, karyotype (to exclude androgen insensitivity syndrome).
- **Imaging.** Transabdominal or translabial ultrasound initially; MRI is the gold standard for characterizing Müllerian remnants, endometrial activity, and associated anomalies. Rudimentary Müllerian structures are found in 90% of patients on MRI.
- **Evaluation for associated anomalies** is necessary; renal imaging is routine (MAC2021 strongly recommends it, and reports a renal anomaly incidence that may approach 40% with Müllerian agenesis, from low-quality case-series literature), and up to 53% have concomitant congenital malformations.
- Laparoscopy is not required for diagnosis but may be useful for pelvic pain evaluation or excision of obstructed uterine horns with active endometrium.

### Genetics

The etiology remains largely elusive, though familial clustering suggests genetic causes with autosomal-dominant inheritance, reduced penetrance, and variable expressivity.<sup>[[6]](#ref6)[[16]](#ref16)</sup> Candidate genes include HNF1B (17q12 deletion), LHX1, WNT4, and PAX2. Loss of HNF1B function in Müllerian duct epithelium has been shown to reproduce the MRKH type II phenotype (uterine hypoplasia and renal anomalies) in mouse models.<sup>[[3]](#ref3)[[6]](#ref6)</sup>

## Obstructive Müllerian Anomalies

Obstruction determines when a patient presents and how urgently she needs surgery. Imperforate hymen, transverse vaginal septum, distal vaginal atresia, cervical atresia, and a non-communicating uterine horn or hemivagina present after menarche with amenorrhea, cyclic or progressive pelvic pain, hematocolpos or hematometra, or a pelvic mass.<sup>[[40]](#ref40)</sup>

- **Examination first.** Inspection distinguishes imperforate hymen (bulging bluish membrane) from labial adhesions, urogenital sinus, transverse septum and distal vaginal atresia. Pelvic ultrasound is the initial study; MRI defines septal thickness, cervical presence and associated anomalies. Prepubertal imaging can mislead because the uterus is small and unstimulated, so an experienced radiologist helps.<sup>[[40]](#ref40)</sup>
- **Timing.** ACOG states these anomalies are generally not surgical emergencies, but urinary retention, severe pain, and ascending infection are indications to expedite surgery.<sup>[[40]](#ref40)</sup> Planned surgery after full evaluation gives the best long-term result. Transverse septum, distal vaginal atresia and cervical atresia carry a high risk of stenosis and complications, so referral to an experienced center is advised.<sup>[[40]](#ref40)</sup> A scoping review of 41 studies (152 transverse-septum cases) found the published literature sparse and heterogeneous, but concluded that delaying resection until the patient is old enough to participate in postoperative dilation, when pain is otherwise controlled, improves outcomes; of 21 reported cases of postoperative stenosis, 11 occurred without postoperative dilation.<sup>[[49]](#ref49)</sup> Imperforate hymen is the simple end of the spectrum: hymenotomy or hymenectomy.
- **Imperforate hymen incision.** In a symptomatic newborn with confirmed imperforate hymen and obstructing hydrocolpos, definitive hymenal opening may be needed; a transverse incision has been described to avoid anterior urethral or posterior rectal injury. For an asymptomatic prepubertal patient, confirm the anatomy and defer surgery until pubertal estrogenization; aspiration alone does not provide definitive drainage.<sup>[[40]](#ref40)[[50]](#ref50)[[52]](#ref52)</sup> In a pubertal patient, excision of the redundant hymenal tissue under general anesthesia is the usual approach.<sup>[[50]](#ref50)</sup>
- **Transverse vaginal septum.** A retrospective series of 46 patients found that septa resected vaginally or laparoscopically had low complication rates and good long-term outcomes, while complex septa needed more extensive surgery with a higher complication risk; a thin, perforate septum (&lt; 1 cm) was typically resected vaginally, a mid or high septum &lt; 2 cm with an adequately distended proximal vagina and no other complex pelvic anomaly could be resected laparoscopically, and a septum &gt; 2 cm or an imperforate septum more often needed an abdomino-perineal approach, occasionally with a bridging segment. A temporary vaginal mould after thick-septum excision may help limit scarring, and dilation is recommended after laparoscopic or abdomino-perineal repair, especially when skin flaps are used, though evidence for dilation itself is limited.<sup>[[45]](#ref45)</sup> For a confirmed high septum causing pain, ultrasound-guided decompression of the hematocolpos with a large-bore (12–14-gauge) needle before definitive repair has been described as a selected temporizing measure, not a routine step; viscous blood may need repeated saline irrigation, and the maneuver may increase the risk of upper-genital-tract infection.<sup>[[43]](#ref43)</sup> For a thick septum, ultrasound-guided access with a 14-gauge angiocatheter, a guidewire and a balloon dilator to create an aperture before excision has been described; this is a single center's reported technique, not a comparative standard.<sup>[[50]](#ref50)</sup>
- **Distal vaginal atresia.** With a patent upper vagina, a transverse incision at the hymenal ring and dissection through the atretic segment to the upper-vaginal mucosa has been described; a distended upper vagina holding retained menstrual blood can itself provide tissue expansion, and the mobilized vagina is then brought down to the introitus, with perineal skin flaps if needed to bridge the gap. A combined abdominoperineal approach is used when exposure requires it.<sup>[[50]](#ref50)</sup>
- **OHVIRA (Herlyn-Werner-Wunderlich).** Uterus didelphys with an obstructed hemivagina and ipsilateral renal agenesis or anomaly. In a 2025 meta-analysis of 526 patients, symptom onset averaged 14.5 years and diagnosis 16.4 years (diagnostic delay). Abdominal pain (67%) and dysmenorrhea (64%) were the commonest symptoms, and pelvic endometriosis occurred in 20%. Vaginal septum resection is the standard treatment (83% of reported surgeries), with a hysteroscopic route reported in a smaller group; the pooled studies are case series.<sup>[[41]](#ref41)</sup> Any girl with unilateral renal agenesis should have her Müllerian anatomy considered; apparent agenesis may conceal a dysplastic kidney with an ectopic ureter. In a three-case OHVIRA series, all three patients had persistent vaginal drainage after septum resection despite the kidney not being visualized on imaging.<sup>[[53]](#ref53)</sup> MAC2021 reports ipsilateral renal agenesis or anomaly in up to 95% of obstructed hemivagina, and states that renal imaging is strongly recommended with Müllerian agenesis and with unilateral obstructed vaginal or uterine anomalies.<sup>[[10]](#ref10)</sup> For longitudinal septum excision, leaving a small anterior remnant to protect the bladder has been described, since full excision of the upper septum is often unnecessary; in OHVIRA, removing the entire inferior margin of the septum avoids a residual pocket that retains menstrual blood or cervical secretions and causes intermenstrual bleeding or discharge.<sup>[[43]](#ref43)</sup>
- **Cervical agenesis or atresia with a functioning uterus** is the main indication for uterovaginal continuity procedures (see utero-colo-neovaginoplasty below), and outcomes are less predictable than for septum excision. Laparoscopic uterovaginal anastomosis with a silicone stent to maintain patency has been described; in a 14-patient series (mean follow-up 3.8 years), 92.8% resumed regular menses, one patient required hysterectomy for genital infection and restenosis, and 3 of 5 sexually active patients achieved pregnancy. Longer-term reproductive outcomes need further study.<sup>[[46]](#ref46)</sup>

## Management of Vaginal Agenesis

### First-Line: Nonsurgical Vaginal Dilation

ACOG recommends primary vaginal dilation as first-line therapy, with reported anatomic and functional success of 90–96% in well-counseled, emotionally prepared patients. ACOG defines functional success by comfortable sexual activity as reported by the patient, with no required vaginal length.<sup>[[2]](#ref2)</sup> Two approaches exist:

- **Frank method.** Progressive self-dilation using graduated dilators applied to the vaginal dimple for 20–30 minutes daily. Mean vaginal depth achieved: 7.3 cm in the Danish nationwide cohort, which also recorded a 35% overall complication rate in the self-dilation group.<sup>[[17]](#ref17)[[18]](#ref18)</sup> The 90–96% figure is ACOG's estimate for well-counseled, emotionally prepared patients; population-level results vary with counseling and adherence.
- **Coital dilation.** In a historical, nonrandomized Danish cohort, 20 patients using coital dilation had a mean vaginal depth of 8.7 cm and one recorded complication (5%); these data do not establish superiority over other methods. It is an option when the patient desires comfortable, consensual penetrative intercourse, never an obligation.<sup>[[17]](#ref17)</sup>

A small comparative study found no significant difference in vaginal length or FSFI; a nonsignificant result does not establish equivalence.<sup>[[18]](#ref18)</sup>

### Second-Line: Surgical Neovagina Creation

Surgery is reserved for patients who fail dilation or prefer surgery after thorough informed consent. ACOG emphasizes that surgery does not eliminate the need for postoperative dilation, and failure to comply with post-surgical dilation can have deleterious effects. Referral to experienced centers is necessary, as the initial procedure is more likely to succeed than revision surgery.<sup>[[2]](#ref2)</sup>

Beyond the McIndoe, Davydov, Vecchietti and bowel techniques detailed below, older and investigational alternatives exist. Williams' vulvovaginoplasty creates a canal from medial labia-majora skin without dissecting the rectovaginal space; the resulting axis is altered, depth is limited, and an elevated perineum can deflect the urinary stream or cause postvoid dribbling. The Creatsas modification lowers the perineal flap to avoid this voiding problem and has been reported in a single-center series of 200 patients with favorable results.<sup>[[47]](#ref47)</sup> A small pilot study implanted autologous tissue-engineered vaginal organs, biodegradable scaffolds seeded with each patient's own epithelial and muscle cells, in four patients with MRKH-related vaginal aplasia; over up to 8 years of follow-up, yearly biopsies showed a trilayered epithelium-muscle structure with no long-term surgical complications, and Female Sexual Function Index scores were in the normal range. This is proof-of-concept evidence from one small, noncomparative cohort, not a validated alternative to the techniques below.<sup>[[48]](#ref48)</sup>

## McIndoe Vaginoplasty (Modified Abbé-McIndoe)

### Technique<sup>[[2]](#ref2)[[19]](#ref19)</sup>

The historically most common surgical approach:

1. **Perineal dissection.** A space is created between the bladder anteriorly and rectum posteriorly using electrocautery, sharp dissection, and blunt finger dissection to the level of the peritoneal reflection. When the perineum is flat, starting through a 3–4 cm posterior-fourchette incision anterior to the anal sphincter, with a rectal finger guiding the dissection plane, has been described.<sup>[[43]](#ref43)</sup>
2. **Graft harvest.** A split-thickness skin graft (typically 10 × 20 cm) is harvested from the buttock or thigh.
3. **Mold placement.** The graft is secured over a condom-covered rubber-sponge mold, which is inserted into the dissected space and held in place with labial stay sutures.
4. **Second stage** (approximately 14 days). The mold is removed, graft take is assessed, and distal edges are secured. A polyethylene mold replaces the sponge mold for continued healing.
5. **Postoperative dilation.** Lifelong intermittent dilation or regular intercourse is required to maintain vaginal patency.

### Modifications

- **Micromucosa graft technique.** In a seven-patient MRKH series, minced vulvar mucosa provided reported epithelial coverage about 20 times the harvested area with minimal visible scarring; four sexually active patients reported spontaneous lubrication at 6–24 months of follow-up. Buccal mucosa was described as an adjunct when vulvar tissue was insufficient.<sup>[[20]](#ref20)</sup>
- **Oxidized cellulose** has been used instead of skin graft in a modified Abbé-McIndoe operation. In a 20-patient nonrandomized study, the oxidized-cellulose surgery group (n = 11) and Frank-dilation group (n = 9) did not differ significantly in vaginal length or FSFI at 3–6 months; this did not compare cellulose with a skin graft.<sup>[[18]](#ref18)</sup>

### Outcomes<sup>[[2]](#ref2)[[17]](#ref17)[[18]](#ref18)</sup>

The McIndoe estimates below describe 54 patients in a historical Danish cohort, not the expected complication rate of every contemporary operation:

- Mean vaginal depth: 7.4 cm (95% CI 6.8–8.1).
- Overall complication rate: 65%.
- Resurgery rate: 33%.
- Complications include graft necrosis, hair-bearing vaginal skin, stenosis, bladder / rectal perforation, and fistulae.

## Davydov Vaginoplasty

### Technique<sup>[[2]](#ref2)[[21]](#ref21)</sup>

Originally a three-stage open procedure, now predominantly performed laparoscopically in 8 steps:

1. **Perineal dissection.** The rectovesical space is dissected from below.
2. **Laparoscopic peritoneal mobilization.** The pelvic peritoneum is incised and mobilized as flaps.
3. **Peritoneal-vaginal anastomosis.** The peritoneal flaps are pulled down through the dissected space and sutured to the vaginal introitus.
4. **Vault reconstruction.** The peritoneal dome is closed laparoscopically to create the neovaginal apex.
5. **Mold placement.** An intravaginal dilator is left in place; leaving a soft vaginal stent for approximately 1 week, then removing it and asking the patient to dilate or attempt intercourse to maintain patency, has been described.<sup>[[44]](#ref44)</sup>

The main advantage is the use of autologous pelvic peritoneum as the lining tissue, avoiding the need for skin grafts, bowel segments, or specialized equipment.<sup>[[21]](#ref21)</sup>

### Outcomes<sup>[[15]](#ref15)[[22]](#ref22)[[23]](#ref23)</sup>

- Mean neovaginal length at 12 months: 8.3 cm (95% CI 8.1–8.6).<sup>[[22]](#ref22)</sup>
- Functional vaginal depth (long-term, 40-year series): 7.8 cm (range 1–13 cm).<sup>[[15]](#ref15)</sup>
- Mean FSFI score: 28.9 (95% CI 26.8–31.1); a group average cannot establish absence of sexual dysfunction in each patient.<sup>[[22]](#ref22)</sup>
- Mean operative time: 126 minutes (laparoscopic).<sup>[[22]](#ref22)[[23]](#ref23)</sup>
- Major complications: granulation tissue (23%) and tendency to obliterate (12%) are the primary concerns.<sup>[[15]](#ref15)</sup>
- Complete epithelialization: 80% at 6 months, 100% at 12 months.<sup>[[23]](#ref23)</sup>

## Vecchietti Procedure

A traction-based technique in which an acrylic olive is placed at the vaginal dimple and connected via sutures passed through the rectovesical space to an external traction device on the abdominal wall. Continuous traction (approximately 1 cm/day for 7–10 days) progressively invaginates the vaginal dimple to create a neovagina. Now performed laparoscopically or via single-port robotic approach.<sup>[[22]](#ref22)[[28]](#ref28)[[29]](#ref29)[[30]](#ref30)</sup> Concurrent cystoscopy and a rectal finger during needle passage, with equal tension kept on the two traction sutures, have been described to help keep the sutures out of the bladder and bowel.<sup>[[43]](#ref43)</sup>

- Operative time: 30 minutes in the cited single-port case report; pooled mean 40 minutes (95% CI 35–45) for laparoscopic Vecchietti versus 126 minutes (95% CI 109–143) for laparoscopic Davydov in a 2024 meta-analysis. The review did not compare every neovagina technique.<sup>[[22]](#ref22)[[29]](#ref29)</sup>
- Neovaginal length: 8.7 cm at 12 months.<sup>[[22]](#ref22)</sup>
- Requires postoperative dilation and close follow-up.<sup>[[30]](#ref30)</sup>

## Bowel (Intestinal) Vaginoplasty

Uses a pedicled segment of sigmoid colon (most common), ileum, or right colon to line the neovaginal canal. Advantages include self-lubricating mucosa, greater canal depth (11.5–14.6 cm), and potentially less intensive dilation requirements; introital stenosis and ongoing follow-up still matter.<sup>[[26]](#ref26)[[31]](#ref31)[[32]](#ref32)</sup> However, it carries the risks of major abdominal surgery:<sup>[[25]](#ref25)[[31]](#ref31)</sup>

- Introital stenosis (approximately 11%).
- Mucorrhea or excessive discharge (approximately 7%).
- Vaginal prolapse (approximately 6%).
- Diversion colitis (rare but potentially serious).
- Anastomotic leak, flap necrosis (rare).
- Overall complication rate: 33% (215 complications across 654 vaginoplasties); return to the operating room approximately 18%. This pooled series is predominantly gender-affirming intestinal vaginoplasty; the 2014 review of 21 retrospective studies (894 patients) reported low complication rates, with introital stenosis needing surgical correction in 4.1% of sigmoid-derived and 1.2% of ileum-derived vaginoplasties.<sup>[[25]](#ref25)[[31]](#ref31)</sup>

In a 16-patient retrospective series of cervicovaginal or distal vaginal agenesis with functional endometrium, utero-colo-neovaginoplasty restored painless menstrual flow in all 16 at a mean nine years of follow-up. Four developed long-term complications, three needed another operation, and none of the five sexually active patients conceived.<sup>[[33]](#ref33)</sup>

A 2024 systematic review and meta-analysis comparing laparoscopic Davydov versus Vecchietti found no superiority of either technique in functional terms (comparable neovaginal length, sexual function, and complication rates), though the Vecchietti procedure is significantly faster (approximately 40 versus approximately 126 minutes).<sup>[[22]](#ref22)</sup> A 2025 single-center retrospective comparison (peritoneal n = 20; sigmoid n = 30) reported shorter operative time, earlier return of bowel function, shorter mold use, and higher sexual satisfaction after peritoneal vaginoplasty. Postoperative complications occurred in 3/20 (15%) and 8/30 (26.7%), respectively, without a statistically significant between-group difference.<sup>[[27]](#ref27)</sup>

## Complications of Vaginoplasty (All Techniques)

ACOG highlights that compared with primary dilation, vaginoplasty complications are much more common and include:<sup>[[2]](#ref2)</sup>

- Bladder or rectal perforation.
- Graft necrosis.
- Hair-bearing vaginal skin (McIndoe).
- Fistulae (vesicovaginal, rectovaginal).
- Neovaginal stenosis / contracture.
- Diversion colitis (bowel vaginoplasty).
- Inflammatory bowel disease (rare).
- **Adenocarcinoma.** Extremely rare, reported in bowel-lined neovaginas.

## Reproductive Options

MRKH syndrome involves **absolute uterine factor infertility (AUFI)**. Options include:<sup>[[2]](#ref2)[[14]](#ref14)</sup>

- **Gestational surrogacy.** IVF with the patient's own oocytes and a gestational carrier. A systematic review (140 MRKH patients; 125 undergoing 369 IVF cycles) reported 71 newborns; the data are mostly case reports and small series.<sup>[[34]](#ref34)</sup>
- **Uterus transplantation (UTx).** The first live birth occurred in 2014 (Gothenburg, Sweden). Counseling should cover IVF, donor procurement and recipient surgery, immunosuppression with rejection surveillance, cesarean delivery, and planned graft removal after childbearing.<sup>[[35]](#ref35)[[37]](#ref37)</sup> The 2026 ISUTx report of activity through 2024 describes 91 transplants entered in the international registry (67 live donors, 24 deceased donors), with 12-month graft survival of 74–75% and 44 singleton live births (live-birth rate 30.3% per embryo transfer). MRKH accounts for 88% of recipients. Preeclampsia occurred in 23% of live-birth pregnancies. Mean gestational age at delivery was 34.5 weeks.<sup>[[35]](#ref35)[[36]](#ref36)[[37]](#ref37)</sup>
- **Adoption.**<sup>[[2]](#ref2)</sup>

The choice of neovagina creation method may impact suitability for subsequent UTx. Vecchietti-based approaches may provide favorable anatomy (sufficient length, natural axis, no prior major abdominal surgery), while bowel vaginoplasty may complicate future UTx.<sup>[[28]](#ref28)</sup>

### Pregnancy in Women With Other Müllerian Anomalies

Women with a uterus need obstetric counseling matched to the anomaly. Compared with normal uterine anatomy, a 2026 meta-analysis of 30 studies found congenital uterine anomalies associated with miscarriage (OR 1.45), preterm birth (OR 3.74), fetal malpresentation (OR 19.3; 95% CI 6.27–59.24, based on two studies with differing definitions), and cesarean delivery (OR 3.52).<sup>[[42]](#ref42)</sup> Septate uterus tracks with early loss, whereas unicornuate and didelphys uteri carry more preterm birth and malpresentation. The authors advise treating these pregnancies as high risk with tailored surveillance; mode of delivery follows obstetric indications such as malpresentation.<sup>[[42]](#ref42)</sup> Routine septum resection to improve live birth is not supported by randomized evidence.<sup>[[11]](#ref11)</sup>

## Psychosocial Considerations

The psychological impact of MRKH diagnosis should not be underestimated. ACOG recommends:<sup>[[2]](#ref2)</sup>

- All patients should be offered counseling and encouraged to connect with peer support groups.
- Future fertility options should be discussed early to help patients cope with the diagnosis.
- Timing of intervention should align with the patient's emotional maturity and readiness to adhere to postoperative dilation.
- Multidisciplinary care involving gynecology, psychology, and reproductive endocrinology is necessary.

## Key Takeaways

- Müllerian anomalies are a continuum of developmental defects classified by the ASRM MAC2021 system into descriptive categories.<sup>[[10]](#ref10)</sup>
- MRKH syndrome (1:5,000 females) is the most common cause of vaginal agenesis; up to 53% have associated congenital anomalies requiring systematic evaluation.<sup>[[2]](#ref2)</sup>
- **Nonsurgical dilation remains first-line** (90–96% reported success in motivated patients); surgery is an option after failure or informed preference.<sup>[[2]](#ref2)</sup>
- Among surgical options, no single technique has demonstrated clear superiority; the choice depends on surgeon expertise, patient anatomy, and patient preference.<sup>[[2]](#ref2)[[22]](#ref22)</sup>
- Uterus transplantation has emerged as a viable fertility treatment, with 44 live births in the ISUTx registry report through 2024; the registry is self-reported and does not include every center.<sup>[[37]](#ref37)</sup>
- For the obstructive transverse vaginal septum, repair depends on septal level, thickness and available tissue; double-opposing Z-plasty is one reconstructive option; see [Z-plasty](/docs/foundations/surgical-principles/flaps/z-plasty).
