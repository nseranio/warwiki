# Hidradenitis Suppurativa — Genitourinary Disease

Hidradenitis suppurativa (HS) is a chronic, inflammatory, recurrent disease of the hair follicle with prevalence estimates that vary by ascertainment (European self-report 1.0%–2.1%; European diagnosis codes 0.09%–0.51%; initial U.S. diagnosis-code estimates approximately 0.1%), presenting with painful nodules, abscesses, draining sinus tracts, and disfiguring scarring in apocrine-bearing intertriginous areas.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[2]](#ref2)</sup> Approximately one-third or more of patients have genital involvement, making this a condition frequently encountered, and often misdiagnosed, by urologists and gynecologists.<sup>[[5]](#ref5)[[7]](#ref7)</sup> For the reconstructive urologist, advanced genitoperineal HS produces draining sinus tracts, urethrocutaneous fistulae, urethral strictures, scrotal or vulvar architectural destruction, secondary lymphedema, and, in long-standing disease, squamous cell carcinoma arising within chronic sinuses.

---

## Epidemiology and Pathogenesis

Women are affected at a 3:1 ratio compared to men; onset typically occurs between ages 18–39.<sup>[[4]](#ref4)[[5]](#ref5)</sup> The average diagnostic delay is approximately 10 years, during which patients consult dermatologists, gynecologists, surgeons, and primary care physicians and receive multiple misdiagnoses.<sup>[[2]](#ref2)</sup>

Pathogenesis begins with follicular plugging and rupture, triggering an inflammatory cascade driven by TNF-α, IL-1β, and IL-17, leading to immune cell infiltration, tissue destruction, and fibrosis.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[6]](#ref6)</sup> Risk factors include genetic predisposition, obesity, smoking, and hormonal influences.<sup>[[6]](#ref6)</sup>

### Hurley Staging

| Stage | Description |
|---|---|
| I | Abscess formation, single or multiple, without sinus tracts or cicatrization |
| II | Recurrent abscesses with tract formation and scarring, single or multiple widely separated lesions |
| III | Diffuse or near-diffuse involvement, or multiple interconnected tracts and abscesses across the entire area |

---

## Genital and Perineal Anatomy of Involvement

Anatomic sites of urologic or urogynecologic relevance: 

- Mons pubis, inguinal folds, and vulva and labia in women 
- Scrotum, penile shaft, and perineum in men
- Perianal and gluteal regions across sexes

Genital HS is particularly challenging due to functional and reconstructive concerns unique to these anatomic zones. The JAMA Surgery multidisciplinary review by Hamad et al. emphasizes that genital HS requires coordinated care among dermatology, surgery, and urology or urogynecology.<sup>[[5]](#ref5)</sup>

## Urologic Complications

| Complication | Notes |
|---|---|
| Urethrocutaneous fistula | Chronic perineal HS can erode into the urethra; documented in case series including fistula with concurrent phimosis<sup>[[8]](#ref8)</sup> |
| Urethral stricture | Chronic perineal inflammation and fibrosis drive stricture formation requiring reconstructive intervention<sup>[[9]](#ref9)[[8]](#ref8)</sup> |
| Phimosis | Scarring from penile/preputial HS produces acquired phimosis<sup>[[8]](#ref8)</sup> |
| Scrotal/genital lymphedema | Lymphatic obstruction from fibrosis and scarring; see [Genital Lymphedema](/docs/clinical-conditions/03g-genital-scrotal/genital-lymphedema)<sup>[[10]](#ref10)</sup> |
| Hospitalized genitourinary infections | In a retrospective claims cohort of adalimumab initiators, GU infection hospitalizations were more frequent in patients with HS (n = 1,650) than in patients with psoriasis (n = 8,699; IRR 2.22, 95% CI 1.22–3.86). This between-disease comparison does not isolate an effect of adalimumab.<sup>[[11]](#ref11)</sup> |
| Urethral and perirectal fistulae (case reports) | Individual reports document urethral fistula and perirectal sinus tracks or fistulae, without establishing incidence or a routine screening indication.<sup>[[30]](#ref30)[[31]](#ref31)</sup> For perianal tunnels or gastrointestinal symptoms, keep coexisting Crohn disease in the differential.<sup>[[2]](#ref2)</sup> |

For urethrocutaneous fistula and urethral stricture technique, see [Urethrocutaneous Fistula](/docs/clinical-conditions/03f-fistulas/in-males/urethrocutaneous) and [Scrotal Reconstruction](/docs/surgical-techniques/04e-genital-reconstruction/scrotal-reconstruction).

## Urogynecologic Considerations

HS disproportionately affects women of childbearing age; obstetrician-gynecologists are uniquely positioned for early diagnosis during routine well-woman examinations.<sup>[[12]](#ref12)[[13]](#ref13)</sup>

**Vulvoperineal HS:** Involvement of the vulva, labia, mons pubis, and perineum causes severe scarring, contractures, and tissue destruction impairing urinary and sexual function. Vulvar HS is frequently misdiagnosed as Bartholin cyst, folliculitis, sexually transmitted infection, or Crohn disease.<sup>[[7]](#ref7)</sup>

**Sexual dysfunction.** Approximately 67% of HS patients report sexual difficulties, driven by pain, malodorous discharge, genital scarring, and psychological distress; validated screening (FSFI, IIEF) is recommended.<sup>[[14]](#ref14)[[2]](#ref2)</sup> 

**PCOS association.** HS carries a &gt;3-fold risk of PCOS (prevalence up to 9%), suggesting shared hormonal and metabolic pathways.<sup>[[3]](#ref3)</sup> 

**Menstrual flares:** Many women report perimenstrual HS flares, indicating hormonal modulation of disease activity.<sup>[[5]](#ref5)</sup>

**Pregnancy and breastfeeding:** HS management during pregnancy and breastfeeding requires careful medication selection, as many systemic therapies have limited safety data in these populations.<sup>[[13]](#ref13)</sup> The 2025 North American guideline recommends continuing a biologic that controls HS through a planned pregnancy (strong; very low certainty) and adalimumab when a biologic is needed while breastfeeding (strong; moderate certainty). It recommends avoiding oral doxycycline in pregnancy (strong; low certainty) and suggests avoiding topical resorcinol because pregnancy safety evidence is lacking (conditional; very low certainty).<sup>[[32]](#ref32)</sup>

## Malignant Transformation

Squamous cell carcinoma (SCC) arising within chronic HS lesions is rare but serious; the following site, metastasis and mortality proportions describe published SCC cases and are not risks for all patients with HS, occurring most commonly in the gluteal (47.5%), perianal (18.9%), and genital (13.9%) regions (122 SCCs in 95 reported patients, 78% men).<sup>[[15]](#ref15)</sup>

| Feature | Data |
|---|---|
| Mean latency from HS onset to SCC | ~25 years<sup>[[15]](#ref15)</sup> |
| Population predominantly affected | Caucasian men, smokers, severe disease<sup>[[16]](#ref16)</sup> |
| Metastasis rate | ~54% (34/63 with reported outcomes)<sup>[[15]](#ref15)</sup> |
| Mortality | ~59% (44/75 with reported outcomes)<sup>[[15]](#ref15)</sup> |
| Malignant degeneration rate | Up to 4.6% in groin/perianal/perineal severe disease<sup>[[17]](#ref17)</sup> |
| Vulvar/perianal/perineal cancers | Tend to present at advanced stage<sup>[[18]](#ref18)</sup> |

**SCC Surveillance.** North American guidelines recommend periodic skin examination of chronic perineal and buttock lesions and biopsy of any new, rapidly growing, or treatment-refractory lesion.<sup>[[3]](#ref3)[[19]](#ref19)</sup> HPV vaccination has been proposed as a potential preventive strategy.<sup>[[20]](#ref20)</sup>

## Surgical Management

Surgical treatment of genitoperineal HS requires a multidisciplinary team (dermatology, reconstructive urology or urogynecology, wound care, rheumatology or immunology).<sup>[[9]](#ref9)</sup> 

**Principles:**
- **Match surgery to lesion extent.** Deroofing can spare surrounding tissue for localized recurrent tunnels; Hurley III genital disease with extensive irreversible tunnels or scarring may require excision tailored to involved tissue and vital structures, without a universal 1 cm margin.<sup>[[3]](#ref3)[[2]](#ref2)[[29]](#ref29)</sup> Reserve incision and drainage for relief of acute abscess pain, not definitive disease control.<sup>[[3]](#ref3)</sup>
- **Perianal, vulvar, and inferior breast locations** carry higher recurrence rates after surgery<sup>[[3]](#ref3)</sup>
- **Coordinate perioperative biologic treatment.** SHARPS supported continuing adalimumab through axillary or inguinal wide excision with secondary-intention healing; it observed no increase in postoperative infection or hemorrhage.<sup>[[25]](#ref25)</sup> In a pragmatic RCT (31 per arm), adalimumab plus surgery improved 12-month IHS4 change (−19.1 versus −7.8) and DLQI change (−8.2 versus −4.0) compared with adalimumab alone; follow-up was too short to assess surgical recurrence.<sup>[[35]](#ref35)</sup> These studies do not establish safety for every biologic, graft or flap operation, or patient with active infection.<sup>[[2]](#ref2)</sup>

**Two-stage reconstruction.** Radical excision, then negative-pressure wound therapy, then delayed meshed split-thickness skin grafting achieved &gt;90% graft take and no recurrence at a median 19 months in a five-patient, single-centre series of men with Hurley III anogenital disease that had failed biologics.<sup>[[21]](#ref21)</sup> For flap and graft options by defect location, see [Scrotal Reconstruction](/docs/surgical-techniques/04e-genital-reconstruction/scrotal-reconstruction) and [Buried Penis Repair](/docs/surgical-techniques/04e-genital-reconstruction/buried-penis-repair).

## Medical Management

Select systemic therapy with dermatology, including infection and TB screening and review of agent-specific contraindications and warnings. Before bimekizumab, obtain baseline liver enzymes, alkaline phosphatase, and bilirubin; review any history of severe depression or suicidal ideation and weigh risks and benefits; and avoid use in active inflammatory bowel disease.<sup>[[24]](#ref24)</sup> The table summarizes options, not a complete prescribing protocol.

| Agent | Class | FDA Status | Notes |
|---|---|---|---|
| Adalimumab (Humira) | Anti-TNF | Approved 2015 | Adult label: 160 mg on day 1 (or split over two days), 80 mg on day 15, then 40 mg weekly or 80 mg every other week from day 29; pediatric dosing (age 12 and older, at least 30 kg) is weight-based.<sup>[[23]](#ref23)</sup> Infliximab is used off-label in refractory disease. |
| Secukinumab (Cosentyx) | Anti-IL-17A | Adults approved 2023; ages 12 and older approved 2026 | Adults: 300 mg at weeks 0–4, then every 4 weeks; consider every 2 weeks if response is inadequate. Ages 12 and older weighing 30 to &lt;90 kg: 150 mg at weeks 0–4, then every 4 weeks; at least 90 kg: 300 mg on the same schedule.<sup>[[27]](#ref27)</sup> SUNSHINE/SUNRISE used week-16 **HiSCR50** as the primary endpoint.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup> |
| Bimekizumab (Bimzelx) | Anti-IL-17A/F | HS indication approved 2024 | Label: 320 mg at weeks 0, 2, 4, 6, 8, 10, 12, 14 and 16, then every 4 weeks.<sup>[[24]](#ref24)</sup> BE HEARD I/II week-16 HiSCR50 was 48% vs 29% and 52% vs 32% for the every-2-week arms versus placebo.<sup>[[26]](#ref26)</sup> |
| Tetracyclines; clindamycin plus rifampicin | Antibiotics | Off-label | Tetracyclines are used for mild-to-severe inflammatory HS; clindamycin plus rifampicin may be considered for selected moderate-to-severe disease for 10–12 weeks.<sup>[[29]](#ref29)</sup> Rifampicin can reduce combined hormonal contraceptive effectiveness; review contraception before prescribing.<sup>[[33]](#ref33)</sup> |
| Hormonal therapy | OCPs, spironolactone | Off-label | Perimenstrual flare suppression |
| Resorcinol 15% cream | Topical | Off-label | Compounded topical option for localized, non-fistulous lesions in mild-to-moderate HS; pregnancy safety data are insufficient.<sup>[[29]](#ref29)</sup> |

Agents under investigation include JAK inhibitors, apremilast, fostamatinib, and others targeting the IL-17 and JAK/STAT pathways.<sup>[[22]](#ref22)</sup> Upadacitinib 30 mg daily reached HiSCR50 in 38.3% at week 12 in a 68-patient phase 2 trial, significant against a historical placebo rate but not against the in-trial placebo group; it is not approved for HS.<sup>[[28]](#ref28)</sup> The 2025 European S2k guideline gives a current treatment algorithm alongside the North American guidance.<sup>[[29]](#ref29)</sup>

## Key Takeaways for the Reconstructive Urologist

- **Differential diagnosis.** Recurrent boils, abscesses, or draining tunnels in the groin, perineum, or genitalia warrant consideration of HS.<sup>[[3]](#ref3)[[5]](#ref5)</sup> The 2026 European guideline specifies at least two painful or suppurating episodes within six months as a diagnostic history criterion.<sup>[[34]](#ref34)</sup>
- **Multidisciplinary care.** Urologists or urogynecologists play a central role in managing complications (fistulae, strictures, lymphedema) and reconstruction after wide excision.<sup>[[9]](#ref9)</sup> 
- **SCC surveillance.** The North American guideline recommends periodic skin examination of chronic perineal and buttock HS (C/II).<sup>[[3]](#ref3)</sup> Promptly biopsy suspicious or treatment-refractory lesions.<sup>[[15]](#ref15)[[19]](#ref19)</sup>
- **Sexual dysfunction screening** should be routine given the high prevalence.<sup>[[14]](#ref14)[[2]](#ref2)</sup>
- **Early dermatology referral** allows assessment for biologic therapy and coordinated surgical planning.<sup>[[13]](#ref13)[[2]](#ref2)</sup> SHARPS did not establish a reduction in planned surgical-site area or in the proportion needing less or no surgery; these endpoints were exploratory and underpowered.<sup>[[25]](#ref25)</sup>
