# Hidradenitis Suppurativa — Genitourinary Disease

Hidradenitis suppurativa (HS) is a chronic, inflammatory, recurrent disease of the hair follicle with a prevalence of approximately 1% in Europe and North America, presenting with painful nodules, abscesses, draining sinus tracts, and disfiguring scarring in apocrine-bearing intertriginous areas.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[2]](#ref2)</sup> Approximately one-third or more of patients have genital involvement, making this a condition frequently encountered — and often misdiagnosed — by urologists and gynecologists.<sup>[[5]](#ref5)[[7]](#ref7)</sup> For the reconstructive urologist, advanced genitoperineal HS produces draining sinus tracts, urethrocutaneous fistulae, urethral strictures, scrotal/vulvar architectural destruction, secondary lymphedema, and — in long-standing disease — squamous cell carcinoma arising within chronic sinuses.

---

## Epidemiology and Pathogenesis

Women are affected at a 3:1 ratio compared to men; onset typically occurs between ages 18–39.<sup>[[4]](#ref4)[[5]](#ref5)</sup> The average diagnostic delay is approximately 10 years, during which patients consult dermatologists, gynecologists, surgeons, and primary care physicians and receive multiple misdiagnoses.<sup>[[2]](#ref2)</sup>

Pathogenesis begins with follicular plugging and rupture, triggering an inflammatory cascade driven by TNF-α, IL-1β, and IL-17, leading to immune cell infiltration, tissue destruction, and fibrosis.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[6]](#ref6)</sup> Risk factors include genetic predisposition, obesity, smoking, and hormonal influences.<sup>[[6]](#ref6)</sup>

### Hurley Staging

| Stage | Description |
|---|---|
| I | Abscess formation, single or multiple, without sinus tracts or cicatrization |
| II | Recurrent abscesses with tract formation and scarring, single or multiple widely separated lesions |
| III | Diffuse or near-diffuse involvement, or multiple interconnected tracts and abscesses across the entire area |

---

## Genital and Perineal Anatomy of Involvement

Anatomic sites of urologic/urogynecologic relevance:

- Mons pubis, inguinal folds, and vulva/labia in women
- Scrotum, penile shaft, and perineum in men
- Perianal and gluteal regions in both sexes

Genital HS is particularly challenging due to functional and reconstructive concerns unique to these anatomic zones. The JAMA Surgery multidisciplinary review by Hamad et al. emphasizes that genital HS requires coordinated care among dermatology, surgery, and urology/urogynecology.<sup>[[5]](#ref5)</sup>

---

## Urologic Complications

| Complication | Notes |
|---|---|
| Urethrocutaneous fistula | Chronic perineal HS can erode into the urethra; documented in case series including fistula with concurrent phimosis<sup>[[8]](#ref8)</sup> |
| Urethral stricture | Chronic perineal inflammation and fibrosis drive stricture formation requiring reconstructive intervention<sup>[[9]](#ref9)[[8]](#ref8)</sup> |
| Phimosis | Scarring from penile/preputial HS produces acquired phimosis<sup>[[8]](#ref8)</sup> |
| Scrotal/genital lymphedema | Lymphatic obstruction from fibrosis and scarring; see [Genital Lymphedema](/docs/clinical-conditions/03g-genital-scrotal/genital-lymphedema)<sup>[[10]](#ref10)</sup> |
| Genitourinary infections | Patients on adalimumab have &gt;2-fold increased serious GU infection rate vs psoriasis patients on the same therapy (IRR 2.22; 95% CI 1.22–3.86)<sup>[[11]](#ref11)</sup> |

For urethrocutaneous fistula and urethral stricture technique, see [Urethrocutaneous Fistula](/docs/clinical-conditions/03f-fistulas/in-males/urethrocutaneous) and [Scrotal Reconstruction](/docs/surgical-techniques/04e-genital-reconstruction/scrotal-reconstruction).

## Urogynecologic Considerations

HS disproportionately affects women of childbearing age; obstetrician-gynecologists are uniquely positioned for early diagnosis during routine well-woman examinations.<sup>[[12]](#ref12)[[13]](#ref13)</sup>

**Vulvoperineal HS:** Involvement of the vulva, labia, mons pubis, and perineum causes severe scarring, contractures, and tissue destruction impairing urinary and sexual function. Vulvar HS is frequently misdiagnosed as Bartholin cyst, folliculitis, sexually transmitted infection, or Crohn disease.<sup>[[7]](#ref7)</sup>

**Sexual dysfunction:** Approximately 67% of HS patients report sexual difficulties, driven by pain, malodorous discharge, genital scarring, and psychological distress; validated screening (FSFI, IIEF) is recommended.<sup>[[14]](#ref14)[[2]](#ref2)</sup>

**PCOS association:** HS carries a &gt;3-fold risk of PCOS (prevalence up to 9%), suggesting shared hormonal and metabolic pathways.<sup>[[3]](#ref3)</sup>

**Menstrual flares:** Many women report perimenstrual HS flares, indicating hormonal modulation of disease activity.<sup>[[5]](#ref5)</sup>

**Pregnancy:** HS management during pregnancy and breastfeeding requires careful medication selection, as many systemic therapies have limited safety data in this population.<sup>[[13]](#ref13)</sup>

## Malignant Transformation

Squamous cell carcinoma (SCC) arising within chronic HS lesions is rare but devastating, occurring most commonly in the gluteal (47.5%), perianal (18.9%), and genital (13.9%) regions.<sup>[[15]](#ref15)</sup>

| Feature | Data |
|---|---|
| Mean latency from HS onset to SCC | ~25 years<sup>[[15]](#ref15)</sup> |
| Population predominantly affected | Caucasian men, smokers, severe disease<sup>[[16]](#ref16)</sup> |
| Metastasis rate | ~54%<sup>[[15]](#ref15)</sup> |
| Mortality | ~59%<sup>[[15]](#ref15)</sup> |
| Malignant degeneration rate | Up to 4.6% in groin/perianal/perineal severe disease<sup>[[17]](#ref17)</sup> |
| Vulvar/perianal/perineal cancers | Tend to present at advanced stage<sup>[[18]](#ref18)</sup> |

**SCC Surveillance.** North American guidelines recommend periodic skin examination of chronic perineal and buttock lesions and **biopsy of any new, rapidly growing, or treatment-refractory lesion**.<sup>[[3]](#ref3)[[19]](#ref19)</sup> HPV vaccination has been proposed as a potential preventive strategy.<sup>[[20]](#ref20)</sup>

## Surgical Management

Surgical treatment of genitoperineal HS requires a multidisciplinary team (dermatology, reconstructive urology/urogynecology, wound care, rheumatology/immunology).<sup>[[9]](#ref9)</sup>

**Principles:**
- **Wide local excision** remains the mainstay for Hurley Stage III genital disease; excision to the subcutaneous plane with 1 cm margins<sup>[[3]](#ref3)[[2]](#ref2)</sup>
- **Perianal, vulvar, and inferior breast locations** carry higher recurrence rates after surgery<sup>[[3]](#ref3)</sup>
- **Biologic therapy should not be interrupted** during the perioperative period<sup>[[2]](#ref2)</sup>

**Two-stage reconstruction:** Radical excision → negative-pressure wound therapy → delayed split-thickness skin grafting has shown &gt;90% graft take and no recurrence at ~19 months.<sup>[[21]](#ref21)</sup> For flap and graft options by defect location, see [Scrotal Reconstruction](/docs/surgical-techniques/04e-genital-reconstruction/scrotal-reconstruction) and [Buried Penis Repair](/docs/surgical-techniques/04e-genital-reconstruction/buried-penis-repair).

## Medical Management

| Agent | Class | FDA Status | Notes |
|---|---|---|---|
| Adalimumab (Humira) | Anti-TNF | Approved 2015 | First biologic approved for HS; 2× weekly dosing |
| Secukinumab (Cosentyx) | Anti-IL-17A | Approved 2023 | SUNSHINE/SUNRISE RCTs (HR IHS4-50 ~45% vs ~33% placebo at Week 16)<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup> |
| Bimekizumab (Bimzelx) | Anti-IL-17A/F | Approved 2023 | Dual IL-17 inhibition; phase 3 data pending full publication |
| Tetracyclines / clindamycin + rifampicin | Antibiotics | Off-label | Moderate disease; combination 12-week course |
| Hormonal therapy | OCPs, spironolactone | Off-label | Perimenstrual flare suppression |
| Resorcinol 15% peel | Topical | Off-label | For individual lesions |

Agents under investigation include JAK inhibitors (upadacitinib), apremilast, fostamatinib, and others targeting the IL-17 and JAK/STAT pathways.<sup>[[22]](#ref22)</sup>

---

## Key Takeaways for the Reconstructive Urologist

- **Differential diagnosis:** HS should be considered for any patient with recurrent "boils," abscesses, or draining sinuses in the groin, perineum, or genitalia present for &gt;6 months.<sup>[[3]](#ref3)[[5]](#ref5)</sup>
- **Multidisciplinary care:** Urologists/urogynecologists play a critical role in managing complications (fistulae, strictures, lymphedema) and reconstruction after wide excision.<sup>[[9]](#ref9)</sup>
- **SCC surveillance is mandatory** in patients with long-standing perineal/genital HS — biopsy any suspicious or refractory lesion without delay.<sup>[[15]](#ref15)[[19]](#ref19)</sup>
- **Sexual dysfunction screening** should be routine given the high prevalence.<sup>[[14]](#ref14)[[2]](#ref2)</sup>
- **Early dermatology referral** for biologic therapy may alter disease trajectory and reduce the need for extensive surgical reconstruction.<sup>[[13]](#ref13)[[2]](#ref2)</sup>
