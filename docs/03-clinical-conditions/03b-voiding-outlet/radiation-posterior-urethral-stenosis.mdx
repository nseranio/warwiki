# Radiation-Induced Posterior Urethral Stenosis

**Radiation-induced posterior urethral stenosis** is fibrotic narrowing of the urethra proximal to the corpus spongiosum after pelvic radiotherapy, almost always for prostate cancer. It most often involves the **bulbomembranous urethra**, where the membranous urethra and external sphincter lie just below the prostatic apex in the high-dose field. The bladder neck, prostatic urethra and a post-prostatectomy vesicourethral anastomosis can also be affected.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[2]](#ref2)</sup>

It is the most difficult form of [posterior urethral stenosis](/docs/clinical-conditions/03b-voiding-outlet/posterior-urethral-stenosis). The scar lies in poorly vascularized tissue beside the continence mechanism. Endoscopic treatment is generally less durable than in nonradiated tissue. Every definitive repair also risks continence or erectile function in exchange for patency.<sup>[[3]](#ref3)[[4]](#ref4)[[1]](#ref1)</sup> For the tissue biology of irradiated fields, see [Radiation & Tissue Effects](/docs/foundations/surgical-principles/radiation-tissue-effects).

## At a Glance

- **Define the whole outlet, not just the narrowing.** Record location, length, obliteration, prostatic-urethral or bladder-neck involvement, baseline continence, bladder capacity and compliance, fistula, necrosis and pubic-bone symptoms before choosing a repair.<sup>[[5]](#ref5)[[6]](#ref6)</sup>
- **Endoscopy is a first step for non-obliterative disease, not a cure.** Avoid deep incisions at 6 o'clock (rectum) and 12 o'clock (pubic symphysis). Do not treat an obliterated segment endoscopically.<sup>[[6]](#ref6)</sup>
- **For a short stenosis, excision and primary anastomosis (EPA) is the most durable repair.** In the largest comparative series, estimated 10-year recurrence was 6.8% after EPA versus 20% after buccal onlay, with new incontinence of 18% in both groups.<sup>[[7]](#ref7)</sup>
- **For longer or non-obliterative stenosis, consider a non-transecting dorsal onlay buccal graft.** It avoids circumferential transection. Its low reported rates of new incontinence partly reflect how many patients were already incontinent.<sup>[[8]](#ref8)[[9]](#ref9)</sup>
- **Plan the artificial urinary sphincter (AUS) from the first consultation.** Confirm stable patency before implantation. Prior radiation and prior urethroplasty, especially transecting repair, increase AUS failure and explantation.<sup>[[10]](#ref10)[[11]](#ref11)</sup>
- **Diversion is a valid reconstructive endpoint** for necrosis, fistula, an end-stage bladder or repeated failed salvage.<sup>[[12]](#ref12)[[13]](#ref13)</sup>

## Where It Occurs

| Presentation | Typical history | Anatomy | Related page |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Bulbomembranous stenosis** | Primary external beam radiotherapy (EBRT), brachytherapy or both | Membranous and proximal bulbar urethra; may extend to the prostatic apex | This page |
| **Anastomotic stenosis after prostatectomy and radiation** | Adjuvant or salvage RT after radical prostatectomy | Vesicourethral anastomosis, sometimes extending into the membranous urethra | [Vesicourethral Anastomotic Stenosis](/docs/clinical-conditions/03b-voiding-outlet/vesicourethral-anastomotic-stenosis) |
| **Radiated bladder-neck or prostatic stenosis** | RT alone, or outlet surgery in a radiated prostate | Bladder neck and prostatic urethra | [Bladder Neck Contracture](/docs/clinical-conditions/03b-voiding-outlet/bladder-neck-stenosis) |

Reconstructive series often combine these groups. Check each study's population before applying its results.

### Why radiated scar behaves differently

Hughes and colleagues compared 19 post-radiation membranous stenosis specimens with 51 nonradiated specimens. Radiated scar had denser, more organized collagen, more hyalinized fibrosis, fat entrapment, spindle-cell change and vacuolar degeneration, with **significantly reduced vascularity**.<sup>[[3]](#ref3)</sup> The underlying mechanism is oxidative injury to small-vessel endothelium, which causes ischemia and extracellular fibrosis. Breakdown of the urothelium can let urine leak into the tissue and drive further fibrosis, and irradiated fibroblasts deposit excess collagen.<sup>[[14]](#ref14)</sup>

This has three practical consequences:

- An incision heals by secondary intention in ischemic scar.
- A free graft depends on a recipient bed that may be poorly perfused.
- Injury continues to evolve for years after treatment.

In a 733-patient cohort with at least 100 months of follow-up after urethroplasty, radiation etiology was independently associated with long-term recurrence (HR 4.25, 95% CI 1.65–10.9).<sup>[[15]](#ref15)</sup>

## Incidence and Timing

| Source | Population | Finding |
|---|---|---|
| **Awad 2018 meta-analysis** | 46 studies; 16,129 men treated with RT for prostate cancer | Pooled stricture prevalence was 2.2% at median 4-year follow-up: 1.5% after EBRT, 1.9% after brachytherapy and 4.9% after both. Median time to stricture was 2.2 years. Longer follow-up was associated with more strictures.<sup>[[16]](#ref16)</sup> |
| **ASCENDE-RT** | Randomized trial of low-dose-rate brachytherapy boost versus dose-escalated EBRT boost | Five-year cumulative incidence of **grade 3 genitourinary events** was 18.4% versus 5.2%. The five-year prevalence was lower (8.6% versus 2.2%). This is a composite toxicity endpoint, not a stricture rate.<sup>[[17]](#ref17)</sup> |
| **Sullivan 2009** | 474 men treated with high-dose-rate brachytherapy, 90% as a boost to EBRT | 38 strictures (8%) at median 41 months; 6-year actuarial risk 12%; 92.1% bulbomembranous; median time to diagnosis 22 months. Prior TURP (HR 2.81), hypertension (HR 2.83) and a higher dose per fraction predicted stricture.<sup>[[18]](#ref18)</sup> |
| **Merrick 2006** | 1,186 men treated with permanent brachytherapy | 29 strictures, all bulbomembranous; 9-year actuarial risk 3.6%; median onset 2.4 years. Higher radiation dose to the bulbomembranous urethra and supplemental EBRT predicted stricture.<sup>[[19]](#ref19)</sup> |
| **Calvo & Rourke 2026** | Alberta registry; 47,387 men; median follow-up 79 months | Fifteen-year incidence of urethral complications (stenosis, fistula or a urethral procedure more than 30 days after treatment): EBRT plus cryotherapy 42%, EBRT plus brachytherapy 26%, prostatectomy plus EBRT 22%, prostatectomy alone 9% and observation or medical therapy 8%. Complications after prostatectomy plateaued by 10 years; radiation-containing treatments continued to accumulate them.<sup>[[20]](#ref20)</sup> |

The dose relationship helps explain why the bulbomembranous urethra, just below the prostatic apex, is the usual site. Presentation years after treatment is common. In Hofer's multi-institutional series, the mean interval from radiotherapy to EPA was 6.4 years (range 1–20).<sup>[[2]](#ref2)</sup> These are population and trial estimates, not an individual risk for a contemporary radiation plan.

## Evaluation

### History

Record:

- Obstructive symptoms, retention and catheter or suprapubic tube (SPT) dependence.
- Recurrent infection.
- Storage symptoms, pain and hematuria from radiation cystitis.
- Continence before any treatment and now, including pad use and previous AUS.
- Erectile function.
- Previous dilations, incisions, TURP and urethroplasty.
- Radiation modality, cancer status, PSA and androgen deprivation.

**Fistula warning signs:** pneumaturia, fecaluria or urine per rectum suggest a [rectourethral fistula](/docs/clinical-conditions/03f-fistulas/in-males/rectourethral). Pubic or groin pain, pain on walking or osteomyelitis suggest a [urethropubic (urosymphyseal) fistula](/docs/clinical-conditions/03f-fistulas/in-males/urethropubic). Either changes the operation, and may change the goal.

### Defining the stenosis

- **Uroflowmetry and post-void residual** establish a functional baseline.<sup>[[5]](#ref5)</sup>
- **[Retrograde urethrography with voiding cystourethrography](/docs/evaluation/imaging/rug-vcug)** defines location and length. When the patient has an SPT and the proximal urethra does not fill, a combined antegrade and retrograde study, or antegrade flexible cystoscopy through the SPT tract, shows the proximal limit.<sup>[[5]](#ref5)[[6]](#ref6)</sup>
- **[Cystoscopy](/docs/evaluation/ancillary-tests/endoscopy)** assesses caliber and tissue quality, including pallor, necrosis, calcification and stones.
- **[MRI](/docs/evaluation/imaging/mri)** is useful when fistula, pubic osteomyelitis or prostatic cavitation is suspected.

### Predicting continence

After bulbomembranous reconstruction, continence depends on the bladder neck and whatever external sphincter survives the stenosis and the repair. Assess the bladder neck at rest on cystogram or antegrade cystoscopy, and document baseline continence carefully.

Chung selected only men with an intact bladder neck on imaging. Even so, **33% (12/36) developed new stress incontinence after EPA**, and 9 of those 12 had stenosis extending into the prostatic urethra.<sup>[[21]](#ref21)</sup> An open bladder neck, or disease extending into the prostatic urethra, should prompt explicit counseling about incontinence, a later AUS, or accepting long-term drainage.

### Bladder function and urethral rest

A patent urethra does not help a small, painful or poorly compliant bladder. Ivan and colleagues consider men poor candidates for reconstruction that keeps the native bladder when they have:<sup>[[14]](#ref14)</sup>

- poor bladder compliance or capacity;
- significant pelvic pain or dysuria, which should prompt a search for fistula;
- refractory radiation cystitis needing repeated intervention.

Obtain urodynamics when storage symptoms dominate or when reconstruction versus diversion is undecided.

Suprapubic drainage before urethroplasty allows the urethra to recover from recent catheterization or dilation.<sup>[[5]](#ref5)</sup> Naser-Tavakolian and Lee advise against evaluating a patient through an indwelling urethral catheter. They place a suprapubic tube and allow about 4 weeks of urethral rest before diagnostic assessment.<sup>[[22]](#ref22)</sup>

## Endoscopic and Conservative Management

### Non-obliterative stenosis

- **Dilation or incision is reasonable as a first step.** SIU and EAU guidance allows repeat endoluminal treatment to stabilize patency.<sup>[[6]](#ref6)</sup>
- **Radiation's effect on endoscopic durability is inconsistent across studies.** A 40-study meta-analysis of endoscopic VUAS treatment (1,452 patients) found lower success with radiotherapy on meta-regression. Overall success was 72.8%, or 62.9% after correction for publication bias. Some individual series found no difference by radiation history (see table below).<sup>[[4]](#ref4)[[23]](#ref23)</sup>
- **Incision position.** EAU strongly advises against deep incisions at 6 o'clock, which risk rectal injury and a rectourethral fistula. It also advises against deep incisions at 12 o'clock, which risk a urosymphyseal fistula.<sup>[[6]](#ref6)</sup>

**Endoscopic outcomes in radiated patients:**

| Series | Population | Outcome |
|---|---|---|
| Merrick 2006 | 29 bulbomembranous strictures after permanent brachytherapy | All but 3 treated successfully with dilation or urethrotomy<sup>[[19]](#ref19)</sup> |
| Sullivan 2009 | 35 bulbomembranous strictures after high-dose-rate brachytherapy, first treated by dilation (15) or urethrotomy (20) | 17 (49%) needed second-line treatment, 3 third-line and 1 urethroplasty<sup>[[18]](#ref18)</sup> |
| Farrell 2017 | 18 recurrent radiation-induced bulbar or bulbomembranous strictures; DVIU with mitomycin C and 1 month of self-catheterization | 66.7% needed no further surgery after one procedure; 14 of 16 (87.5%) after a second<sup>[[24]](#ref24)</sup> |
| Veerman 2022 | 90 bladder-neck incisions for VUAS after robotic prostatectomy | Recurrence 12% without radiation, versus 57% (VUAS before salvage RT), 29% (VUAS after salvage RT) and 50% (after salvage prostatectomy). Severe incontinence (more than 1 pad per day): 6% versus 16%, 10% and 29%<sup>[[25]](#ref25)</sup> |
| Rozanski 2021 | 86 recurrent BNC or VUAS; DVIU with low-dose mitomycin C | Overall success 94% without radiation versus 76% with radiation<sup>[[26]](#ref26)</sup> |
| Neu 2021 | 18 recalcitrant VUAS (78% radiated; median 5 prior failed treatments); incision with triamcinolone | 83% success at mean 16.3 months; complications in 28%<sup>[[27]](#ref27)</sup> |
| Pfalzgraf 2021 | 103 endoscopically treated VUAS, 37% radiated | Success 42% with radiation versus 43% without<sup>[[28]](#ref28)</sup> |

These series differ in location (bulbomembranous versus anastomotic), prior treatment and definitions of success.

### Obliterative stenosis

EAU strongly discourages endoluminal treatment of obliterative radiation-induced stenosis and favors reconstruction or diversion.<sup>[[6]](#ref6)</sup> Do not core through an obliterated radiated segment without direct control of both lumens.

### Maintenance and palliation

Intermittent self-dilation or catheterization and long-term suprapubic drainage are legitimate choices. EAU cites a series of radiation-induced bulbomembranous strictures managed with an SPT. Only 51% went on to urethroplasty (mean follow-up 25 months), and 27% of those who kept the catheter had catheter-related complications.<sup>[[13]](#ref13)</sup>

### When to stop repeating endoscopy

Refer for reconstruction in any of these situations:

- Recurrence after one or two technically adequate endoscopic treatments.
- Obliteration.
- Fistula or osteomyelitis.
- When a future AUS depends on preserving the urethra.

See [VUAS: when to stop repeating endoscopy](/docs/clinical-conditions/03b-voiding-outlet/vesicourethral-anastomotic-stenosis#when-to-stop-repeating-endoscopy).

## Choosing a Reconstruction

For radiation-induced bulbomembranous strictures refractory to endoscopic treatment, EAU 2026 allows **EPA or augmentation urethroplasty for short strictures below 2.5 cm**, according to anatomy and expertise. It recommends augmentation for longer strictures and counseling about new incontinence and erectile dysfunction.<sup>[[13]](#ref13)</sup> A meta-analysis of eight retrospective studies (256 patients, 83% bulbomembranous) found pooled stenosis resolution of 80% (95% CI 74–86%). It found no significant association with technique or radiation modality. Pooled new stress incontinence was 19% (95% CI 10–31%).<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup>

| Findings | Favored approach | Basis |
|---|---|---|
| Short (about 2–2.5 cm or less), obliterative or near-obliterative bulbomembranous segment; healthy proximal urethra reachable; competent bladder neck | **EPA** | Lowest long-term recurrence in comparative data<sup>[[7]](#ref7)</sup> |
| Longer (more than 2.5 cm) or non-obliterative stenosis with a usable dorsal urethral plate | **Dorsal onlay buccal mucosa graft (BMG)** | Guideline preference for augmentation of longer segments; avoids circumferential excision under tension<sup>[[13]](#ref13)[[8]](#ref8)</sup> |
| Pre-existing incontinence, or an AUS is likely to be needed | **Non-transecting repair** (dorsal onlay BMG or non-transecting anastomotic technique) | Transecting urethroplasty is associated with more AUS erosion and explantation<sup>[[11]](#ref11)[[33]](#ref33)</sup> |
| Long segment, poor graft bed or previous failed repair | **Ventral BMG with a gracilis muscle flap** | 16 of 20 (80%) successful in a high-risk series<sup>[[34]](#ref34)</sup> |
| Radionecrosis, cavitation, dystrophic calcification, fistula, pubic osteomyelitis, a small or painful bladder, or repeated failure | **Diversion or long-term suprapubic drainage**; subtotal (salvage) prostatectomy or fistula repair where feasible | Patency alone will not restore function<sup>[[12]](#ref12)[[13]](#ref13)[[14]](#ref14)</sup> |

## Excision and Primary Anastomosis

### Technique in the radiated field

The general operation is described on [Excision and Primary Anastomosis](/docs/surgical-techniques/04a-urethral-reconstruction/excision-primary-anastomosis). The radiation-specific points are:

1. **Exposure.** Use lithotomy through a perineal incision (see [incisions and approaches](/docs/surgical-techniques/04a-urethral-reconstruction/male-urethroplasty-incisions-approaches)), and keep lithotomy time as short as practical. Mobilize the bulbar urethra to the bulbomembranous junction.
2. **Find the proximal lumen.** Pass a sound, [Gelman visualizing sound](/docs/foundations/tools/instruments/sounds-bougies/gelman-visualizing-sound) or flexible cystoscope antegrade through the SPT tract to locate the proximal urethra.
3. **Excise all radiated scar back to healthy, bleeding mucosa.** The healthy proximal margin often lies in the prostatic urethra. In Chung's series, dissection extended into the prostatic urethra in 67% of radiation cases.<sup>[[21]](#ref21)</sup>
4. **Build a spatulated, tension-free, mucosa-to-mucosa anastomosis.** This follows the core principles of [urethral reconstruction](/docs/surgical-techniques/04a-urethral-reconstruction/urethral-reconstruction-principles).
5. **Gain length only as needed.** Use the progressive perineal maneuvers (crural separation, inferior pubectomy and, rarely, supracrural rerouting) described for [PFUI](/docs/special-populations/05a-trauma-emergencies/pfui).
6. **Consider tissue interposition selectively.** In the 137-patient TURNS series, [gracilis](/docs/foundations/surgical-principles/flaps/gracilis) use was not associated with recurrence.<sup>[[35]](#ref35)</sup>
7. **Drain and confirm.** Leave urethral and suprapubic catheters, and image before removing the urethral catheter. Schedule early surveillance cystoscopy; reported protocols use cystoscopy at 4 months, or at 6 and 12 months.<sup>[[36]](#ref36)[[37]](#ref37)</sup>

**Vessel-sparing and non-transecting variants.** [Vessel-sparing EPA](/docs/surgical-techniques/04a-urethral-reconstruction/jordan-vessel-sparing-epa) and [non-transecting anastomotic repair](/docs/surgical-techniques/04a-urethral-reconstruction/non-transecting-bulbar) preserve bulbar blood supply in nonradiated tissue. No radiation-specific comparison shows a continence advantage, and radiation may already have compromised the bulbar arteries. The AUS literature nevertheless favors non-transecting repair when a sphincter is anticipated; see [AUS after reconstruction](#continence-and-the-artificial-urinary-sphincter).<sup>[[11]](#ref11)</sup>

| Series | Patients | Patency | Continence and other outcomes | Follow-up |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Meeks 2011, 3 centers | 30 (24 EPA, 4 flap, 2 BMG) | 73% | Persistent incontinence 40%, transient 10%; 13% received an AUS; ED unchanged (47% to 50%)<sup>[[38]](#ref38)</sup> | Mean 21 months |
| Hofer 2014, 3 centers | 66 EPA (of 72 treated) | 69.7% | New incontinence in 12 (18.5%), associated with length over 2 cm and treating center<sup>[[2]](#ref2)</sup> | Mean 3.5 years |
| Rourke 2016 | 35 (23 EPA, 7 BMG, 5 flap) | 85.7% (whole group) | Adverse change in continence 25.7% (13.3% without prior TURP); adverse erectile change 30.4%, all after EPA<sup>[[37]](#ref37)</sup> | Mean 50.5 months |

| Voelzke 2021, 10 centers (TURNS) | 137 EPA; mean length 2.3 cm | 86.9% | Later AUS in 30 (21.9%): 25 transcorporal cuffs, 5 erosions<sup>[[35]](#ref35)</sup> | Mean 32.3 months |

**Adjunct maneuvers.** In the TURNS EPA series, surgeons used corporal splitting in 71.5% of cases, partial prostatectomy in 37.2%, partial pubectomy in 12.4% and a gracilis flap in 23.4%. Ivan and colleagues' summary of the full report gives new stress incontinence of 32.1%.<sup>[[35]](#ref35)[[14]](#ref14)</sup>

**Predictors of EPA failure.** In the TURNS series, older age (HR 1.09) and longer stenosis (HR 2.62) independently predicted recurrence; combined-modality radiotherapy was significant only on univariate analysis.<sup>[[35]](#ref35)</sup> Keith's rising recurrence with longer follow-up shows why short-term patency overstates durability.<sup>[[39]](#ref39)</sup> Several series share centers and possibly patients, so do not add them together.

## Buccal Mucosa Graft Substitution

### Dorsal onlay (non-transecting)

A dorsal onlay opens the stenosis along its dorsal surface and augments it with oral mucosa, avoiding circumferential transection and extensive mobilization of the membranous urethra. Kaldany, Cedars and Nikolavsky describe their technique as follows:<sup>[[40]](#ref40)</sup>

1. Moderate lithotomy with slight Trendelenburg; midline perineal incision.
2. **Unilateral bulbospongiosus dissection**, usually on the left, to limit disturbance of neurovascular supply and muscular support.
3. Dorsal longitudinal urethrotomy over a bougie à boule, with **excision of a dorsal wedge of fibrosis between 11 and 1 o'clock**. Dorsal dissection is kept limited to protect the rhabdosphincter.
4. The buccal graft is fixed distally with a temporary stay. Proximal apical sutures are passed outside-in with a bent [J-hook ("ski") needle](/docs/foundations/tools/instruments/urethral-specialty/ski-needle-j-hook), or with the [RD180 device](/docs/foundations/tools/instruments/urethral-specialty/rd180-suturing-device) for deep defects. The graft is then parachuted into place.
5. The graft is quilted with running sutures; alternatives include the RD180, fibrin sealant or barbed suture. A [lighted urethral speculum](/docs/foundations/tools/instruments/urethral-specialty/ravini) aids exposure.
6. A 16 Fr silicone catheter remains for 3 weeks, with retrograde urethrography before removal. Cystoscopy is done at 1 year.

In their institutional subset of 18 men with VUAS after prostatectomy and radiation, success was 89% at median 39.9 months, with no new incontinence.<sup>[[40]](#ref40)</sup> See [Dorsal Onlay BMG for BNC / VUAS](/docs/surgical-techniques/04ab-bladder-neck-reconstruction/bnc-vuas/dorsal-bmg-bnc), [Dorsal Onlay Oral Mucosal Graft Urethroplasty](/docs/surgical-techniques/04a-urethral-reconstruction/dorsal-onlay-omg) and [Buccal Mucosa Graft](/docs/foundations/surgical-principles/grafts/buccal-mucosa) for graft harvest and other technical variants.

### Ventral onlay and graft-plus-flap repair

[Ventral onlay](/docs/surgical-techniques/04a-urethral-reconstruction/ventral-onlay-omg) needs less dorsal dissection. In radiated series, however, recurrence was 29–33%, and new or persistent incontinence was common (see table below).<sup>[[41]](#ref41)[[42]](#ref42)</sup> Barbagli's sphincter-preserving ventral technique reported incontinence in 3 of 69 men, but its population had post-TURP stenosis without radiation.<sup>[[43]](#ref43)</sup> For long strictures in a poor bed, a ventral BMG placed on a vascularized gracilis flap is an option for the highest-risk cases.<sup>[[34]](#ref34)</sup>

## EPA Versus BMG

**Rourke and Gelman 2026** is the largest comparison. It reviewed 212 men with radiation-induced bulbomembranous stenosis at two centers (2001–2024): 163 underwent anastomotic urethroplasty and 49 buccal onlay. Median stenosis length was 2.0 cm, and median follow-up in men without recurrence was 97 months. The radiation was brachytherapy in 46%, EBRT in 45% and combined in 9%. Recurrence was defined as a stricture narrower than 16 Fr on cystoscopy.<sup>[[7]](#ref7)</sup>

- BMG was independently associated with recurrence (HR 3.43, 95% CI 1.03–11.47; P = .046).
- Estimated recurrence at 1, 2 and 10 years was 17%, 18% and 20% after BMG, versus 3.3%, 5.0% and 6.8% after EPA.
- The groups did not differ in complications (10% versus 4.1%), satisfaction (90% versus 88%), new erectile dysfunction (15% versus 8.2%) or **new incontinence (18% versus 18%)**.

The authors concluded that anastomotic repair is preferable **when technically feasible**. The study was retrospective, surgeons chose the technique, and the confidence interval is wide. It shows an association, not proof that EPA would have succeeded in the men who received a graft.

### Why the continence data appear to conflict

Dorsal onlay series report very low new-incontinence rates, yet the head-to-head comparison found no difference. Three points reconcile this:

1. **Denominators.** Many dorsal onlay patients were already incontinent: 28 of 45 in Sterling and 29 of 79 in Policastro. Only continent men can develop new incontinence. Policastro's 8.1% applies to its 37 continent men.<sup>[[9]](#ref9)[[8]](#ref8)</sup>
2. **Anatomy.** EPA-associated incontinence concentrates in stenoses extending into the prostatic urethra and in those longer than 2 cm.<sup>[[21]](#ref21)[[2]](#ref2)</sup> These are the stenoses most often grafted.
3. **Direct comparison.** When the same two centers compared techniques, new incontinence was identical at 18%.<sup>[[7]](#ref7)</sup>

**Practical synthesis.** For a short, obliterative bulbomembranous stenosis with a competent bladder neck, EPA gives the most durable patency and is preferred when technically feasible. For longer or non-obliterative disease, pre-existing incontinence, or when preserving the urethra for a future AUS is the priority, a non-transecting dorsal onlay graft is reasonable. The trade-off is a higher long-term recurrence risk.<sup>[[7]](#ref7)[[13]](#ref13)[[11]](#ref11)</sup>

## Robotic Reconstruction After Radiation

A robotic transabdominal repair can stay above the external sphincter. Options include reanastomosis, an anterior bladder-flap Y-V plasty or a buccal graft, and the approach is best suited to anastomotic stenosis. Data after radiation are small and less favorable than in nonradiated men:

- **Bearrick 2022 (single surgeon, 21 men).** The 5 radiated men had 40% complications at 30 days and 80% reintervention. Functional success was 60%, with a median of 10.5 pads per day, and 80% later received an AUS. After prostatectomy without radiation, the corresponding figures were 20% complications, 10% reintervention, 100% functional success, a median of 0 pads per day and 30% AUS placement.<sup>[[44]](#ref44)</sup>
- **TURNS robotic series.** Half of the 32 men were radiated, and 75% of the whole cohort were patent at median 12 months.<sup>[[45]](#ref45)</sup> Ivan and colleagues note that 7 of the 16 radiated men needed a perineal counterincision, and that this could not be predicted before surgery. Counsel patients that a perineal incision may be added.<sup>[[14]](#ref14)</sup>
- **Single-port robotic buccal graft urethroplasty (Liu 2022).** Nine patients, eight after prostate cancer treatment; mean defect length 3.9 cm. Five needed flaps (rectus abdominis in 3, omental in 1, gracilis in 1). Median operative time was 377 minutes. Within 30 days, 1 stricture recurred and 1 patient had a small-bowel obstruction requiring surgery.<sup>[[46]](#ref46)</sup>
- **One center's routine adjuncts.** Naser-Tavakolian and Lee routinely interpose omentum between the repair and the pubic bone, using rectus if omentum is unavailable. They add a gracilis flap for combined approaches and give hyperbaric oxygen (40 sessions at 2.5 ATA) to every radiated patient. This is institutional practice, not comparative evidence, and the senior author consults for a robotic manufacturer. See [Hyperbaric Oxygen Therapy](/docs/foundations/surgical-principles/hyperbaric-oxygen-therapy) for the trial evidence.<sup>[[22]](#ref22)</sup>

See [Robotic Posterior Urethroplasty](/docs/surgical-techniques/04a-urethral-reconstruction/robotic-posterior-urethroplasty), [Robotic Bladder Flap Posterior Urethroplasty](/docs/surgical-techniques/04ab-bladder-neck-reconstruction/bnc-vuas/robotic-bladder-flap-urethroplasty) and the [VUAS treatment atlas](/docs/surgical-techniques/04ab-bladder-neck-reconstruction/vuas).

## Continence and the Artificial Urinary Sphincter

**Sequence.** Resolve the stenosis, confirm stable patency cystoscopically, then treat stress incontinence. EAU advises deferring incontinence surgery for at least 6 months after a redo vesicourethral anastomosis.<sup>[[6]](#ref6)</sup> Implanting a sphincter over an unstable urethra risks erosion and makes recurrent stenosis harder to treat. See [Artificial Urinary Sphincter (Procedure)](/docs/surgical-techniques/04f-incontinence-procedures/procedures/artificial-urinary-sphincter) and [Stress Urinary Incontinence (Male)](/docs/clinical-conditions/03a-storage-incontinence/sui-male).

**Risk after radiation and urethroplasty:**

| Study | Design | Finding |
|---|---|---|
| McGeady 2014 | 86 AUS at one institution | Compared with an uncompromised urethra, failure risk increased with prior radiation (HR 4.78), prior urethroplasty (HR 8.61) and prior AUS (HR 8.14). A 3.5 cm cuff carried higher failure risk (HR 8.62); transcorporal placement did not (HR 1.21). Confidence intervals were wide.<sup>[[10]](#ref10)</sup> |
| Davis 2026 | 178 AUS after urethroplasty; 15 institutions | Complications requiring explantation occurred in 56.2% after transecting urethroplasty versus 23.5% after non-transecting urethroplasty.<sup>[[11]](#ref11)</sup> |
| Deameh 2026 | Meta-analysis; 4 studies, 533 patients | Prior urethroplasty was associated with explantation (RR 2.05). Transecting repair was associated with erosion (RR 2.34) and explantation (RR 2.38) compared with non-transecting repair. All included studies were observational.<sup>[[33]](#ref33)</sup> |
| Voelzke 2021 | AUS after EPA for radiation stenosis | 30 of 137 men received an AUS; 25 needed a transcorporal cuff and 5 eroded.<sup>[[35]](#ref35)</sup> |

When an AUS is likely, choose a repair that keeps the urethra intact where anatomy allows. Consider a transcorporal cuff in a compromised urethra, and counsel patients about the higher erosion and explantation risk.

## Devastated Outlet, Fistula and Diversion

A **devastated outlet** is recalcitrant stenosis with loss of useful voiding function, often with fistula, necrosis, pubic-bone involvement or an end-stage bladder. It calls for a decision between reconstruction and planned diversion, not another endoscopic attempt.

- **Diversion series.** Faris reviewed 30 men diverted after prostate RT. Indications were fistula (37%), end-stage bladder (20%), devastated outlet (27%), or end-stage bladder plus devastated outlet (17%). Patients had undergone a mean of 4.4 salvage procedures first. Options included cystectomy with conduit, conduit alone and a long-term indwelling SPT. Every patient with a rectourethral fistula had received brachytherapy seeds.<sup>[[12]](#ref12)</sup>
- **Guidance.** EAU suggests diversion for an incapacitated bladder or disabling local symptoms, and cystectomy for intractable bladder pain, spasms or hematuria (weak recommendations).<sup>[[6]](#ref6)</sup> See [Urinary Diversion Principles](/docs/surgical-techniques/04c-urinary-diversion/urinary-diversion-principles) and [Cancer Survivorship](/docs/special-populations/05d-cancer-survivorship).
- **Orthotopic salvage.** Patil described salvage cystectomy, ileal neobladder and urethral pull-through with staged AUS in 8 highly selected patients, 6 after radiation. All were socially continent at median 58 months, after a median of 2 AUS revisions.<sup>[[47]](#ref47)</sup>
- **Rectourethral fistula.** In a 201-patient multi-institutional series, repair succeeded in 87% after energy ablation versus 99% after prostatectomy alone. Concomitant BNC or stricture was present in 26% versus 14%.<sup>[[48]](#ref48)</sup> See [Rectourethral Fistula](/docs/clinical-conditions/03f-fistulas/in-males/rectourethral) and [Transperineal Approach to RUF](/docs/surgical-techniques/04h-fistula-repair/male/transperineal-ruf-repair).
- **Radiated bladder-neck or prostatic stenosis** in a fit man with a functional bladder may be treated by [reconstructive salvage prostatectomy](/docs/surgical-techniques/04ab-bladder-neck-reconstruction/outlet-continence/salvage-prostatectomy) (EAU weak recommendation).<sup>[[6]](#ref6)</sup>

## Follow-Up

- **Surveillance cystoscopy.** In a 304-patient TURNS urethroplasty cohort, a lumen narrower than 17 Fr at the first postoperative urethroscopy (median 3.7 months) led to secondary intervention in 64%, versus 15% with a normal lumen.<sup>[[49]](#ref49)</sup> In a prospective endoscopic follow-up series, 26 of 27 recurrences appeared within the first year, and 7 of the 11 recurrences with flow data had a peak flow above 15 mL/s.<sup>[[50]](#ref50)</sup> Neither cohort was specific to radiation.
- **Long-term review.** Radiation-related urethral complications keep accumulating, and radiation predicts late recurrence, so follow these patients longer than the one-year minimum used after routine urethroplasty.<sup>[[20]](#ref20)[[15]](#ref15)</sup>
- **Beyond patency.** Reassess continence, erectile function, storage symptoms, hematuria and fistula symptoms. Coordinate cancer surveillance with the treating team.

---

## Evidence Limitations

- Almost all data are retrospective series from high-volume referral centers, and several cohorts overlap.
- Success definitions differ: cystoscopic caliber of 16–17 Fr, need for further instrumentation, or voiding without a catheter.
- The surgeon chose the technique based on anatomy; there are no randomized comparisons.
- New-incontinence rates depend on whether the denominator includes men who were already incontinent.
- Few studies separate EBRT, brachytherapy, combined treatment and radiation after prostatectomy.
