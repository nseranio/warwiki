# Underactive Bladder

**Underactive bladder (UAB)** is a symptom complex of slow stream, hesitancy, straining, and prolonged voiding, with or without a feeling of incomplete emptying; storage symptoms may coexist.<sup>[[16]](#ref16)</sup> Elevated PVR is a measured finding, not part of the symptom definition. **Detrusor underactivity (DU)** is the urodynamic diagnosis: a detrusor contraction of reduced strength and/or duration, causing prolonged emptying or failure to empty completely within a normal time span.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[2]](#ref2)</sup>

The distinction matters. Patients describe UAB; urodynamics proves DU. Low flow alone does not distinguish DU from bladder outlet obstruction (BOO), and many patients have both.

---

## Terminology

| Term | Meaning | Practical use |
|---|---|---|
| **Underactive bladder (UAB)** | Symptom complex associated with impaired emptying | Clinical syndrome; analogous to OAB |
| **Detrusor underactivity (DU)** | Urodynamic diagnosis of weak and/or short detrusor contraction | Requires pressure-flow testing |
| **Acontractile detrusor (ACD)** | No demonstrable detrusor contraction on urodynamics | Worst contractility phenotype; catheter dependence more likely |
| **Impaired detrusor contractility** | Reduced detrusor strength, speed, or persistence | Broader physiologic concept |
| **DHIC** | Detrusor hyperactivity with impaired contractility | Storage overactivity plus poor emptying |

DU is not synonymous with retention. A patient can void with low efficiency, empty by abdominal straining, or have apparently acceptable PVR early in the disease.

Voiding mechanism also varies among women without a distinct disease process. In a urodynamic study of 13 women with stress incontinence, five initiated voiding with Valsalva, three mainly by lowering maximal urethral pressure, and five by the pattern observed in healthy controls.<sup>[[24]](#ref24)</sup> In a separate study of 70 women before and after retropubic urethropexy, 35 (50%) voided without a detrusor contraction preoperatively; of these, half developed a detrusor contraction once surgery raised urethral resistance, and the other half continued to strain, with more flow reduction and a longer need for postoperative bladder drainage.<sup>[[25]](#ref25)</sup> A urodynamic trace without a measurable detrusor contraction in a woman who empties adequately does not by itself indicate an acontractile bladder.

## Epidemiology

True prevalence is unknown because most data come from referral cohorts undergoing urodynamics.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[3]](#ref3)</sup>

Approximate ranges in urodynamic series:

- **Men with non-neurogenic LUTS:** 10-48% (EAU 2026 cites 10% in men referred for videourodynamics, up to 48% at age 70 or older)<sup>[[23]](#ref23)</sup>
- **Older women with non-neurogenic LUTS undergoing urodynamic evaluation:** 12-45%<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup>
- **Women undergoing urodynamics for pelvic floor disorders:** 4.1-37.0% in one 2,092-woman cohort, depending on the definition used<sup>[[35]](#ref35)</sup>
- **Institutionalized older adults with incontinence:** DU and DHIC are common contributors

Ambulatory urodynamic series in women put DU nearer 12-19%, and about 30% of older women in one series had detrusor overactivity with DU.<sup>[[17]](#ref17)</sup> Prevalence is definition-sensitive. A strict pressure-flow index captures a different population than a voiding-efficiency definition, and many published thresholds were derived in men before being applied to women.

## Pathophysiology

DU is a final common pathway, not a single disease.

### Myogenic failure

- Loss of detrusor smooth muscle cells
- Collagen deposition and fibrosis
- Impaired excitation-contraction coupling
- Reduced contraction speed, duration, and reserve power

### Neurogenic failure

- Sacral / cauda equina lesions
- Peripheral neuropathy, especially diabetes
- Pelvic autonomic nerve injury after radical pelvic surgery
- Spinal cord disease, multiple sclerosis, stroke, Parkinson disease, and other NLUTD phenotypes
- Impaired afferent sensation: bladder fills but the patient does not generate an adequate voiding reflex

### Vascular and metabolic injury

Microvascular disease, diabetes, aging, and cardiovascular disease may reduce detrusor blood supply and impair neural and muscular reserve.<sup>[[4]](#ref4)</sup>

### BOO-related decompensation

A three-stage model of bladder remodeling with BOO has been hypothesized from limited human evidence:<sup>[[5]](#ref5)</sup>

1. **Hypertrophy.** The detrusor works against higher outlet resistance.
2. **Compensation.** Voiding pressure rises; filling-phase detrusor overactivity may appear.
3. **Decompensation.** Contractility fails, PVR rises, and DU develops.<sup>[[5]](#ref5)</sup>

This is why outlet surgery can help some men with DU: removing obstruction may let a weak but still viable detrusor empty better. It will not rescue an acontractile, insensate bladder reliably.

## Risk Factors and Etiology

| Category | Examples |
|---|---|
| **Age-related** | Detrusor muscle aging, sensory decline, comorbidity accumulation |
| **Metabolic / vascular** | Diabetes, metabolic syndrome, cardiovascular disease, microvascular disease |
| **Neurogenic** | Spinal cord injury, cauda equina syndrome, multiple sclerosis, stroke, Parkinson disease, peripheral neuropathy |
| **Iatrogenic** | Radical pelvic surgery, hysterectomy, rectal surgery, pelvic radiation, medications |
| **Outlet-related** | Chronic BPH/BPO, urethral stricture, bladder neck contracture, dysfunctional voiding |
| **Medication-related** | Anticholinergics, opioids, antihistamines, sympathomimetics, tricyclics, some antipsychotics |

In practice, the most common patient is mixed: older, diabetic or vascular, exposed to anticholinergic medications, with some degree of outlet obstruction or pelvic-floor dysfunction.

## Clinical Presentation

### Voiding symptoms

- Weak stream
- Hesitancy
- Intermittency
- Straining
- Prolonged voiding time
- Terminal dribbling
- Need to double void

### Post-micturition symptoms

- Incomplete emptying
- Post-void dribbling
- Early return of urge after voiding

The sensation of incomplete emptying can reflect retained urine, altered bladder sensation, or a detrusor after-contraction (a post-void rise in detrusor pressure, most often seen with detrusor overactivity, whose significance is not established). In a series of 331 symptomatic women undergoing ambulatory urodynamics, after-contractions were identified in 37% and were associated with detrusor overactivity.<sup>[[26]](#ref26)</sup>

### Storage symptoms

- Frequency
- Nocturia
- Urgency
- Urgency incontinence

Storage symptoms can reflect DHIC, chronic retention, recurrent infection, high residual volumes, or coexisting OAB.

### Signs

- Elevated PVR
- Palpable bladder
- Overflow incontinence
- Recurrent UTI
- Reduced or absent bladder sensation
- Hydronephrosis in severe chronic retention

---

## DU vs BOO

Symptoms overlap heavily. The reconstructive trap is assuming that every weak stream is obstruction or that every low-flow tracing is DU.

| Feature | More suggestive of DU | More suggestive of BOO |
|---|---|---|
| Sensation | Reduced filling sensation, delayed first desire | Normal sensation, urgency from obstruction |
| Flow | Low flow with low detrusor pressure | Low flow with high detrusor pressure |
| PVR | Often high, may be very high | Variable; rises with decompensation |
| Prostate / urethra | Small prostate or no clear anatomic narrowing | BPH, stricture, bladder neck contracture, sling obstruction |
| Urodynamics | Low Pdet@Qmax, low voiding efficiency | High pressure-low flow |

**Pressure-flow urodynamics is the decisive test** when the diagnosis will change treatment.

---

## DHIC

**Detrusor hyperactivity with impaired contractility (DHIC)** is the combination of detrusor overactivity during filling and impaired contractility during voiding. It is common in frail older adults and may present as incontinence, urgency, recurrent cystitis, retention, or both.<sup>[[6]](#ref6)</sup>

The management problem is obvious: OAB drugs may worsen emptying, while cholinergic drugs do not reliably improve emptying. Treatment usually prioritizes safe emptying first, then storage control.

Practical sequence:

1. Measure PVR before starting antimuscarinic or botulinum therapy.
2. Treat infection, constipation, and medication contributors.
3. Establish reliable emptying if PVR is high: CIC, caregiver catheterization, or suprapubic tube.
4. Add storage therapy cautiously: beta-3 agonist, low-burden antimuscarinic, or botulinum only if catheterization is feasible.

## Evaluation

### Initial assessment

- LUTS history: voiding, storage, post-micturition, duration, bother
- Retention history: acute retention, catheter episodes, failed void trials
- Neurologic history: spine disease, diabetes, stroke, MS, Parkinson disease, pelvic surgery
- Medication review
- Bowel function and constipation
- Pelvic / prostate / neurologic exam
- Urinalysis and urine culture when symptomatic
- PVR measurement, repeated at least once if unexpectedly high (fluid intake, incomplete voiding time, or straining can produce a false-positive reading, and no single PVR value defines chronic retention in women)
- In suspected non-neurogenic chronic urinary retention, obtain serum creatinine and renal ultrasonography to assess renal function and upper-tract dilation.<sup>[[34]](#ref34)</sup>
- Uroflowmetry
- Voiding diary when frequency or nocturia is prominent

### Urodynamics

Urodynamics is required for a definitive diagnosis of DU.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[7]](#ref7)</sup>

Typical findings:

- Low Qmax
- Low Pdet@Qmax
- Slowly rising or brief detrusor contraction
- Intermittent or unsustained contraction
- Large PVR
- Low voiding efficiency
- Reduced bladder sensation
- Abdominal straining during voiding

### Contractility metrics

No single criterion is universally accepted.

| Metric | Formula / concept | Use |
|---|---|---|
| **BCI** | Pdet@Qmax + 5 x Qmax<sup>[[20]](#ref20)</sup> | Common in men; BCI &lt;100 is the usual research surrogate for DU, though DU is a spectrum and no single cutoff is universally accepted |
| **Voiding efficiency** | Voided volume / bladder capacity | Simple functional endpoint; useful across sexes |
| **Pdet@Qmax** | Detrusor pressure at maximum flow | Separates low-pressure-low-flow from high-pressure-low-flow |
| **Video-UDS** | Pressure-flow plus fluoroscopy | Best for mixed DU/BOO, bladder neck dysfunction, sphincter non-relaxation |

Schäfer-nomogram-derived metrics such as BCI were developed in men and can overestimate detrusor contractility in women. A modified index, PIP1 (Pdet@Qmax + Qmax), was derived retrospectively from baseline data in 100 symptomatic women 53 years or older with urge incontinence; PIP1 typically ranged from 30 to 75 cm H2O in that population, a population-specific figure that has not been validated as a normal reference value or in younger women.<sup>[[27]](#ref27)</sup> In the same cohort, a continuous mechanical stop test was more reliable than a voluntary stop test for measuring detrusor contraction strength, though interrupting flow adds a step that limits routine use.<sup>[[28]](#ref28)</sup>

### When to obtain video-urodynamics

- Prior outlet surgery or pelvic reconstruction
- Suspected BOO plus weak detrusor
- Neurogenic disease
- Elevated PVR before continence surgery
- Women with suspected sling obstruction or dysfunctional voiding
- Men with small prostate and severe low-flow symptoms
- Failed outlet surgery or unexpected postoperative retention

---

## Management Principles

The goals are:

1. Prevent overdistension, UTI, stones, hydronephrosis, and renal injury.
2. Make bladder emptying reliable.
3. Treat storage symptoms without worsening retention.
4. Avoid unnecessary outlet surgery in acontractile or insensate bladders.

---

## Conservative and Drainage Management

### Clean intermittent catheterization

**Clean intermittent catheterization (CIC)** is the preferred long-term drainage strategy when emptying is inadequate and the patient or caregiver can perform it; AUA/SUFU recommends it over indwelling catheters in NLUTD (statement 35, strong recommendation, Grade C), and EAU makes it a strong recommendation with thorough training in technique and risks.<sup>[[8]](#ref8)[[9]](#ref9)[[16]](#ref16)</sup> In a tertiary retrospective cohort of 133 patients already performing CIC for DU or acontractility, 75% still required bladder drainage long term.<sup>[[8]](#ref8)</sup> Learning success also varies by age and sex: in one service teaching CIC with structured before-visit information, supervised practice, and follow-up support, 6-week success was 91% in men and 80% in women under 65, and 86% in men and 68% in women 65 or older.<sup>[[29]](#ref29)</sup>

Advantages:

- Lower urethral erosion risk than indwelling catheter
- May reduce stone burden and urethral complications; symptomatic UTI susceptibility does not clearly differ from indwelling drainage in the general neuro-urological population.<sup>[[15]](#ref15)[[23]](#ref23)</sup>
- Preserves sexual function and mobility better than urethral catheter
- Allows measured emptying schedule

Practical targets:

- Catheterize often enough to avoid painful distension and high residual volumes.
- Use hydrophilic or closed systems when recurrent trauma or UTI is an issue.
- Match plan to dexterity, cognition, caregiver support, and urethral anatomy.
- Warn patients, particularly women with Fowler-type sphincter non-relaxation, that catheterization can be uncomfortable: 69% of 91 women with Fowler's syndrome reported a gripping sensation and discomfort, particularly on withdrawal, in one survey.<sup>[[30]](#ref30)</sup> Teach with an experienced clinician and consider alternative drainage if CIC is not tolerated.

### Suprapubic catheter

Use when CIC is not feasible, urethral catheterization is traumatic, or long-term drainage is required. SPC is generally preferable to chronic urethral catheterization when a durable catheter route is needed.

### Behavioral measures

- Timed voiding
- Double voiding
- Avoid bladder overdistension
- Treat constipation
- Reduce sedating and anticholinergic medication burden
- Avoid Valsalva voiding as the only plan in high-risk or obstructed patients

Valsalva voiding may empty an areflexic, low-resistance outlet, but it is a poor solution when outlet resistance is high, pelvic organ prolapse is present, or upper-tract risk exists. Warn women who strain to void that it raises prolapse risk.<sup>[[16]](#ref16)</sup> EAU gives weak recommendations against routine intravesical electrical stimulation for women with UAB and against intravesical prostaglandins for female retention outside a well-regulated, closely monitored research study.<sup>[[16]](#ref16)</sup> An 8-year single-center retrospective series of 105 patients with detrusor underactivity of mixed cause treated with intravesical electrical stimulation reported a greater-than-50% reduction in postvoid residual in 47% of the 89 patients with pre/post measurements.<sup>[[31]](#ref31)</sup>

## Pharmacologic Therapy

Drug therapy for DU is disappointing. The EAU Neuro-Urology guideline (2026) strongly recommends against prescribing parasympathomimetics for underactive detrusor; the EAU non-neurogenic female LUTS guideline (2026) strongly recommends against their routine use for women with UAB.<sup>[[15]](#ref15)[[16]](#ref16)</sup> Its evidence review found that oral agents do not improve clinical or urodynamic parameters and can cause gastrointestinal upset, blurred vision, bronchospasm, and bradycardia.<sup>[[15]](#ref15)[[16]](#ref16)</sup> A 2022 meta-analysis found low-quality evidence with short follow-up: an early post-void volume reduction that was gone by one month.<sup>[[21]](#ref21)</sup>

| Treatment | Role | Caveat |
|---|---|---|
| **Bethanechol / cholinergic agonists** | Legacy therapy for detrusor atony | Limited efficacy, frequent adverse effects; rarely transformative<sup>[[10]](#ref10)</sup> |
| **Distigmine / anticholinesterase agents** | Used in some regions | Side effects and limited evidence |
| **Alpha blockers** | Reduce bladder neck / prostatic smooth muscle tone | Useful when outlet resistance contributes. AUA/SUFU allows them to improve voiding parameters in NLUTD patients who void spontaneously (statement 34, conditional recommendation, Grade C), EAU strongly recommends them to lower outlet resistance in NLUTD and weakly suggests offering them to women with UAB before invasive options (small studies)<sup>[[9]](#ref9)[[15]](#ref15)[[16]](#ref16)</sup> |
| **Alpha blocker + cholinergic agent** | In one 4-week Japanese trial (n=119), the combination raised flow and lowered PVR; cholinergic alone did not, and the alpha blocker alone lowered IPSS and PVR | Small, short, and superseded by the guideline position above<sup>[[11]](#ref11)</sup> |
| **Beta-3 agonists / antimuscarinics** | Treat storage symptoms or DHIC | Check and monitor PVR; catheterization plan must be clear |

Medication works best when the problem is mixed DU plus functional outlet resistance, not a truly acontractile detrusor.

## Sacral Neuromodulation

Sacral neuromodulation (SNM) is one of the few therapies with meaningful evidence for non-obstructive urinary retention and selected DU.<sup>[[12]](#ref12)[[13]](#ref13)</sup> For NLUTD patients, AUA/SUFU 2021 advises against SNM in SCI or spina bifida (moderate recommendation, Grade C), while EAU Neuro-Urology 2026 recommends considering SNM in selected neurological patients (strong).<sup>[[9]](#ref9)[[15]](#ref15)</sup>

Best candidates:

- Non-obstructive retention
- Preserved bladder sensation
- Some preserved contractility
- Younger patients
- No fixed anatomic obstruction
- Able to participate in trial-phase voiding diary and catheter-volume tracking

Less favorable:

- Acontractile detrusor
- Absent bladder sensation
- Severe chronic overdistension
- Advanced neurogenic disease with poor sacral reflex arc
- Uncorrected outlet obstruction

Published series report roughly half of DU patients responding to the test phase (51% in one 69-patient series; 33% with acontractile detrusor versus 57% with measurable contractility).<sup>[[12]](#ref12)</sup> Younger age also predicted response.<sup>[[12]](#ref12)</sup> EAU strongly recommends SNM for women with UAB refractory to conservative management; trial-phase response ranges 33-90% and permanent-implant success 55-100%, so selection matters.<sup>[[16]](#ref16)</sup>

## Outlet-Directed Treatment

Outlet treatment can help a weak bladder if outlet resistance is meaningfully contributing.

### Men

Consider outlet surgery when video-UDS or cystoscopy shows:

- BPH or BPO with obstruction
- Tight bladder neck
- Urethral stricture
- Bladder neck contracture

Poor external-sphincter relaxation requires a functional-voiding assessment and targeted therapy; it is not by itself an indication to incise a structurally normal outlet.

In a male video-urodynamic DU cohort, voiding efficiency above 66.7% was reached in 68% after outlet surgery, 59% after urethral botulinum toxin, and 18% with conservative care alone.<sup>[[14]](#ref14)</sup> Success was 41% with acontractile detrusor and 18% with absent sensation.<sup>[[14]](#ref14)</sup> A meta-analysis of BPE surgery (17 nonrandomized comparative studies) found no significant perioperative retention difference between DU and normal contractility, with worse Qmax and IPSS at 3–6 months but similar IPSS and quality of life from 12 months.<sup>[[18]](#ref18)</sup> A newer meta-analysis (14 studies; 2,060 men with DU and 2,285 controls) found higher postoperative retention with DU (OR 1.63, 95% CI 1.21–2.20); most studies were retrospective.<sup>[[32]](#ref32)</sup> In a small HoLEP series with at least 24 months of follow-up, 89% of men with DU and 63% with acontractility were catheter-free, and some still needed intermittent catheterization for high PVR.<sup>[[19]](#ref19)</sup> Counsel that catheter dependence can persist.

EAU 2026 male LUTS guideline recommendations for men (all weak unless stated):<sup>[[23]](#ref23)</sup>

- Start intermittent catheterization when PVR exceeds 300 mL or the upper tract is at risk.<sup>[[23]](#ref23)</sup> EAU notes that UTI risk rises above 300 mL.<sup>[[23]](#ref23)</sup> Use an indwelling catheter only when other drainage has failed or is unsuitable, and prefer a suprapubic catheter to avoid traumatic hypospadias.
- EAU describes double voiding and pelvic floor relaxation for men with underactive bladder, and weakly recommends offering an alpha blocker before more invasive techniques.<sup>[[23]](#ref23)</sup>
- Offer an alpha blocker before more invasive treatment. Evidence is limited (level 4). Do not routinely recommend parasympathomimetics (strong).
- Counsel men with DU or an acontractile detrusor and concomitant prostatic enlargement about the potential benefit of prostate surgery. EAU cites a systematic review with IPSS changes of −3 to −19.5 points and mixed comparisons against men without DU. It reports worse outcomes after PVP or TURP than after HoLEP, and with older age, absent obstruction, detrusor overactivity and lower contractility. Case series in men with DU or acontractility and concomitant prostatic obstruction report return of contractility in 78% at 6 months after HoLEP (94.7% voiding spontaneously); a TURP/TUIP series reported improved quality of life and voiding efficiency &gt;50% in 81.7%, with durability data limited.<sup>[[23]](#ref23)</sup>
- In men with DU and no prostatic obstruction, offer a sacral neuromodulation test phase. In a multicenter retrospective series of 35 men, 51.4% responded to the first stage and 72% of implanted men had a favorable response.<sup>[[23]](#ref23)</sup> In a separate 69-patient cohort of men and women, residual contractility was associated with better trial response than acontractility.<sup>[[12]](#ref12)</sup>

### Women

Look for:

- Prior sling obstruction
- Pelvic organ prolapse
- Fowler-type sphincter non-relaxation
- Dysfunctional voiding
- Urethral stricture
- Medication-induced retention

Sling incision or [urethrolysis](/docs/surgical-techniques/04f-incontinence-procedures/procedures/urethrolysis) helps obstruction; it does not treat primary DU and may unmask stress incontinence.

Transurethral bladder neck incision has been described in an earlier retrospective series of 31 women with refractory DU.<sup>[[22]](#ref22)</sup> In a later 82-woman series (59 with neurogenic etiology; median follow-up five years), 40 (48.8%) achieved spontaneous voiding with voiding efficiency of at least 50%; five (6.1%) developed SUI and two (2.4%) developed vesicovaginal fistula.<sup>[[16]](#ref16)[[33]](#ref33)</sup> EAU gives it no recommendation and advises against routine latissimus dorsi detrusor myoplasty, which has one-centre reports only.<sup>[[16]](#ref16)</sup>

### Botulinum toxin to outlet

Urethral sphincter botulinum toxin may help selected patients with poor sphincter relaxation, DSD, or functional outlet obstruction. It is not a detrusor-strengthening therapy. Evidence is retrospective and relief lasts about three months; EAU weakly suggests offering it to women with UAB before more invasive options.<sup>[[16]](#ref16)</sup>

## Reconstructive Decision Points

- **Before SUI surgery:** measure PVR and consider urodynamics when symptoms suggest impaired emptying. A sling or AUS in an underactive bladder can convert borderline emptying into retention.
- **Before BPH surgery:** distinguish BOO from DU. If both are present, counsel that surgery may improve emptying but urgency, weak stream, or catheter need may persist.
- **Before botulinum for OAB:** confirm the patient can catheterize if retention worsens.
- **Before urinary diversion or bladder neck closure:** establish whether the issue is unsafe storage, unsafe emptying, outlet incompetence, or patient-care logistics.
- **After pelvic surgery:** new retention can be neuropraxia, pain or opioids, hematoma, sling obstruction, or true denervation. Do not label permanent DU too early.

---

## Prognosis

Long-term prognostic factors for recovery from DU are poorly established.<sup>[[16]](#ref16)[[23]](#ref23)</sup> In a retrospective cohort of 298 men with DU, normal or increased bladder sensation and measurable detrusor contraction were associated with higher rates of voiding efficiency &gt;66.7% within one year after treatment; absent sensation and acontractility had lower rates. Videourodynamic outlet narrowing informed outlet-directed treatment. Baseline PVR was not associated with treatment outcome in that cohort.<sup>[[14]](#ref14)</sup>

Record retention duration and baseline PVR; assess prolonged overdistension, diabetes, and neurologic disease as possible contributors to DU.<sup>[[16]](#ref16)[[34]](#ref34)</sup> In chronic retention, recurrent culture-proven UTI, stones, and hydronephrosis warrant assessment as complications; ability of the patient or a caregiver to perform CIC informs the drainage plan.<sup>[[34]](#ref34)</sup>

Complications of untreated DU include recurrent UTI, acute retention, overflow incontinence, bladder stones, upper-tract dilation, and renal deterioration.
