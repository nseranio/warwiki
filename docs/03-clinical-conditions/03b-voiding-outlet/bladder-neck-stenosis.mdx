# Bladder Neck Contracture & Vesicourethral Anastomotic Stricture (BNC/VUAS)

**Bladder neck contracture (BNC)** and **vesicourethral anastomotic stricture (VUAS)** are related but distinct obstructive processes at the bladder outlet. They are frequently conflated in the literature, but differ in etiology, anatomy, and management strategy.

---

## Distinguishing BNC from VUAS

| Feature | **BNC** | **VUAS** |
|---|---|---|
| **Setting** | Prostate still present | Post-radical prostatectomy (no prostate) |
| **Prior procedure** | TURP, HoLEP, laser vaporization, TORP, cryotherapy, brachytherapy | Radical prostatectomy (open, laparoscopic, or robotic) |
| **Anatomy** | Fibrosis/scarring of native bladder neck tissue | Circumferential scar at the neovesicourethral anastomosis |
| **Incontinence risk** | Lower (sphincter is preserved below) | Higher — external sphincter is immediately distal to the anastomosis |
| **Recurrence pattern** | Variable; often responds to endoscopic Rx | High recurrence; often requires staged or repeated treatment |
| **Reconstruction** | Bladder neck incision or resection with bladder neck plasty | Vesicourethral anastomotic reconstruction (robotic or open) |

:::note
This page focuses primarily on **VUAS** — the post-prostatectomy entity — as it is more prevalent in the reconstructive urology literature and carries the greater reconstructive complexity. BNC after outlet procedures is addressed where distinctions are clinically relevant.
:::

---

## Vesicourethral Anastomotic Stricture (VUAS)

### Definition

VUAS is defined as symptomatic obstruction at the vesicourethral anastomosis following radical prostatectomy (RP), resulting from fibrosis and cicatricial narrowing at the suture line. It represents one of the most challenging complications of prostate cancer surgery, requiring multimodal management and carrying significant implications for continence and quality of life.

### Incidence

- Overall incidence after RP: **approximately 0.5–32%**, with wide variation depending on definition, follow-up duration, and technique<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup>
- Robot-assisted RP (RARP): 1–3% symptomatic VUAS
- Open retropubic RP: 3–10%
- Higher rates reported after salvage RP, post-radiation RP, or with anastomotic leak
- Median time to presentation: 3–6 months postoperatively; may present years later

---

## Risk Factors

| Category | Risk Factors |
|---|---|
| **Patient** | Diabetes mellitus, obesity, smoking, cardiovascular disease, prior pelvic radiation |
| **Disease** | High-volume cancer, locally advanced disease (pT3), positive surgical margins |
| **Perioperative** | Anastomotic urine leak, prolonged catheterization, urinary tract infection, hematoma/pelvic collection |
| **Technical** | Mucosal eversion technique, posterior reconstruction omitted, suture ischemia, inadequate tissue apposition |
| **Adjuvant therapy** | Salvage radiation therapy, prior radiation (brachytherapy or EBRT) → especially high risk |

Patients treated with **salvage RP** (after radiation failure) or **adjuvant/salvage radiation** after RP are at substantially elevated risk for VUAS and should be counseled accordingly.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup>

## Diagnostic Workup

### Symptoms
- Obstructive voiding symptoms: weak stream, straining, incomplete emptying
- Urinary retention (acute or chronic)
- Recurrent urinary tract infections
- New or worsening urinary incontinence (paradoxical/overflow)
- Hematuria

### Evaluation

**1. Uroflowmetry + post-void residual (PVR)**
- Obstructive flow curve; Qmax typically < 10 mL/s
- Elevated PVR supports significant obstruction

**2. Cystoscopy** *(gold standard)*
- Directly visualizes the anastomotic lumen
- Characterizes stricture: length, caliber, spongiofibrosis, mucosal quality
- Assesses sphincteric function (proximal to stricture) and bladder trabeculation
- Identifies concurrent pathology (bladder stones, tumor recurrence)

**3. Retrograde urethrogram (RUG) + voiding cystourethrogram (VCUG)**
- Delineates stricture length, location, and anatomy
- VCUG demonstrates bladder capacity and neck morphology during voiding
- Essential before surgical reconstruction

**4. Urodynamics (UDS)**
- Indicated when incontinence is concurrent or when sphincter function is uncertain
- Leak point pressure and compliance data inform reconstruction planning
- Distinguishes true obstruction from sphincteric incompetence

**5. Cross-sectional imaging (CT/MRI)**
- Evaluate for local cancer recurrence
- Assess pelvic anatomy before robotic/open reconstruction
- MRI urography if ureteral involvement suspected

**6. PSA**
- Biochemical recurrence assessment; rising PSA may alter management priorities

## Endoscopic Management

Endoscopic treatment is the first-line approach for most VUAS cases. Limitations include high recurrence rates (particularly with longer, more obliterative strictures) and the risk of sphincter injury with aggressive resection.

---

### Urethral Dilation

Simple dilation — with sequential dilators or balloon — is the least invasive option.

- **Mechanism**: Radial force fractures the fibrotic scar
- **Technique**: Performed under direct vision or fluoroscopic guidance; calibrate to 24–26 Fr
- **Outcomes**: High early recurrence; rarely curative as a standalone strategy
- **Role**: Initial temporizing measure; appropriate for tight but short-segment strictures
- **Risk**: Inadvertent sphincter trauma if performed blindly at the anastomotic level

---

### Transurethral Incision of Bladder Neck (TUIBN)

Cold knife or electrosurgical incision of the cicatricial ring at the anastomosis.

- **Technique**: Incisions placed at the **3 and 9 o'clock positions** to avoid the sphincter (12 o'clock risks dorsal venous bleeding; 6 o'clock risks rectum)
- **Depth**: Full-thickness through the scar into periurethral fat — confirmed by blanching/white fat visualization
- **Adjuncts**: Some surgeons administer intralesional triamcinolone or mitomycin C at the time of incision (see below)
- **Outcomes**: Success rates 40–80% for initial VUAS; recurrence common, especially with radiation-associated or obliterative strictures<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup>
- **Catheter**: Foley typically left 24–72 hours postoperatively

---

### Transurethral Resection of Bladder Neck (TURBN)

Electrosurgical or laser resection of the obstructive scar tissue.

- **Technique**: Loop resection of the cicatricial ridge to restore luminal patency; typically combined with incision rather than resection alone
- **Depth of treatment**: Resection to level of normal soft tissue; care to preserve sphincter distally
- **HoLEP/Thulium/laser**: Some centers prefer laser energy (Holmium, Thulium) for tissue precision and hemostasis
- **Limitation**: Removes tissue but does not address the underlying fibrotic process — adjunctive pharmacotherapy may improve durability
- **Recurrence**: High for obliterative or radiation-associated strictures; repeated TURBN associated with progressive sphincter damage and increasing incontinence risk<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup>

---

### Mitomycin C (MMC)

Antifibrotic agent applied topically or intraluminally to inhibit fibroblast proliferation and collagen synthesis at the anastomotic scar.

- **Mechanism**: Alkylating agent; cross-links DNA in fibroblasts → impairs scar reformation
- **Delivery**: Topical application via soaked pledget, intralesional injection, or intravesical instillation at time of endoscopic Rx
- **TURNS (Transurethral Resection of the Bladder Neck with MMC) data**:
  - Application of 0.3–0.4 mg/mL MMC-soaked pledget held at incision site × 5 minutes following TUIBN/TURBN
  - Significantly improved stricture-free survival in comparative studies
  - Data from the TURNS multicenter registry (Shakir et al.) support improved patency rates at 12 months<sup>[[2]](#ref2)</sup>
- **Safety**: Minimal systemic absorption; local tissue toxicity theoretical but not observed at standard concentrations
- **Protocol variation**: Some centers use 4-week intravesical MMC instillation course post-procedure

---

### Intralesional Triamcinolone

Corticosteroid injection to reduce fibroblast activity and modulate the inflammatory phase of scar maturation.

- **Mechanism**: Suppresses TGF-β signaling; reduces collagen cross-linking and fibroblast proliferation
- **Delivery**: Direct intralesional injection (40 mg/mL triamcinolone acetonide) into the scar tissue at time of endoscopic procedure
- **Outcomes**: Shaw et al. (2022) demonstrated that intralesional triamcinolone combined with TUIBN significantly reduced recurrence compared to incision alone in a prospective series<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup>
- **Use case**: Most effective adjunct for early, non-obliterative VUAS in non-irradiated patients
- **Repeat dosing**: Can be administered at subsequent endoscopic sessions

---

### Endoscopic Urethroplasty (Ventral Buccal Mucosa Graft via Endoscope)

An emerging, minimally invasive technique adapting urethroplasty principles to the endoscopic platform.

- **Technique** (Abramowitz et al., 2021 description):
  1. TUIBN to open the stricture lumen
  2. Buccal mucosa graft (BMG) harvested from inner cheek
  3. Graft affixed to the dorsal/ventral wall of the anastomotic lumen using endoscopic suturing or fibrin glue under direct visualization
  4. Catheter left in situ 3–4 weeks
- **Rationale**: Provides a non-scarring mucosal surface to resurface the anastomosis — replacing the fibrotic endpoint with vascularized tissue
- **Outcomes**: Early series demonstrate promising stricture-free rates in patients who have failed prior endoscopic treatment<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup>
- **Limitations**: Technically demanding; requires endoscopic suturing skills; limited comparative data
- **Patient selection**: Reasonable option before open/robotic reconstruction in appropriately selected patients with partial-lumen strictures

## Surgical Reconstruction

Surgical reconstruction is indicated for:
- Failed endoscopic management (typically ≥2–3 prior endoscopic treatments)
- Obliterative VUAS (no visible lumen)
- Radiation-associated VUAS
- Concurrent complex pathology (fistula, diverticulum, incontinence requiring simultaneous AUS)

### Robotic Vesicourethral Anastomotic Reconstruction

Robot-assisted reconstruction has emerged as the preferred platform at high-volume centers — offering the advantages of magnified visualization, articulated instrumentation, and fine suture handling in a confined pelvic space.

#### TURNS Robotic Reconstruction (Shakir et al., 2022)

The **Transurethral Resection and Urethral Reconstruction Study (TURNS)** multi-institutional series represents the largest prospective dataset for robotic VUAS reconstruction.<sup>[[2]](#ref2)</sup>

- **n = 32** patients; median follow-up 22 months
- **Technique**: Robotic excision of the anastomotic scar, reanastomosis of the bladder neck to the membranous urethra
- **Success**: **75% stricture-free patency** at last follow-up
- **Continence**: Most patients required AUS or other continence procedure — sphincter damage from prior failed endoscopic treatment common
- **Conclusion**: Robotic reconstruction achieves durable patency but sphincter management requires careful planning

#### Extraperitoneal Robotic VU Reconstruction (Lavollé et al., 2019)

- **n = 6** patients with refractory VUAS after RP<sup>[[3]](#ref3)</sup>
- **Technique**: Fully extraperitoneal robotic approach — avoids peritoneal cavity, facilitates direct bladder neck / urethral anastomotic access
- **Outcomes**: 5/6 patients stricture-free at follow-up; no major complications
- **Advantage**: Extraperitoneal approach preserves peritoneal integrity for future AUS/sling procedures; reduced ileus risk

#### Robotic Transabdominal VU Bladder Neck Reconstruction (RTV-BNR) (Lee et al., 2025)

The **RTV-BNR** technique represents a refinement of robotic VUAS reconstruction with specific attention to incontinence preservation.<sup>[[4]](#ref4)</sup>

- **n = 11** patients; median follow-up 14 months
- **Technique**:
  1. Transabdominal robotic approach
  2. Meticulous scar excision at the anastomotic level
  3. Bladder neck mobilization and spatulation
  4. Tension-free reanastomosis with mucosa-to-mucosa apposition
  5. Preservation of sphincteric mechanism throughout
- **Success rate: 91%** stricture-free patency
- **Incontinence: 0 patients with de novo stress urinary incontinence**
- **Significance**: Highest reported success rate; unique emphasis on sphincter-sparing technique distinguishes this from prior series where incontinence was a common sequela
- **Conclusion**: RTV-BNR may represent the current benchmark for robotic VUAS reconstruction

### Open Reconstruction

Open vesicourethral reconstruction remains an option and is preferred in select cases:

- **Perineal approach**: Access to posterior urethra/anastomosis via perineal incision — familiar anatomy for urologists trained in open urethroplasty
- **Retropubic approach**: For concurrent bladder neck revision, diverticulectomy, or AUS placement
- **Posterior urethral reconstruction**: Complex obliterative cases with significant gap may require elaborated posterior urethral reconstruction techniques (Bearrick et al., 2022)<sup>[[5]](#ref5)</sup>

**Concomitant AUS placement:**
- In patients with known or anticipated sphincteric incompetence, simultaneous AUS placement at reconstruction is often planned
- Staging (reconstruction first, AUS 3–6 months later) is preferred at many centers to allow healing assessment before prosthetic implantation

## Bladder Neck Contracture (BNC) — After Outlet Procedures

BNC after TURP, HoLEP, laser vaporization, or cryotherapy differs from VUAS in that the native bladder neck and sphincteric complex are intact below.

### Etiology
- Ischemic injury at the bladder neck mucosa during resection/ablation
- Suture-related fibrosis (post-TORP)
- Underdrainage with pelvic hematoma
- Radiation (brachytherapy or EBRT) as primary treatment

### Management Principles

- **Endoscopic first**: TUIBN at 3 and 9 o'clock; intralesional triamcinolone adjunct
- **Self-dilation protocol**: Patients taught to perform intermittent self-catheterization to maintain lumen patency after incision
- **Repeat endoscopic Rx**: Generally well-tolerated given intact distal sphincter
- **Definitive reconstruction**: Rarely required; reserved for obliterative BNC after multiple failed endoscopic attempts
- **Y-V plasty / bladder neck plasty**: Surgical reconstruction for refractory BNC in selected patients with adequate sphincteric function

## Summary: Treatment Algorithm for VUAS

```
VUAS Confirmed (Cystoscopy + VCUG + UDS)
│
├── Patent lumen present?
│   ├── YES → Endoscopic treatment
│   │          TUIBN (3 & 9 o'clock)
│   │          + Intralesional triamcinolone
│   │          + MMC (TURNS protocol)
│   │          ↓
│   │          Recurrence?
│   │          ├── 1st recurrence → Repeat TUIBN + adjuncts; consider endoscopic urethroplasty (BMG)
│   │          └── 2nd+ recurrence → Robotic/open reconstruction
│   │
│   └── NO (obliterative) → Robotic/open reconstruction
│                             RTV-BNR or TURNS technique
│                             ± Concomitant/staged AUS
│
└── Radiation-associated → Higher reconstruction threshold;
                            Pedicled flap interposition consider
                            Staged AUS after reconstruction
```
