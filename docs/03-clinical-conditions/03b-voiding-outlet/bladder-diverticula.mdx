# Bladder Diverticula

A **bladder diverticulum** is an outpouching of bladder mucosa through the detrusor muscle, lined by urothelium but lacking a complete muscular wall of its own. Acquired diverticula in adults form at sites of relative muscular weakness when the detrusor works against chronic outlet resistance or sustained high voiding pressure, and they most often accompany bladder outlet obstruction or neurogenic voiding dysfunction rather than occurring in isolation.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[2]](#ref2)</sup>

The operation has its own page, [Bladder Diverticulectomy](/docs/surgical-techniques/04b-bladder-reconstruction/bladder-diverticulectomy). A ureterocele, a cystic dilation of the intravesical ureter, is covered on [Ectopic Ureter and Ureterocele](/docs/clinical-conditions/03e-upper-tract/ectopic-ureter-ureterocele).<sup>[[15]](#ref15)</sup>

---

## Etiology

Most acquired diverticula in adult men follow long-standing bladder outlet obstruction from benign prostatic hyperplasia; a minority occur in women and in men with detrusor overactivity, neurogenic voiding dysfunction, or prior outlet or bladder-neck surgery.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[2]](#ref2)</sup> Mucosa herniates through the detrusor at a point of structural weakness, classically near the ureteral orifice or along the posterolateral bladder wall.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup> The congenital paraureteral (Hutch) diverticulum of childhood also arises near the ureteral orifice, at the ureterovesical junction, where muscular support is deficient.<sup>[[14]](#ref14)</sup>

---

## Clinical Presentation

Many diverticula are asymptomatic and found incidentally on cystoscopy or imaging performed for another indication. When symptomatic, presentations include:

- Recurrent urinary tract infection, from urinary stasis within the sac
- Irritative or obstructive voiding symptoms attributable to the underlying outlet process
- Bladder or diverticular stones
- Hematuria
- Two-stage or double voiding, when a large diverticulum empties into the bladder after the main void
- A palpable suprapubic mass
- Acute peritonitis from spontaneous intraperitoneal rupture, in case reports<sup>[[16]](#ref16)</sup>

---

## Evaluation

Evaluation defines the diverticulum and its relationship to the ureters, and separates an outlet or neurogenic problem that can be treated on its own from a diverticulum that needs direct treatment.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[2]](#ref2)</sup>

- **Cystoscopy** localizes the diverticular neck, its relationship to the ureteral orifices, and intravesical or intradiverticular pathology. A ureteral orifice opening into the sac changes operative planning; in a six-patient robotic series, ipsilateral ureteroneocystostomy was performed for this anatomy with renal dysfunction.<sup>[[12]](#ref12)</sup>
- **Cross-sectional imaging** (CT or MR urography) defines diverticulum number, size, wall thickness, and proximity to the ureter, and screens for a mass within the sac.
- **Voiding cystourethrography or videourodynamics** assesses filling and emptying of the diverticulum, reflux into it, and the functional contribution of the underlying outlet or detrusor process.
- **Urodynamics** characterizes detrusor contractility and voiding pressures when the cause of poor emptying or upper-tract dilation is unclear. A large diverticulum may fill during voiding and drain back afterward. Men with benign prostatic hyperplasia and a diverticulum (n = 24) had a higher postvoid residual after free flowmetry than men with benign prostatic hyperplasia alone (n = 67; 221.4 versus 45.9 mL), with no significant difference in standard contractility indices and a shorter contraction duration.<sup>[[3]](#ref3)[[4]](#ref4)</sup>
- **Urine cytology and directed biopsy** are considered when imaging or cystoscopy raises concern for a tumor within the sac. Biopsy can perforate the thin diverticular wall, and absent muscularis propria makes conventional T2 pathological staging inapplicable.<sup>[[11]](#ref11)</sup>

### Cancer risk within a diverticulum

Bladder diverticula are associated with bladder cancer: in a Taiwanese population-based cohort, 2,134 hospitalized urology patients with a documented diverticulum had an adjusted hazard ratio of 2.63 (95% CI 1.74–3.97) for subsequent bladder cancer compared with 8,528 matched controls; the association was stronger in men and in patients without comorbidities.<sup>[[5]](#ref5)</sup> Tumors can arise within the diverticulum itself: in a single-institution review of 764 patients with diverticula, 102 (13.3%) had bladder cancer and 36 of those (35.3%) arose within the diverticulum; male sex (odds ratio 2.6) and increasing age (odds ratio 1.02 per year) independently predicted a cancer diagnosis, while an indwelling catheter, recurrent UTI and stones did not.<sup>[[6]](#ref6)</sup>

Urinary stasis has been proposed as a mechanism of tumor development, and a 14-case series proposed that the thin or absent muscular wall may permit early peridiverticular invasion.<sup>[[11]](#ref11)[[13]](#ref13)</sup>

Surgery can reveal unsuspected cancer: most diverticula in the 764-patient review were managed without surgery, and of the 104 patients who underwent diverticulectomy or partial cystectomy, 76% were presumed to have benign disease preoperatively; among 79 patients who underwent diverticulectomy without preoperative suspicion for cancer, 5 had an incidental malignancy on final pathology.<sup>[[6]](#ref6)</sup> These observational cohorts did not establish a surveillance protocol; they support a low threshold for imaging and cystoscopic inspection, with biopsy of suspicious areas.

## Management

### Treat the outlet first when obstruction is the driver

When bladder outlet obstruction or a treatable neurogenic process appears to be driving diverticulum formation, outlet treatment is addressed first and the patient is reassessed for symptoms and diverticular emptying before diverticulectomy is considered, unless a stone, suspected tumor, recurrent infection or urosepsis attributable to the diverticulum, or upper-tract deterioration from ureteral involvement requires earlier intervention.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[2]](#ref2)[[12]](#ref12)</sup> Diverticulectomy, with or without concomitant outlet surgery, is considered for persistent symptoms attributable to the diverticulum, recurrent infection, bladder or diverticular stones, upper-tract deterioration from ureteral involvement, or suspected or confirmed malignancy. Diverticulum size alone is not an indication for excision. The choice between open, laparoscopic and robotic approaches, and the combination with concurrent outlet surgery, is covered on the [Bladder Diverticulectomy](/docs/surgical-techniques/04b-bladder-reconstruction/bladder-diverticulectomy) page.

### Nonoperative and minimally invasive alternatives

Some patients are poor candidates for excisional surgery because of comorbidity, frailty, or anesthetic risk. Options include:

- **Observation** for an asymptomatic diverticulum without stones, significant residual, recurrent infection, or suspicious imaging findings.
- **Catheter drainage** (indwelling or intermittent) when emptying is the dominant problem and surgery is not feasible.
- **Transurethral endoscopic treatment** of the diverticular neck with fulguration of the lining, usually combined with transurethral treatment of the obstructing prostate.

Endoscopic treatment has been described as a lower-morbidity alternative to excision in selected patients. Reported experience consists of small, mostly noncomparative single-center series, without a randomized comparison with excision. In the largest reported series (39 men with diverticula &gt;4 cm, treated 2004–2018), 30 (76.9%) had a reduction of more than 80% of the initial diameter at 3 months, with no significant difference between monopolar and bipolar energy.<sup>[[7]](#ref7)</sup> In a two-center retrospective comparison in which all men also had transurethral prostate resection, therapeutic success (complete resolution or at least 80% size reduction) was 75% after endoscopic fulguration (20 men) and 100% after laparoscopic excision (13 men); the laparoscopic arm had two Clavien-Dindo grade III complications and a much longer operative time.<sup>[[8]](#ref8)</sup> Endoscopic treatment of a large diverticular neck can also precipitate acute urinary retention by abruptly altering bladder hydrodynamics, which has required conventional diverticulectomy to resolve.<sup>[[9]](#ref9)</sup>
