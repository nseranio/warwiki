# Female Urethral & Periurethral Masses

Female urethral and periurethral masses include benign cystic lesions, benign solid tumors, urethral mucosal lesions, urethral diverticula, and rare malignancies. They are diagnostically tricky because symptoms overlap: recurrent UTI, dysuria, dyspareunia, post-void dribbling, urethral bleeding, vaginal discharge, obstructive voiding, incontinence, and "anterior vaginal wall mass" can all describe different pathologies.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[2]](#ref2)</sup>

The practical frame is: most lesions are benign, but malignancy must stay in the differential. Malignancy accounted for 1.6% of all periurethral and anterior vaginal wall masses in one academic series, but about 6% of urethral diverticula contained cancer in two diverticulectomy series, so unexpected malignancy may be found in diverticulum specimens.<sup>[[3]](#ref3)[[4]](#ref4)[[18]](#ref18)</sup>

---

## Epidemiology and Etiology

The dominant etiologies are urethral diverticulum, Skene gland cyst / abscess, urethral caruncle, and other periurethral cysts.<sup>[[3]](#ref3)[[5]](#ref5)</sup>

| Lesion | Typical location | Clue |
|---|---|---|
| **Urethral diverticulum** | Posterior / posterolateral periurethral tissue, often mid-urethral | Communicates with urethra; post-void dribbling, recurrent UTI, dyspareunia, expressible discharge |
| **Skene gland cyst / abscess** | Distal paraurethral glands flanking the meatus | Anterolateral distal periurethral mass; pain or purulent drainage if infected |
| **Urethral caruncle** | Posterior lip of urethral meatus | Postmenopausal, red friable meatal polyp |
| **Urethral mucosal prolapse** | Circumferential meatal mucosal eversion | "Doughnut" mass around central meatus |
| **Gartner / Mullerian / inclusion cyst** | Anterior or anterolateral vaginal wall | No urethral communication; may mimic urethral diverticulum |
| **Leiomyoma** | Urethral or paraurethral smooth muscle | Solid, well-defined, vascular lesion |
| **Primary urethral carcinoma** | Distal or proximal urethra, sometimes within diverticulum | Solid / fixed / bleeding mass, hematuria, obstructive symptoms, suspicious imaging |

In a 126-patient academic series of periurethral and anterior vaginal wall masses, urethral diverticula accounted for 39.7%, Skene gland cysts / abscesses for 30.2%, and malignancy for 1.6%.<sup>[[3]](#ref3)</sup> In a tertiary series of 79 women with periurethral masses, 84% were urethral diverticula (6% of which contained malignancy), 5% leiomyomas, and 2.5% vaginal squamous cell carcinomas.<sup>[[4]](#ref4)</sup> In a 95-patient ultrasound series (98 masses), 39 were urethral diverticula, 35 paraurethral cysts, 13 leiomyomas, 8 caruncles (one with malignant transformation), 2 carcinomas and 1 condyloma.<sup>[[5]](#ref5)</sup>

## Diagnostic Approach

### Start with anatomy

The first diagnostic move is anatomic: decide whether the lesion is meatal, urethral-dependent, periurethral, or vaginal wall / paravaginal.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[15]](#ref15)[[26]](#ref26)[[40]](#ref40)</sup>

| Finding | Most likely diagnoses | Next step |
|---|---|---|
| **Posterior meatal polyp** | Caruncle, carcinoma mimicking caruncle | Trial estrogen if classic and mild; biopsy / excise if atypical, persistent, bleeding, growing, or painful |
| **Circumferential meatal ring** | Urethral prolapse | Estrogen / sitz baths if mild; excise if thrombosed, necrotic, obstructing, or refractory |
| **Anterior vaginal wall mass with expressible urethral fluid** | Urethral diverticulum | Pelvic MRI for surgical mapping; cystoscopy as needed |
| **Distal anterolateral periurethral mass** | Skene gland cyst / abscess | MRI or ultrasound if uncertain; excision or drainage / excision if symptomatic |
| **Solid, vascular, fixed, rapidly growing, or bleeding mass** | Leiomyoma, carcinoma, condyloma, other tumor | MRI + cystourethroscopy + biopsy |
| **Noncommunicating anterior vaginal wall cyst** | Gartner duct, Mullerian, inclusion cyst | See [Vaginal Cysts & Masses](/docs/clinical-conditions/03g-genital-scrotal/vaginal-cysts-masses) |

Clinical examination alone is often informative; in one series, clinical prediction matched final diagnosis in about 81% of cases.<sup>[[17]](#ref17)</sup> The examination should include palpation of the anterior vaginal wall along the entire urethra, inspection of the meatus, attempt to express fluid or debris, cough stress testing if prolapse / mass reduction changes continence, and documentation of tenderness, fixation, bleeding, ulceration, or rapid growth.

### Imaging

**MRI is the preferred modality** for most complex periurethral masses because it determines whether the lesion communicates with the urethra, whether it is cystic or solid, whether there are suspicious enhancing components, and how it relates to the continence mechanism.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[12]](#ref12)[[15]](#ref15)[[26]](#ref26)[[40]](#ref40)</sup> MRI is strongly useful for operative mapping; alternative imaging may be needed when MRI is unavailable or contraindicated.

Transvaginal or transperineal ultrasound is a useful first-line alternative when expertise is available. It can separate cystic from solid lesions, show vascularity on Doppler, and identify common masses such as diverticula, paraurethral cysts, leiomyomas, caruncles, and carcinomas.<sup>[[5]](#ref5)[[17]](#ref17)</sup>

### Cystourethroscopy and biopsy

Cystourethroscopy helps identify diverticular ostia, intraluminal lesions, stones, mucosal irregularity, and lesions requiring biopsy. Biopsy is required for any solid, fixed, ulcerated, rapidly growing, bleeding, recurrent, or otherwise suspicious mass. All excised urethral diverticula and periurethral masses should be sent for pathologic examination.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[18]](#ref18)[[24]](#ref24)</sup>

## Benign Urethral and Periurethral Masses

### Urethral caruncle

Urethral caruncle is the most common urethral mass in postmenopausal women. It is a benign polypoid outgrowth at the urethral meatus, usually from the posterior lip.<sup>[[6]](#ref6)[[7]](#ref7)</sup>

| Feature | Practical point |
|---|---|
| **Presentation** | Often incidental; when symptomatic, pain, hematuria, bleeding, dysuria, or local irritation are common.<sup>[[7]](#ref7)</sup> |
| **Histology** | Hyperplastic urothelial / squamous lining over inflamed, edematous, vascular stroma; pseudoneoplastic stromal atypia can mimic lymphoma or sarcoma.<sup>[[7]](#ref7)[[8]](#ref8)</sup> |
| **Cancer mimic** | Urothelial carcinoma can clinically mimic caruncle; concurrent or subsequent urothelial carcinoma has been reported in caruncle series.<sup>[[7]](#ref7)[[9]](#ref9)</sup> |
| **Initial treatment** | Topical estrogen and observation for classic, mild lesions.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[10]](#ref10)</sup> |
| **Biopsy / excision threshold** | Thrombosis, significant or recurrent bleeding, urinary retention, persistent pain, atypical appearance, progression, recurrence, or symptoms persisting after 1-2 months of conservative therapy.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[7]](#ref7)[[10]](#ref10)[[11]](#ref11)</sup> |

### Urethral mucosal prolapse

Urethral prolapse is circumferential eversion of urethral mucosa through the meatus, classically in prepubertal girls and postmenopausal women. The exam finding is a doughnut-shaped edematous mass around the central urethral opening. Mild cases are treated with topical estrogen, sitz baths, and local care; thrombosed, necrotic, obstructing, or refractory prolapse is treated surgically.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup> See [Urethral Prolapse](/docs/clinical-conditions/03b-voiding-outlet/urethral-prolapse).

### Urethral diverticulum

Urethral diverticulum is the most common periurethral mass overall and is covered in detail in [Urethral Diverticulum](/docs/clinical-conditions/03b-voiding-outlet/urethral-diverticula). The classic "3 Ds" - dysuria, dyspareunia, and post-void dribbling - are memorable but insensitive. More useful symptom clusters include recurrent UTI, post-void dribbling, dyspareunia, discharge, anterior vaginal wall mass, and urinary incontinence.<sup>[[3]](#ref3)[[4]](#ref4)[[12]](#ref12)[[13]](#ref13)</sup>

MRI is the preferred preoperative test. In a 60-patient surgical series it had 100% sensitivity and negative predictive value, with 83% specificity and 92% positive predictive value; cystourethroscopy, by contrast, detected only 33% and expressible urethral fluid on palpation was highly predictive.<sup>[[14]](#ref14)</sup> MRI maps size, number, configuration, ostium, sphincter relationship, circumferential involvement, and suspicious wall features.<sup>[[15]](#ref15)[[12]](#ref12)</sup> CT or MR urography is also appropriate when a woman with complicated recurrent UTI is being evaluated.<sup>[[16]](#ref16)</sup> Ultrasound is a cost-effective alternative when expertise is available.<sup>[[5]](#ref5)[[17]](#ref17)</sup>

#### Classification

The Leach L/N/S/C3 system records **L**ocation (distal, mid, proximal, with or without extension beneath the bladder neck), **N**umber, **S**ize, and **C**onfiguration (single, multiloculated, saddle), **C**ommunication (site of the ostium), and **C**ontinence (presence of stress incontinence).<sup>[[41]](#ref41)</sup> Configuration matters operatively: in one MRI-classified series, initial cure after transvaginal diverticulectomy was 100% for simple, 75% for U-shaped and 64% for circumferential diverticula, and circumferential shape independently predicted failure.<sup>[[23]](#ref23)</sup>

#### Diverticulectomy outcomes

Transvaginal diverticulectomy with layered closure is the standard operation for symptomatic female diverticulum.<sup>[[19]](#ref19)</sup>

| Outcome | Typical range / signal |
|---|---|
| **Symptom-free after surgery** | About 69-77% in a systematic review and single-center series (69% of 1,044 patients with reported outcomes; 73% continent and 72% dry and cured in two tertiary series). Narrative reviews report 83-97% success; the primary literature is non-comparative and reported inconsistently.<sup>[[19]](#ref19)[[20]](#ref20)[[21]](#ref21)[[24]](#ref24)[[42]](#ref42)[[43]](#ref43)</sup> |
| **Recurrence** | About 3-10% in large series (3.4% in 89 patients; 4% in 121; 10.1% in a 485-patient multicenter cohort); wider ranges in reviews. Higher for complex, circumferential, or recurrent lesions.<sup>[[20]](#ref20)[[22]](#ref22)[[23]](#ref23)[[24]](#ref24)[[42]](#ref42)</sup> |
| **De novo SUI** | About 5.6-16%.<sup>[[19]](#ref19)[[20]](#ref20)[[22]](#ref22)</sup> |
| **Unexpected malignancy** | About 2-6% in excised specimens.<sup>[[18]](#ref18)[[24]](#ref24)</sup> |

Surgical principles include complete sac excision, watertight urethral closure, multilayer non-overlapping closure, preservation of continence mechanism, Martius flap interposition for complex or recurrent defects, and mandatory pathology for all specimens. Distal diverticula may be candidates for marsupialization in selected cases, but this is not the default for mid-urethral or complex lesions.<sup>[[18]](#ref18)[[20]](#ref20)[[22]](#ref22)[[23]](#ref23)[[24]](#ref24)[[25]](#ref25)</sup>

Concomitant pubovaginal sling can improve resolution of pre-existing SUI but increases retention and recurrent UTI risk, and it does not protect against de novo SUI or diverticulum recurrence. Many surgeons therefore favor staged continence treatment after diverticulectomy healing; in one tertiary series, 54% of women with pre-existing SUI were continent after excision alone.<sup>[[20]](#ref20)[[22]](#ref22)</sup>

### Skene gland cysts and abscesses

Skene glands flank the distal urethra and can form cysts or abscesses that mimic urethral diverticulum, Bartholin cyst, or anterior vaginal wall cyst. They usually present as distal anterolateral periurethral masses with pain, dyspareunia, discharge, obstructive voiding, urinary spraying, or recurrent infection.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[3]](#ref3)</sup>

MRI helps confirm that the lesion does not communicate with the urethral lumen. Symptomatic cysts and abscesses are generally treated with surgical excision or drainage / excision, with low recurrence in small series.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[3]](#ref3)[[15]](#ref15)[[26]](#ref26)</sup>

### Other periurethral cysts

Gartner duct cysts, Mullerian duct cysts, and dermoid or epidermal inclusion cysts can present as anterior vaginal wall or periurethral masses. The distinction from diverticulum is absence of urethral communication. MRI is the best single modality when anatomy is uncertain.<sup>[[15]](#ref15)[[26]](#ref26)</sup> See [Vaginal Cysts & Masses](/docs/clinical-conditions/03g-genital-scrotal/vaginal-cysts-masses).

### Urethral leiomyoma

Urethral and paraurethral leiomyomas are rare benign smooth-muscle tumors. Ultrasound typically shows a well-defined hypoechoic solid mass with Doppler blood flow.<sup>[[5]](#ref5)[[27]](#ref27)</sup> Asymptomatic, nonobstructive lesions can be observed with imaging and cystoscopy; symptomatic or obstructing lesions are usually excised transvaginally. In a six-patient paraurethral series there were no recurrences at 32-72 months, but two women developed stress incontinence needing a sling and one needed fascial sling repair of an intraoperative urethral injury.<sup>[[28]](#ref28)[[29]](#ref29)</sup>

### Condyloma acuminatum

HPV-related condyloma can involve the urethral meatus or distal urethra and may appear as a hypoechoic solid mass with Doppler blood flow on ultrasound.<sup>[[5]](#ref5)</sup> Management depends on extent, symptoms, and exclusion of dysplasia or carcinoma.

## Malignant Urethral Masses

### Primary female urethral carcinoma

Primary urethral carcinoma is extremely rare, and women often present with nonspecific symptoms: hematuria, urethral bleeding, obstructive or irritative LUTS, palpable mass, dysuria, pain, or vaginal discharge.<sup>[[30]](#ref30)[[31]](#ref31)[[32]](#ref32)[[33]](#ref33)</sup> Diagnostic delay is common because benign urethral masses produce the same symptoms.

Female histologic subtypes include adenocarcinoma, squamous cell carcinoma, and urothelial carcinoma. Contemporary pathology reviews emphasize that adenocarcinoma, including clear cell and Skene gland-type adenocarcinoma, is particularly important in women.<sup>[[30]](#ref30)</sup>

Poor prognostic factors include tumor size &gt;=3 cm, stage &gt;T2, proximal disease, nodal involvement, and adenocarcinoma histology.<sup>[[31]](#ref31)[[32]](#ref32)[[34]](#ref34)[[35]](#ref35)</sup> Five-year overall survival was 41% in both a 33-woman cohort and a 165-patient mixed-sex series (5-year disease-specific survival 48% in the latter), with better outcomes for early-stage or distal disease than for advanced proximal disease.<sup>[[34]](#ref34)[[36]](#ref36)[[37]](#ref37)</sup>

### Workup for suspected carcinoma

Current EAU guidance recommends tissue diagnosis and complete staging rather than treating every urethral mass as a benign lesion:<sup>[[38]](#ref38)</sup>

- Urethrocystoscopy with biopsy and urinary cytology.
- Pelvic examination, including assessment of inguinal nodes; examination under anesthesia when needed.
- CT chest, abdomen and pelvis for distant staging.
- Pelvic MRI for local extent and regional nodes.

Treatment depends on stage, location and histology. Organ preservation is possible in selected distal tumors with clear margins; advanced disease requires specialist multidisciplinary planning and may need combined systemic, radiation and surgical treatment.<sup>[[38]](#ref38)</sup>

### Carcinoma arising in urethral diverticulum

Carcinoma arising within a urethral diverticulum is rare but high consequence. Adenocarcinoma, including clear cell adenocarcinoma, is the dominant histology. In a 90-patient diverticulectomy series, 5 patients (6%) had invasive adenocarcinoma, and intestinal metaplasia and high-grade dysplasia were identified as precursor lesions.<sup>[[18]](#ref18)</sup> Invasive diverticular carcinoma is treated with radical excision rather than simple diverticulectomy; all five invasive cases in that series underwent anterior pelvic exenteration with urinary diversion, and a recent case report describes urethrectomy.<sup>[[18]](#ref18)[[39]](#ref39)</sup>

## Red Flags

Biopsy or urgent specialist evaluation is warranted for:

- Solid, fixed, ulcerated, friable, or rapidly enlarging lesion.
- Hematuria, urethral bleeding, or recurrent bleeding from a presumed caruncle.
- Persistent pain or symptoms after 1-2 months of conservative caruncle treatment.
- Suspicious MRI findings: enhancing solid component, irregular wall thickening, restricted diffusion, invasion, or lymphadenopathy.
- Recurrent mass after excision.
- Urethral diverticulum with stones, firm wall, hematuria, or atypical symptoms.
- Any excised diverticulum or periurethral mass without pathology review.

## Clinical Pearls

- Urethral diverticulum and Skene gland cyst account for most periurethral / anterior vaginal wall masses in women.<sup>[[3]](#ref3)</sup>
- A meatal lesion that looks like a caruncle can still be cancer; progression, bleeding, recurrence, atypical appearance, or failure to improve should trigger biopsy.<sup>[[7]](#ref7)[[9]](#ref9)[[11]](#ref11)</sup>
- MRI is the best preoperative test when a lesion may communicate with the urethra or when surgery is planned.<sup>[[15]](#ref15)[[26]](#ref26)[[40]](#ref40)</sup>
- Asymptomatic, nonobstructive benign masses such as small leiomyomas or cysts can be observed if malignancy has been reasonably excluded.<sup>[[28]](#ref28)[[2]](#ref2)</sup>
- Diverticulectomy pathology is not optional: unexpected malignancy is rare but real.<sup>[[18]](#ref18)[[24]](#ref24)</sup>
