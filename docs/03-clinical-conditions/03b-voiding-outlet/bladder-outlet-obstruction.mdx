# Benign Prostatic Hyperplasia

**Benign prostatic hyperplasia (BPH)** is histologic proliferation of epithelial and stromal cells in the prostate transition zone. It becomes clinically relevant when benign prostatic enlargement produces **lower urinary tract symptoms (LUTS)**, **benign prostatic obstruction (BPO)**, urinary retention, infection, bladder stones, hematuria, renal deterioration, or quality-of-life impairment.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[2]](#ref2)[[3]](#ref3)</sup>

For the reconstructive urologist, BPH is the most common reversible cause of male outlet obstruction, the background condition that complicates neurogenic and OAB phenotypes, and the upstream driver of many iatrogenic problems: bladder neck contracture, urethral stricture after instrumentation, post-TURP incontinence, recurrent retention, and difficult catheterization.

For procedural selection across endoscopic, minimally invasive, and surgical options, see the [BPH &amp; Male LUTS treatment atlas](/docs/surgical-techniques/bph-male-luts).

---

## Terminology

| Term | Meaning | Practical use |
|---|---|---|
| **BPH** | Histologic hyperplasia of prostatic epithelial and stromal cells | Tissue diagnosis; often used clinically as shorthand |
| **BPE** | Benign prostatic enlargement | Size / volume descriptor |
| **BPO** | Benign prostatic obstruction | Outlet obstruction caused by BPE |
| **BOO** | Bladder outlet obstruction | Obstruction at the bladder outlet from any cause: BPH, urethral stricture, bladder neck contracture, dysfunctional voiding |
| **LUTS** | Lower urinary tract symptoms | Symptom syndrome; may be storage, voiding, or post-micturition |

The distinction matters because not all male LUTS are BPH, and not all enlarged prostates are obstructive.

---

## Epidemiology

BPH increases with age and is nearly universal histologically in older men, but symptomatic disease is the clinically important phenotype.<sup>[[2]](#ref2)[[3]](#ref3)</sup>

- Histologic BPH begins after age 40 and rises steadily with age.
- Moderate to severe LUTS affect roughly one-third of men aged 50-80 in population surveys.
- More than half of men in their 60s and most octogenarians report some LUTS.
- BPH/LUTS produces a large outpatient, emergency, and procedural burden, including acute urinary retention, medication use, and outlet surgery.

Clinical heterogeneity is the main point: two men with the same prostate volume can have very different symptoms, flow rates, PVRs, bladder compliance, and treatment response.

---

## Pathophysiology

BPH has **static**, **dynamic**, and **bladder-response** components.

### Static obstruction

Transition-zone enlargement compresses or distorts the prostatic urethra. Median lobe / intravesical protrusion can create a ball-valve outlet obstruction even when total prostate volume is not enormous.

### Dynamic obstruction

Alpha-1 adrenergic tone in prostatic stroma and bladder neck smooth muscle increases outlet resistance. This is why alpha blockers can improve symptoms within days without shrinking the prostate.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[4]](#ref4)</sup>

### Androgen dependence

Dihydrotestosterone (DHT), produced from testosterone by 5-alpha reductase, is central to prostate growth. 5-alpha reductase inhibitors reduce DHT, shrink the gland over months, lower PSA by roughly half, and reduce progression risk in men with enlarged prostates.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[5]](#ref5)</sup>

### Bladder remodeling

Chronic obstruction causes detrusor hypertrophy, trabeculation, diverticula, impaired compliance, detrusor overactivity, and eventually detrusor underactivity. This is the reason outlet surgery can fix the obstruction but not always the storage symptoms or weak bladder.

## Risk Factors

| Category | Risk factors |
|---|---|
| **Non-modifiable** | Age, family history, androgen exposure |
| **Metabolic** | Obesity, metabolic syndrome, diabetes, hypertension |
| **Inflammatory** | Prostatic inflammation, recurrent infection, elevated inflammatory markers |
| **Lifestyle** | Sedentary behavior, smoking, diet-associated cardiometabolic disease |
| **Anatomic / disease** | Large prostate volume, intravesical median lobe, high PSA as a surrogate for epithelial volume |

## Clinical Presentation

### Voiding symptoms

- Hesitancy
- Weak stream
- Intermittency
- Straining
- Prolonged voiding
- Terminal dribbling
- Incomplete emptying
- Acute or chronic retention

### Storage symptoms

- Frequency
- Urgency
- Nocturia
- Urgency urinary incontinence
- Dysuria or bladder discomfort

Storage symptoms may be caused by obstruction-driven detrusor overactivity, coexisting OAB, nocturnal polyuria, sleep disease, diabetes, diuretics, or neurogenic lower urinary tract dysfunction.

### Post-micturition symptoms

- Post-void dribbling
- Sensation of incomplete emptying

## Evaluation

Evaluation should answer four questions:

1. Are symptoms bothersome enough to treat?
2. Is BPH likely to be the driver?
3. Is there a complication that requires active intervention?
4. Is the prostate anatomy appropriate for medication, MIST, endoscopic surgery, or simple prostatectomy?

### Core evaluation

| Step | Why it matters |
|---|---|
| **History** | LUTS subtype, bother, retention episodes, infections, hematuria, stones, neurologic disease, constipation, sleep symptoms, fluid/caffeine/alcohol pattern |
| **Medication review** | Diuretics, antihistamines, anticholinergics, sympathomimetics, opioids, antidepressants, decongestants |
| **IPSS / QoL score** | Best routine tool to quantify severity and follow response<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup> |
| **Focused exam + DRE** | Nodules, tenderness, anal tone, neurologic clues; DRE estimates size poorly |
| **Urinalysis** | Screens for infection, hematuria, glycosuria, proteinuria |
| **PVR** | Detects incomplete emptying and retention risk |
| **Uroflowmetry** | Low Qmax supports obstruction or detrusor weakness; shape of curve matters |

### IPSS interpretation

| IPSS | Severity | Usual implication |
|---|---|---|
| **0-7** | Mild | Watchful waiting if not bothered |
| **8-19** | Moderate | Behavioral therapy, medication, or procedure depending on bother |
| **20-35** | Severe | Active therapy; assess for complications and anatomy |

A decrease of 3 points or more is usually considered clinically meaningful.

EAU 2026 makes these evaluation recommendations for men with LUTS:<sup>[[12]](#ref12)</sup>

| Test | EAU 2026 statement (strength) |
|---|---|
| Bladder diary | Use for male LUTS, particularly nocturia, for at least 3 days (strong) |
| Uroflowmetry | Include in the initial assessment (weak) and perform before medical or invasive treatment (strong) |
| Prostate imaging | Before surgery (strong); before drug treatment only if it helps choose the drug (weak) |
| Urodynamics | Only for specific indications before invasive treatment (weak). These include prior unsuccessful invasive treatment, inability to void more than 150 mL, Qmax above 10 mL/s with predominantly voiding LUTS, and predominantly voiding LUTS at age over 80 or under 50 (all weak) |
| PSA | When cancer detection would change management or it helps treatment decisions, with counseling about the implications of a raised value (strong) |

### PSA and prostate size

PSA is not required for every LUTS evaluation. Use shared decision-making for prostate cancer screening, and obtain PSA when the result would change management, life expectancy makes cancer detection relevant, or PSA will help estimate prostate volume and progression risk.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[4]](#ref4)</sup>

Before procedural treatment, image the prostate for volume and shape, usually with transabdominal or transrectal ultrasound; available CT or MRI can also inform sizing. Use cystoscopy to assess outlet or urethral anatomy, not to measure volume. Before a 5-alpha reductase inhibitor, image only if sizing changes drug selection; AUA 2026 accepts volume &gt;30 cc and/or PSA &gt;1.5 ng/mL as enlargement criteria.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[11]](#ref11)[[12]](#ref12)</sup> At TRUS, volume is estimated with the prolate-ellipsoid formula (height x width x length x 0.52) from three orthogonal measurements. Lower-frequency transducers (6-8 MHz) penetrate 2-8 cm and are preferred for transition-zone visualization and volume measurement; procedures with a volume gate (for example UroLift or Rezum) depend on this measurement being accurate.<sup>[[16]](#ref16)</sup>

PSA as a volume surrogate is age-dependent: in a multicenter BPH-trial cohort, PSA above 1.6 ng/mL, 2.0 ng/mL, and 2.3 ng/mL identified a gland exceeding 40 mL in men in their 50s, 60s, and 70s, respectively, at roughly 70% specificity and 65% to 70% sensitivity. A single fixed PSA cutoff, unadjusted for age, is therefore an approximation.<sup>[[15]](#ref15)</sup>

### Additional testing before intervention

| Test | Use |
|---|---|
| **Cystoscopy** | Median lobe, bladder neck, urethral stricture, bladder stones, tumors, prior surgery anatomy |
| **Prostate imaging** | Volume, shape, median lobe, surgical planning |
| **Pressure-flow urodynamics** | Distinguishes obstruction from detrusor underactivity when the diagnosis is uncertain |
| **Renal ultrasound / creatinine** | High PVR, retention, hydronephrosis concern, recurrent infection, renal insufficiency |
| **Voiding diary** | Nocturia, polyuria, frequency-volume mismatch |

## Red Flags and Alternative Diagnoses

Do not anchor on BPH when the pattern suggests another outlet or bladder disorder.

| Finding | Consider |
|---|---|
| Hematuria | Malignancy, stone, infection, BPH bleeding |
| Recurrent UTI | Retention, stone, chronic prostatitis, stricture |
| Very low flow with small prostate | Urethral stricture, bladder neck contracture, detrusor underactivity |
| Severe urgency with normal flow/PVR | OAB, nocturnal polyuria, neurologic disease |
| New neurologic symptoms | Spinal disease, Parkinson disease, stroke, diabetic neuropathy |
| Prior TURP / HoLEP / prostatectomy | Bladder neck contracture, VUAS, urethral stricture |

## Natural History

BPH/LUTS may remain stable, improve with self-management, or progress.<sup>[[4]](#ref4)[[5]](#ref5)</sup>

Clinical progression includes:

- IPSS increase of 4 points or more
- Acute urinary retention
- Recurrent UTI from incomplete emptying
- Bladder stones
- New overflow or urgency incontinence
- Renal insufficiency or hydronephrosis
- Need for invasive BPH therapy

Risk factors for progression include older age, higher baseline IPSS, low Qmax, high PVR, large prostate volume, and higher PSA.

---

## Treatment Strategy

Treatment is symptom- and bother-driven unless complications force intervention. The practical sequence is:

1. **Mild or low-bother symptoms.** Watchful waiting and behavioral therapy.
2. **Bothersome LUTS without complications.** Medication selected by phenotype and prostate size.
3. **Medication failure, retention, complications, or patient preference.** Procedural therapy selected by prostate anatomy, durability goals, sexual-function priorities, anticoagulation, and anesthetic risk.

---

## Conservative Management

Self-management can meaningfully improve LUTS and should be offered before or alongside medication.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup>

- Limit evening fluids.
- Reduce caffeine and alcohol.
- Avoid large fluid boluses.
- Time diuretics earlier in the day when medically safe.
- Treat constipation.
- Use timed voiding or double voiding.
- Use bladder training for urgency/frequency.
- Consider pelvic floor therapy for urgency suppression and post-void dribbling.
- Address sleep apnea and nocturnal polyuria when nocturia is the dominant complaint.

## Pharmacologic Therapy

### Alpha blockers

Alpha blockers are first-line medication for bothersome moderate to severe LUTS when rapid symptom relief is desired.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup> AUA 2026 recommends offering a uroselective agent (alfuzosin, silodosin or tamsulosin; Strong Recommendation, Grade A), notes that symptom improvement is similar across agents, and advises selecting by side-effect profile and comorbidity.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[4]](#ref4)[[11]](#ref11)</sup>

| Agent group | Examples | Best use | Main trade-offs |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Uroselective** | Tamsulosin, silodosin, alfuzosin | Rapid LUTS relief; alfuzosin is clinically uroselective without alpha-1A subtype selectivity | Ejaculatory effects vary; orthostasis and intraoperative floppy iris syndrome remain relevant |
| **Non-uroselective** | Doxazosin, terazosin | Men already doing well on them, or selected patients after assessing blood pressure and medication interactions | Higher rates of orthostatic hypotension and dizziness, so not ideal when uroselective agents are available; titration required |

Expected onset is days to weeks. Alpha blockers do not shrink the prostate and do not reliably prevent long-term progression by themselves. Counsel men planning cataract surgery about intraoperative floppy iris syndrome and ask them to tell their ophthalmologist. The risk persists for up to a year after stopping, tamsulosin carries the highest risk, and AUA 2026 suggests cataract surgery before starting an alpha blocker when both need treatment.<sup>[[11]](#ref11)</sup>

### 5-alpha reductase inhibitors

For symptom improvement, AUA 2026 recommends offering a 5-ARI for symptom relief in men with demonstrable enlargement (volume >30 cc and/or PSA >1.5 ng/mL; Moderate Recommendation, Grade A). In the same men, a 5-ARI may be offered to lower the risk of progression (worsening symptoms, retention, surgery); this is a Clinical Principle, and these medicines do not offer rapid symptom relief. A PSA is not needed solely to establish 5-ARI eligibility.<sup>[[11]](#ref11)</sup>

Clinical points:

- Onset is slow: 3-6 months for noticeable benefit, up to 12 months for full effect.
- With 5-alpha-reductase inhibitors, PSA falls by up to 50%; adjust interpretation for cancer screening.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup>
- Sexual adverse effects include lower libido, erectile dysfunction, ejaculatory change, and gynecomastia.
- Best evidence is in men with larger glands and progression risk.
- Men on active surveillance for prostate cancer are treated for LUTS as usual; 5-ARIs are unlikely to raise the rate of high-risk cancer, but PSA kinetics change and must be considered in monitoring (AUA 2026 Expert Opinion).<sup>[[11]](#ref11)</sup>

### Daily tadalafil

AUA 2026 recommends offering daily 5 mg tadalafil for LUTS/BPH with or without erectile dysfunction (Moderate Recommendation, Grade B). It can improve LUTS and erectile function; use the [PDE5 inhibitor prescribing hub](/docs/foundations/pharmacology/sexual-medicine-andrology/pde5-inhibitors) for regimen and adjustment. It is useful when ED and LUTS coexist, but it is contraindicated with nitrates and must be used carefully with hypotension risk.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[4]](#ref4)</sup>

### Combination therapy

| Combination | Use |
|---|---|
| **Alpha blocker + 5-ARI** | Enlarged prostate (&gt;30 cc and/or PSA &gt;1.5 ng/mL) plus bothersome symptoms; AUA 2026 Moderate Recommendation, Grade B; reduced overall clinical progression more than either drug alone in MTOPS; combination therapy and finasteride alone both reduced retention and invasive-therapy risk versus placebo<sup>[[5]](#ref5)[[11]](#ref11)</sup> |
| **Alpha blocker + antimuscarinic** | Moderate-to-severe predominant storage symptoms; an antimuscarinic may be offered alone or with the alpha blocker (AUA 2026 Conditional Recommendation, Grade C). Check PVR first and at follow-up. EAU 2026 weakly recommends against an antimuscarinic, alone or with an alpha blocker, when PVR exceeds 150 mL, and offers the combination weakly when storage symptoms persist on either drug alone<sup>[[11]](#ref11)[[12]](#ref12)</sup> |
| **Alpha blocker + β3 agonist** | Moderate-to-severe predominant storage symptoms; a β3 agonist may be offered alone or with the alpha blocker (AUA 2026 Conditional Recommendation, Grade C), and avoids the cognitive burden of antimuscarinics. EAU 2026 strongly recommends a β3 agonist for men with moderate-to-severe storage LUTS, and weakly recommends adding one for persistent storage symptoms on an alpha blocker<sup>[[11]](#ref11)[[12]](#ref12)</sup> |

In a double-blind, placebo-controlled trial at 95 US urology clinics, 879 men aged 40 or older with IPSS of 12 or more and diary-documented frequency and urgency were randomized to placebo (n = 222), tolterodine ER 4 mg (n = 217), tamsulosin 0.4 mg (n = 215) or both (n = 225) for 12 weeks. In the protocol-specified week-12 analysis, patient-perceived treatment benefit was reported by 172/215 (80%) men with an available response on combination therapy versus 132/214 (62%) with placebo (P &lt; .001); neither tamsulosin alone (146/207 [71%], P = .06) nor tolterodine alone (136/209 [65%], P = .48) differed significantly from placebo. Acute urinary retention requiring catheterization occurred in 0.4% of the combination group. The trial reports 12-week outcomes only.<sup>[[17]](#ref17)</sup>

The AUA 2026 guideline permits daily tadalafil with a uroselective alpha blocker (conditional recommendation, Grade C). Average additional LUTS improvement is small; monitor for additive hypotension. Tadalafil plus a 5-ARI is another option in men with demonstrable enlargement (Conditional Recommendation, Grade C). Select combinations through shared decision-making rather than treating every combination as necessary or ineffective. When storage symptoms persist despite medication, add behavioral and lifestyle measures (AUA 2026 Expert Opinion); in the MOTIVE and COBALT trials, behavioral therapy matched or outperformed oxybutynin, with or without tamsulosin, for storage LUTS in men.<sup>[[11]](#ref11)</sup>

### Phytotherapy

Pooled randomized trials found no clinically important symptom improvement with *Serenoa repens* versus placebo, but extracts are not interchangeable.<sup>[[12]](#ref12)</sup> Some data suggest possible benefit from *Pygeum africanum* or beta-sitosterol, but product variability and weaker evidence make them adjuncts rather than core therapy.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup> EAU 2026 makes a weak recommendation to offer hexane-extracted *Serenoa repens* to men who want to avoid adverse events, particularly sexual ones, and strongly advises telling them that the benefit may be modest.<sup>[[12]](#ref12)</sup>

## Procedural Therapy

### Indications

Procedural treatment is appropriate for:

- Bothersome LUTS despite medication
- Medication intolerance or preference to avoid chronic medication
- Refractory or recurrent urinary retention attributable to BPO
- Recurrent UTI from incomplete emptying
- Bladder stones
- BPH-related gross hematuria refractory to medical therapy
- Renal deterioration or hydronephrosis from obstruction
- Persistently high PVR with complications or bothersome symptoms after assessment of obstruction and detrusor function; PVR alone does not establish a surgical indication

### Procedure selection

Procedure choice depends on prostate volume, median lobe, anticoagulation, anesthesia risk, sexual-function priorities, retention status, and need for durability.

| Procedure | Best fit | Strengths | Trade-offs |
|---|---|---|---|
| **TURP** | Moderate-size glands without need for enucleation (bipolar or monopolar; Strong Recommendation, Grade A) | Durable benchmark, widely available | Retrograde ejaculation common; bleeding, TUR syndrome risk lower with bipolar |
| **HoLEP / laser enucleation** | Any size, especially large glands or retention (HoLEP or ThuLEP; Strong Recommendation, Grade A) | Size-independent, durable, excellent debulking | Learning curve, transient incontinence, retrograde ejaculation common |
| **Simple prostatectomy** | Very large glands (>80 cc), large median lobe, stones/diverticula needing surgery | Maximal tissue removal | More invasive; bleeding/anesthesia burden |
| **GreenLight / laser vaporization** | Bleeding-risk patients, moderate glands | Hemostatic outpatient option | Less tissue for pathology; retreatment risk in large glands |
| **Aquablation** | 30-80 mL glands (AUA 2026 Moderate Recommendation, Grade B; 80-150 mL Expert Opinion), ejaculation-preservation priority, selected median lobes | Strong symptom improvement with lower ejaculatory dysfunction than TURP | Bleeding management, availability, retreatment data still maturing<sup>[[6]](#ref6)[[11]](#ref11)</sup> |
| **Prostatic urethral lift** | 30-80 mL glands without an obstructing median lobe (AUA 2026 Moderate Recommendation, Grade B), ejaculation preservation | Fast recovery, preserves ejaculation | Less symptom and flow improvement than TURP at 24 months in the BPH6 randomized trial; EAU 2026 advises counseling about higher retreatment than TURP (weak recommendation)<sup>[[7]](#ref7)[[11]](#ref11)[[12]](#ref12)[[18]](#ref18)</sup> |
| **Water vapor therapy** | 30-80 mL glands (AUA 2026 Moderate Recommendation, Grade B)<sup>[[11]](#ref11)</sup>, office-based treatment, sexual preservation | FDA indication covers central-zone and/or median-lobe hyperplasia<sup>[[14]](#ref14)</sup>; low sexual adverse events | Delayed symptom improvement, catheter period, retreatment risk |
| **iTIND** | 25-75 mL glands without an obstructing median lobe (AUA 2026 Expert Opinion) | Temporary implant, ejaculation-preserving intent | Shorter evidence horizon |
| **Optilume BPH (drug-coated balloon)** | 20-80 cc, prostatic urethral length 32-55 mm, no obstructing median lobe (AUA 2026 Conditional Recommendation, Grade C) | Outpatient balloon dilation without resection or implant | PINNACLE compared 100 active-treated men with 48 sham-treated men using 12-month active versus 3-month sham symptom change; two-year data lack a sham comparator, and no trial compares it with resection. See [Optilume BPH](/docs/surgical-techniques/bph-male-luts/optilume-bph).<sup>[[11]](#ref11)[[12]](#ref12)</sup> |
| **Prostatic artery embolization** | Prostates of 50 cc or more treated with particle embolics (AUA 2026 Conditional Recommendation, Grade B); after urologic assessment, especially when surgical risk is high, the gland is very large, or transurethral surgery is declined | Low sexual adverse-event signal, outpatient IR approach | Less predictable deobstruction and higher retreatment than TURP; anatomy-dependent technical success; counsel about radiation and non-target embolization. Treatment requires an IR operator trained in PAE.<sup>[[8]](#ref8)[[11]](#ref11)[[12]](#ref12)</sup> |

AUA 2026 asks clinicians to discuss BPH-specific retreatment rates (Moderate Recommendation, Grade B) and notes that tissue-preserving, ejaculation-sparing procedures carry a higher retreatment risk. Network meta-analyses consistently show the trade-off: TURP/HoLEP/simple prostatectomy provide stronger objective deobstruction and durability, while MISTs generally preserve sexual function better and carry lower perioperative morbidity at the cost of more retreatment.<sup>[[9]](#ref9)[[10]](#ref10)</sup>

## Retention Pathway

Acute urinary retention from presumed BPH:

1. Place urethral catheter gently; use coude catheter early if resistance is expected.
2. If urethral catheterization fails, avoid repeated traumatic attempts; suprapubic drainage is an option after further assessment.
3. Start an alpha blocker unless contraindicated.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup>
4. Trial without catheter after alpha-blocker exposure of at least three days (AUA 2026 Clinical Principle). Tell men who pass that they remain at increased risk of recurrent retention.
5. If trial fails or retention recurs, maintain safe drainage and evaluate anatomy, reversible factors and bladder function. For catheter-dependent retention, AUA 2026 recommends a resective technology (TURP, enucleation or simple prostatectomy), weighing comorbidity (Expert Opinion); do not assume every failed trial requires immediate surgery.<sup>[[11]](#ref11)[[12]](#ref12)</sup>

Chronic retention is different. Check renal function, hydronephrosis risk, PVR trend, bladder compliance when relevant, and detrusor contractility before promising that outlet surgery will restore spontaneous voiding.

## Complications of BPH

- Acute urinary retention
- Chronic urinary retention
- Recurrent UTI
- Bladder stones
- Gross hematuria
- Hydronephrosis or renal insufficiency
- [Bladder diverticula](/docs/clinical-conditions/03b-voiding-outlet/bladder-diverticula) and trabeculation
- Overflow incontinence
- Detrusor overactivity or impaired compliance
- Detrusor underactivity after long-standing obstruction

## Reconstructive Pearls

1. **BPH is a diagnosis of probability, not a reflex label.** In men with prior instrumentation, pelvic radiation, urethroplasty, TURP, HoLEP, prostatectomy, or traumatic catheterization, rule out stricture or bladder neck stenosis.
2. **Low flow does not prove obstruction.** A weak detrusor and an obstructed prostate can look identical on uroflow; pressure-flow studies separate them when the answer will change management (routine urodynamics in uncomplicated men did not reduce surgery or improve symptoms in UPSTREAM).<sup>[[13]](#ref13)</sup>
3. **Storage symptoms may persist after perfect outlet surgery.** Counsel men with severe urgency, diabetes, neurologic disease, or long-standing obstruction.
4. **Median lobe changes the menu.** PUL is less suitable in many obstructive median-lobe anatomies; water vapor therapy, TURP, HoLEP, aquablation, or simple prostatectomy may be better.
5. **Compare ejaculation, symptom relief, and retreatment by procedure.** In randomized evidence, PUL improved symptoms less than TURP through 24 months but may better preserve ejaculation; aquablation had similar symptom relief to TURP through 12 months and may better preserve ejaculation. Both comparisons have uncertain retreatment estimates. Match the choice to the patient's priorities.<sup>[[6]](#ref6)[[7]](#ref7)</sup>
6. **Treat the outlet before continence surgery.** In men with SUI plus BPH/BOO, stabilize emptying before sling or AUS planning.
