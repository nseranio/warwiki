# Ureteropelvic Junction Obstruction

**Ureteropelvic junction obstruction (UPJO)** is a functionally significant impairment of urine flow from the renal pelvis into the proximal ureter that can cause progressive hydronephrosis, parenchymal loss, recurrent pyelonephritis, and stone formation; progression is not inevitable in every patient.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[2]](#ref2)</sup> It is the most common cause of congenital obstructive uropathy (incidence about 1 in 1,500 births, with a 2:1 male-to-female ratio in newborns; usually unilateral, more often left-sided) and the prototype upper-tract reconstruction problem for the urologic surgeon; pyeloplasty success of about 95% is the benchmark against which other ureteral reconstructions are measured.<sup>[[21]](#ref21)[[7]](#ref7)</sup>

For operative selection and comparative repair options, see the [Upper Tract Reconstruction database](/docs/surgical-techniques/04d-upper-tract-reconstruction) and the [Pyeloplasty](/docs/surgical-techniques/04d-upper-tract-reconstruction/pyeloplasty) technique page.

---

## Classification

UPJO is split first by timing, then by mechanism:<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[3]](#ref3)</sup>

- **Primary (congenital).** Intrinsic developmental abnormality, typically diagnosed antenatally or in childhood
- **Secondary (acquired).** Develops later from stones, prior endoscopic or open surgery, ischemic or radiation stricture, malignancy, retroperitoneal fibrosis, or endometriosis

| Mechanism | Pathology | Examples |
|---|---|---|
| **Mural (intrinsic)** | Aperistaltic UPJ segment from abnormal smooth muscle, increased collagen, and disordered innervation; failure of complete recanalization in development | Adynamic segment — most common cause |
| **Intramural (intraluminal)** | Obstruction within the lumen | Fibroepithelial polyp, impacted stone |
| **Extramural (extrinsic)** | External compression, often intermittent | Lower-pole crossing vessel, kinks, fibrous bands, high ureteral insertion, malrotated or fused kidney |

A lower-pole crossing vessel is identified in roughly 30–60% of adults with UPJO and affects operative planning. A causative crossing vessel favors pyeloplasty over endopyelotomy; preserve it and select the final vessel–UPJ relationship according to intraoperative anatomy, rather than requiring transposition in every case.<sup>[[22]](#ref22)</sup><sup>[[14]](#ref14)[[17]](#ref17)</sup>

## Pathophysiology

The dominant lesion in primary UPJO is functional, not anatomic: an aperistaltic segment fails to propagate the peristaltic wave, so the renal pelvis cannot eject its bolus despite a grossly patent lumen.<sup>[[5]](#ref5)</sup> Histology shows smooth-muscle disarray, increased collagen deposition, and stroma-like cells at the obstructing segment; activated Hedgehog–Gli signaling has been implicated in the developmental defect.<sup>[[4]](#ref4)</sup>

The clinical course reflects the renal pelvis acting first as a hydraulic buffer and then as a failed one:<sup>[[5]](#ref5)</sup>

1. **Compensated phase.** The compliant pelvis dilates and damps intrapelvic pressure, sparing parenchyma
2. **Decompensated phase.** Sustained tubular stretch drives interstitial inflammation, myofibroblast activation, tubulointerstitial fibrosis, and tubular apoptosis, with progressive loss of differential function

For the reconstructive surgeon, the operative implication is simple: timing matters. Pyeloplasty aims to improve drainage, relieve symptoms and preserve remaining function. Recovery of split function is variable; poor baseline function does not reliably predict a larger gain, and failure remains possible.<sup>[[23]](#ref23)</sup><sup>[[19]](#ref19)</sup>

## Clinical Presentation

### Pediatric

Most cases are now identified on prenatal or neonatal ultrasound for antenatal hydronephrosis, which is detected in up to 4.5% of pregnancies.<sup>[[3]](#ref3)[[7]](#ref7)</sup> Postnatal symptoms when present include flank or abdominal pain, palpable mass in the neonate, febrile UTI, hematuria, and failure to thrive.

### Adult

In adults UPJO is typically symptomatic or detected incidentally on imaging for unrelated complaints:<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[2]](#ref2)</sup>

- **Flank pain.** The classic presentation; often intermittent, exacerbated by diuresis (high fluid intake, alcohol, caffeine; "Dietl's crisis")
- **Recurrent pyelonephritis** from urinary stasis
- **Stone formation.** Both a presenting sign and a downstream complication
- **Hematuria** (gross or microscopic)
- **Renal insufficiency** when bilateral or in a solitary kidney
- **Asymptomatic hydronephrosis** found on cross-sectional imaging for an unrelated indication

A non-trivial fraction of adult UPJO is silent until decompensation; preserve a low threshold for functional imaging when hydronephrosis is found incidentally.<sup>[[6]](#ref6)</sup>

## Diagnostic Evaluation

The reconstructive workup pairs anatomic imaging (to identify crossing vessels, stones, malrotation, and the level of obstruction) with functional imaging (to confirm true obstruction and quantify split function).<sup>[[2]](#ref2)[[6]](#ref6)[[9]](#ref9)</sup>

| Modality | Role | Strengths | Limitations |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Renal ultrasound** | First-line screen; quantify hydronephrosis (SFU grade or UTD score) | Non-invasive, no radiation | No functional or anatomic detail beyond dilation |
| **CT urography** | Anatomic gold standard in adults | Crossing vessels, stones, malrotation, parenchymal thickness | Radiation; no function |
| **MR urography** | Anatomic + some functional data; preferred in children and pregnancy | No radiation; soft-tissue detail | Availability, cost, scan time |
| **Diuretic renography (Tc-99m MAG3)** | Functional gold standard | Quantifies drainage and split function | Radiation; needs adequate hydration and bladder drainage |
| **Retrograde pyelogram** | Maps stricture length and confirms anatomy at the time of intervention | Direct anatomic detail | Invasive; usually combined with the operation itself |
| **Whitaker test** | Direct pressure–flow measurement when renography is equivocal | Resolves the equivocal case | Invasive; rarely needed in modern practice |

### Diuretic renography

MAG3 (preferred) or DTPA is administered intravenously; furosemide is given to provoke diuresis once the collecting system is filled. Time–activity curves yield two outputs that drive operative decision-making:<sup>[[2]](#ref2)</sup>

- **T½ of drainage.** Prompt clearance (&lt;10 minutes) is reassuring, but a prolonged half-time, including &gt;20 minutes, is not diagnostic by itself. Review the images, curve, renal function, hydration, bladder emptying and postvoid or gravity-assisted drainage.<sup>[[20]](#ref20)</sup>
- **Differential renal function.** Compare standardized serial studies. In children, EAU criteria include obstruction with split function &lt;40% or a fall of &gt;10 percentage points, alongside symptoms and imaging progression; this is not a universal adult numerical rule.<sup>[[21]](#ref21)</sup>

### Pediatric grading — UTD classification

The 2014 multidisciplinary Urinary Tract Dilation (UTD) consensus proposed a unified pre- and postnatal score, used alongside the older SFU grading (grades 0–4), based on anteroposterior renal pelvis diameter, calyceal dilation, parenchymal thickness, parenchymal appearance, ureter, and bladder findings.<sup>[[10]](#ref10)[[11]](#ref11)</sup> Anteroposterior renal pelvic diameter (APRPD) is the most useful single ultrasound predictor of needing intervention; cutoffs of roughly 16–24 mm have been reported, and a sizeable minority of high-grade hydronephrosis (SFU 3–4 or UTD P3) progresses to surgery on conservative management.<sup>[[7]](#ref7)[[11]](#ref11)</sup> EAU lists SFU grade III–IV dilatation among the features that inform the decision to operate.<sup>[[21]](#ref21)</sup>

## Management

### Indications for intervention

Surgery is indicated for:<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[2]](#ref2)[[10]](#ref10)</sup>

- Symptomatic obstruction (pain, recurrent infection)
- Substantially impaired or declining differential renal function attributable to obstruction, interpreted with serial imaging and symptoms; see the pediatric thresholds above
- Progressive hydronephrosis
- Stone formation in the obstructed system
- Pyelonephritis attributable to obstruction

### Active surveillance

Asymptomatic adults with stable function can be safely surveilled. In a series of 27 minimally symptomatic adults followed with renography every 6–12 months (21 with adequate follow-up), 71% (15/21) remained on surveillance at a mean of 48 months, and 29% (6/21) crossed over to surgery at an average of 34 months. Progression was mostly pain; 2 of 21 (9.5%) lost function. Failure criteria were &gt;10% loss of relative function, pyelonephritis, or repeated pain.<sup>[[12]](#ref12)</sup>

### Surgical options

| Procedure | Success rate | Best fit | Caveats |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Open dismembered (Anderson–Hynes) pyeloplasty** | &gt;95% | Historic gold standard; redo pyeloplasty, complex anatomy | Larger incision; longer recovery |
| **Laparoscopic pyeloplasty** | &gt;90% | Centers without robotic access | Steep learning curve; longer operative time |
| **Robot-assisted laparoscopic pyeloplasty (RALP)** | 90–97% | Established minimally invasive option in appropriately selected adults and children, including infants at experienced centers | Cost; equipment availability |
| **Endopyelotomy (antegrade or retrograde)** | 42–90% by approach; about 60–73% in the cited institutional series | Short intrinsic stenosis without crossing vessel; salvage after failed pyeloplasty | Inferior to pyeloplasty; generally unfavorable with a causative crossing vessel, long stricture, severe hydronephrosis, or poor function |

### Pyeloplasty technique

The Anderson–Hynes dismembered pyeloplasty is the reference operation and works for essentially every UPJO anatomy:<sup>[[13]](#ref13)[[14]](#ref14)</sup>

1. Expose the UPJ (transperitoneal or retroperitoneal; open, laparoscopic, or robotic)
2. Identify and preserve any crossing vessel
3. Excise the diseased aperistaltic segment
4. Spatulate the proximal ureter laterally for about 1.5–2 cm
5. Reduce the redundant pelvis if needed
6. Construct a tension-free, watertight, dependent anastomosis over a JJ stent (typically 4–6 weeks)
7. Preserve crossing vessels and position the anastomosis to avoid compression or kinking; anterior transposition is selected when the anatomy requires it.<sup>[[22]](#ref22)</sup>

**Non-dismembered alternatives** are reserved for specific anatomy and rarely used today:<sup>[[14]](#ref14)</sup>

- **Foley Y-V plasty.** High ureteral insertion with a dependent pelvis
- **Fenger plasty.** Short stricture, Heineke–Mikulicz principle
- **Culp–DeWeerd or Scardino flap.** Long strictures with a generous pelvis

### Endopyelotomy

Antegrade or retrograde incision of the stenotic segment through a single full-thickness cut, stented for 4–6 weeks. Long-term failure rates substantially exceed pyeloplasty (adjusted hazard ratio for treatment failure 1.78 versus open pyeloplasty, 1.9 versus minimally invasive pyeloplasty, from claims data in adults aged 18–64), and a causative crossing vessel, stricture &gt;2 cm, high-grade hydronephrosis, or poor split function generally favors reconstruction instead. These are selection and failure-risk factors, not interchangeable absolute contraindications.<sup>[[14]](#ref14)[[17]](#ref17)[[18]](#ref18)</sup>

### Comparative outcomes

A systematic review and meta-analysis of robot-assisted versus laparoscopic versus open pyeloplasty showed equivalent success and complication rates across approaches, with shorter hospital stay favoring minimally invasive surgery in adults.<sup>[[13]](#ref13)</sup> A 2023 network meta-analysis of six small RCTs (381 patients) favored transperitoneal RALP, but concluded all four minimally invasive approaches were comparable and that retroperitoneal versus transperitoneal access is a matter of surgeon preference.<sup>[[15]](#ref15)</sup> A single-institution series of 129 RALPs reported a 96.9% success rate (125/129) with 8-year failure-free survival of 91.5%, and a claims-based comparative-effectiveness analysis found endopyelotomy inferior (15% failure versus 7% MIS pyeloplasty versus 9% open).<sup>[[16]](#ref16)[[18]](#ref18)</sup>

## Pediatric considerations

Most antenatally detected hydronephrosis does not need surgery; only a minority requires it. In the Cochrane review of infants (two small, high-risk-of-bias trials, 107 infants under 6 months), about one in five managed non-surgically crossed over to surgery and split function did not differ at 6–12 months, but follow-up was short.<sup>[[3]](#ref3)</sup> Operative thresholds for the pediatric reconstructive surgeon:

- **Open pyeloplasty** remains common in young infants but is no longer mandatory
- **Robotic or laparoscopic pyeloplasty** can be performed in selected infants at experienced centers. EAU reports comparable success and complications to open surgery but longer operative times; there is no universal 6-month or 15-cm eligibility rule.<sup>[[21]](#ref21)</sup>
- Indications mirror adult practice: declining split function, recurrent infection, symptoms, or progressive UTD/SFU grade. EAU recommends surgery for split function &lt;40% or a fall of more than 10 percentage points on serial studies.<sup>[[21]](#ref21)</sup>
- A meta-analysis of 46 mostly retrospective pediatric RALP series reported a mean success rate of 95.4% and a 12% overall complication rate (6.5% Clavien grade 3 or higher).<sup>[[24]](#ref24)</sup>

Urinary biomarker panels (NGAL, KIM-1, MCP-1, and broader urinary proteomic screens) have been proposed to predict which equivocal cases will progress, but none is yet clinical-grade.<sup>[[7]](#ref7)</sup>

## Special situations

### Crossing vessel

Identify and preserve lower-pole vessels during planning and surgery. Dismembered pyeloplasty can address both intrinsic narrowing and extrinsic compression; whether to transpose the UPJ relative to a vessel depends on the reconstructed anatomy.<sup>[[22]](#ref22)</sup> Pure vascular hitch (Hellström-style transposition without dismemberment) risks leaving an undiagnosed intrinsic component and is generally avoided in adults.<sup>[[14]](#ref14)[[17]](#ref17)</sup>

### Failed pyeloplasty

About 3% of primary pediatric pyeloplasties need a redo. In a meta-analysis of 287 pediatric redo pyeloplasties, success was 93.1% open, 92.1% laparoscopic and 89.4% robotic, without significant differences.<sup>[[25]](#ref25)</sup> Adult redo data are limited to small series. Endopyelotomy is a reasonable salvage option for short recurrent strictures without a crossing vessel; longer redo strictures generally need formal redo dismembered pyeloplasty, ureterocalicostomy (for an intrarenal pelvis with a thinned lower pole), or buccal mucosa onlay ureteroplasty.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup>

### Concomitant stones

Stones in an obstructed pelvis or calyces can be cleared at the time of pyeloplasty, including through a nephroscope during laparoscopic repair; the evidence is small case series.<sup>[[26]](#ref26)</sup>

### Anatomic anomalies

UPJO is over-represented in horseshoe and malrotated kidneys; aberrant rotation or fusion alters the location of the crossing vessel and the dependent point of the pelvis, and dismembered pyeloplasty must be tailored accordingly.<sup>[[8]](#ref8)</sup>

## Outcomes

Improved drainage or pain does not guarantee recovery of split function. A systematic review of adults with baseline differential function ≤30% found symptom improvement in 73.3–93.3%, while a &gt;5-point functional gain occurred in 13.3–53.8% of those with paired scans; evidence quality was low and no recovery predictor was consistent.<sup>[[23]](#ref23)</sup> Long-term durability data: in a representative RALP cohort, 8-year failure-free survival was **91.5%** (96.3% for stented procedures), with the bulk of failures occurring in the first 24 months.<sup>[[16]](#ref16)</sup>
