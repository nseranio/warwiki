# Ureteral Stricture

**Ureteral stricture disease** is pathologic narrowing of the ureteral lumen that obstructs urine flow and threatens the renal unit. Modern stricture disease is predominantly iatrogenic and stone-related rather than congenital, and the operative algorithm is dictated by four variables: location, length, etiology, and the integrity of the surrounding vascular bed.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[2]](#ref2)[[3]](#ref3)</sup> Reconstruction is the durable treatment. Endoscopic options remain reasonable for a narrow set of short, well-vascularized strictures but carry substantially higher long-term failure rates.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup>

For congenital ureteropelvic-junction obstruction (the dominant proximal-stricture etiology in pediatrics and the most common primary indication for pyeloplasty), see [UPJ Obstruction](/docs/clinical-conditions/03e-upper-tract/upj-obstruction). This article focuses on acquired ureteral strictures of the upper, mid, and distal ureter.

For operative selection and the full menu of repairs by location and length, see the [Upper Tract Reconstruction database](/docs/surgical-techniques/04d-upper-tract-reconstruction).

## Etiology and Epidemiology

Acquired benign ureteral strictures are dominated by stone disease and iatrogenic injury, with the distribution varying by referral population. Stone-related factors accounted for 85.2% in one 142-patient single-center cohort; this is not a general population estimate.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup>

| Category | Mechanism | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| **Stone-related** | Impacted stone, stone granuloma, mucosal injury during ureteroscopy | Stricture in 2.9% after URS versus 1.5% after shock wave lithotripsy in a claims database (literature range 0.5–5%); up to 24% after URS for impacted stones<sup>[[2]](#ref2)[[9]](#ref9)</sup> |
| **Iatrogenic — gynecologic** | Hysterectomy, cesarean section, endometriosis surgery, prolapse repair | Laparoscopic hysterectomy carries ~2× the ureteral injury risk vs open (OR 2.12)<sup>[[5]](#ref5)[[6]](#ref6)[[7]](#ref7)</sup> |
| **Iatrogenic — colorectal** | LAR, APR, sigmoidectomy | Retroperitoneal dissection near the pelvic brim and sacral promontory |
| **Iatrogenic — urologic** | Pyeloplasty, ureteral reimplant, prior endoscopic surgery | |
| **Post-transplant** | Ischemia at the ureterovesical anastomosis, BK virus, rejection | Incidence 1.4–10%; 81% at the UVJ<sup>[[30]](#ref30)[[31]](#ref31)</sup> |
| **Radiation** | Cervical, prostate, rectal cancer treatment | Long, ischemic, often progressive; poor candidates for endoscopic repair |
| **Inflammatory / infiltrative** | [Retroperitoneal fibrosis](/docs/clinical-conditions/03e-upper-tract/retroperitoneal-fibrosis), tuberculosis, endometriosis | |
| **Idiopathic** | No identifiable cause | Diagnosis of exclusion |

**Iatrogenic ureteral injury and the operative reality the reconstructive surgeon manages:**<sup>[[5]](#ref5)[[6]](#ref6)[[7]](#ref7)[[8]](#ref8)</sup>

- Ureteral injury complicates approximately 0.08% of gynecologic laparoscopy for benign indications
- 62% of injuries during hysterectomy are unrecognized intraoperatively, and missed injury drives the morbidity: adjusted odds ratio for urinary fistula 124 (versus 5.9 with intraoperative recognition), sepsis 11.9 (versus 2.0), and acute renal failure 23.8
- Intraoperative recognition collapses complication management failure (OR 0.22)
- Prophylactic stents can aid ureteral identification in selected complex cases, but a protective effect is not established. A 2024 review found no significant reduction in injury in three RCTs (3,277 patients; RR 0.90, 95% CI 0.49–1.65); they do not replace careful dissection.<sup>[[34]](#ref34)[[35]](#ref35)</sup>

These data are the case for early consult, intraoperative ureteral identification, and a low threshold for direct repair when called to the table. Operative detail is covered under [Intraoperative Consultation](/docs/special-populations/05a-trauma-emergencies/intraoperative-consultation).

## Pathophysiology

Stricture formation follows a stereotyped cascade of inflammation, then fibroproliferation, then collagen deposition after mucosal, thermal, ischemic, or mechanical insult.<sup>[[4]](#ref4)</sup> Histologic series classify benign strictures into three overlapping patterns: inflammatory cell infiltration (approximately 32%), fibroplasia (approximately 45%), and hyalinization (approximately 23%), with progressive type I and III collagen deposition as the lesion matures.<sup>[[4]](#ref4)</sup>

Two practical points for the reconstructive surgeon:

- **Vascular supply determines the operation.** Strictures with intact periureteral blood supply tolerate dilation or incision; ischemic strictures (radiation, devascularization at hysterectomy, prolonged stenting) tolerate them poorly. In one series balloon dilation succeeded in 89% of short strictures with intact supply but only 17–40% when supply was compromised (small numbers; endoureterotomy with stenting fared better, 17 of 19).<sup>[[12]](#ref12)</sup>
- **Indwelling stents are not benign.** In a porcine model, 14 days of stenting caused urothelial-mesenchymal transition and fibrosis that had not fully recovered 7 days after removal. This is relevant when planning long-term diversion versus definitive repair.<sup>[[10]](#ref10)</sup>

**Risk factors for post-ureteroscopy stricture:**<sup>[[2]](#ref2)[[9]](#ref9)</sup> ureteral perforation (strongest predictor), impacted stone, large stone burden, embedded fragments, failed initial URS, preoperative hydronephrosis, prior ureteral intervention.

## Clinical Presentation

Acquired ureteral strictures present with flank pain, recurrent UTI, hydronephrosis on imaging, hematuria, or asymptomatic renal-function decline. As with UPJO, the failure mode is often silent: a strictured upper tract can lose function for months before producing symptoms, a strong argument for surveillance imaging after at-risk pelvic surgery, ureteroscopy for impacted stones, and pelvic radiation.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[2]](#ref2)</sup>

## Diagnostic Evaluation

The reconstructive workup answers four questions: *Where is it? How long? What's the function? Is the bed vascularized?*

| Modality | Role | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| **Renal ultrasound** | Initial detection / serial surveillance | Quantifies hydronephrosis only |
| **CT urography** | Anatomic workhorse in adults | Identifies stone, mass, periureteral pathology, level of obstruction |
| **MR urography** | Noncontrast techniques can help when iodinated contrast is unsuitable; assess gadolinium use separately | **89% sensitivity** for non-calculous obstruction with serum creatinine &gt;2.5 mg/dL vs 40% for non-contrast CT (n=149)<sup>[[11]](#ref11)</sup> |
| **Retrograde pyelogram** | Defines stricture length, location, and the proximal/distal ureteral caliber | Usually performed at the time of stent or definitive repair |
| **Antegrade nephrostogram** | When retrograde access fails or a nephrostomy is already in place | Pairs naturally with antegrade endoureterotomy |
| **Diuretic renography (MAG3)** | Quantifies split function and confirms true obstruction | Same interpretive thresholds as in [UPJO](/docs/clinical-conditions/03e-upper-tract/upj-obstruction) |

The single most operative-decision-relevant finding from imaging is stricture length, which separates the small population that may benefit from endoscopic management (≤ 2 cm) from the large population that needs reconstruction.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[12]](#ref12)</sup>

## Management

### Principles

- **Reconstruction offers durable repair.** A single-center observational cohort reported 95.7% success with reconstruction versus 51.6% with endourological treatment. These are not pooled or randomized comparative estimates; selection differs by stricture complexity.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup>
- **Endoscopic management** is reasonable as first-line therapy only for short (≤ 2 cm), recent (&lt;3 months), well-vascularized strictures with mild-to-moderate hydronephrosis.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[12]](#ref12)[[13]](#ref13)</sup>
- The surgical principles are unchanged across all reconstructions: tension-free, watertight, well-vascularized, spatulated, stented.<sup>[[15]](#ref15)</sup>
- Most recurrences after endoscopic treatment occur early (4 of 6 within 12 months and all within 36 months in one series) but can appear later (median 51 months in another). Surveillance is mandatory.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[28]](#ref28)</sup>

### Endoscopic Management

| Technique | Reported success | Best candidate | Caveats |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Balloon dilation** | Technical 89%; long-term ~54% | Short (≤ 2 cm), recent, intact vascular supply | Drops to 17–40% if vascular supply compromised<sup>[[12]](#ref12)[[13]](#ref13)</sup> |
| **Endoureterotomy** (laser, cold knife, electrocautery) and dilation, mixed series | 51.6% (n=95) to 72% (n=43) overall in retrospective cohorts; higher in short strictures | Short benign stricture; antegrade preferred for upper-tract, retrograde for distal | Inferior to reconstruction for long or ischemic strictures<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[28]](#ref28)</sup> |
| **Segmental metallic stents (Allium, Memokath, Resonance)** | Pooled primary success 73% under 1 yr, 66% at 1–2 yr, 39% beyond 2 yr (Allium 81% and 65% at 1–2 and &gt;2 yr) | Short strictures from point injury; frail or non-operative patients as maintenance drainage | Obstruction, migration, encrustation, UTI; high failure in long, radiated, inflammatory or thermally injured strictures<sup>[[14]](#ref14)[[36]](#ref36)</sup> |

Chronic drainage is a bridge, not a repair. Polymer stents and nephrostomy tubes need regular exchange and are prone to failure; in one 129-episode series of metallic stents for chronic ureteral obstruction, functional failure occurred in 19% of Resonance, 48% of Allium and 65% of Memokath insertions, with no Resonance failures for benign indications.<sup>[[40]](#ref40)</sup> Extrinsic malignant obstruction and radiation strictures behave differently from short benign strictures, and reconstruction candidacy should be settled before committing to indefinite drainage.

### Reconstruction by Location

The choice of reconstruction follows the level of the stricture and the length of the defect.

| Stricture location | Short (≤ 2–3 cm) | Mid (3–8 cm) | Long (&gt; 8 cm) or panureteral |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Distal** | Ureteroneocystostomy | **Psoas hitch** ± reimplant | **Boari flap** ± psoas hitch |
| **Mid** | Ureteroureterostomy | **BMG ureteroplasty** | Boari flap + psoas hitch (reach midureter); ileal ureter |
| **Proximal** | Ureteroureterostomy | **BMG ureteroplasty**; ureterocalicostomy if intrarenal pelvis | **Ileal ureter**; renal autotransplantation |
| **Panureteral** | — | — | **Ileal ureter** (workhorse); renal autotransplantation (salvage) |

Robotic reconstruction is now widely used for the full range of repairs, from ureteroureterostomy and reimplantation to Boari flap, BMG ureteroplasty, ileal ureter and autotransplantation; multi-institutional retrospective series report success above 90% for ureteroureterostomy and pyeloplasty and low high-grade complication rates.<sup>[[3]](#ref3)[[16]](#ref16)[[17]](#ref17)</sup>

### Distal Reconstruction — Reimplant, Psoas Hitch, Boari Flap

A graded set of operations on the bladder, deployed by length of distal defect:<sup>[[15]](#ref15)</sup>

1. **Ureteroneocystostomy (refluxing or anti-refluxing).** This is the usual choice for the truly distal stricture. Reported success is high in retrospective series. For adult acquired injury or stricture, neither refluxing nor nonrefluxing reimplantation is universally preferred: balance reflux against anastomotic obstruction, bladder function and available ureteral length.<sup>[[34]](#ref34)</sup>
2. **Psoas hitch.** Used for defects that prevent tension-free reimplant. The bladder is mobilized contralaterally, fixed to the ipsilateral psoas tendon (caudal to the genitofemoral nerve to spare it), and the ureter implanted into the cephalad bladder dome. Reported success is comparably high.
3. **Boari flap.** Used for defects of 8–12 cm. A wide-based, anteriorly oriented bladder flap is tubularized over a stent and rotated cephalad to bridge the gap; combined with a psoas hitch when even more length is needed. In a 50-patient prospective multi-institutional robotic series, 90% were successful at median 15 months, and all failures occurred within 2 months.<sup>[[37]](#ref37)</sup>

### Mid-Ureteral Reconstruction

- **Ureteroureterostomy.** Used for short defects (≤ 2–3 cm) with mobile, healthy ureter. Spatulated end-to-end anastomosis over a stent. Good results when tension-free: 90% success at median 55 months in 22 robotic distal ureteroureterostomies (median stricture 1.5 cm).<sup>[[16]](#ref16)[[17]](#ref17)</sup>
- **Buccal mucosa graft (BMG) ureteroplasty.** See below.
- **Transureteroureterostomy.** Historically used for irreplaceable distal segments; rarely employed today given the durability of BMG and ileal ureter and the morbidity of jeopardizing the contralateral renal unit.

### Buccal Mucosa Graft Ureteroplasty

The most important technical advance in ureteral reconstruction of the past decade. BMG is a free graft that must acquire a blood supply from its recipient bed, rather than a vascularized flap. It avoids bowel harvest and is an option for selected complex strictures; the cited early cohorts chiefly concern proximal and midureteral disease.<sup>[[18]](#ref18)[[19]](#ref19)[[20]](#ref20)</sup>

**Technique:**

- **Approach:** robotic, transperitoneal, with the stricture exposed by mobilizing the ureter on its periureteral fat
- **Configuration:** onlay graft (approximately 79%), with anterior longitudinal stricturotomy and the graft sutured as a roof, or augmented anastomotic repair when the back wall is also diseased
- **Vascular cover:** omental wrap in approximately 95%. The graft requires neovascularization from the surrounding bed, and omentum is the reliable bed
- **Stent for 4–6 weeks**, retrograde pyelogram or MAG3 at stent removal

**Outcomes:**

- Multi-institutional 10-year cohort (163 patients): 92.0% success at median 29 months<sup>[[21]](#ref21)</sup>
- Earlier series report 87–90% at median follow-up 26–28 months<sup>[[19]](#ref19)[[20]](#ref20)</sup>
- Effective in redo cases, with 33–47% of patients in published series having failed prior reconstruction<sup>[[20]](#ref20)[[21]](#ref21)</sup>
- Median LOS 1–2 days for the robotic approach

**Appendiceal flap ureteroplasty** is a useful right-sided alternative when a normal appendix is in the field. It is used as an onlay or interposition and avoids harvesting a separate ileal segment or oral graft, but uses the appendix itself. A 14-patient multi-institutional series reported 92.9% patency (median stricture 4.75 cm; 71% onlay, 29% interposition).<sup>[[3]](#ref3)[[38]](#ref38)</sup>

### Ileal Ureter Substitution

Reserved for panureteral or long-segment (&gt; 12 cm) defects where local options fail: extensive radiation injury, multiple failed prior reconstructions, recurrent strictures from systemic disease.<sup>[[22]](#ref22)[[23]](#ref23)</sup>

- **Technique:** isolated ileal segment (12–15 cm typical), isoperistaltic orientation, proximal anastomosis to renal pelvis or proximal ureter, distal anastomosis to bladder
- **Antireflux maneuvers:** the iliopsoas tunnel technique (extramural ileal segment buried in psoas) reduces reflux-related complications<sup>[[24]](#ref24)</sup>
- **Combined techniques** for full-length defects: ileal segment plus Boari flap and psoas hitch to extend bladder reach<sup>[[23]](#ref23)</sup>
- **Success approximately 83%** (no further open intervention) at mean 4.4 years in a 46-patient single-institution series, with a 24% major-complication rate; mean stricture length 9.1 cm and 26% of cases radiation-related<sup>[[22]](#ref22)</sup>
- **Morbidity unique to ileum:** mucus production (chronic intermittent obstruction), hyperchloremic metabolic acidosis (particularly with impaired renal function), B12 deficiency over years, and bowel-anastomosis morbidity. Assess renal reserve and the health and availability of the proposed bowel segment; EAU advises avoiding ileal substitution with impaired renal function or known intestinal disease. A serum-creatinine cutoff alone is not a complete assessment, and prior pelvic radiation requires evaluating the bowel actually selected.<sup>[[34]](#ref34)</sup>

### Renal Autotransplantation

Salvage operation for the most extensive reconstructive failures: total ureteral loss after multiple prior attempts, panureteral radiation injury where ileum is unavailable, or extensive periureteral malignancy precluding orthotopic reconstruction.<sup>[[25]](#ref25)[[27]](#ref27)</sup>

- **Renal function** was preserved (no significant creatinine change) in a 51-patient, 54-procedure two-institution series with 2 graft losses; ureteral stricture was the indication in only 20%. Median follow-up was 21.5 months, and early Clavien IIIa or higher complications occurred in 14.8%<sup>[[27]](#ref27)</sup>
- **Cold ischemia time** was the only predictor of complications in that series
- In an eight-centre comparison (15 RAIUR, 39 RAKAT), robotic kidney autotransplantation (RAKAT) and robotic ileal ureter (RAIUR) gave similar renal function and Clavien III+ complications (13% versus 10%); RAKAT had a shorter stay (5 versus 8 days) and fewer complications of any grade (31% versus 73%). A 2026 meta-analysis (low certainty, 1,044 patients, 41 autotransplants) found success of 93.0% for ileal ureter and 87.8% for autotransplantation, not significantly different. Choice follows anatomy, prior surgery, bowel and renal status, and surgeon experience<sup>[[26]](#ref26)[[39]](#ref39)</sup>

## Post-Transplant Ureteral Strictures

Stricture in a transplanted ureter is a distinct problem because the ureter is short, end-arterial, and the recipient has comorbid CKD that limits diagnostic and operative tolerance.<sup>[[29]](#ref29)[[30]](#ref30)[[31]](#ref31)</sup>

- **Incidence** roughly 5–10% in recent series
- **Most strictures arise at the ureterovesical anastomosis** (approximately 81%), reflecting the watershed blood supply of the distal transplant ureter
- **Etiology:** ischemia (dominant), technical, BK virus reactivation, acute or chronic rejection
- **Endoscopic management** (balloon dilation, metallic stents): reasonable for short, early strictures; a systematic review found endourologic management safe with good outcomes, particularly for short, early strictures<sup>[[29]](#ref29)</sup>
- **Definitive reconstruction** (pyeloureterostomy to native ureter, transplant-to-native ureteroureterostomy, or redo ureteroneocystostomy with a Boari flap) yields 87–93% success and is preferred over chronic stenting, which is associated with progressive eGFR decline<sup>[[30]](#ref30)</sup>
- **Robotic transplant-ureter reconstruction**, including single-port platforms, is feasible and durable, with 92.6% success at median 15 months in a 27-patient single-center series (median stay 3 days; 52% had complications, mostly Clavien II)<sup>[[31]](#ref31)[[32]](#ref32)</sup>

## Prevention

The majority of acquired ureteral strictures are preventable. The operative window is in the original case, not at the reconstruction.<sup>[[5]](#ref5)[[8]](#ref8)[[33]](#ref33)</sup>

- **Ureteroscopy:** avoid perforation, minimize mucosal trauma at impacted stones, size the access sheath to the ureter, stent any procedure with intraoperative concern
- **Pelvic surgery:** anatomic identification of the ureter at the pelvic brim and along the cardinal ligament; selective prophylactic stenting in high-risk gynecologic cases
- **Intraoperative recognition** of injury is the single most important determinant of long-term outcome. When in doubt, obtain a retrograde study or inspect directly on the table
- **Indocyanine green (ICG) fluorescence** is increasingly used at the time of reconstruction to confirm ureteral perfusion and define healthy margins for resection

For the acute consult on a recognized intraoperative ureteral injury, see [Intraoperative Consultation](/docs/special-populations/05a-trauma-emergencies/intraoperative-consultation).
