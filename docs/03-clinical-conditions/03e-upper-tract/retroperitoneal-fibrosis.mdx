# Retroperitoneal Fibrosis (RPF)

Retroperitoneal fibrosis (RPF) is a rare immune-mediated fibro-inflammatory disease characterized by tissue surrounding the abdominal aorta and iliac arteries that can encase the ureters, IVC, renal vessels, and adjacent retroperitoneal structures.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[2]](#ref2)</sup> For the reconstructive urologist, RPF matters because ureteral obstruction occurs in about 60–80% of idiopathic cases and, in one 26-patient RPF referral cohort, 40% had renal function deterioration at diagnosis, making prompt drainage and coordinated medical and surgical management central reconstructive priorities.<sup>[[3]](#ref3)[[4]](#ref4)[[5]](#ref5)[[6]](#ref6)</sup> Referral-series figures vary with case mix (hydronephrosis was present in about half of patients in pooled retrospective series).<sup>[[5]](#ref5)</sup>

The pathway for RPF-related obstruction (medical therapy, drainage for moderate-to-severe obstruction or threatened renal function, and ureterolysis for refractory obstruction) is covered in detail on the [Ureterolysis](/docs/surgical-techniques/04d-upper-tract-reconstruction/ureterolysis) technique page.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup>

---

## Epidemiology

- Estimated incidence 0.1 per 100,000 person-years; prevalence about 1.38 per 100,000.<sup>[[3]](#ref3)</sup>
- Men affected 2–3× more often than women; mean age at presentation 50–60 years.<sup>[[3]](#ref3)[[5]](#ref5)</sup>
- No clear ethnic predisposition or familial clustering.<sup>[[3]](#ref3)</sup>

---

## Classification and Etiology

RPF is broadly divided into idiopathic (&gt; two-thirds of cases) and secondary forms.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[6]](#ref6)</sup>

### Idiopathic RPF

- May be isolated or part of IgG4-related disease (IgG4-RD). IgG4-related RPF shows greater male predominance, higher serum IgG4 or IgE, elevated eosinophils, and multi-organ involvement versus non-IgG4 idiopathic RPF (in one 132-patient cohort, 89% of IgG4-related RPF had other organ involvement, and none of the non-IgG4 group did).<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[7]](#ref7)</sup>
- The 2019 ACR/EULAR classification criteria for IgG4-RD require involvement of a characteristic organ (retroperitoneum/aorta is one of 11), apply 32 exclusion criteria, then score weighted clinical, serologic, radiologic and pathologic domains; a total of 20 points or more classifies IgG4-RD (specificity 99.2%, sensitivity 85.5% in the first validation cohort). These are classification, not diagnostic, criteria, and RPF itself has no validated criteria.<sup>[[25]](#ref25)</sup>
- Often associated with other autoimmune conditions (Hashimoto thyroiditis, psoriasis) and fibro-inflammatory disorders.<sup>[[4]](#ref4)</sup>
- Multifactorial: genetic susceptibility (notably HLA-DRB1*03), environmental exposures (asbestos, smoking), and immune dysregulation.<sup>[[4]](#ref4)[[8]](#ref8)</sup>

### Major randomized updates

Idiopathic RPF, steroid sparing (2025): a two-center, open-label noninferiority trial randomized 60 adults with eGFR >30 mL/min/1.73 m² to methotrexate plus lower-dose prednisone or standard-dose prednisone. Remission maintained through month 9, defined as symptom resolution, freedom from ureteral obstruction and drainage devices, and normal acute-phase reactants, occurred in 26/29 (89.7%) versus 25/31 (80.6%); difference 9.1 percentage points (95% CI −9.9 to 27.3), meeting the prespecified noninferiority criterion with lower cumulative steroid exposure. It does not prove superiority, safety in severe renal impairment, or elimination of late relapse.<sup>[[22]](#ref22)</sup>

IgG4-related disease (MITIGATE): in 135 adults receiving the same glucocorticoid taper, a treated adjudicated flare occurred in 7/68 (10%) with inebilizumab versus 40/67 (60%) with placebo over 52 weeks (HR 0.13, 95% CI 0.06–0.28); serious adverse events occurred in 18% versus 9%.<sup>[[23]](#ref23)</sup> FDA approved inebilizumab for adult IgG4-RD on April 3, 2025.<sup>[[24]](#ref24)</sup> This supports specialist management of confirmed IgG4-related RPF (see the general IgG4-RD treatment review),<sup>[[15]](#ref15)</sup> not automatic use in all idiopathic fibrosis or a claim of ureteral-obstruction cure. Review the full label for infection screening, immunoglobulins, vaccination and infusion precautions; urgent drainage remains a separate decision.<sup>[[23]](#ref23)[[24]](#ref24)</sup>

### Secondary RPF Causes

| Category | Examples |
|---|---|
| **Drugs** | Ergot derivatives (methysergide, ergotamine), ergot-derived dopamine agonists (pergolide), hydralazine, analgesics; β-blockers have been reported but a causal role remains unproven.<sup>[[30]](#ref30)</sup> |
| **Malignancies** | Desmoplastic reaction to metastases (prostate, breast, colon) or primary tumors (lymphoma, sarcoma, carcinoid) |
| **Infections** | Local spread from contiguous foci (tuberculosis) |
| **Radiotherapy** | Fibrosis limited to the radiation field |
| **Histiocytosis** | Erdheim–Chester disease (perirenal “hairy kidney” pattern)<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup> |
| **Other** | Prior surgery, pancreatitis |

## Pathophysiology

The immunopathogenesis involves complex B- and T-lymphocyte crosstalk, macrophage and fibrocyte chemotaxis, and eosinophil or mast cell recruitment.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup> Mediators include chemokines (CXCL12, CCL11) and cytokines (IL-6, IL-12, IL-13) that simultaneously orchestrate inflammation and promote fibrosis.<sup>[[4]](#ref4)</sup> The disease was historically thought to result from a local autoimmune reaction to antigens within atherosclerotic aortic plaques, but the systemic features and autoimmune associations suggest a broader immune-mediated process.<sup>[[3]](#ref3)</sup>

---

## Clinical Presentation

- **Abdominal pain** and low back pain are the most common symptoms (65% and 42% in one 26-patient referral cohort).<sup>[[5]](#ref5)</sup>
- **Systemic symptoms.** Fatigue, weight loss, malaise.<sup>[[6]](#ref6)</sup>
- Less common: constipation, testicular pain, lower-extremity claudication.<sup>[[6]](#ref6)</sup>
- **Ureteral obstruction in about 60–80% of idiopathic RPF cases**, often causing acute or chronic kidney injury.<sup>[[6]](#ref6)[[4]](#ref4)</sup>
- Other complications: caval occlusion, deep vein thrombosis, renal artery or vein stenosis, renal atrophy.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[2]](#ref2)</sup>
- Elevated acute-phase reactants (ESR, CRP) in about 75% and renal function deterioration in about 40% at diagnosis in that same cohort.<sup>[[5]](#ref5)</sup>
- **Presentation pattern.** RPF often begins insidiously with slowly progressive renal dysfunction, but ureteral obstruction can also cause acute colic-like pain and acute kidney injury, particularly when bilateral; severe obstruction may cause oliguria or anuria.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[3]](#ref3)</sup>

---

## Diagnosis

There are no standardized diagnostic criteria. Diagnosis combines clinical, laboratory, and imaging findings.<sup>[[3]](#ref3)</sup>

### Laboratory

- Elevated ESR and CRP (supportive but nonspecific).
- Serum IgG4 can support assessment for IgG4-related disease when elevated, but a normal value does not exclude IgG4-related RPF.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[7]](#ref7)</sup>
- Renal function assessment (creatinine, eGFR), urinalysis, and urine protein quantification. In suspected IgG4-related RPF, consider concurrent parenchymal kidney disease when impairment is disproportionate to obstruction or persists after drainage.<sup>[[31]](#ref31)</sup>

### Imaging

- **CT.** Used to diagnose 24/26 patients (92%) in one referral cohort; CT and MRI are the imaging modalities of choice.<sup>[[5]](#ref5)[[9]](#ref9)</sup> RPF typically appears as a homogeneous soft-tissue plaque, isodense with muscle, surrounding the infrarenal aorta and iliac arteries, often enveloping the ureters and IVC. Contrast enhancement is variable depending on disease stage.<sup>[[5]](#ref5)[[9]](#ref9)[[3]](#ref3)</sup>
- **MRI.** Avoids iodinated CT contrast and offers soft-tissue characterization; contrast-enhanced MRI uses a gadolinium agent, so protocol selection still considers kidney function.<sup>[[3]](#ref3)</sup>
- **FDG-PET/CT.** Used to assess disease extent, metabolic activity, treatment response, and suspected relapse.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup> In a 50-patient imaging cohort, baseline metabolic active volume was associated with PET-defined metabolic progression in 8 of 30 evaluable patients at later follow-up; that endpoint did not measure clinical relapse or obstruction.<sup>[[10]](#ref10)</sup> In a separate 115-patient treated cohort, persistent end-of-treatment PET activity was associated with subsequent relapse.<sup>[[11]](#ref11)</sup>
- **Imaging distinction.** Idiopathic RPF classically does not displace the aorta anteriorly and causes medial ureteral deviation, whereas malignant infiltration tends to displace the aorta anteriorly and ureters laterally.<sup>[[3]](#ref3)</sup> This is a tendency, not a rule: in a 92-patient referral series, 18 of 47 patients with a peri-aortic mass and final diagnosis of RPF still had anterior aortic displacement, and 2 of 6 malignant masses did not. A characteristic non-displacing peri-aortic mass supports a benign diagnosis and can spare biopsy; atypical morphology or location does not.<sup>[[12]](#ref12)</sup>

### Biopsy

Retroperitoneal biopsy is reserved for atypical cases: atypical location (pelvic, peripancreatic), suspicion of malignancy, or when clinical or laboratory findings are inconclusive.<sup>[[3]](#ref3)[[13]](#ref13)</sup> Laparoscopic or open biopsy is often more informative than needle biopsy but is more invasive.<sup>[[14]](#ref14)</sup> Assess other IgG4-RD organ involvement and exclude secondary causes (drugs, malignancy, infection, radiation) before labeling RPF idiopathic.<sup>[[14]](#ref14)</sup> Histology shows abundant fibrosis with mononuclear inflammatory infiltrate (lymphocytes, plasma cells, macrophages).<sup>[[6]](#ref6)</sup>

## Treatment

Treatment aims to relieve ureteral obstruction and induce disease regression. Medical therapy is the cornerstone; surgical drainage or ureterolysis address mechanical obstruction.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[13]](#ref13)</sup>

### Surgical Drainage and Ureterolysis

Surgical pathway covered in full on the [Ureterolysis](/docs/surgical-techniques/04d-upper-tract-reconstruction/ureterolysis) technique page. Briefly:

- **Ureteral stenting or PCN** promptly decompresses moderate-to-severe obstruction, acute kidney injury, or threatened renal function.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[14]](#ref14)[[17]](#ref17)</sup> Mild hydronephrosis with stable renal function may permit medical treatment with close renal and imaging follow-up.<sup>[[4]](#ref4)</sup> In a retrospective series of 30 patients with 44 obstructed urinary units, initial stenting succeeded in 25 of 32 attempted units. Complication rates were 17.9% with stents and 21% with nephrostomy (P = 0.79); the authors considered the drainage methods complementary because some patients needed both over time.<sup>[[17]](#ref17)</sup> The cumulative incidence of acute pyelonephritis during drainage follow-up was 30%.<sup>[[17]](#ref17)</sup>
- **Surgical ureterolysis** (open, laparoscopic, or robotic, usually with omental wrapping) is generally considered for refractory obstruction, nephrostomy dependence, stent failure, or severe stent symptoms.<sup>[[13]](#ref13)[[14]](#ref14)[[27]](#ref27)</sup> In a retrospective obstructive-RPF cohort, medication intolerance, absent radiographic response, and persistent pain were also reported reasons for surgery.<sup>[[16]](#ref16)</sup> Ureterolysis relieves ureteral obstruction but does not treat systemic RPF.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup> In a prospective 50-patient open ureterolysis series managed by a high-volume multidisciplinary team at one specialist center, 47/50 (94%) were stent-free at 12 months; 11/50 (22%) underwent additional procedures, including nephrectomy in 7/50.<sup>[[27]](#ref27)</sup> At 1 year, median GFR changed by +25% in the stent-failure group and −10% in the nephrostomy-dependent group (the latter not significant); Clavien III–IV complications occurred in 12%.<sup>[[27]](#ref27)</sup> The authors argue for considering ureterolysis early when obstruction does not respond quickly to standard treatment; this is a single-center uncontrolled series.<sup>[[27]](#ref27)</sup>
- In a 52-patient retrospective cohort, medical treatment with temporary renal drainage as needed resolved obstruction without ureterolysis in 36/52 (69%); median stent duration was 16 months, with median clinical and imaging follow-up of 4.2 and 3.3 years. Ureterolysis was later performed in 8/52 (15%), and 9 patients (18%) had recurrent obstruction after a stent-free period.<sup>[[16]](#ref16)</sup>

### Medical Therapy

- **Glucocorticoids** remain first-line, inducing rapid symptom remission, reduction in acute-phase reactants, and often mass shrinkage. Typical induction: prednisone 0.5–1 mg/kg/day with gradual taper over 6–9 months.<sup>[[6]](#ref6)[[4]](#ref4)[[11]](#ref11)</sup> In the Parma protocol, induction was prednisone 1 mg/kg/day for 1 month, then 0.5 mg/kg/day tapered over 8 months.<sup>[[6]](#ref6)</sup> Higher steroid dose at 1 year and longer courses were associated with fewer relapses in retrospective cohorts.<sup>[[20]](#ref20)[[21]](#ref21)</sup>
- **Prednisone versus tamoxifen.** The 2011 trial randomized 36 responders after prednisone induction; continued tapering prednisone prevented relapse better than switching to tamoxifen (relapse by month 8: 1/18 [6%] versus 7/18 [39%]; estimated 26-month cumulative relapse 17% versus 50%), at the cost of more Cushingoid features and hypercholesterolemia. This was a remission-maintenance comparison, and is no longer the only RPF randomized trial.<sup>[[6]](#ref6)</sup>
- **Combination therapy:** Across 11 mostly observational studies (224 patients), heterogeneous pooled response estimates were 98.9% versus 85.1% (P = 0.08), without evidence that every immunosuppressant combination is superior.<sup>[[18]](#ref18)</sup> Commonly used agents:
  - **Mycophenolate mofetil.** In a prospective 28-patient case series (prednisone 40 mg/day tapered over 6 months plus MMF 1000 mg twice daily for a mean 24 months), systemic symptoms resolved in all, 89% had ≥ 25% mass reduction, and disease recurred in 2 (7%).<sup>[[26]](#ref26)</sup> Uncontrolled.
  - **Methotrexate** and azathioprine are steroid-sparing agents.<sup>[[4]](#ref4)</sup> The 2025 methotrexate trial provides randomized steroid-sparing evidence specifically for idiopathic RPF.<sup>[[22]](#ref22)</sup>
  - **Tocilizumab.** An open pilot enrolling 12 patients with active chronic periaortitis reported that 10/11 evaluable patients (91%) met the partial or complete remission endpoint after 3 months of tocilizumab monotherapy; this is preliminary evidence from a broader periaortitis population.<sup>[[28]](#ref28)</sup>
  - **Cyclophosphamide.** Reserved for severe or refractory disease because of toxicity; not first line.<sup>[[4]](#ref4)</sup>
- **Rituximab** (anti-CD20 B-cell depletion) is increasingly used for refractory or relapsing disease. In a retrospective series (n = 26; 19 IgG4-related, 7 idiopathic), all 19 with pain improved symptomatically, and 22/25 patients with follow-up imaging (88%) had radiologic improvement; 73% received rituximab without additional glucocorticoids. Severe infections in 12%. Of 10 patients with stents or nephrostomies, 4 became tube-free.<sup>[[19]](#ref19)</sup>

### Secondary RPF

- **Drug-induced.** Withdrawal of the offending agent often leads to resolution; steroids may still be needed.<sup>[[3]](#ref3)</sup>
- **Infection-related.** Antimicrobial therapy; steroids generally contraindicated.<sup>[[3]](#ref3)</sup>
- **Malignancy-related.** Chemotherapy or radiotherapy directed at the underlying neoplasm.<sup>[[3]](#ref3)</sup>

## Monitoring and Follow-Up

Serial assessment includes symptoms, ESR/CRP, renal function, and imaging.<sup>[[3]](#ref3)</sup> Relapse can occur without symptoms or an acute-phase-reactant rise.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup> At stent or PCN exchange, retrograde or antegrade pyelography can confirm ureteral patency before removal; renal ultrasound after removal checks for recurrent hydronephrosis.<sup>[[4]](#ref4)[[14]](#ref14)</sup> CT and MRI track mass size; FDG-PET helps assess metabolic activity and relapse risk.<sup>[[10]](#ref10)[[11]](#ref11)</sup> A nonavid residual mass may represent inactive fibrosis, but the decision to reduce or stop treatment also requires clinical and renal assessment, and surveillance must continue.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[3]](#ref3)</sup> Positive FDG-PET at end of treatment independently predicts relapse (HR 3.47).<sup>[[11]](#ref11)</sup>

---

## Prognosis and Relapse

In a retrospective 50-patient idiopathic RPF cohort followed for a median 8.9 years, patient survival was 95% at 5 years, 84% at 10 years, and 68% at 15 years.<sup>[[20]](#ref20)</sup> However, relapse is a major challenge:

- A review of idiopathic RPF reports relapse in about 10–50% after treatment interruption.<sup>[[4]](#ref4)</sup> In a retrospective 50-patient cohort followed a median 8.9 years, 19/50 (38%) had at least one relapse.<sup>[[20]](#ref20)</sup> In a separate 155-patient cohort followed a median 45.3 months, 27/153 (17.6%) evaluable patients had at least one relapse.<sup>[[21]](#ref21)</sup>
- Cumulative relapse incidence: 21% at 5 years, 41% at 10 years, 48% at 15 years.<sup>[[20]](#ref20)</sup>

Predictors of relapse: male sex, antinuclear-antibody positivity, lower steroid dose at 1 year, higher baseline ESR, shorter glucocorticoid courses, thoracic vessel involvement, and persistent FDG-PET activity after treatment.<sup>[[20]](#ref20)[[11]](#ref11)[[21]](#ref21)</sup> Smoking and atypical (for example pelvic) localization predicted lower likelihood of remission rather than relapse; pre-treatment FDG-PET activity predicted remission.<sup>[[11]](#ref11)</sup> In one large cohort, 42% relapsed over a median of 33 months.<sup>[[11]](#ref11)</sup>

Prompt relief of hydronephrosis is needed to prevent irreversible renal atrophy. The reconstructive surgeon's central responsibility in the multidisciplinary RPF team.<sup>[[21]](#ref21)</sup>
