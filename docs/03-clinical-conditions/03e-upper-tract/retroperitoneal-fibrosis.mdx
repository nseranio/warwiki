# Retroperitoneal Fibrosis (RPF)

**Retroperitoneal fibrosis (RPF)** is a rare immune-mediated fibro-inflammatory disease characterized by tissue surrounding the abdominal aorta and iliac arteries that can encase the **ureters, IVC, renal vessels, and adjacent retroperitoneal structures**.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[2]](#ref2)</sup> For the reconstructive urologist, RPF matters because **60–80% of patients develop ureteral obstruction** and ~ 40% have renal function deterioration at diagnosis — making prompt drainage and coordinated medical + surgical management the central reconstructive priorities.<sup>[[3]](#ref3)[[4]](#ref4)</sup> Referral-series figures vary with case mix (hydronephrosis was present in about half of patients in pooled retrospective series).<sup>[[5]](#ref5)</sup>

The surgical pathway for RPF-related obstruction (stenting / nephrostomy → medical induction → ureterolysis for refractory disease) is covered in detail on the [Ureterolysis](/docs/surgical-techniques/04d-upper-tract-reconstruction/ureterolysis) technique page.

---

## Epidemiology

- Estimated incidence **0.1 per 100,000 person-years**; prevalence ~ 1.38 per 100,000.<sup>[[3]](#ref3)</sup>
- Men affected **2–3× more often** than women; mean age at presentation **50–60 years**.<sup>[[3]](#ref3)[[5]](#ref5)</sup>
- No clear ethnic predisposition or familial clustering.<sup>[[3]](#ref3)</sup>

---

## Classification and Etiology

RPF is broadly divided into **idiopathic (&gt; two-thirds of cases)** and **secondary** forms.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[6]](#ref6)</sup>

### Idiopathic RPF

- May be isolated or part of **IgG4-related disease (IgG4-RD)**. IgG4-related RPF shows greater male predominance, higher serum IgG4 / IgE, elevated eosinophils, and multi-organ involvement vs non-IgG4 idiopathic RPF (in one 132-patient cohort, 89% of IgG4-related RPF had other organ involvement, and none of the non-IgG4 group did).<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[7]](#ref7)</sup>
- The **2019 ACR/EULAR classification criteria** for IgG4-RD require involvement of a characteristic organ (retroperitoneum/aorta is one of 11), apply 32 exclusion criteria, then score weighted clinical, serologic, radiologic and pathologic domains; a total of 20 points or more classifies IgG4-RD (specificity 99.2%, sensitivity 85.5% in the first validation cohort). These are classification, not diagnostic, criteria, and RPF itself has no validated criteria.<sup>[[26]](#ref26)</sup>
- Often associated with other autoimmune conditions (Hashimoto thyroiditis, psoriasis) and fibro-inflammatory disorders.<sup>[[8]](#ref8)</sup>
- Multifactorial: genetic susceptibility (notably **HLA-DRB1*03**), environmental exposures (**asbestos, smoking**), and immune dysregulation.<sup>[[8]](#ref8)[[9]](#ref9)</sup>

### Major randomized updates

**Idiopathic RPF — steroid sparing (2025):** a two-center, open-label noninferiority trial randomized 60 adults with eGFR >30 mL/min/1.73 m² to methotrexate plus lower-dose prednisone or standard-dose prednisone. Nine-month maintained remission was 26/29 (89.7%) versus 25/31 (80.6%); difference 9.1 percentage points (95% CI −9.9 to 27.3), meeting the prespecified noninferiority criterion with lower cumulative steroid exposure. It does not prove superiority, safety in severe renal impairment, or elimination of late relapse.<sup>[[23]](#ref23)</sup>

**IgG4-related disease — MITIGATE:** in 135 adults receiving the same glucocorticoid taper, a treated adjudicated flare occurred in 7/68 (10%) with inebilizumab versus 40/67 (60%) with placebo over 52 weeks (HR 0.13, 95% CI 0.06–0.28); serious adverse events occurred in 18% versus 9%. FDA approved inebilizumab for adult IgG4-RD on April 3, 2025. This supports specialist management of confirmed **IgG4-related** RPF (see the general IgG4-RD treatment review),<sup>[[16]](#ref16)</sup> not automatic use in all idiopathic fibrosis or a claim of ureteral-obstruction cure. Review the full label for infection screening, immunoglobulins, vaccination and infusion precautions; urgent drainage remains a separate decision.<sup>[[24]](#ref24)[[25]](#ref25)</sup>

### Secondary RPF Causes

| Category | Examples |
|---|---|
| **Drugs** | Ergot derivatives (methysergide, ergotamine), dopamine agonists (pergolide), β-blockers, hydralazine, analgesics |
| **Malignancies** | Desmoplastic reaction to metastases (prostate, breast, colon) or primary tumors (lymphoma, sarcoma, carcinoid) |
| **Infections** | Local spread from contiguous foci (tuberculosis) |
| **Radiotherapy** | Fibrosis limited to the radiation field |
| **Other** | Prior surgery, pancreatitis |

## Pathophysiology

The immunopathogenesis involves complex B- and T-lymphocyte crosstalk, macrophage and fibrocyte chemotaxis, and eosinophil / mast cell recruitment.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup> Key mediators include chemokines (CXCL12, CCL11) and cytokines (IL-6, IL-12, IL-13) that simultaneously orchestrate inflammation and promote fibrosis.<sup>[[8]](#ref8)</sup> The disease was historically thought to result from a local autoimmune reaction to antigens within atherosclerotic aortic plaques, but the systemic features and autoimmune associations suggest a broader immune-mediated process.<sup>[[3]](#ref3)</sup>

---

## Clinical Presentation

- **Abdominal pain** and **low back pain** are the most common symptoms (65% and 42% in one 26-patient referral cohort).<sup>[[5]](#ref5)</sup>
- **Systemic symptoms** — fatigue, weight loss, malaise.<sup>[[6]](#ref6)</sup>
- Less common — constipation, testicular pain, lower-extremity claudication.<sup>[[6]](#ref6)</sup>
- **Ureteral obstruction in 60–80%**, often causing acute or chronic kidney injury.<sup>[[6]](#ref6)[[8]](#ref8)</sup>
- Other complications — **caval occlusion**, **deep vein thrombosis**, renal artery / vein stenosis, renal atrophy.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[2]](#ref2)</sup>
- Elevated acute-phase reactants (ESR, CRP) in **~ 75%** and renal function deterioration in **~ 40%** at diagnosis in that same cohort.<sup>[[5]](#ref5)</sup>

---

## Diagnosis

There are no standardized diagnostic criteria. Diagnosis combines clinical, laboratory, and imaging findings.<sup>[[3]](#ref3)</sup>

### Laboratory

- Elevated ESR and CRP (supportive but nonspecific).
- Serum **IgG4 levels** (elevated in IgG4-related RPF).<sup>[[7]](#ref7)</sup>
- Renal function assessment (creatinine, eGFR).

### Imaging

- **CT** — Most commonly used (~ 92% of patients in pooled retrospective series) and the imaging modality of choice along with MRI. RPF typically appears as a **homogeneous soft-tissue plaque, isodense with muscle**, surrounding the infrarenal aorta and iliac arteries, often enveloping the ureters and IVC. Contrast enhancement is variable depending on disease stage.<sup>[[5]](#ref5)[[10]](#ref10)[[3]](#ref3)</sup>
- **MRI** — Avoids iodinated CT contrast and offers soft-tissue characterization; contrast-enhanced MRI uses a gadolinium agent, so protocol selection still considers kidney function.<sup>[[3]](#ref3)</sup>
- **FDG-PET/CT** — Increasingly used for disease staging, monitoring treatment response, and detecting relapse. **Superior to CT alone in detecting disease progression**; metabolic active volume (MAV) at baseline may predict progression.<sup>[[11]](#ref11)[[12]](#ref12)</sup>
- **Key imaging distinction** — Idiopathic RPF classically does **not displace the aorta anteriorly** and causes **medial ureteral deviation**, whereas malignant infiltration tends to displace the aorta anteriorly and ureters laterally.<sup>[[3]](#ref3)</sup> This is a tendency, not a rule: in a 92-patient referral series, 18 of 47 patients with a peri-aortic mass and final diagnosis of RPF still had anterior aortic displacement, and 2 of 6 malignant masses did not. A characteristic non-displacing peri-aortic mass supports a benign diagnosis and can spare biopsy; atypical morphology or location does not.<sup>[[13]](#ref13)</sup>

### Biopsy

Retroperitoneal biopsy is reserved for **atypical cases** — atypical location (pelvic, peripancreatic), suspicion of malignancy, or when clinical / laboratory findings are inconclusive.<sup>[[3]](#ref3)[[14]](#ref14)</sup> **Laparoscopic or open biopsy is often more informative than needle biopsy** but is more invasive.<sup>[[15]](#ref15)</sup> Assess other IgG4-RD organ involvement and exclude secondary causes (drugs, malignancy, infection, radiation) before labeling RPF idiopathic.<sup>[[15]](#ref15)</sup> Histology shows abundant fibrosis with mononuclear inflammatory infiltrate (lymphocytes, plasma cells, macrophages).<sup>[[6]](#ref6)</sup>

## Treatment

Treatment aims to **relieve ureteral obstruction** and **induce disease regression**. Medical therapy is the cornerstone; surgical drainage / ureterolysis address mechanical obstruction.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[14]](#ref14)</sup>

### Surgical Drainage and Ureterolysis

Surgical pathway covered in full on the [Ureterolysis](/docs/surgical-techniques/04d-upper-tract-reconstruction/ureterolysis) technique page. Briefly:

- **Ureteral stenting or PCN** is the **first priority** for hydronephrosis — relieve obstruction and preserve renal function.<sup>[[15]](#ref15)[[18]](#ref18)</sup> In 30 patients with idiopathic RPF, stenting was successful at first attempt in 79%, and complication rates were comparable for stent and nephrostomy (17.9% vs 21%); the two are complementary, and many patients need both over time. Pyelonephritis occurred in 30%.<sup>[[18]](#ref18)</sup>
- **Surgical ureterolysis** (open / laparoscopic / robotic, with omental wrapping) for refractory cases: no radiographic response to medication, medication intolerance, persistent pain or stent symptoms.<sup>[[15]](#ref15)[[14]](#ref14)[[17]](#ref17)</sup> In a 50-patient open series from a high-volume multidisciplinary team, 94% were stent-free at 12 months; median GFR change at 1 year was +25% in the stent-failure group versus −10% (not significant) in the nephrostomy-dependent group, and Clavien III–IV complications occurred in 12%. The authors argue for considering ureterolysis early when obstruction does not respond quickly to standard treatment; this is a single-center uncontrolled series.<sup>[[28]](#ref28)</sup>
- Medical management with temporary stenting alone achieves resolution in **~ 69%** of patients (median stent duration 16 months); ~ 15% ultimately require ureterolysis, and 18% had recurrent obstruction after a stent-free period.<sup>[[17]](#ref17)</sup>

### Medical Therapy

- **Glucocorticoids** remain first-line — induce rapid symptom remission, reduction in acute-phase reactants, and often mass shrinkage. Typical induction: **prednisone 0.5–1 mg/kg/day** with gradual taper over **6–9 months**.<sup>[[6]](#ref6)[[8]](#ref8)[[12]](#ref12)</sup> In the Parma protocol, induction was prednisone 1 mg/kg/day for 1 month, then 0.5 mg/kg/day tapered over 8 months.<sup>[[6]](#ref6)</sup> Higher steroid dose at 1 year and longer courses were associated with fewer relapses in retrospective cohorts.<sup>[[21]](#ref21)[[22]](#ref22)</sup>
- **Prednisone vs tamoxifen** — The 2011 trial randomized 36 responders **after prednisone induction**; continued tapering prednisone prevented relapse better than switching to tamoxifen (relapse by month 8: 1/18 [6%] versus 7/18 [39%]; estimated 26-month cumulative relapse 17% versus 50%), at the cost of more Cushingoid features and hypercholesterolemia. This was a remission-maintenance comparison, and is no longer the only RPF randomized trial.<sup>[[6]](#ref6)</sup>
- **Combination therapy:** heterogeneous pooled response estimates (**98.9% vs 85.1%**, P = 0.08, 11 mostly observational studies, 224 patients) do not establish universal superiority of every immunosuppressant combination.<sup>[[19]](#ref19)</sup> Commonly used agents:
  - **Mycophenolate mofetil** — in a prospective 28-patient case series (prednisone 40 mg/day tapered over 6 months plus MMF 1000 mg twice daily for a mean 24 months), systemic symptoms resolved in all, 89% had ≥ 25% mass reduction, and disease recurred in 2 (7%).<sup>[[27]](#ref27)</sup> Uncontrolled.
  - **Methotrexate** and **azathioprine** — steroid-sparing agents.<sup>[[8]](#ref8)</sup> Methotrexate plus low-dose prednisone is the only steroid-sparing regimen tested in a randomized trial (2025 trial above).
  - **Tocilizumab** — a 12-patient open pilot of monotherapy in active chronic periaortitis reported 91% remission at 3 months; evidence is preliminary and includes non-RPF periaortitis.<sup>[[29]](#ref29)</sup>
  - **Cyclophosphamide** — reserved for severe or refractory disease because of toxicity; not first line.<sup>[[8]](#ref8)</sup>
- **Rituximab** (anti-CD20 B-cell depletion) — increasingly used for refractory or relapsing disease. In a retrospective series (n = 26; 19 IgG4-related, 7 idiopathic), all 19 with pain improved symptomatically, and **88% had radiologic improvement**; 73% received rituximab without additional glucocorticoids. Severe infections in 12%. Of 10 patients with stents or nephrostomies, 4 became tube-free.<sup>[[20]](#ref20)</sup>

### Secondary RPF

- **Drug-induced** — withdrawal of the offending agent often leads to resolution; steroids may still be needed.<sup>[[3]](#ref3)</sup>
- **Infection-related** — antimicrobial therapy; steroids generally **contraindicated**.<sup>[[3]](#ref3)</sup>
- **Malignancy-related** — chemotherapy / radiotherapy directed at the underlying neoplasm.<sup>[[3]](#ref3)</sup>

## Monitoring and Follow-Up

Serial assessment of symptoms, ESR/CRP, renal function, and imaging.<sup>[[3]](#ref3)</sup> CT and MRI track mass size; **FDG-PET is superior** for assessing metabolic activity and predicting progression — residual masses with absent FDG uptake likely represent inactive sclerotic tissue not requiring further treatment.<sup>[[11]](#ref11)[[3]](#ref3)</sup> **Positive FDG-PET at end of treatment independently predicts relapse (HR 3.47)**.<sup>[[12]](#ref12)</sup>

---

## Prognosis and Relapse

With appropriate treatment the overall prognosis is **good**, with patient survival 95% at 5 years, 84% at 10 years, 68% at 15 years.<sup>[[21]](#ref21)</sup> However, **relapse is a major challenge**:

- Up to **38–50%** of patients experience at least one relapse (38% in a 50-patient cohort followed a median 8.9 years); relapse rates were lower (18%) in a 155-patient cohort with shorter follow-up.<sup>[[8]](#ref8)[[21]](#ref21)[[22]](#ref22)</sup>
- Cumulative relapse incidence: **21% at 5 years, 41% at 10 years, 48% at 15 years**.<sup>[[21]](#ref21)</sup>

**Predictors of relapse**: male sex, antinuclear-antibody positivity, lower steroid dose at 1 year, higher baseline ESR, shorter glucocorticoid courses, thoracic vessel involvement, and **persistent FDG-PET activity after treatment**.<sup>[[21]](#ref21)[[12]](#ref12)[[22]](#ref22)</sup> **Smoking** and **atypical (for example pelvic) localization** predicted lower likelihood of remission rather than relapse; pre-treatment FDG-PET activity predicted remission.<sup>[[12]](#ref12)</sup> In one large cohort, 42% relapsed over a median of 33 months.<sup>[[12]](#ref12)</sup>

**Prompt relief of hydronephrosis is critical to prevent irreversible renal atrophy** — the reconstructive surgeon's central responsibility in the multidisciplinary RPF team.<sup>[[22]](#ref22)</sup>
