# Vesicouterine Fistula

A **vesicouterine fistula (VUF)** is an epithelialized communication between the bladder and the uterine cavity. It accounts for roughly 1–4% of reported GU fistulas, and up to 88% of reported VUFs are related to cesarean delivery; increasing cesarean rates raise concern about future VUF burden.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[2]](#ref2)[[3]](#ref3)[[19]](#ref19)</sup> An intact cervix can act as a one-way valve, so VUF may present with menouria and preserved continence rather than vaginal urine leakage; counseling should address closure, symptoms, uterine preservation and fertility goals when relevant.<sup>[[2]](#ref2)</sup>

For the operation that produces nearly every VUF, see [Cesarean Section](/docs/special-populations/05a-trauma-emergencies/intraoperative-consultation/procedures-causing-gu-injury/cesarean-section). For the broader fistula framework, see the [Fistulas landing page](/docs/clinical-conditions/03f-fistulas). For operative selection across all repair routes, see the [Female Fistula Repair database](/docs/surgical-techniques/04h-fistula-repair/female-fistula).

---

## Epidemiology

- VUF accounts for 1–4% of all urogenital fistulas in reported series.<sup>[[2]](#ref2)[[5]](#ref5)[[19]](#ref19)</sup>
- Historically rare before 1947 when low-segment cesarean replaced the classical incision; recognition has risen with cesarean rates.<sup>[[2]](#ref2)</sup>
- Reproductive-age population: mean age was 19 years among 12 patients with minimum follow-up in one Indian series, 27 in a Serbian series and 31 in another Indian series.<sup>[[6]](#ref6)[[7]](#ref7)[[8]](#ref8)</sup>

---

## Etiology and Pathogenesis

| Cause | Notes |
|---|---|
| **Cesarean section** | Up to 88% of reported VUFs are related to cesarean section. In a review restricted to post-cesarean surgical repairs, 78.6% of patients had &gt;1 prior cesarean; this case mix does not measure VUF risk per repeat operation<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[2]](#ref2)[[3]](#ref3)</sup> |
| **Uterine rupture / VBAC** | Less common; reported after vaginal birth after cesarean and after vaginal delivery (4 of 17 in one series)<sup>[[5]](#ref5)[[6]](#ref6)[[9]](#ref9)[[10]](#ref10)</sup> |
| **Placenta accreta spectrum** | Manual removal of morbidly adherent placenta during cesarean<sup>[[11]](#ref11)</sup> |
| **IUD erosion** | Rare; migration of a retained or forgotten device through the uterine wall into the bladder, sometimes with a vesical calculus and without classic Youssef symptoms<sup>[[29]](#ref29)</sup> |
| **Pelvic trauma, uterine curettage, radiation** | Rare<sup>[[2]](#ref2)[[4]](#ref4)</sup> |

**Mechanism after cesarean:**<sup>[[3]](#ref3)</sup>

- Inadequate reflection of bladder peritoneum from the lower uterine segment
- Unrecognized cystotomy at hysterotomy or closure
- Bladder wall caught in the uterine closure suture
- Approximately 10% of patients in a review of surgically repaired post-cesarean VUF had recognized intraoperative bladder injury or uterine rupture<sup>[[3]](#ref3)</sup>
- The supratrigonal bladder was involved in 92.5% of cases in the post-cesarean surgical-repair review<sup>[[3]](#ref3)</sup>

## Classification

### Youssef syndrome (1957)

Classic triad when the fistula sits above the internal cervical os and the cervix functions as a competent valve:<sup>[[7]](#ref7)[[14]](#ref14)[[15]](#ref15)</sup>

1. **Menouria:** cyclic hematuria with menstruation
2. **Amenorrhea:** no vaginal menstrual flow
3. **Preserved urinary continence:** no leakage per vagina

### Józwik classification (2000)

This descriptive classification uses the route of menstrual flow:<sup>[[16]](#ref16)</sup>

| Type | Menstrual route | Typical presentation |
|---|---|---|
| **I** | Bladder only | Classic Youssef syndrome — menouria, amenorrhea, continent |
| **II** | Bladder + vagina | Menouria with vaginal menses; vaginal urinary leakage may coexist<sup>[[20]](#ref20)</sup> |
| **III** | Vagina only | Normal vaginal menses without menouria; vaginal urinary leakage may occur<sup>[[20]](#ref20)</sup> |

The classification describes symptoms; it is not a validated rule for selecting hormonal treatment versus surgery.<sup>[[16]](#ref16)</sup>

## Clinical Presentation

The intact cervix changes the symptom profile fundamentally compared with VVF.<sup>[[6]](#ref6)[[7]](#ref7)[[17]](#ref17)</sup>

- **Menouria** is the most characteristic feature; present in 7 of 17 patients in one series<sup>[[6]](#ref6)</sup>
- **Urinary leakage per vagina:** reported in 11 of 17 patients (65%) in one retrospective surgical series<sup>[[6]](#ref6)</sup>
- **Amenorrhea:** when all menstrual flow diverts through the bladder
- **Recurrent UTI**
- **Secondary infertility:** diversion of menstrual flow and altered uterine environment
- **First-trimester miscarriage:** reported<sup>[[18]](#ref18)</sup>

**Timing:**

- **Early** (days to weeks post-cesarean): vaginal urine leakage
- **Delayed** (months to years, occasionally decades): menouria and amenorrhea; one patient presented 22 years after the index cesarean and underwent repair 26 years after it<sup>[[19]](#ref19)</sup>

**Pregnancy with a VUF:** a 2025 systematic review of case reports identified 22 intrauterine pregnancies with a VUF, all after prior cesarean: 14 fetal deaths, one surgically terminated pregnancy, and seven live births (five preterm, four before 32 weeks). Fetus in the bladder through the tract is a separate, very rare presentation.<sup>[[18]](#ref18)[[30]](#ref30)</sup>

## Diagnostic Evaluation

The EAU guideline recommends a complete history and focused physical examination with direct visual inspection for suspected urinary fistula (Strong).<sup>[[28]](#ref28)</sup> Because flow may be unidirectional, bladder-side contrast can be nondiagnostic; uterine-side contrast has been described to demonstrate the tract.<sup>[[20]](#ref20)[[21]](#ref21)</sup>

| Modality | Role | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| **Hysterosalpingography (HSG)** | First-line in many series | Contrast injected through the uterus opacifies the bladder<sup>[[2]](#ref2)</sup> |
| **Cystography** | Often false-negative | When positive, classic "**flower on top of the bladder**" appearance<sup>[[21]](#ref21)</sup> |
| **Cystoscopy** | Maps fistula location, relationship to ureteral orifices, presence of endometrial / granulation tissue at the site<sup>[[9]](#ref9)[[10]](#ref10)</sup> | |
| **Hysteroscopy** | Visualizes the fistula from the uterine side; methylene-blue confirmation<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[10]](#ref10)</sup> | |
| **MRI pelvis** | Tissue planes, fibrosis, fistula tract for surgical planning<sup>[[20]](#ref20)</sup> | |
| **Contrast-enhanced ultrasound (CEUS)** | Case reports describe intravesical or **intrauterine** contrast; an intrauterine route may demonstrate unidirectional flow, but this is not a required or established first-line test<sup>[[4]](#ref4)[[20]](#ref20)</sup> | |
| **CT urogram with late excretory phase** | Evaluates concurrent ureteral injury and can help map a difficult fistula<sup>[[28]](#ref28)</sup> | |

## Management

Selected small, recent VUFs may close with bladder drainage with or without menstrual suppression. The EAU 2026 guideline notes case reports of closure with induced amenorrhea (estrogen, estrogen-progestin or LHRH analogues) for small fistulae (&lt;7 mm) after cesarean; evidence is limited to case reports and small series, not comparative trials.<sup>[[22]](#ref22)[[23]](#ref23)[[28]](#ref28)</sup>

### Conservative — bladder drainage

- Indwelling Foley for 4–8 weeks
- Closure has been reported in small, early-recognized fistulas without significant fibrosis; one report suggests simple fistulas under 0.5 cm and surgery if unresolved after about 2 months<sup>[[24]](#ref24)[[13]](#ref13)[[12]](#ref12)</sup>
- Suprapubic drainage is an alternative
- Reassess persistent fistulas after a planned drainage trial; surgical timing depends on tissue condition and patient goals<sup>[[24]](#ref24)</sup>

### Conservative — induced amenorrhea

**Endometrial-like tissue with estrogen and progesterone receptors** has been demonstrated in a small histologic study, providing a rationale for menstrual suppression; this does not establish predictable closure.<sup>[[22]](#ref22)[[23]](#ref23)</sup>

- **GnRH agonists** (e.g., leuprolide) have been used in case reports; selection, duration and management of hypoestrogenic adverse effects require gynecologic input<sup>[[22]](#ref22)</sup>
- **Continuous combined oral contraceptive** as an alternative<sup>[[13]](#ref13)</sup>
- Consider only selected small fistulas with favorable tissue and symptoms; the Józwik type alone does not establish suitability
- Drainage and induced amenorrhea have each been reported in small series, sometimes in the same report<sup>[[13]](#ref13)</sup>

### Surgical repair — timing and approach

**Timing:** Delayed repair through laparotomy accounted for 88.8% of patients in the post-cesarean surgical-repair review.<sup>[[3]](#ref3)</sup> This describes published practice; it does not establish a mandatory three-month wait. Individualize timing after edema, inflammation, necrosis and infection have resolved.<sup>[[3]](#ref3)[[28]](#ref28)</sup>

**Approach selection:**

| Approach | Best fit | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| **Transabdominal (open)** | Established option for high or complex fistulas | Delayed laparotomy was used in 88.8% of patients in the post-cesarean review; midline or Pfannenstiel access and sagittal cystotomy describe a transvesical O'Conor variant<sup>[[3]](#ref3)[[6]](#ref6)[[8]](#ref8)</sup> |
| **Robotic-assisted** | Option at centers with appropriate expertise | Mirrors open technique; magnified visualization for the dense vesicouterine adhesion plane; a mixed VUF/vesicocervical series of three patients reported discharge on postoperative day 2; uterine-sparing repair has been described<sup>[[9]](#ref9)[[19]](#ref19)[[25]](#ref25)</sup> |
| **Laparoscopic** | Same indications as robotic | Demonstrated in case reports / small series; in the systematic review, minimally invasive repair had shorter stay and less blood loss<sup>[[10]](#ref10)[[3]](#ref3)</sup> |
| **Transvaginal** | Selected low fistulas in surgeons experienced with vaginal repair | 3 of 17 patients in one series successfully repaired vaginally; prone-jackknife position useful<sup>[[6]](#ref6)[[11]](#ref11)[[26]](#ref26)</sup> |

### Operative principles (every approach)

1. **Adhesiolysis** of the vesicouterine plane; adhesions at the cesarean scar are routinely dense
2. **Excision of the fistula tract** with debridement of fibrotic edges
3. **Separation of bladder from uterus** to allow non-overlapping suture lines
4. Double-layer bladder closure and single-layer uterine closure is the most-reported pattern in the systematic review; check bladder integrity intraoperatively after closure<sup>[[3]](#ref3)[[19]](#ref19)</sup>
5. **Tissue interposition** may separate suture lines when tissue quality or repair complexity warrants it; omentum or peritoneum are options. One small series had recurrence in 1/5 repairs without and 0/9 with a flap; this cannot establish a universal benefit<sup>[[3]](#ref3)[[8]](#ref8)</sup>
6. **Ureteral stents** selectively for identification where anatomy is uncertain; they do not guarantee protection from injury<sup>[[9]](#ref9)</sup>
7. **Prolonged catheter drainage** with cystogram before removal in selected cases

### Hysterectomy vs uterine conservation

- **Discuss uterine conservation and fertility goals**. Conservation was performed in 10/17 patients in one series, with all three patients desiring pregnancy conceiving spontaneously after repair; these are small selected denominators.<sup>[[6]](#ref6)[[19]](#ref19)[[25]](#ref25)</sup>
- **Hysterectomy** may be appropriate for extensive uterine destruction, concurrent uterine pathology or an informed patient preference; completed childbearing alone does not require hysterectomy.<sup>[[2]](#ref2)</sup>

## Outcomes

A review of 67 studies and 284 patients, mostly case reports or small observational series, reported very high surgical closure. Its reported 100% first-attempt success should not be treated as a patient-level guarantee or evidence that one approach is superior: selection, publication bias and inconsistent outcome reporting limit inference.<sup>[[3]](#ref3)</sup> Recurrence has been reported, including one in the 14-patient series.<sup>[[8]](#ref8)</sup>

### Fertility after repair

For patients who desire pregnancy, discuss the limited fertility evidence:<sup>[[3]](#ref3)[[6]](#ref6)[[8]](#ref8)[[27]](#ref27)</sup>

- **8 of 14** patients in one series became pregnant after repair and delivered by elective cesarean.<sup>[[8]](#ref8)</sup>
- **3 of 3** patients desiring pregnancy conceived spontaneously in another.<sup>[[6]](#ref6)</sup>
- In a Polish series of 16 women, 5 pregnancies occurred after repair (31%), 4 reaching term (25%); these are not validated probabilities of conception among patients actively attempting pregnancy.<sup>[[27]](#ref27)</sup>
- **23 patients with a reported live birth after repair** in the systematic-review cohort; this is not a fertility rate among patients attempting conception.<sup>[[3]](#ref3)</sup>
- Subsequent pregnancy warrants specialist obstetric planning; published series commonly used planned cesarean delivery. The uterine scar, repair details and obstetric circumstances guide timing and delivery counseling.<sup>[[6]](#ref6)[[8]](#ref8)</sup>
- Secondary infertility may be the presenting complaint; pregnancy after repair is possible, but recovery of fertility is not assured.<sup>[[17]](#ref17)</sup>

## Prevention

Prevention is operative-technique driven at the index cesarean:<sup>[[3]](#ref3)</sup>

- **Identify the bladder and vesicouterine plane** before hysterotomy, especially when adhesions obscure anatomy; mobilize the bladder as needed for safe exposure.<sup>[[3]](#ref3)</sup> Randomized trials have not established routine bladder-flap creation as protective against bladder injury; VUF prevention was not studied.<sup>[[31]](#ref31)</sup>
- **Avoid deep suture bites** at hysterotomy closure that may incorporate the bladder wall
- **Confirm the bladder dome is clear** before placement of every closure suture
- **Recognize and immediately repair** any cystotomy on the table
- Counsel about bladder-injury risk associated with repeat cesarean, adhesions, second-stage labor and emergency surgery; the post-cesarean VUF repair review does not estimate VUF risk per operation.<sup>[[3]](#ref3)[[32]](#ref32)</sup>

## VUF vs VVF — quick comparison

| Feature | VUF | VVF |
|---|---|---|
| **Frequency** | 1–4% of reported GU fistulas; 2.6% in one tertiary referral cohort<sup>[[19]](#ref19)[[33]](#ref33)</sup> | 79.7% in the same tertiary referral cohort<sup>[[33]](#ref33)</sup> |
| **Primary etiology** | Cesarean section | Hysterectomy (HIC); obstructed labor (LMIC) |
| **Hallmark symptom** | Menouria; often continent | Continuous urine leakage per vagina |
| **Conservative management** | Selected small, recent fistulas; hormonal evidence mainly case reports | Selected small, recent fistulas may close with drainage |
| **Fertility implications** | Discuss uterine preservation and pregnancy goals | Relevant, particularly after obstetric injury; assess associated reproductive tract damage |
| **Closure outcomes** | High in selected reports; uncertain generalizability<sup>[[3]](#ref3)</sup> | Depend on size, tissue quality, etiology and prior repairs |
