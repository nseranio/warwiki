# Obstetric Fistula

**Obstetric fistula** is the urogenital and/or rectovaginal fistula produced by prolonged obstructed labor without timely access to emergency obstetric care. It is the global paradigm of catastrophic obstetric injury, rare in high-income countries but persistent in sub-Saharan Africa, South Asia, and conflict-affected regions, with widely quoted (WHO/UNFPA-attributed) estimates of more than 2 million women living with untreated fistula and 50,000–100,000 new cases per year.<sup>[[3]](#ref3)[[38]](#ref38)</sup> These are imprecise historical figures: Wall estimated at least 3 million unrepaired cases and 30,000–130,000 new cases per year in Africa alone, while the pooled population-based data below suggest lower prevalence.<sup>[[3]](#ref3)[[38]](#ref38)</sup> The clinical character is fundamentally different from iatrogenic VVF: this is a massive ischemic field injury that destroys soft tissue across the maternal pelvis, often involves the urethra, and clusters with multisystem injuries known collectively as the obstructed labour injury complex.<sup>[[3]](#ref3)</sup>

For the iatrogenic counterpart and the broader operative principles, see [Vesicovaginal Fistula](/docs/clinical-conditions/03f-fistulas/in-females/vesicovaginal) and the [Fistulas section landing](/docs/clinical-conditions/03f-fistulas). For operative selection across all repair routes, see the [Female Fistula Repair database](/docs/surgical-techniques/04h-fistula-repair/female-fistula).

## Epidemiology

- **Prevalence in the 2013 review.** Ten community studies (34,505 women) in sampled low- and middle-income settings yielded 0.29 per 1,000 women of reproductive age (95% CI 0–1.07). Estimates of 1.60 per 1,000 in sub-Saharan Africa and 1.20 per 1,000 in South Asia came from two studies each; the authors judged prevalence lower than previously reported but cautioned against generalizing these regional estimates.<sup>[[38]](#ref38)</sup>
- **Incidence**: approximately 0.09 per 1,000 recently pregnant women in three community-based studies (95% CI 0.01–0.25).<sup>[[38]](#ref38)</sup>
- **Burden.** Historical estimates of 2–3 million women living with untreated fistula are imprecise, and prevalence remains uncertain. A 2025 survey-based model corrected for clinical misclassification estimated 457,000 current cases (95% CI 303,000–709,000) among women aged 15–64 in 55 Campaign to End Fistula countries.<sup>[[47]](#ref47)</sup> The treatment gap remains large relative to the skilled-surgeon workforce.<sup>[[3]](#ref3)[[5]](#ref5)[[47]](#ref47)</sup>
- **Age.** Most patients are &lt; 25 years; many are 13–14. Bimodal distribution with peaks in primigravid women and women with ≥ 4 pregnancies.<sup>[[3]](#ref3)</sup>
- **Time to treatment.** In the Fistula Foundation series, women reported an average 5.7 years living with fistula before their current surgery (which could be a repeat repair); 4% of 19,238 women with referral data sought care independently.<sup>[[6]](#ref6)</sup>

## Pathogenesis — The Obstructed Labour Injury Complex

The mechanism is field ischemia, not point injury:<sup>[[3]](#ref3)</sup>

1. The fetal head impacts in the maternal pelvis under sustained uterine contractions
2. Soft tissues of the vagina, bladder, and rectum are crushed against the maternal pelvic bones
3. Perfusion to the compressed tissues is progressively shut off
4. Widespread ischemic necrosis develops across the pelvis
5. In a retrospective series of 4,396 women presenting for obstetric fistula repair after facility delivery in nine East/Central African countries, 3,695 (84.1%) reported stillbirth in the index pregnancy.<sup>[[50]](#ref50)</sup>
6. After 1–2 days, the macerated fetus is expelled vaginally
7. Several days later, the necrotic vesicovaginal (and often rectovaginal) tissue sloughs, leaving a fistula

The fistula sits within a much wider zone of sublethal ischemic injury that becomes dense fibrous scar; this is the principal reason obstetric fistula is harder to repair than iatrogenic VVF and the reason recurrence rates are higher.

### Multisystem injuries clustered with the fistula

| System | Injuries |
|---|---|
| **Urological** | VVF; urethrovaginal, vesicocervical, ureterovaginal, complete urethral destruction; stress incontinence; bladder stones; hydroureteronephrosis; chronic pyelonephritis; renal failure |
| **Gynecologic** | Severe vaginal stenosis (sometimes near-obliteration); cervical destruction; secondary amenorrhea; secondary infertility; PID |
| **Gastrointestinal** | Rectovaginal fistula; rectal stenosis or atresia; anal sphincter incompetence |
| **Musculoskeletal** | Osteitis pubis |
| **Neurologic** | Foot-drop (lumbosacral plexus or common peroneal nerve compression); neuropathic bladder |
| **Dermatologic** | Chronic excoriation from urine and fecal maceration |
| **Social** | Stigma, divorce, abandonment, displacement, malnutrition, depression, suicide |

## Risk Factors

A complex interplay of biological, social, and economic forces.<sup>[[2]](#ref2)[[3]](#ref3)[[7]](#ref7)[[8]](#ref8)</sup>

### Biological / obstetric

| Factor | Effect |
|---|---|
| Prolonged labor (&gt; 24 h) | AOR 4.0<sup>[[8]](#ref8)</sup> |
| Cephalopelvic disproportion | Most common direct cause (~65% of obstructed labor)<sup>[[9]](#ref9)</sup> |
| Short maternal stature (≤ 150 cm) | AOR 2.63<sup>[[2]](#ref2)</sup> |
| Birth weight ≥ 3.5 kg (study-specific threshold, not a universal definition of macrosomia) | AOR 1.52<sup>[[2]](#ref2)</sup> |
| Primiparity | Highest risk in young primigravidas<sup>[[3]](#ref3)</sup> |
| Post-term pregnancy | AOR 8.0<sup>[[8]](#ref8)</sup> |
| Malpresentation | ~31% of obstructed labor cases<sup>[[9]](#ref9)</sup> |

### Socioeconomic

- **Poverty** is the fundamental enabling condition<sup>[[3]](#ref3)</sup>
- Early marriage and childbearing (age &lt; 18 at first pregnancy)
- Low or no education; post-primary education is protective (AOR 0.31)<sup>[[2]](#ref2)</sup>
- Rural residence (AOR 4.0)<sup>[[8]](#ref8)</sup>; lack of antenatal care (AOR 5.0); no modern contraception (AOR 5.0)
- **Lack of access to emergency obstetric services** (the single most important factor)<sup>[[3]](#ref3)</sup>
- Adolescent malnutrition and stunted pelvic growth

### Iatrogenic contribution

In some modern series cesarean section itself contributes to fistula. In one Ugandan cohort, approximately 25% of fistulas were iatrogenic injuries during cesarean delivery (adjusted OR 13.30 for cesarean delivery in that case-control study). This association does not show that timely, appropriately performed cesarean delivery causes fistula; labor severity and surgical injury must be distinguished.<sup>[[2]](#ref2)</sup>

## Classification

No single system is universally accepted; three are in active use, with Goh carrying the strongest predictive validity.<sup>[[10]](#ref10)[[11]](#ref11)[[12]](#ref12)</sup>

### Waaldijk (1995)

| Type | Definition |
|---|---|
| **I** | Fistula not involving the urethral closing mechanism |
| **II** | Fistula involving the urethral closing mechanism — IIA without (sub)total urethral involvement; IIB with (sub)total urethral involvement; further (a) without and (b) with circumferential defect |
| **III** | Ureteral and other exceptional fistulas |

Surgical complexity worsens progressively from I to IIBb.<sup>[[11]](#ref11)</sup>

### Goh

Goh classification records distance from the external urethral meatus (Types 1–4), fistula size (a/b/c), and fibrosis or vaginal capacity (i/ii); category iii denotes special circumstances such as circumferential or ureteric involvement.<sup>[[46]](#ref46)</sup> In a head-to-head comparison, Goh outperformed Waaldijk for predicting closure (p = 0.04), and Goh Type 4 fistulas were significantly less likely to close than Types 1 or 2 (p = 0.014).<sup>[[10]](#ref10)</sup>

In Goh and colleagues' original validation cohort of 987 obstetric fistula repairs, repair breakdown and residual urinary incontinence after closure both rose with each axis:<sup>[[42]](#ref42)</sup>

| Axis | Category | Repair breakdown | Incontinence after closure |
|---|---|---|---|
| Site | Type 1 | 2% | 3% |
| Site | Type 2 | 1.6% | 20% |
| Site | Type 3 | 3% | 32% |
| Site | Type 4 | 3% | 46% |
| Size | a | 2% | 10% |
| Size | b | 0.5% | 22% |
| Size | c | 4% | 30% |
| Scarring | i | 2% | 7% |
| Scarring | ii | 2% | 24% |
| Special circumstances | iii | 4% | 41% |

Before discharge, 27 of 987 repairs (2.7%) had not closed; among the 960 women with closure, 229 (23.9%) reported residual urinary incontinence. The study did not report a denominator for each subgroup, so these historical rates describe the cohort rather than predict an individual patient's risk.<sup>[[42]](#ref42)</sup>

### Panzi score

A parsimonious data-driven score derived from Goh and Waaldijk that distills three predictors of failure: circumferential defect, proximity to the external urethral orifice, and size. Each one-point increase in the 0–3 score multiplies the odds of surgical failure by approximately 1.65.<sup>[[12]](#ref12)</sup>

### Prognostic factors regardless of system

Degree of scarring at the operative field, urethral continence-mechanism involvement, fistula size and bladder tissue loss, and concurrent injuries (especially RVF).<sup>[[3]](#ref3)</sup>

## Clinical Presentation

- **Continuous urinary leakage per vagina.** The hallmark; begins approximately 3–10 days postpartum as the necrotic slough separates<sup>[[3]](#ref3)</sup>
- **Concurrent fecal incontinence** when RVF is present
- **Foul odor** from chronic soiling
- **Severe perineal and inner-thigh skin excoriation**
- **Amenorrhea / secondary infertility**
- **Foot-drop** from intrapartum nerve compression
- **Severe vaginal stenosis** with sometimes near-total obliteration
- **Stillbirth** of the index pregnancy in the vast majority

---

## Diagnostic Evaluation

Diagnosis in low-resource settings is overwhelmingly clinical:

- **Speculum exam.** Direct visualization
- **Dye test and cystoscopy.** Methylene blue instilled into the bladder can demonstrate vaginal leakage on gauze; the 2026 EAU guideline weakly recommends cystoscopy with retrograde colored-fluid filling to confirm urinary fistula when available.<sup>[[34]](#ref34)</sup>
- **Systematic mapping.** Fistula location, size, scarring, urethral involvement, circumferential defect (prognostic finding), concurrent RVF, vaginal canal patency, sphincter integrity
- **Imaging** when available: CT urogram or IVP for the upper tracts (rule out ureteral involvement); MRI in complex repairs

## Management

### Conservative

If a patient presents within approximately 3 months of injury, continuous bladder drainage with an indwelling catheter can allow closure of a small fistula (&lt; 1 cm) in selected cases.<sup>[[3]](#ref3)</sup> In a prospective immediate-management series of 1,716 women presenting 3–75 days postpartum, 264 (15.4%) healed with catheter treatment alone.<sup>[[48]](#ref48)</sup> The EAU guideline’s approximately 13% spontaneous-closure estimate concerns VVF across etiologies, largely small defects.<sup>[[34]](#ref34)</sup> In practice, the great majority of patients present months to years after injury and require operative repair.

### Surgical repair

The first operation offers the best chance of success; every subsequent attempt is harder and less likely to close.<sup>[[3]](#ref3)[[13]](#ref13)</sup>

**Timing.** Repair when tissue necrosis, edema, inflammation and infection have resolved and the patient is nutritionally and medically ready; a fixed three-month wait is not mandatory. Evidence comparing early and delayed repair is limited to small uncontrolled series, which show no difference in success between repair within four weeks and after three months.<sup>[[34]](#ref34)</sup>

**Approach.** Most accessible obstetric fistulas can be repaired transvaginally. Choose the route by exposure, ureteric involvement, tissue quality and surgeon expertise; comparative evidence does not establish one superior route across patients. The 2026 Cochrane item is a review protocol, not a completed comparative review.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[34]](#ref34)</sup> Two main vaginal techniques:

- **Latzko partial colpocleisis.** Imbrication without tract excision in selected accessible fistulas; consider the risk of shortening an already scarred or short vagina.
- **Layered flap-splitting repair.** Mobilize viable bladder and vaginal tissues and close without tension. Routine wide excision of the fistula edge is not required and can enlarge the defect.<sup>[[34]](#ref34)</sup>

**Transabdominal repair** is reserved for: ureteric orifices that need reimplantation, very high or surgically inaccessible fistulas, and the rare case requiring concurrent bladder augmentation.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup>

### Operative principles

- Mobilize sufficiently for tension-free closure while preserving viable tissue and blood supply; avoid unnecessary scar or edge excision.
- Edges must coapt without tension
- The repair should be watertight at the time of closure
- **Multi-layered, non-overlapping** suture lines
- **Circumferential defect** (bladder completely detached from the residual urethra). Mobilizing the bladder off the vagina and surrounding tissue and extending the dissection behind the pubic symphysis, high into the space of Retzius, allows the bladder to be advanced as a unit and anastomosed to the remaining urethra; this approach has been described for this rare, severe injury pattern.<sup>[[43]](#ref43)</sup>
- Continuous drainage until healing is confirmed. WHO recommends 7–10 days for simple obstetric fistulas. In the WHO-supported multicountry RCT, 524 women were randomized after a simple repair remained closed on day 7; multiple, radiation-related and cancer-related fistulas were excluded. In the per-protocol analysis, repair breakdown between 8 days after catheter removal and three months after surgery occurred in 10/250 (4%) after 7 days of catheterization versus 8/251 (3%) after 14 days, meeting the prespecified 10% noninferiority margin.<sup>[[39]](#ref39)</sup> The 2026 IUGA/ICS Delphi consensus sets a minimum of 10–14 days of continuous drainage after obstetric VVF repair (statement 37), noting that 7 days may suffice for simple cases; complex repairs need individualized longer drainage. These recommendations have different scopes and evidence bases.<sup>[[35]](#ref35)[[36]](#ref36)[[39]](#ref39)</sup> Once the patient begins to mobilize, securing the indwelling catheter with tape or a suture keeps the balloon from pulling on the fresh repair.<sup>[[43]](#ref43)</sup>
- **Tissue interposition** (Martius, gracilis, omental flap) for large defects, urethral involvement, recurrent fistula, or radiation; the [Browning data](/docs/clinical-conditions/03f-fistulas/in-females/vesicovaginal#tissue-interposition) suggest interposition is not a default for routine obstetric VVF<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[14]](#ref14)</sup>

### Adjunctive innovations

In a 16-year retrospective single-team series of 1,185 female genital fistula repairs across 12 countries (59% obstetric, 34% iatrogenic), adjuncts including platelet-rich plasma, small intestinal submucosa, fibrin glue, and buccal mucosa grafts were used in 71% of complex or recurrent repairs. Closure in that subgroup was 72%; the series does not isolate an effect of the adjuncts.<sup>[[14]](#ref14)</sup>

## Outcomes

**Record closure, continence and follow-up separately.** The 2026 IUGA/ICS consensus defines surgical success as anatomical closure without residual urinary incontinence (statement 3), recommends a dye test at catheter removal 10–14 days after surgery (statement 2), and further evaluation for persistent leakage. A fistula that breaks down within 3–6 months is counted as a failed repair (statement 15, near consensus). Large size (&gt; 3 cm), multiple fistulas, scarring, urethral involvement, proximity to the ureteric orifices and bladder capacity &lt; 100 mL predict poorer closure with continence (statement 14). “Incurable” should only be considered after three adequately performed failed repairs plus independent assessments by two expert surgeons; it is not an automatic label after the third operation.<sup>[[36]](#ref36)</sup>

The cohorts below use different endpoints and include different proportions of primary and repeat repairs; they are not a comparative ranking.

| Source | N | Reported outcome |
|---|---|---|
| **Fistula Foundation** (2019–2021, 110 hospitals, 27 countries) | 24,568 fistula and related obstetric-injury procedures, including perineal-tear repairs | **87%** assessed dry/continent at discharge across fistula and perineal-tear surgeries<sup>[[6]](#ref6)</sup> |
| **Systematic review / meta-analysis (LMICs)** | 79 studies included; 76 in pooled estimate | **77.9%** pooled reported repair success (closure and urinary and/or fecal continence considered; I² = 94.1%)<sup>[[13]](#ref13)</sup> |
| **Hilton & Ward large series** | 2,484 total; 2,360 operated | **82.8%** apparently dry after one operation (81.2% corrected first-procedure cure); 65% cure per procedure among patients requiring ≥ 2 operations<sup>[[3]](#ref3)[[49]](#ref49)</sup> |
| **Mekelle Hamlin Center, Ethiopia** | 328 | **89.3%** (VVF 86.9%; RVF 100%)<sup>[[15]](#ref15)</sup> |
| **Multinational single-team** | 1,185 | **82%** overall closure; 91% simple repairs; 85% primary repairs<sup>[[14]](#ref14)</sup> |

**Predictors of successful closure:** primary education or above, married status, alive neonatal outcome, primary (first) repair.<sup>[[13]](#ref13)</sup>

**Predictors of failure:** female genital cutting, primiparity, large size, Goh ≥ Type II, urethral involvement, vaginal scarring, circumferential defect, multiple fistulas, prior repair, postoperative complications.<sup>[[13]](#ref13)</sup>

## Post-Obstetric-Fistula-Repair Incontinence (POFRI)

A major and underappreciated problem is that anatomic closure does not equal continence.<sup>[[16]](#ref16)[[17]](#ref17)[[18]](#ref18)[[19]](#ref19)</sup> The IUGA/ICS consensus estimates that this "continence gap" affects 15% to 20% of women after repair, with de novo storage symptoms and stress incontinence both contributing.<sup>[[36]](#ref36)</sup>

- **Prevalence** of persistent incontinence after successful closure: 16–55%<sup>[[16]](#ref16)[[17]](#ref17)</sup>
- **Type.** Stress, urgency, mixed and overflow incontinence all require consideration. Selected Ugandan and Ethiopian cohorts report symptoms and urodynamic findings; OAB may occur in other women despite absent detrusor overactivity in one cohort.<sup>[[16]](#ref16)[[19]](#ref19)[[36]](#ref36)</sup>
- **Severity in a selected Ugandan cohort.** Among 36 women with persistent incontinence after closure, 53% rated leakage "very severe" and 86% had &gt; 4 g gain on a two-hour pad test.<sup>[[16]](#ref16)</sup>
- **Risk factors.** Fistula in first delivery, younger age at fistula, ≥ 2 prior fistula surgeries, Goh Type 3 or 4.<sup>[[16]](#ref16)[[17]](#ref17)</sup>

**FIGO 2025 expert opinion on POFRI management:**<sup>[[18]](#ref18)</sup>

- Standardized assessment: residual urine, voiding diary, pad test, urodynamics or single-channel cystometry where available. The IUGA/ICS consensus lists history, examination, post-void residual, cough test, bladder diary and simple urodynamics as the evaluation (statement 11) and calls simple urodynamics necessary for residual incontinence after successful closure (statement 40); it did not reach consensus that urodynamics separates a closed but severely incontinent woman from one whose fistula cannot be closed (statement 10).<sup>[[36]](#ref36)</sup>
- **Stress incontinence.** Offer pelvic floor muscle training when feasible; consider individualized surgery only after confirming closure, adequate emptying and bladder function. Counsel about retention and repeat-fistula risk. The IUGA/ICS consensus endorses pelvic floor muscle training as the preferred conservative treatment (statement 23), and it did not reach consensus on a rectus fascial sling as the preferred operation (statement 24).<sup>[[36]](#ref36)</sup>
- **Pure overactive bladder.** Conservative therapy (anticholinergics). The IUGA/ICS consensus lists bladder training, pelvic floor muscle training and antimuscarinics or β3-agonists as beneficial for urgency incontinence (statement 13), and reached no consensus on intravesical botulinum toxin as a last resort (statement 26).<sup>[[36]](#ref36)</sup>
- **Mixed (OAB-predominant).** Careful preoperative consideration
- **Symptomatic bridge.** A pilot study describes a urethral plug as a short-term aid to keep women with severe post-repair leakage dry while they return for follow-up and possible secondary continence surgery, with minimal complications in a small sample; longer follow-up in larger numbers is needed.<sup>[[44]](#ref44)</sup> In a separate 6-month follow-up study at an Ethiopian fistula center, 50% of returning patients with mild urethral incontinence were cured after conservative therapy and 18% of those with more severe incontinence improved; the report does not give the number of women in each severity group, and these are conservative-therapy outcomes, not plug outcomes.<sup>[[45]](#ref45)</sup>
- In the multinational series, residual incontinence after successful closure affected 12% of patients; among women receiving salvage treatment, 72% improved with bulking agents and 91% with pubovaginal slings.<sup>[[14]](#ref14)</sup>

### Pregnancy and delivery after repair

Women who conceive after repair should be counseled that vaginal delivery and emergency cesarean were associated with stillbirth, fistula recurrence and maternal death in a scoping review of 16 sub-Saharan studies; among 459 postrepair deliveries with mode reported, 45% were elective cesareans and 38% were emergency cesareans, and precise risks remain uncertain.<sup>[[40]](#ref40)</sup> Planned cesarean delivery at a facility able to manage complications is the commonly advised approach.<sup>[[40]](#ref40)[[41]](#ref41)</sup>

## Psychosocial Impact and Reintegration

The injury is profoundly social as well as physical.<sup>[[20]](#ref20)[[21]](#ref21)[[22]](#ref22)[[23]](#ref23)</sup>

- **Depression.** In a Mali repair-program cohort, 36.5% of 167 women screened at intake had moderate or severe depression, compared with 16.9% of 130 screened three months after discharge.<sup>[[20]](#ref20)</sup>
- **Coping behaviors.** Restricting fluids, multilayer clothing, self-isolation, allowing the husband to remarry, alcohol use, spiritual seeking<sup>[[21]](#ref21)</sup>
- **Recovery trajectory.** Most physical and psychosocial improvement occurs in the first 6 months after repair; by 12 months, urinary incontinence falls from 98% to 33%, while self-rated good, very good or excellent health rises from 0% at baseline to 60% at 3 months and about 69% at 12 months<sup>[[23]](#ref23)</sup>
- **Mental-health intervention.** The COFFEE group CBT program (CBT with Obstetric Fistula for Education and Empowerment) was studied in an uncontrolled pilot of 24 postsurgical women; depression, anxiety, and traumatic stress scores declined significantly from pretreatment to discharge, but the program’s separate effect cannot be determined.<sup>[[24]](#ref24)</sup>
- **Outcome measurement.** The IUGA/ICS consensus treats improvement in quality of life as part of clinical success (statements 1 and 4) and advises assessing it 3–12 months after repair (statement 41) and sexual function with a validated questionnaire at 6–12 months (statement 19). It rated PGI-I, I-QOL, UDI and ICIQ-UI SF as suitable (statements 28, 30, 31, 44) and reached no consensus on KHQ, PISQ-12, SF-36 or EQ-5D (statements 29, 32, 33, 34). It calls for a specialized, validated short fistula questionnaire (statement 43).<sup>[[36]](#ref36)</sup>
- **Reintegration programming.** Health education, physical therapy, social support, psychosocial counseling, and economic empowerment improve both physical and psychosocial outcomes and should be standard adjunct care<sup>[[25]](#ref25)</sup>

## Prevention — The Three Delays

Prevention requires addressing the Three Delays Model.<sup>[[26]](#ref26)[[27]](#ref27)[[28]](#ref28)</sup>

1. **Delay in deciding to seek care.** Failure to recognize prolonged labor; competing care pathways (traditional birth attendants); lack of women's autonomy; fear of hospitals; cost
2. **Delay in reaching care.** Geographic and transport barriers; distance of 10–50 km from the hospital was associated with obstructed labour (AOR 3.89) in an Ethiopian case-control study<sup>[[29]](#ref29)</sup>
3. **Delay in receiving care once present.** Inadequate emergency obstetric services, shortage of skilled providers and gaps in labor monitoring. A survey of partograph use identifies implementation barriers; it does not establish a causal fistula-prevention effect.<sup>[[30]](#ref30)</sup>

**Effective interventions:** skilled labor monitoring with timely escalation, timely cesarean section for obstructed labor, skilled birth attendance, family planning, delaying age of first pregnancy, education for girls, community economic development, and gender-equity programming.<sup>[[3]](#ref3)[[26]](#ref26)[[31]](#ref31)</sup> The WHO Labour Care Guide replaces the traditional WHO partograph; the 2025 implementation package supports its adoption rather than universal reliance on the older 1 cm/hour rule.<sup>[[37]](#ref37)</sup>
