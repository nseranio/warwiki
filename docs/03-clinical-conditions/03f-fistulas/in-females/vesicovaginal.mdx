# Vesicovaginal Fistula

A **vesicovaginal fistula (VVF)** is an epithelialized communication between the bladder and vagina that produces continuous, painless urinary leakage per vaginum. In high-income countries it is overwhelmingly iatrogenic, most commonly after benign gynecologic surgery, while in low-resource settings it is dominated by prolonged obstructed labor and remains a global public-health problem.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[2]](#ref2)[[3]](#ref3)</sup> VVF is the prototype reconstructive fistula: the principles refined here (anatomic definition, tension-free watertight multilayer closure with non-overlapping suture lines, selective fistula-edge management, selective vascularized tissue interposition, and prolonged drainage) generalize across the entire fistula chapter.<sup>[[26]](#ref26)[[27]](#ref27)[[40]](#ref40)</sup>

For the operative settings that produce VVF, see [Cesarean Section](/docs/special-populations/05a-trauma-emergencies/intraoperative-consultation/procedures-causing-gu-injury/cesarean-section), [Hysterectomy](/docs/special-populations/05a-trauma-emergencies/intraoperative-consultation/procedures-causing-gu-injury/hysterectomy), [Radical Hysterectomy and Pelvic Lymphadenectomy](/docs/special-populations/05a-trauma-emergencies/intraoperative-consultation/procedures-causing-gu-injury/radical-hysterectomy-pelvic-lymphadenectomy) and [Intraoperative Consultation](/docs/special-populations/05a-trauma-emergencies/intraoperative-consultation). Cross-compartment and complex-fistula principles are kept on the [Fistulas section landing](/docs/clinical-conditions/03f-fistulas). For operative selection across all repair routes, see the [Female Fistula Repair database](/docs/surgical-techniques/04h-fistula-repair/female-fistula).

## Epidemiology and Etiology

VVF is the most common acquired GU fistula in women.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[4]](#ref4)</sup>

| Etiology | Setting | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| **Benign gynecologic surgery** | Hysterectomy is the dominant cause in high-income countries; hysterectomy for benign disease accounts for roughly 60–75% of post-surgical VVF and cesarean about 6%<sup>[[39]](#ref39)</sup> | In English NHS data the overall vesicovaginal or urethrovaginal fistula rate within 1 year was about 1 in 788 (0.13%), ranging from 1 in 3,861 after vaginal hysterectomy for prolapse to 1 in 87 (~1.1%) after radical hysterectomy for cervical cancer.<sup>[[5]](#ref5)</sup> EAU quotes 0.1–4% across hysterectomy types and about 9-fold higher risk after radical than simple hysterectomy.<sup>[[39]](#ref39)</sup> Randomized trials show more urinary tract (mainly ureteric) injury with laparoscopic than abdominal hysterectomy (OR 2.16); fistula rates by route are not established in trials<sup>[[43]](#ref43)</sup> |
| **Cesarean delivery** | Uterine incision into the bladder, bladder-flap injury, **placenta accreta spectrum** | Often vesicouterine rather than vesicovaginal; see [Vesicouterine Fistula](/docs/clinical-conditions/03f-fistulas/in-females/vesicouterine) |
| **Obstetric — prolonged obstructed labor** | Dominant cause in sub-Saharan Africa and South Asia | Pressure necrosis of the anterior vaginal wall and bladder against the symphysis; addressed in detail in [Obstetric Fistula](/docs/clinical-conditions/03f-fistulas/in-females/obstetric) |
| **Pelvic radiation** | Cervical, endometrial, vaginal, rectal cancer treatment | Late presentation (months to years); poor tissue quality; high recurrence; strongly favors vascularized tissue interposition or diversion discussion. Fistula after primary radiotherapy may signal tumor recurrence. EAU reports fistula proportions of about 1.9% after postoperative external-beam radiation and 0.8% after intravaginal brachytherapy; tumor, stage, radiation-site and dose heterogeneity limits direct comparison.<sup>[[6]](#ref6)[[39]](#ref39)</sup> |
| **Pelvic malignancy** | Direct invasion or post-treatment necrosis | Workup must rule out recurrence before reconstruction |
| **Mesh erosion** | Synthetic midurethral sling or transvaginal mesh erosion through bladder | Mesh excision concurrent with repair |
| **Trauma** | Penetrating, foreign body, sexual violence | Rare in high-income settings |

### Risk factors for VVF after incidental cystotomy

Two academic-center case-control series of incidental cystotomy during benign hysterectomy identify a consistent dominant predictor. A higher-grade cystotomy (AAST grade V bladder injury) carried the strongest association with subsequent VVF in both: grade V injury was present in 75% of the four VVF cases versus 7% of 30 cystotomies without VVF in a 1,317-hysterectomy single-center cohort (p = 0.004), and grade V injury carried an OR of 93.0 (95% CI 10.3–838.9) in a 5,698-hysterectomy multicenter cohort.<sup>[[48]](#ref48)[[49]](#ref49)</sup> In the multicenter series, larger uterine weight (&gt;250 g, 83% vs 36%, p = 0.03) and longer operative time (317 vs 208 minutes, p = 0.02) were also associated with VVF formation; tobacco use and greater blood loss trended toward association without reaching significance in the smaller series.<sup>[[48]](#ref48)[[49]](#ref49)</sup> A Swedish national registry found hysterectomy increased the risk of pelvic-organ fistula surgery roughly fourfold overall (23.8 versus 6.3 per 100,000 person-years), with the highest fistula rates after laparoscopic hysterectomy, the lowest after subtotal abdominal hysterectomy, and higher rates among older women.<sup>[[50]](#ref50)</sup>

## Pathophysiology

Three injury mechanisms drive VVF formation, and they predict tissue quality at the time of repair:<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[2]](#ref2)</sup>

1. **Direct surgical injury.** Unrecognized cystotomy at the time of hysterectomy or cesarean, or a suture placed through bladder wall during cuff closure. Tissue is otherwise healthy; fistula declares itself 1–3 weeks postoperatively as the suture line breaks down.
2. **Ischemic.** Devascularization of the bladder base from electrothermal injury, hemostatic suture, or compressive packing. Presents at 2–6 weeks; surrounding tissue may be inflamed but generally heals well after excision.
3. **Pressure necrosis.** Sustained compression of the vesicovaginal septum between the fetal head and the symphysis pubis (obstetric) or by retained foreign body. Tissue loss is broader than the visible defect; calcification, fibrosis, and field damage extend well beyond the fistula edge.

Radiation VVF is a fourth category. Endarteritis obliterans produces progressive ischemia, the tissue bed is hostile and worsens with time, and recurrence risk is high. Vascularized interposition is often considered, but comparative evidence is limited.<sup>[[6]](#ref6)</sup>

## Classification

### Simple vs complex (operative phenotype)

The single most important categorization at the bedside:<sup>[[7]](#ref7)[[8]](#ref8)</sup>

| Feature | Simple | Complex |
|---|---|---|
| Number | Single | Multiple |
| Size | Small (cutoffs vary: about 2–2.5 cm in urologic series; WHO/EAU adapted classification uses &lt; 4 cm) | Larger (≥ 2.5 cm, or &gt; 4 cm by WHO/EAU criteria) |
| Location | Supratrigonal, away from ureteral orifices | Trigonal / juxta-ureteral, involving bladder neck |
| Etiology | Iatrogenic (post-hysterectomy), non-irradiated | Obstetric, post-radiation, malignancy-related, mesh |
| Prior repair | None | One or more failed |
| Tissue | Healthy | Scarred, ischemic, irradiated |

### Goh classification (most widely used global system)

[Goh](/docs/roots/surgeons/a-g/judith-goh)'s system grades fistulas on three axes: distance from external urethral meatus, fistula size, and degree of fibrosis / vaginal length. It predicts continence outcome after closure.<sup>[[9]](#ref9)</sup>

| Axis | Grades |
|---|---|
| **Distance from meatus** | I &gt; 3.5 cm; II 2.5–3.5 cm; III 1.5–2.5 cm; IV &lt; 1.5 cm |
| **Fistula size (largest diameter)** | a &lt; 1.5 cm; b 1.5–3 cm; c &gt; 3 cm |
| **Special considerations** | i none/mild fibrosis and preserved vaginal length; ii moderate/severe fibrosis and/or reduced vaginal length; iii special circumstances such as ureteric involvement, circumferential defect, radiation or previous repair |

### Waaldijk classification (obstetric-fistula-focused)

Type I fistulas do not involve the urethral closing mechanism; Type II fistulas do (subdivided A/B by sub-total or total urethral involvement and a/b by absence or presence of a circumferential defect); Type III covers ureteric and other exceptional fistulas. Closure and continence worsen progressively from Type I to Type IIBb. Used primarily in obstetric-fistula practice and outcome reporting in low-resource settings.<sup>[[10]](#ref10)</sup>

EAU notes that Waaldijk and Goh were designed for obstetric fistula and are less relevant to iatrogenic fistula; it recommends a detailed anatomic description that distinguishes simple from complex fistula, using the WHO-adapted criteria (simple: single, &lt; 4 cm, no sphincter, ureter or circumferential involvement, minimal tissue loss, first repair; complex: larger, multiple, scarred, radiation-related, or previously repaired).<sup>[[39]](#ref39)</sup>

## Clinical Presentation

- **Continuous, painless urinary leakage per vaginum** beginning 1–3 weeks postoperatively (iatrogenic) or after a difficult delivery (obstetric)
- Leakage may be positional, worse supine and sometimes provoked by bladder filling
- Reduced or absent voiding per urethra when the fistula is large
- Recurrent UTI, vaginal candidiasis, vulvar excoriation
- Psychosocial morbidity, particularly in obstetric fistula, where social isolation and depression are part of the syndrome<sup>[[3]](#ref3)</sup>
- In a retrospective series of 31 patients with post-hysterectomy VVF, clinical anxiety and depression each affected 51.6% before repair and 16.1% and 12.9%, respectively, at the 3- or 6-month assessment; none had fistula leakage after repair.<sup>[[57]](#ref57)</sup>

A surgeon called to evaluate "post-hysterectomy incontinence" must rule out VVF before attributing leakage to detrusor overactivity, sphincter injury, or ureterovaginal fistula.

## Diagnostic Evaluation

The workup answers four questions: *Is it VVF? Where is it relative to the ureters? Is the upper tract involved? Is there cancer recurrence?*

### Office maneuvers

- **Speculum exam:** pooled urine in the vault, visible fistula tract on the anterior vaginal wall (often after reduction of inflammation)
- **Dye test (single-tampon or three-swab):** intravesical blue dye appearing in the vagina supports a bladder communication. Oral phenazopyridine may help distinguish orange upper-tract urine from blue bladder fluid, but swab position alone is not diagnostic, mixed staining does not prove combined fistulas, and a positive bladder dye test does not exclude synchronous ureteral injury. Confirm suspected upper-tract involvement with appropriate imaging.<sup>[[11]](#ref11)</sup>
- **Vaginoscopy** with a cystoscope: useful when the fistula is small or covered by granulation tissue

### Endoscopy and imaging

- **Cystoscopy** helps confirm and map the fistula. EAU weakly recommends cystoscopy with retrograde colored bladder filling for diagnostic confirmation. Cystoscopy defines the tract relative to both ureteral orifices, identifies multiple tracts, and assesses for mesh, foreign bodies or intravesical lesions.<sup>[[2]](#ref2)[[39]](#ref39)</sup>
- **CT urogram:** assesses for synchronous ureterovaginal fistula or upper-tract obstruction; in Hilton’s 348-referral urogenital fistula series, 13 women (3.7%) had combined vesicovaginal and ureterovaginal lesions<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup>. EAU weakly recommends contrast-enhanced CT with a late excretory phase and/or MRI when the diagnosis is difficult or ureterovaginal fistula is suspected; the guideline also notes that creatinine testing of collected fluid can confirm it is urine<sup>[[39]](#ref39)</sup>
- **Pelvic MRI:** preferred for radiation VVF, recurrent fistula, and suspected malignancy recurrence; characterizes tissue quality and defect anatomy
- **Retrograde or antegrade pyelogram:** when CT urogram is equivocal for ureteral involvement

### Adjuncts

- Urine culture before any repair attempt
- **EUA** when in-office examination is non-diagnostic, particularly for the small, late-presenting fistula
- Biopsy of the fistula edge if there is any concern for malignancy or recurrence in the radiated patient
- **Videourodynamics** is not a routine part of the fistula workup but is considered selectively when a suspected or confirmed fistula coexists with lower urinary tract symptoms, or when bladder or urethral function could change counseling or reconstruction; it can help distinguish stress or urgency incontinence and other causes of leakage from fistula, and it can assess capacity, compliance, and urethral function relevant to augmentation, diversion, or continence planning. For a large fistula that prevents adequate bladder filling, vaginal packing, supine positioning, or temporary occlusion of the tract with a catheter or balloon have been described to allow the study to proceed<sup>[[51]](#ref51)</sup>

## Management

### Conservative — bladder drainage alone

A small (about &lt; 1 cm), early-recognized, non-irradiated VVF may close with continuous catheter drainage, usually assessed after 2–4 weeks and extended selectively to 4–8 weeks if the leak is clearly improving. Reported spontaneous closure averages about 13% (± 23%) and applies mainly to small fistulas; across reviewed reports it ranged from 0 to 100%, with a shorter interval from injury to drainage the best predictor of success, before the tract epithelializes.<sup>[[12]](#ref12)[[39]](#ref39)</sup> Antimuscarinics may help bladder spasms. Conservative catheter drainage is reasonable for selected small, recent fistulas, but most fistulas require operative closure; persistent leakage after a limited drainage trial warrants reassessment for repair.<sup>[[12]](#ref12)[[39]](#ref39)</sup>

### Timing of repair

The classic teaching of a 3–6 month delay to allow inflammation to resolve has been challenged by contemporary series and guideline review. The practical rule is not "early" or "late" by calendar alone; repair when infection is controlled, necrosis has declared itself, exposure is adequate, and the tissue will hold sutures.<sup>[[8]](#ref8)[[39]](#ref39)[[40]](#ref40)</sup>

| Timing | Indication |
|---|---|
| **Immediate** (intraoperative recognition) | Iatrogenic injury identified at the index operation — repair on the table after urologic consult |
| **Brief drainage trial** (2–4 wk, extend to 4–8 wk if improving) | Small, fresh, non-irradiated VVF with healthy surrounding tissue and no distal obstruction (early catheter-and-close data come largely from a 170-patient obstetric series)<sup>[[13]](#ref13)</sup> |
| **Early repair** (within 4 wk in the cited guideline comparison) | Simple post-hysterectomy or post-cesarean VVF once acute inflammation is quiet and exposure is good; a 16-patient post-hysterectomy series reported complete continence at 1 month<sup>[[22]](#ref22)</sup>, while EAU cites small uncontrolled series suggesting no difference in success rates between early (within 4 wk) and delayed (after 3 mo) repair<sup>[[39]](#ref39)</sup> |
| **Delayed repair** (often ≥ 3 mo) | Persistent inflammation, large or multiple fistulas, prior failed repair, complex anatomy, malnutrition, infection, or uncertain tissue viability |
| **Long delay / diversion discussion** (6–12 mo or longer) | **Radiation VVF** or malignancy-related field injury — tissue quality dominates, recurrence risk is high, and urinary diversion may be the most durable endpoint<sup>[[6]](#ref6)[[21]](#ref21)</sup> |

EAU cites small uncontrolled series showing no difference in success between early (within 4 weeks) and delayed (after 3 months) closure, and a 2026 retrospective laparoscopic comparison (n = 23) found leak-free, cystoscopically confirmed closure at 3 months in 10/11 early repairs (within 3 weeks) and 11/12 delayed repairs (at ≥ 3 months).<sup>[[39]](#ref39)[[45]](#ref45)</sup> Retrospective timing comparisons are vulnerable to confounding because complex or inflamed fistulas are more likely to be delayed. Prioritize tissue readiness and patient circumstances.<sup>[[21]](#ref21)[[39]](#ref39)</sup>

### Surgical principles (apply to every approach)

1. **Cystoscopic mapping.** Confirm fistula location and rule out ureteral involvement; place ureteral stents if either orifice is within 1 cm of the tract
2. **Exposure with viable tissue preservation.** Mobilize bladder and vagina for a flap-splitting repair; Latzko repair instead denudes the surrounding vaginal epithelium while retaining the tract.<sup>[[38]](#ref38)[[39]](#ref39)</sup>
3. **Selective fistula-edge / tract management.** EAU describes one RCT, while a 2024 systematic review identified two RCTs (140 women); the trials did not show improved closure with trimming, and failed trimmed repairs may leave larger recurrent defects. Tailor trimming, freshening or excision to approach, fibrosis, tissue viability and defect size.<sup>[[23]](#ref23)[[25]](#ref25)[[39]](#ref39)[[40]](#ref40)</sup>
4. **Tension-free closure suited to the approach.** Flap-splitting repair closes bladder mucosa and detrusor/fibromuscular tissue separately from vaginal wall, with offset suture lines; Latzko repair retains the tract, places a purse-string outside it, imbricates denuded vaginal tissue, then closes the vaginal epithelium.<sup>[[38]](#ref38)[[39]](#ref39)</sup>
5. **Vascularized tissue interposition when indicated.** Not routine for simple, non-irradiated primary VVF; strongly favored for radiation, recurrent, large, mesh-associated, or tissue-deficient fistulas<sup>[[7]](#ref7)[[18]](#ref18)[[28]](#ref28)[[29]](#ref29)</sup>
6. **Continuous catheter drainage.** WHO recommends 7–10 days after repair of a simple obstetric urinary fistula, supported by a noninferiority RCT that randomized 524 women with simple female genital fistulas (mostly obstetric) whose repairs remained closed at postoperative day 7; by 3 months, repair breakdown in the 501-patient per-protocol analysis was 10/250 (4.0%) after 7-day versus 8/251 (3.2%) after 14-day drainage (radiation and complex fistulas excluded).<sup>[[41]](#ref41)[[42]](#ref42)</sup> For post-surgical and complex repairs, EAU states that no high-level evidence determines catheter duration and weakly recommends continuous drainage until healing is confirmed; expert opinion suggests 10–14 days for simple or post-surgical and 14–21 days for complex or post-radiation fistulas.<sup>[[39]](#ref39)</sup> Individualize duration and leak testing; a cystogram without a voiding phase can miss a persistent bladder-neck fistula.<sup>[[39]](#ref39)[[41]](#ref41)[[42]](#ref42)</sup>
7. **Bladder rest.** Anticholinergics to suppress detrusor activity and avoid suture-line stress

## Fistula Tract Excision: Not a Universal Rule

The 2026 Cochrane publication is a protocol, not a completed effectiveness review.<sup>[[20]](#ref20)</sup> The evidence on fistula tract excision is mixed, and no high-quality comparative trial has definitively shown that excision improves VVF closure compared with non-excision techniques. The decision is best understood as an approach-specific maneuver, not an independent surgical principle.<sup>[[26]](#ref26)[[40]](#ref40)</sup>

| Setting | Typical tract strategy | Evidence signal |
|---|---|---|
| **Latzko partial colpocleisis** | No tract excision; vaginal epithelium around the fistula is denuded and the tract is inverted / imbricated | Selected non-irradiated apical VVF series report 100% closure, including a 108-patient modified Latzko series with 100% closure at mean 41-month follow-up and an 11-patient series; a video description of the technique is also available.<sup>[[15]](#ref15)[[37]](#ref37)[[38]](#ref38)</sup> |
| **Transvaginal multilayered closure** | Variable: mobilize bladder and vagina widely; freshen or trim edges only if needed | Edge freshening remains debated; trimming can enlarge the defect or weaken an already scarred suture line.<sup>[[25]](#ref25)[[40]](#ref40)</sup> |
| **Open / laparoscopic / robotic abdominal repair** | Tract excision is commonly performed because exposure permits separation of bladder and vagina around the fistula | Robotic and laparoscopic series using systematic excision report high closure, but they usually combine excision with meticulous mobilization, multilayer closure, drainage, and frequent interposition.<sup>[[23]](#ref23)[[24]](#ref24)</sup> |
| **Scarred, recurrent, or obstetric tissue** | Preserve viable tissue; avoid converting a small tract into a large defect | A 1,185-repair multinational experience (12 countries; 64% VVF) reported overall closure of 82%, 91% in simple fistulas and 85% in primary repairs; complexity was the dominant determinant of closure.<sup>[[25]](#ref25)</sup> |

The operative endpoint is therefore not "excised tract" but healthy, mobile, watertight tissue layers under no tension. If excision helps achieve that, it is reasonable; if it enlarges the hole or compromises blood supply, non-excision or minimal freshening is more reconstructive.

## Surgical Approach

The choice of approach depends on fistula location, vaginal access, surgeon expertise, tissue quality, and the need for concurrent ureteral reimplant or augmentation.<sup>[[8]](#ref8)[[14]](#ref14)[[15]](#ref15)[[26]](#ref26)</sup>

| Approach | Best fit | Caveats |
|---|---|---|
| **Transvaginal — classic flap repair (Sims-style)** | Most simple supratrigonal VVFs with good vaginal access | Mobilizes bladder and vagina for layered closure; fistula edges may be freshened, but routine wide excision is debated |
| **Transvaginal — Latzko partial colpocleisis** | Small, post-hysterectomy, vault-apex fistula in a patient with a deep vagina | Inverts the fistula without tract excision; possible vaginal shortening; both cited apical series reported 100% closure (11/11 and 108/108)<sup>[[15]](#ref15)[[37]](#ref37)</sup> |
| **Transabdominal — open or laparoscopic O'Conor** | Large, juxta-ureteral, complex, or recurrent VVF; concurrent need for ureteral reimplant or augmentation | Sagittal cystotomy carried down to the fistula; tract usually excised; bladder closed in two layers with omental or peritoneal interposition between bladder and vagina<sup>[[16]](#ref16)[[24]](#ref24)</sup> |
| **Robotic O'Conor / extravesical repair** | Same indications as open abdominal repair, especially when high or peri-orifice anatomy is hard to expose vaginally | A four-patient robotic series reported no recurrence and mean LOS 1.75 days; a 22-patient series reported no recurrence at median 15 months; an earlier laparoscopic review reported 80–100% success across heterogeneous series at 1–74 months; techniques vary between transvesical and extravesical routes<sup>[[14]](#ref14)[[17]](#ref17)[[23]](#ref23)</sup> |

### Transvaginal repair — operative pearls

- **Position.** High lithotomy or prone jackknife (the latter for the difficult-to-expose vault fistula in a foreshortened vagina)
- **Schuchardt incision** to widen vaginal access when needed
- **Hold the fistula edge with stay sutures.** Convert a hole into a slit by tagging at 12 and 6 o'clock to deliver the edges
- Avoid aggressive tract excision in the small fistula; Latzko-style mucosal denudation and inverting closure preserves bladder tissue but may shorten the vagina
- **Martius flap:** labial fat-pad flap mobilized on its inferior pedicle, tunneled subcutaneously to the fistula bed (see [Martius flap](/docs/foundations/surgical-principles/flaps/martius))
- Test with retrograde fill of dilute methylene blue before closing the vaginal layer

### Transabdominal (O'Conor) repair — operative pearls

- **Bladder bivalving.** Sagittal cystotomy from the dome down to (but not through) the fistula
- **Tract excision or controlled edge freshening** when it helps create healthy margins; avoid unnecessary enlargement of a small defect
- **Wide bladder–vagina dissection** to allow non-overlapping closure
- **Omental pedicle interposition** between the closed bladder and vagina is the standard interposition for the abdominal approach; alternatives are peritoneal flap (laparoscopic / robotic) or rectus muscle flap
- Robotic reports include transvesical and extravesical or transvaginal approaches; select exposure and interposition tissue to fit anatomy rather than infer a universal preferred route<sup>[[14]](#ref14)[[17]](#ref17)</sup>

### Tissue interposition

Vascularized tissue between the closed bladder and vagina is best viewed as an indicated adjunct, not a reflex step. It is most useful when the tissue bed is irradiated, recurrent, mesh-injured, ischemic, large, or difficult to close without tension; it is not required for many simple, primary, non-irradiated VVFs.<sup>[[6]](#ref6)[[7]](#ref7)[[18]](#ref18)[[28]](#ref28)[[29]](#ref29)</sup>

| Tissue | Best use | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| **[Martius flap](/docs/foundations/surgical-principles/flaps/martius)** | Selected transvaginal repairs of vaginal or perineal fistulas when the pedicle reaches the defect | Often low morbidity, but a mixed 97-patient vaginal-reconstruction series (20 VVF) reported labial numbness in 13, pain in five and distortion in nine at mean 85 months; counsel about these donor-site effects.<sup>[[28]](#ref28)[[58]](#ref58)</sup> |
| **[Omental pedicle](/docs/foundations/surgical-principles/flaps/omental)** | Transabdominal / robotic O'Conor; radiation cases | Excellent bulk; depends on omental reach |
| **[Peritoneal flap](/docs/foundations/surgical-principles/flaps/peritoneal)** | Robotic / laparoscopic; when omentum unavailable | Limited bulk; reliable for non-radiated repair |
| **[Gracilis muscle flap](/docs/foundations/surgical-principles/flaps/gracilis)** | Recurrent, large, or radiated VVF; failed prior interposition | Requires medial-thigh harvest; substantial bulk |
| **Urachal flap** | Transabdominal / robotic O'Conor, when omentum is unavailable or hard to reach | Ligated below the umbilicus and mobilized with its lateral peritoneal wings before being folded between bladder and vagina; a 13-patient series reported success in 12 (92%)<sup>[[53]](#ref53)</sup> |

The Martius flap deserves a specific caveat in obstetric-fistula practice: Browning's retrospective study of 440 repairs found no significant difference in breakdown rates and more residual incontinence in three Martius subgroups. Nonrandomized allocation prevents a causal conclusion that the flap itself caused those differences. This reinforces that interposition is a tool for specific indications (radiation, recurrence, large defect, hostile bed), not a default for every repair.<sup>[[18]](#ref18)</sup>

### Emerging adjuncts

Adjunct materials are most useful when ordinary layered closure lacks a healthy second layer, not as substitutes for exposure, mobilization, drainage, or tissue optimization. For a full flap-and-adjunct selection framework, see [Tissue Interposition Flaps](/docs/surgical-techniques/04h-fistula-repair/vaginal-fistula-flaps).

| Adjunct | Potential role | Evidence signal |
|---|---|---|
| **Buccal mucosal graft (BMG)** | Second layer for scarred, shortened, or tissue-deficient vaginal repairs; robotic interposition when vaginal tissue is inadequate | Small contemporary series and case reports suggest feasibility, including 100% closure in a 10-patient transvaginal series and successful robotic BMG interposition with omentum.<sup>[[30]](#ref30)[[31]](#ref31)</sup> |
| **Platelet-rich plasma / fibrin** | Biological adjunct for recurrent or poorly healing tissue; peri-fistula injection and/or interposition after de-epithelialization | Small uncontrolled VVF series report high closure, including 11/12 standalone PRP/PRF cures and recurrent-case neoadjuvant PRP before Latzko repair; evidence remains low quality.<sup>[[32]](#ref32)[[33]](#ref33)[[34]](#ref34)</sup> |
| **Small intestinal submucosa (SIS)** | Off-the-shelf acellular matrix for complicated VVF when local tissue is thin or scarred | A 23-patient complicated-VVF pilot reported 21/23 women dry through 6 months after SIS interposition, without reported allergic or inflammatory reactions.<sup>[[35]](#ref35)</sup> |
| **Fibrin glue / sealant interposition** | Interposed layer between bladder and vaginal closures in transvaginal repair | In an RCT of complicated obstetric VVF, 13/19 (68.4%) were dry with glue and 11/19 (57.9%) with a Martius flap through 3 months, without a statistically significant difference<sup>[[59]](#ref59)</sup>, and a 64-patient prospective randomized study reported leak-free status on postoperative day 21, after catheter removal, of 90.9% with glue, 90.5% with Martius and 81% with primary repair alone (p = 0.541); a systematic review found no significant improvement in cure with fibrin glue.<sup>[[39]](#ref39)[[40]](#ref40)[[46]](#ref46)</sup> |
| **Regenerative bioglues / scaffolds** | Investigational sealants or matrices | Preclinical bioglue models are promising but not ready to replace vascularized tissue in irradiated or recurrent human VVF.<sup>[[36]](#ref36)</sup> |

## Outcomes

Reported closure rates span 58–100% across VVF series (EAU), and a review reported 70–100% success for non-radiated fistulas; these series include mixed repair histories and fistula complexity. Prior failed repair and adverse fistula characteristics are associated with lower success.<sup>[[2]](#ref2)[[7]](#ref7)[[8]](#ref8)[[19]](#ref19)</sup> A large multinational experience illustrates the gradient: anatomic closure was 91% for simple fistulas, 79% for recurrent fistulas, and 68% for complex fistulas, underscoring that tissue compromise and case complexity often matter more than any single technical maneuver.<sup>[[25]](#ref25)</sup>

| Setting | Typical first-attempt closure | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| Non-radiated VVF, vaginal or abdominal | No comparable first-attempt estimate | Reviewed series reported 70–100% success (mean 91% vaginal, 97% abdominal); a four-patient robotic series reported no recurrence<sup>[[8]](#ref8)[[14]](#ref14)</sup> |
| Robotic / laparoscopic abdominal repair | No comparable first-attempt estimate (14 robotic series, 206 patients: recurrence 2.91%; earlier laparoscopic/robotic review: reported success 80–100% at 1–74 months) | Most stays under 5 days; transvesical and extravesical routes similar; no randomized comparison with open repair<sup>[[14]](#ref14)[[17]](#ref17)[[23]](#ref23)[[44]](#ref44)</sup> |
| Latzko (selected vault VVF) | No comparable first-attempt estimate (11/11 and 108/108 closed at follow-up in the cited series) | Possible vaginal shortening; preserves bladder<sup>[[15]](#ref15)[[37]](#ref37)</sup> |
| Obstetric VVF (high-volume centers) | **~80–95% closure**; **continence** lower | Among Malawi women who returned for follow-up within 120 days after first VVF repair and had successful closure, 85/372 (23%) had residual incontinence; a separate multinational series of mixed female genital fistulas (64% VVF; 59% obstetric) reported 12% after successful closure.<sup>[[3]](#ref3)[[25]](#ref25)[[47]](#ref47)</sup> |
| Radiation VVF | **48.1%** at first repair, 66.6% cumulatively after a second repair, and 80.4% after all operations (216 patients treated; 210 vaginal repairs are the outcome denominator); permanent diversion is a legitimate alternative | Tissue quality dominates outcome; results with the same techniques are consistently poorer than for surgical fistulas<sup>[[6]](#ref6)[[39]](#ref39)</sup> |

A retrospective ACS-NSQIP database analysis of 200 VVF repairs from 2006 to 2013 (65% vaginal, the remainder abdominal) found the abdominal approach associated with higher 30-day morbidity than the vaginal approach: overall complications 22% versus 7%, sepsis 4.3% versus 0%, transfusion 7.1% versus 0.8%, and readmission about 10% versus 1% (p ≤ 0.02 for each). The database does not capture fistula characteristics or closure outcomes, so this is a multivariable association with 30-day perioperative morbidity; unrecorded fistula complexity may still confound the route comparison, and closure was not measured.<sup>[[52]](#ref52)</sup>

**Residual incontinence** occurred in 85/372 women (23%) within 120 days of successful VVF closure in a Malawi cohort; a separate mixed female genital-fistula series reported 12% after closure. Urethral involvement, vaginal scarring and circumferential defects were associated with poorer continence outcomes; some patients underwent bulking or pubovaginal sling treatment.<sup>[[19]](#ref19)[[25]](#ref25)[[47]](#ref47)</sup> After repair, EAU also lists de novo overactive bladder, ureteric obstruction, bladder contracture, vaginal stenosis and dyspareunia among complications to counsel about.<sup>[[39]](#ref39)</sup>

In selected obstetric fistulas with bladder-neck or urethral involvement, a concomitant continence procedure at the time of repair is an option. A retrospective comparison of a pubococcygeal sling, refixation of the pubocervical fascia, or both reported negative postoperative dye tests in 142/169 (84.0%), 62/69 (89.9%), and 30/32 (93.8%) of those tested, respectively; clinical stress incontinence was recorded in 74/157 (47.1%), 32/67 (47.8%), and 14/32 (43.8%) with continence assessments, respectively. The dye-test and overall continence-status comparisons were not statistically significant, and there was no repair-only comparison arm.<sup>[[54]](#ref54)</sup> A small randomized trial comparing a pubococcygeal sling with an autologous fascial sling at the time of repair found no significant difference in pad weight or quality of life between the two, and was stopped early after repair breakdowns in both arms.<sup>[[55]](#ref55)</sup> These cohorts are from obstetric-fistula practice and may not generalize to simple postsurgical VVF.

## Special Situations

### Radiation VVF

Tissue quality is the rate-limiting factor.<sup>[[6]](#ref6)</sup>

- Wait for tissue stabilization after radiation and complete malignancy workup before attempting repair; many cases require 6–12 months or longer
- **Hyperbaric oxygen** may be considered for selected radiation tissue injury, but evidence does not establish a standard regimen that improves VVF closure; it does not replace drainage, malignancy assessment or reconstructive planning
- Strongly favor vascularized tissue interposition: omentum, Martius, gracilis, or rectus flap depending on route and defect
- Counsel realistically about recurrence and about **permanent urinary diversion** as a dignified, durable alternative when reconstruction is not feasible; if bowel is used, EAU weakly recommends avoiding irradiated segments.<sup>[[39]](#ref39)</sup>

### Mesh-erosion VVF

- Concurrent mesh excision with VVF repair
- Avoid leaving residual synthetic material at the suture line
- Tissue interposition recommended even for "simple"-appearing mesh-related VVFs

### Recurrent VVF

- Reassess exposure and tissue quality; a different plane or approach may help, but repeat repair by the same route can be appropriate
- Vascularized interposition or another healthy second layer is usually required
- **Diversion** (continent or incontinent) is a valid endpoint after multiple failures; framing this as a quality-of-life decision rather than as failure is part of the conversation

### Large or trigonal VVF involving the ureteral orifice

- Stent both ureters preoperatively if either is within 1 cm of the tract
- Consider concurrent ureteral reimplant at the time of repair when the fistula involves the ureteral orifice
- Consider an abdominal or robotic approach when reimplantation or adequate exposure requires it; selected high fistulas remain accessible vaginally
