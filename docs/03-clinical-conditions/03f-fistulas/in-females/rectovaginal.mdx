# Rectovaginal Fistula

A **rectovaginal fistula (RVF)** is an epithelialized communication between the rectum (or anal canal) and the vagina, allowing passage of flatus and feces per vagina. Although RVF repair is primarily performed by colorectal surgery, the urogynecologist and reconstructive pelvic surgeon are routinely involved; RVF clusters with sphincter injury after obstetric trauma, frequently coexists with VVF or rectourethral fistula in multi-compartment cases, and is the prototypical setting for Martius and gracilis interposition in the female perineum.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[2]](#ref2)</sup> Modern management follows the ASCRS 2022 Clinical Practice Guidelines, which recommend an etiology-driven, stepwise approach without prescribing a single technique for all fistulas.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup>

For complex multi-compartment cases, see the [Fistulas section landing](/docs/clinical-conditions/03f-fistulas). For the male equivalent, see [Rectourethral Fistula](/docs/clinical-conditions/03f-fistulas/in-males/rectourethral). For operative selection across all repair routes, see the [Female Fistula Repair database](/docs/surgical-techniques/04h-fistula-repair/female-fistula).

## Epidemiology and Etiology

RVF is uncommon in high-resource settings but a significant problem in low-resource countries due to obstructed labor.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[3]](#ref3)</sup> Etiology distribution differs by setting. Globally, obstetric trauma accounts for up to 88%; in high-resource referral series, the mix is more diverse, with iatrogenic and Crohn's-related fistulas making up substantial fractions.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[2]](#ref2)[[6]](#ref6)</sup>

| Setting / cause | Notes |
|---|---|
| **Obstetric trauma** | Dominant cause globally; fourth-degree perineal tear, or unrecognized or failed sphincter repair; in the Australian urogynecology series of 41 repairs, obstetric injury explained only 27% of RVF<sup>[[2]](#ref2)</sup> |
| **Crohn's disease** | Up to 43% in referral-center series; tied to active rectal inflammation<sup>[[6]](#ref6)[[7]](#ref7)</sup> |
| **Iatrogenic — colorectal anastomosis** | RVF complicates ~1.6% of low anterior resection (~2.2% of colorectal anastomoses overall); higher with very low tumors and unsatisfactory anastomosis<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[5]](#ref5)</sup> |
| **Iatrogenic — gynecologic surgery** | Hysterectomy (cuff into rectum), prolapse repair (especially mesh), anorectal surgery |
| **Cryptoglandular** | Perianal abscess eroding into vagina |
| **Pelvic radiation** | Cervical, endometrial, or rectal cancer treatment; median onset ~20 months after radiotherapy<sup>[[8]](#ref8)</sup> |
| **Malignancy** | Direct invasion from rectal, cervical, or vaginal cancer |

## Classification

The single most operative-decision-relevant categorization:<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[9]](#ref9)</sup>

| | Simple | Complex |
|---|---|---|
| **Location** | Low (anal canal ↔ lower vagina) | Mid- or high-vaginal septum |
| **Diameter** | &lt; 2.5 cm | ≥ 2.5 cm |
| **Etiology** | Obstetric or traumatic | Crohn's, radiation, malignancy, anastomotic |
| **Tissue bed** | Healthy | Inflamed, irradiated, scarred |
| **Prior repair** | None | One or more failed |

**Anatomic level** is also commonly described (low, at or near the dentate line; mid, mid-septum, the most common; high, vault, post-hysterectomy or anastomotic) and dictates the operative approach.

---

## Clinical Presentation

- **Passage of flatus per vagina:** often the earliest symptom
- **Passage of feces per vagina:** pathognomonic
- **Foul-smelling vaginal discharge**
- **Recurrent vaginitis and UTI**
- **Fecal incontinence:** frequent in obstetric RVF because of concurrent sphincter damage; assess specifically rather than equating vaginal fecal leakage with anal incontinence<sup>[[2]](#ref2)</sup>
- **Dyspareunia** and significant psychosocial morbidity

## Diagnostic Evaluation

The ASCRS guideline emphasizes that the workup must define both the fistula and the sphincter complex, since sphincter status drives the choice of repair.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup>

### Bedside

- Inspection of the perineum: scarring, deformity, external opening
- Speculum exam: vaginal opening of the tract
- Digital rectal exam: palpation of the tract; resting and squeeze sphincter tone; integrity assessment
- Anoscopy or proctoscopy: rectal opening
- **Examination under anesthesia** is often required to fully define the anatomy<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup>

### Imaging

| Modality | Role | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| **MRI pelvis (with vaginal gel)** | The diagnostic workhorse for RVF | Best characterization of tract, secondary extensions, abscess, sphincter destruction, neoplasm; contrast-enhanced MRI carries the highest accuracy<sup>[[3]](#ref3)[[9]](#ref9)[[10]](#ref10)</sup> |
| **3D endoanal ultrasound (EAUS)** | First-line for sphincter mapping; comparable to MRI for simple tracts | Both techniques can be useful; performance data from anal-fistula studies should not be treated as RVF-specific accuracy<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[10]](#ref10)</sup> |
| **Anorectal manometry** | Quantifies resting and squeeze sphincter pressures | Adjunct to EAUS; informs whether sphincteroplasty is required |
| **Vaginography / contrast enema** | Defines the tract when MRI is equivocal | |
| **Proctoscopy / sigmoidoscopy** | Mandatory when Crohn's is suspected | Elective definitive closure generally follows control of active proctitis; urgent drainage of sepsis must not wait for mucosal healing<sup>[[7]](#ref7)</sup> |

## Management — ASCRS Stepwise Algorithm

### 1. Conservative management (Recommendation 12, Weak / 2C)

For obstetric and minimally symptomatic benign RVF: sitz baths, perineal hygiene, infection control, and stool-bulking fiber for 3–6 months. Reported spontaneous closure in 52–66% of obstetric RVF.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup> This window applies to an established, minimally symptomatic fistula. A fresh, directly recognized obstetric or surgical injury follows a separate timing framework: repair within 72 hours of injury, or delay up to 12 weeks once local inflammation has settled.<sup>[[19]](#ref19)</sup>

### 2. Seton drainage (Recommendation 13, Strong / 1C)

A **draining seton** controls acute inflammation or sepsis around the tract, provides durable symptomatic relief in patients who are not surgical candidates, and improves the success of subsequent definitive repair. Setons are particularly relevant in Crohn's, where they should be placed before initiation of immunosuppression.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[7]](#ref7)</sup>

### 3. Endorectal advancement flap (ERAF) — procedure of choice for simple RVF (Recommendation 14, Strong / 1C)

The guideline names endorectal advancement flap, with or without sphincteroplasty, the procedure of choice for most patients with RVF, without limiting it to simple fistulas; for recurrent or complex fistulas it separately recommends interposition (below).<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup>

**Technique:**

1. Trans-anal exposure (prone-jackknife)
2. Full- or partial-thickness flap of rectal mucosa, submucosa, ± internal sphincter raised proximal to the fistula opening
3. Excise or curette the tract
4. Close the rectal opening
5. Advance the flap distally to cover the closed defect
6. Leave the vaginal side open to drain

**Outcomes:**

| Setting | Healing |
|---|---|
| ERAF alone | **41–78%** (etiology-dependent)<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup> |
| Sphincteroplasty ± levatorplasty for obstetric RVF (Tsang: 28/35 versus 11/27 for ERAF alone); retrospective, not randomized, and not ERAF combined with sphincteroplasty | **80%** (ERAF alone 41%)<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup> |
| Repeat ERAF after initial failure | **55–93%**<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup> |

In a low-quality network meta-analysis of obstetric RVF (32 studies, 595 patients), sphincter-repair-type operations (transvaginal or transperineal excision with layered closure, Musset procedure) had higher anatomic success than ERAF, and no randomized trial compares ERAF with sphincteroplasty.<sup>[[18]](#ref18)</sup> Failure predictors: sphincter defect on EAUS or manometry, Crohn's disease, prior pelvic radiation, recurrent fistula. Diverting stoma is not consistently shown to improve ERAF outcomes and is decided on a per-patient basis.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup>

### 4. Episioproctotomy (Recommendation 15, Strong / 1C)

A transperineal "convert and repair" technique for low RVF with anterior sphincter defect (classically obstetric or cryptoglandular). The anterior anal sphincter complex and rectovaginal septum are intentionally divided to lay open the fistula, then reconstructed in layers with sphincter repair. ASCRS recommends it for obstetric or cryptoglandular RVF in patients with an anal sphincter defect.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup>

- Healing 78–100%
- Diversion was used in 55% (Hull 2007, 23/42) to 72% (36/50 in two later series) and was not significantly associated with outcome in Hull 2007<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup>

### 5. Tissue interposition flaps (Recommendation 16, Strong / 1C)

For recurrent or complex RVF, ASCRS supports Martius or gracilis interposition, selected by anatomy, tissue availability and expertise. Pastier’s retrospective cohort included 62 patients and 79 flap procedures: immediate success was 35% versus 31%, rising to 69% in both groups at median 23 months after additional procedures. Absence of a significant difference does not prove equivalence or require a universal Martius-before-gracilis sequence.<sup>[[11]](#ref11)</sup> A 2024 systematic review and proportional meta-analysis of Martius flaps reported 91.4% healing for primary RVF and 94.6% for radiation-induced RVF.<sup>[[12]](#ref12)</sup>

| Flap | Healing | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| **[Martius flap](/docs/foundations/surgical-principles/flaps/martius)** (bulbocavernosus / labial fat pad) | 65–94% (primary); 77.5% (recurrent); 91.4% pooled (primary); 94.6% (radiation)<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[11]](#ref11)[[12]](#ref12)</sup> | Shorter LOS than gracilis; **stoma not mandatory** (27% in Pastier series done without diversion); one interposition option |
| **[Gracilis muscle flap](/docs/foundations/surgical-principles/flaps/gracilis)** | 50–92% (individual series); 64% pooled (20 mostly small series, 384 patients); 69% at 23 months<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[4]](#ref4)[[11]](#ref11)</sup> | Higher morbidity (28–47%): wound infection, thigh numbness, hematoma; sexual function is often impaired, and smoking, Crohn's disease and more than two prior repairs predicted failure while a stoma was associated with better closure<sup>[[4]](#ref4)</sup>; may be primary interposition or salvage depending on defect and prior surgery |

**Smoking** was a negative predictor in the Pastier cohort; this does not exclude other modifiable contributors to healing (p = 0.02).<sup>[[11]](#ref11)</sup>

### 6. Anastomotic-related RVF (Recommendation 17, Strong / 1C)

RVF after low anterior resection or other colorectal anastomosis usually requires a transabdominal approach.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup>

- **Fecal diversion** is the typical first step; healing with diversion alone was approximately 37% in the guideline's summary and 15 of 21 (71%) after ostomy in a Chinese series of 24 post-LAR fistulas<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[5]](#ref5)</sup>
- Persistent fistulas need redo colorectal anastomosis, ERAF, or transperineal interposition

### 7. Radiation-induced RVF (Recommendation 18, Weak / 2C)

The most challenging subset; endarteritis obliterans produces a hostile, progressive bed.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[8]](#ref8)</sup>

- Median time to radiation-induced RVF: 20 months (range 5–240) after radiotherapy<sup>[[8]](#ref8)</sup>
- **Fecal diversion alone** was the only feasible option in 96% (48/50) of patients in one series; only 12% (6/50) healed; healing was associated with fistula &gt; 7 cm from the anal verge (OR 18) and loop ileostomy (OR 17) on univariate analysis, and only distance from the anal verge remained significant in multivariate analysis<sup>[[8]](#ref8)</sup>
- **Martius flap** for radiation RVF: healing in 11/12 and 13/14 in two series, a high success rate<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[12]](#ref12)</sup>
- **Proctectomy with coloanal anastomosis** (sleeve excision or pull-through): healing 75–100%<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[13]](#ref13)</sup>
- **Delayed coloanal anastomosis (Turnbull–Cutait):** 81% success in the multicenter GRECCAR cohort, but with substantial morbidity (45% overall, 23% major) and durable functional sequelae (42% with severe LARS or permanent stoma)<sup>[[14]](#ref14)</sup>
- In a small retrospective comparison, selected **restorative-resection** patients had greater symptom improvement and 30% underwent stoma reversal versus none after colostomy alone; selection and operative fitness limit causal comparison<sup>[[15]](#ref15)</sup>

### 8. Crohn's-disease-associated RVF

Combined medical–surgical management is mandatory.<sup>[[7]](#ref7)[[16]](#ref16)</sup>

- **Infliximab** has the strongest medical evidence for perianal fistulizing Crohn’s disease. Select advanced therapy and any immunomodulator with gastroenterology; perianal-trial results do not guarantee RVF closure
- **Antibiotics** for infection control or a selected short adjunctive course; routine long-term dual antibiotics are not definitive fistula therapy
- Drain abscesses and control sepsis before immunosuppression; use a loose seton when appropriate to maintain tract drainage
- **Elective definitive repair** is favored after control of active rectal inflammation; urgent drainage or diversion for sepsis does not wait for endoscopic healing
- **Diverting stoma** for refractory disease; long-term success of diversion alone in perianal Crohn's is poor
- **Proctectomy / total proctocolectomy** with permanent stoma in the most severe cases

**Rectal sleeve advancement** has been described for a low, Crohn's-related RVF: submucosal dissection begins at the dentate line and continues circumferentially and cephalad into the supralevator space, the rectum is mobilized until a tension-free anastomosis is possible, the fistula tract is cored out and closed, the vaginal defect is left open for drainage, and the distal rectum is advanced and sutured to the anoderm. Some patients require a repeat sleeve advancement to achieve healing.<sup>[[24]](#ref24)</sup> Marchesa's circumferential transanal sleeve advancement flap healed 8 of 13 patients (62%) with severe perianal Crohn's disease at 1 year, 3 of them after an additional procedure.<sup>[[22]](#ref22)</sup> Hull's earlier series of 35 women undergoing transanal repair of Crohn's-related anovaginal fistula, using three flap types including 5 sleeve advancements, reported healing in 19 of 35 (54%) after the index procedure and 24 of 35 (68%) after repeat surgery.<sup>[[21]](#ref21)</sup> The technique was first described by Parks for post-irradiation RVF, with cure in 4 of 5 patients.<sup>[[23]](#ref23)</sup>

In one retrospective cohort, inflammatory etiologies (Crohn's, cryptoglandular, anastomotic) had healing of only 7.1% after index surgery and 46.4% at final follow-up, versus 45.9% and 91.7% for traumatic (obstetric or iatrogenic) fistulas (55 women, single center; 67% healed overall).<sup>[[17]](#ref17)</sup>

## Outcomes and Prognostic Factors

| Factor | Reported association (mostly retrospective) |
|---|---|
| **Traumatic etiology** (obstetric / iatrogenic) | Best — 91.7% final healing<sup>[[17]](#ref17)</sup> |
| **Inflammatory etiology** (Crohn's, cryptoglandular, anastomotic) | Worst — 46.4% final healing (p = 0.001)<sup>[[17]](#ref17)</sup> |
| **Major procedure** (gracilis, coloanal anastomosis) for failed local repair | OR 6.4 for success per procedure (79 patients, 286 procedures; 72% overall success)<sup>[[6]](#ref6)</sup> |
| **Diverting stoma** in complex cases | OR 3.5 for success<sup>[[6]](#ref6)</sup> |
| **Early surgery** (within 9 months of diagnosis) | OR 2.3 for success<sup>[[6]](#ref6)</sup> |
| **Repair at a specialized center** | OR 3.2 for success<sup>[[6]](#ref6)</sup> |
| **Smoking** | Negative predictor in one interposition cohort<sup>[[11]](#ref11)</sup> |
| **Sphincter defect**, **prior radiation**, **recurrent fistula** | Predict ERAF failure<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup> |

In the Corte 79-patient / 286-procedure series, the operative algorithm of early temporary stoma and escalation to a major procedure after failure of local repair delivered the highest cumulative success rates.<sup>[[6]](#ref6)</sup>

## Operative Principles

- **Etiology drives strategy:** obstetric, Crohn's, radiation, and anastomotic RVF have distinct pathways
- **Assess sphincter integrity and continence:** a clinically important defect may favor concurrent sphincteroplasty or episioproctotomy
- **Control infection and inflammation first:** drain, divert, and treat before definitive repair
- **Repair from the high-pressure side:** the rectal advancement flap covers the rectal opening, directing intraluminal pressure away from the suture line
- **Vascularized tissue interposition** is an option for recurrent or complex fistulas; select Martius, gracilis or another reconstruction by anatomy and tissue quality
- **Local transvaginal repair** for a low, accessible RVF with an intact sphincter has been described: a finger in the rectum guides dissection of the posterior vaginal wall off the rectum, using an inverted-U perineal incision for a low fistula or, when the fistula is higher and the perineum is intact, an incision directly over the posterior vaginal-wall opening; the rectal defect is then closed extramucosally with 3-0 or 4-0 absorbable suture (4-0 chromic catgut in one description), followed by an imbricating layer and, when available, a perirectal-fascial layer, before the vaginal epithelium is reapproximated. Some authors describe mobilizing 1.5–2 cm around the opening, though no ideal margin has been established.<sup>[[19]](#ref19)[[20]](#ref20)</sup>
- **Fecal diversion** is individualized for sepsis, symptoms and reconstruction complexity; comparative evidence does not establish a universal closure benefit
- **Smoking cessation** is advisable; smoking predicted poorer outcomes in one retrospective interposition cohort<sup>[[11]](#ref11)</sup>
- **Fibrin glue and fistula plugs** have prohibitively poor success rates in RVF and are not recommended<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup>
- **Watch for stapler entrapment of the septum:** during stapled colorectal anastomosis, rotating the device while palpating and directly viewing the posterior vaginal wall has been described as a way to detect the rectovaginal septum caught in the staple path, seen as dimpling of the vaginal wall.<sup>[[24]](#ref24)</sup>
