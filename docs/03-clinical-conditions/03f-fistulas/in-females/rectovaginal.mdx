# Rectovaginal Fistula

A **rectovaginal fistula (RVF)** is an epithelialized communication between the rectum (or anal canal) and the vagina, allowing passage of flatus and feces per vagina. Although RVF is owned operatively by colorectal surgery, the urogynecologist and reconstructive pelvic surgeon are routinely involved — RVF clusters with sphincter injury after obstetric trauma, frequently coexists with VVF or rectourethral fistula in multi-compartment cases, and is the prototypical setting for **Martius and gracilis interposition** in the female perineum.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[2]](#ref2)</sup> Modern management follows the **ASCRS 2022 Clinical Practice Guidelines**, which dictate an **etiology-driven, stepwise** approach.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup>

For complex multi-compartment cases, see the [Fistulas section landing](/docs/clinical-conditions/03f-fistulas). For the male equivalent, see [Rectourethral Fistula](/docs/clinical-conditions/03f-fistulas/in-males/rectourethral). For operative selection across all repair routes, see the [Female Fistula Repair database](/docs/surgical-techniques/04h-fistula-repair/female-fistula).

## Epidemiology and Etiology

RVF is uncommon in high-resource settings but a significant problem in low-resource countries due to obstructed labor.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[3]](#ref3)</sup> Etiology distribution differs by setting — globally, **obstetric trauma accounts for up to 88%**; in high-resource referral series, the mix is more diverse, with iatrogenic and Crohn's-related fistulas making up substantial fractions.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[2]](#ref2)[[6]](#ref6)</sup>

| Setting / cause | Notes |
|---|---|
| **Obstetric trauma** | Dominant cause globally; fourth-degree perineal tear with unrecognized sphincter injury; reported RVF rate **0.4–3.0%** after fourth-degree tear<sup>[[4]](#ref4)</sup> |
| **Crohn's disease** | Up to 43% in referral-center series; tied to active rectal inflammation<sup>[[6]](#ref6)[[7]](#ref7)</sup> |
| **Iatrogenic — colorectal anastomosis** | RVF complicates ~1.6% of low anterior resection (~2.2% of colorectal anastomoses overall); higher with very low tumors and unsatisfactory anastomosis<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[5]](#ref5)</sup> |
| **Iatrogenic — gynecologic surgery** | Hysterectomy (cuff into rectum), prolapse repair (especially mesh), anorectal surgery |
| **Cryptoglandular** | Perianal abscess eroding into vagina |
| **Pelvic radiation** | Cervical, endometrial, or rectal cancer treatment; median onset ~20 months after radiotherapy<sup>[[8]](#ref8)</sup> |
| **Malignancy** | Direct invasion from rectal, cervical, or vaginal cancer |

## Classification

The single most operative-decision-relevant categorization:<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[9]](#ref9)</sup>

| | Simple | Complex |
|---|---|---|
| **Location** | Low (anal canal ↔ lower vagina) | Mid- or high-vaginal septum |
| **Diameter** | &lt; 2.5 cm | ≥ 2.5 cm |
| **Etiology** | Obstetric or traumatic | Crohn's, radiation, malignancy, anastomotic |
| **Tissue bed** | Healthy | Inflamed, irradiated, scarred |
| **Prior repair** | None | One or more failed |

**Anatomic level** is also commonly described — low (at or near the dentate line), mid (mid-septum, the most common), and high (vault, post-hysterectomy or anastomotic) — and dictates the operative approach.

---

## Clinical Presentation

- **Passage of flatus per vagina** — often the earliest symptom
- **Passage of feces per vagina** — pathognomonic
- **Foul-smelling vaginal discharge**
- **Recurrent vaginitis and UTI**
- **Fecal incontinence** — frequent in obstetric RVF because of concurrent sphincter damage; nearly universal as a presenting complaint in modern series<sup>[[2]](#ref2)</sup>
- **Dyspareunia** and significant **psychosocial morbidity**

## Diagnostic Evaluation

The ASCRS guideline emphasizes that the workup must define both **the fistula** and **the sphincter complex**, since sphincter status drives the choice of repair.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup>

### Bedside

- Inspection of the perineum — scarring, deformity, external opening
- Speculum exam — vaginal opening of the tract
- Digital rectal exam — palpation of the tract; resting and squeeze sphincter tone; integrity assessment
- Anoscopy / proctoscopy — rectal opening
- **Examination under anesthesia** is often required to fully define the anatomy<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup>

### Imaging

| Modality | Role | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| **MRI pelvis (with vaginal gel)** | The diagnostic workhorse for RVF | Best characterization of tract, secondary extensions, abscess, sphincter destruction, neoplasm; contrast-enhanced MRI carries the highest accuracy<sup>[[3]](#ref3)[[9]](#ref9)[[10]](#ref10)</sup> |
| **3D endoanal ultrasound (EAUS)** | First-line for sphincter mapping; comparable to MRI for simple tracts | MRI superior for complex/secondary extensions (97% vs 74% sensitivity)<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[10]](#ref10)</sup> |
| **Anorectal manometry** | Quantifies resting and squeeze sphincter pressures | Adjunct to EAUS; informs whether sphincteroplasty is required |
| **Vaginography / contrast enema** | Defines the tract when MRI is equivocal | |
| **Proctoscopy / sigmoidoscopy** | Mandatory when Crohn's is suspected | Surgery is deferred until **endoscopic mucosal healing** is achieved<sup>[[7]](#ref7)</sup> |

## Management — ASCRS Stepwise Algorithm

### 1. Conservative management (Recommendation 12, Weak / 2C)

For obstetric and minimally symptomatic benign RVF: sitz baths, perineal hygiene, infection control, and **stool-bulking fiber** for **3–6 months**. Reported spontaneous closure in **52–66%** of obstetric RVF.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup>

### 2. Seton drainage (Recommendation 13, Strong / 1C)

A **draining seton** controls acute inflammation or sepsis around the tract, provides durable symptomatic relief in patients who are not surgical candidates, and improves the success of subsequent definitive repair. Particularly important in Crohn's, where setons should be placed **before initiation of immunosuppression**.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[7]](#ref7)</sup>

### 3. Endorectal advancement flap (ERAF) — procedure of choice (Recommendation 14, Strong / 1C)

The default operation for most RVF.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup>

**Technique:**

1. Trans-anal exposure (prone-jackknife)
2. Full- or partial-thickness flap of rectal mucosa, submucosa, ± internal sphincter raised proximal to the fistula opening
3. Excise or curette the tract
4. Close the rectal opening
5. Advance the flap distally to cover the closed defect
6. Leave the vaginal side open to drain

**Outcomes:**

| Setting | Healing |
|---|---|
| ERAF alone | **41–78%** (etiology-dependent)<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup> |
| ERAF + sphincteroplasty (when sphincter defect present) | **80%**<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup> |
| Repeat ERAF after initial failure | **55–93%**<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup> |

Failure predictors: sphincter defect on EAUS or manometry, Crohn's disease, prior pelvic radiation, recurrent fistula. **Diverting stoma is not consistently shown to improve ERAF outcomes** and is decided on a per-patient basis.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup>

### 4. Episioproctotomy (Recommendation 15, Strong / 1C)

A transperineal "convert and repair" technique for **low RVF with anterior sphincter defect** (classically obstetric or cryptoglandular). The anterior anal sphincter complex and rectovaginal septum are intentionally divided to lay open the fistula, then reconstructed in layers with overlapping sphincteroplasty.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup>

- Healing **78–100%**
- Postoperative fecal incontinence reported as "rare" in 92% of patients
- Diversion used in ~50–72% but **not significantly associated with outcome**

### 5. Tissue interposition flaps (Recommendation 16, Strong / 1C)

For **recurrent or complex RVF**, vascularized tissue interposition is the operative inflection point. The Pastier head-to-head series found Martius and gracilis to have **equivalent success at 23 months (~69% each)** with shorter LOS and similar morbidity for Martius — supporting Martius as the **first-line interposition** and gracilis as **salvage** after Martius failure.<sup>[[11]](#ref11)</sup> A 2024 systematic review and proportional meta-analysis of Martius flaps reported **91.4% healing for primary RVF** and **94.6% for radiation-induced RVF**.<sup>[[12]](#ref12)</sup>

| Flap | Healing | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| **[Martius flap](/docs/foundations/surgical-principles/flaps/martius)** (bulbocavernosus / labial fat pad) | 65–94% (primary); 77.5% (recurrent); 91.4% pooled (primary); 94.6% (radiation)<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[11]](#ref11)[[12]](#ref12)</sup> | Shorter LOS than gracilis; **stoma not mandatory** (27% in Pastier series done without diversion); first-line tissue interposition |
| **[Gracilis muscle flap](/docs/foundations/surgical-principles/flaps/gracilis)** | 50–92% (individual series); 69% at 23 months<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[11]](#ref11)</sup> | Higher morbidity (28–47%): wound infection, thigh numbness, hematoma; reserve for salvage after Martius failure |

The only modifiable negative predictor of interposition success in the Pastier cohort was **smoking** (p = 0.02).<sup>[[11]](#ref11)</sup>

### 6. Anastomotic-related RVF (Recommendation 17, Strong / 1C)

RVF after low anterior resection or other colorectal anastomosis usually requires a **transabdominal approach**.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup>

- **Fecal diversion** is the typical first step; healing with diversion alone ~37%
- Persistent fistulas need redo colorectal anastomosis, ERAF, or transperineal interposition

### 7. Radiation-induced RVF (Recommendation 18, Weak / 2C)

The most challenging subset — endarteritis obliterans produces a hostile, progressive bed.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[8]](#ref8)</sup>

- Median time to radiation-induced RVF: **20 months** (range 5–240) after radiotherapy<sup>[[8]](#ref8)</sup>
- **Fecal diversion alone** was the only feasible option in **96%** of patients in one series; only **12%** healed spontaneously; healing was associated with fistula &gt; 7 cm from anal verge (OR 18) and loop ileostomy (OR 17)<sup>[[8]](#ref8)</sup>
- **Martius flap** for radiation RVF: healing in 11/12 and 13/14 in two series — high success<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[12]](#ref12)</sup>
- **Proctectomy with coloanal anastomosis** (sleeve excision or pull-through): healing **75–100%**<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[13]](#ref13)</sup>
- **Delayed coloanal anastomosis (Turnbull–Cutait)**: 81% success in the multicenter GRECCAR cohort, but with substantial morbidity (45% overall, 23% major) and durable functional sequelae (42% with severe LARS or permanent stoma)<sup>[[14]](#ref14)</sup>
- **Restorative resection** outperforms colostomy alone for symptom relief and quality of life — 30% of restorative-resection patients ultimately reverse stomas vs 0% in colostomy-only<sup>[[15]](#ref15)</sup>

### 8. Crohn's-disease-associated RVF

Combined medical–surgical management is mandatory.<sup>[[7]](#ref7)[[16]](#ref16)</sup>

- **Anti-TNF therapy (infliximab)** is first-line; combination with an immunomodulator is preferred for fistulizing disease
- **Antibiotics** (ciprofloxacin + metronidazole) as adjuncts
- **Setons before immunosuppression** for any complex tract
- **Surgical repair** only when **endoscopic healing of the rectosigmoid** has been achieved
- **Diverting stoma** for refractory disease; long-term success of diversion alone in perianal Crohn's is poor
- **Proctectomy / total proctocolectomy** with permanent stoma in the most severe cases

Outcomes are markedly worse than for traumatic RVF — **inflammatory etiologies (Crohn's, cryptoglandular, anastomotic)** had healing of only **7.1% after index surgery** and **46.4% at final follow-up**, vs **45.9% / 91.7%** for traumatic (obstetric / iatrogenic) fistulas.<sup>[[17]](#ref17)</sup>

## Outcomes and Prognostic Factors

| Factor | Effect |
|---|---|
| **Traumatic etiology** (obstetric / iatrogenic) | Best — 91.7% final healing<sup>[[17]](#ref17)</sup> |
| **Inflammatory etiology** (Crohn's, cryptoglandular, anastomotic) | Worst — 46.4% final healing (p = 0.001)<sup>[[17]](#ref17)</sup> |
| **Major procedure** (gracilis, coloanal anastomosis) for failed local repair | OR 6.4 for success<sup>[[6]](#ref6)</sup> |
| **Diverting stoma** in complex cases | OR 3.5 for success<sup>[[6]](#ref6)</sup> |
| **Early surgery** (within 9 months of diagnosis) | OR 2.3 for success<sup>[[6]](#ref6)</sup> |
| **Repair at a specialized center** | OR 3.2 for success<sup>[[6]](#ref6)</sup> |
| **Smoking** | Only modifiable negative predictor of interposition success<sup>[[11]](#ref11)</sup> |
| **Sphincter defect**, **prior radiation**, **recurrent fistula** | Predict ERAF failure<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup> |

In the Corte 79-patient / 286-procedure series, the operative algorithm of **early temporary stoma + escalating to a major procedure after failure of local repair** delivered the highest cumulative success rates.<sup>[[6]](#ref6)</sup>

---

## Operative Principles

- **Etiology drives strategy** — obstetric, Crohn's, radiation, and anastomotic RVF have distinct pathways
- **Sphincter assessment is mandatory** — sphincter defects mandate sphincteroplasty or episioproctotomy
- **Control infection and inflammation first** — drain, divert, and treat before definitive repair
- **Repair from the high-pressure side** — the rectal advancement flap covers the rectal opening, directing intraluminal pressure away from the suture line
- **Vascularized tissue interposition** for any recurrent, complex, or radiated fistula — Martius first, gracilis as salvage
- **Fecal diversion** is decided per patient — unequivocally helpful with major reconstruction and radiation, less essential for ERAF alone
- **Smoking cessation** is the only modifiable negative predictor in tissue-interposition repair<sup>[[11]](#ref11)</sup>
- **Fibrin glue and fistula plugs** have prohibitively poor success rates in RVF and are not recommended<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup>
