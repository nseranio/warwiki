# Rectourethral Fistula

A **rectourethral fistula (RUF)** is an epithelialized communication between the rectum and the urethra, prostatic fossa, or bladder neck. It is rare and operatively demanding. Acquired cases often follow prostate cancer treatment: radical prostatectomy or energy-based therapy (radiation, brachytherapy, cryotherapy, HIFU); but other causes are important. Transperineal reconstruction with vascularized tissue interposition is a commonly reported approach; closure estimates depend on radiation exposure, tissue injury, prior treatment and the definition of success.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[2]](#ref2)[[3]](#ref3)[[4]](#ref4)</sup>

For the female-perineum equivalent and the broader interposition-flap framework, see [Rectovaginal Fistula](/docs/clinical-conditions/03f-fistulas/in-females/rectovaginal). For the operative steps and donor-site anatomy of the gracilis flap itself, see [Gracilis Flap](/docs/foundations/surgical-principles/flaps/gracilis). For operative selection across all repair routes, see the [Male Fistula Repair database](/docs/surgical-techniques/04h-fistula-repair/male-fistula).

## Epidemiology

The following are historical study-specific incidence estimates, not directly comparable contemporary risks for choosing a prostate-cancer treatment.<sup>[[4]](#ref4)[[5]](#ref5)[[6]](#ref6)[[7]](#ref7)[[8]](#ref8)[[9]](#ref9)</sup>

| Etiology | Incidence |
|---|---|
| **Radical prostatectomy** | 0.34% (retropubic) – 1.04% (perineal)<sup>[[5]](#ref5)</sup> |
| **Brachytherapy monotherapy** | 0.2–0.26% (1 of 640; 12 of 4,690)<sup>[[6]](#ref6)[[7]](#ref7)</sup> |
| **Brachytherapy + EBRT (combined)** | 2.9% (2 of 69)<sup>[[7]](#ref7)</sup> |
| **Salvage brachytherapy** | 8.8% (4 of 45)<sup>[[7]](#ref7)</sup> |
| **Primary whole-gland cryotherapy** | 1.2% (~0.55% in modern era)<sup>[[9]](#ref9)</sup> |
| **HIFU — single session** | 1.17%<sup>[[8]](#ref8)</sup> |
| **HIFU — repeat sessions** | 13.6% (3 of 22)<sup>[[8]](#ref8)</sup> |
| **Salvage HIFU** | 4.5% (1 of 22)<sup>[[8]](#ref8)</sup> |

In the largest multi-institutional series (201 patients), 48.2% of RUFs followed radical prostatectomy alone and 51.8% followed energy ablation.<sup>[[4]](#ref4)</sup>

## Etiology and Mechanism

| Cause | Notes |
|---|---|
| **Radical prostatectomy** | Unrecognized rectal injury during posterior dissection at Denonvilliers' fascia; ~54% of post-RP RUFs had a rectal lesion primarily closed at the index operation; perineal approach 3.06× the risk of retropubic in one series of 2,447 (7 of 675 vs 6 of 1,772; p = 0.074, not statistically significant)<sup>[[5]](#ref5)</sup> |
| **Radiation / ablation** | Ischemic necrosis of the rectourethral septum weeks–months after treatment. **Post-treatment rectal biopsy of radiation ulcers, argon-plasma coagulation, and TURP** may increase fistula risk after brachytherapy; avoid biopsy of an anterior radiation-type rectal lesion over the prostate<sup>[[6]](#ref6)[[7]](#ref7)</sup> |
| **Trauma** | Penetrating perineal / pelvic injury (blast, gunshot, stab); pelvic fracture<sup>[[14]](#ref14)[[15]](#ref15)</sup> |
| **Crohn's disease** | ~0.3% of Crohn's patients; 6–11% of all GU fistulas in Crohn's (historical literature estimates)<sup>[[13]](#ref13)</sup> |
| **Cryptoglandular / perirectal sepsis** | Perianal abscess eroding into urethra |
| **Iatrogenic non-prostate** | Rectal surgery, transanal excision |

## Classification

### Muñoz etiologic (1998)<sup>[[11]](#ref11)</sup>

- **Benign:** Crohn's, trauma, perirectal sepsis, iatrogenic
- **Malignancy-related:** neoplasm at fistula site, radiation-induced, surgery-induced, combined

### Mundy & Andrich complexity (2011)<sup>[[17]](#ref17)</sup>

- **Simple.** Post-surgical (prostatectomy), no cavitation, no bladder neck contracture; amenable to primary repair
- **Complex.** Post-irradiation or post-ablation, **cavitation** (tissue loss creating a rectourethral cavity), bladder neck contracture, or extensive ischemia; requires interposition, often permanent diversion. Cavitation is most common after salvage HIFU following combined EBRT and brachytherapy

---

## Clinical Presentation

Symptoms develop days to weeks after surgery, weeks to months after radiation or ablation.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[5]](#ref5)[[12]](#ref12)</sup> The frequencies below come from one post-prostatectomy series of 29 men.<sup>[[18]](#ref18)</sup>

| Symptom | Frequency |
|---|---|
| **Pneumaturia** — often the earliest finding | ~24% |
| **Fecaluria** — pathognomonic | ~10% as presenting symptom |
| **Urine per rectum** | ~48% |
| **Recurrent UTI** | ~21% |
| **Dysuria** | ~21% |
| **Concurrent urethral stricture / BNC** | 14% non-irradiated; 26% irradiated<sup>[[4]](#ref4)[[19]](#ref19)</sup> |

A small fistula without fecaluria has a meaningful chance of conservative closure; fecaluria strongly favors considering fecal diversion, alongside sepsis, defect size and planned repair.<sup>[[5]](#ref5)</sup>

## Diagnostic Evaluation

| Step | Role |
|---|---|
| **Cystourethroscopy** | Maps urethral / prostatic-fossa opening; identifies concurrent stricture or BNC<sup>[[12]](#ref12)</sup> |
| **Proctoscopy / sigmoidoscopy** | Rectal opening; surrounding mucosa<sup>[[12]](#ref12)</sup> |
| **VCUG** | Confirms tract, demonstrates rectal extravasation<sup>[[12]](#ref12)</sup> |
| **CT with rectal contrast** | Tract anatomy, abscess, periureteral pathology |
| **MRI pelvis** | Best soft-tissue characterization; tissue quality and radiation-injury extent<sup>[[12]](#ref12)</sup> |
| **Examination under anesthesia** | Often necessary to fully define anatomy and tissue quality<sup>[[12]](#ref12)</sup> |
| **Biopsy of fistula edge** | Obtain when examination, imaging or the cancer history raises concern for recurrence; avoid unnecessary trauma in irradiated tissue<sup>[[12]](#ref12)</sup> |

## Management

The algorithm is etiology-driven, with the dominant axis being non-irradiated versus irradiated or ablation-induced.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[4]](#ref4)[[12]](#ref12)[[20]](#ref20)</sup>

### 1. Conservative management (selected non-irradiated patients)

For small fistulas without fecaluria: urethral catheter with or without suprapubic tube, bowel rest, antibiotics for sepsis. In one post-prostatectomy series, all 3 of 13 (23%) fistulas managed conservatively closed spontaneously (none had fecaluria), and in 47% in an algorithm-based cohort (27% before diversion, 20% after).<sup>[[5]](#ref5)[[16]](#ref16)</sup> Spontaneous closure is rare after radiation or ablation.<sup>[[20]](#ref20)</sup>

### 2. Fecal diversion

Fecal diversion may use colostomy or ileostomy. Consider it for substantial fecal contamination, sepsis, a large defect, failed conservative management or protection of a planned repair; selection and timing are individualized. Performed in 65–84% of patients before definitive repair.<sup>[[4]](#ref4)</sup> Diversion alone closes about 33% of post-prostatectomy RUFs and about 46% of post-traumatic RUFs.<sup>[[5]](#ref5)[[15]](#ref15)</sup>

**Interpret the endpoint correctly:** the Lahey series reported that 97% of nonirradiated patients had their bowel undiverted after repair. This is a restoration-of-continuity outcome, not evidence that 97% underwent fistula repair without a preceding stoma; initial diversion remains individualized.<sup>[[2]](#ref2)</sup>

### 3. Surgical repair — first repair is the best repair

Subsequent repairs are progressively harder; success drops with each attempt.<sup>[[21]](#ref21)[[27]](#ref27)</sup>

#### Surgical approach selection

| Approach | Best fit | Success |
|---|---|---|
| **Transperineal + gracilis flap** ± BMG | Often selected for complex reconstruction and compromised tissue | Selected cohort estimates; see denominators below<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[2]](#ref2)[[3]](#ref3)[[4]](#ref4)</sup> |
| **Transsphincteric (York-Mason)** | Selected defects suitable for direct posterior closure, often nonirradiated | Separate first-repair closure, eventual closure and bowel function; see below<sup>[[10]](#ref10)[[27]](#ref27)[[28]](#ref28)[[29]](#ref29)[[33]](#ref33)</sup> |
| **Transanal** (advancement flap, MITAR, robotic TAMIS) | Selected local repairs; the 1.5-cm threshold belongs to the Nicita MITAR series | Small, heterogeneous series; no established platform ranking<sup>[[25]](#ref25)[[35]](#ref35)</sup> |
| **Transabdominal / robotic** | Complex irradiated; needs salvage prostatectomy, omental flap, proctectomy, or concurrent VUAS repair | Variable<sup>[[26]](#ref26)[[30]](#ref30)[[36]](#ref36)</sup> |

---

### A. Transperineal repair with gracilis flap (Lahey / Vanni–Zinman–Buckley)

The reference operation for both non-irradiated and irradiated RUF.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[3]](#ref3)[[12]](#ref12)[[23]](#ref23)</sup>

**Steps:**

1. Exaggerated lithotomy
2. Vertical or inverted-U perineal incision
3. Dissection through the perineal body to the fistula tract
4. Separation of rectum from urethra / prostatic fossa
5. Excision of the tract
6. **Two-layer rectal closure**
7. **Urethral closure.** Primary or with **buccal mucosal graft (BMG) onlay** when there is a concurrent urethral stricture or significant urethral tissue loss
8. **Gracilis harvest** from the medial thigh on the medial circumflex femoral pedicle, tunneled subcutaneously to the perineum, interposed between rectal and urethral suture lines
9. Suprapubic and urethral catheter
10. Cystogram at 3–4 weeks before catheter removal

**Outcomes:**

| Series | N | Non-irradiated | Irradiated |
|---|---|---|---|
| Vanni 2010 | 74 | **100%** | **84%**<sup>[[2]](#ref2)</sup> |
| Kaufman / Lahey 2016 | 98 | **98%** | **86%**<sup>[[32]](#ref32)</sup> |
| Harris multi-institutional 2017 | 201 | **99%** | **87%**<sup>[[4]](#ref4)</sup> |
| Sbizzera Eur Urol 2022 | 21 | **20/21 overall** | Mixed cohort; not an irradiated-only estimate<sup>[[22]](#ref22)</sup> |
| Muñoz-Duyos 2017 | 9 | **100%** | —<sup>[[31]](#ref31)</sup> |

**Concurrent urethral stricture (BMG patch onlay):** present in 11% of non-irradiated and 28% of irradiated RUFs; in a series of 23 patients with concurrent posterior urethral stenosis, simultaneous urethroplasty and RUF repair achieved 87% fistula closure at median 56 months.<sup>[[2]](#ref2)[[19]](#ref19)</sup>

---

### B. York-Mason transsphincteric repair

**Technique:**<sup>[[10]](#ref10)[[12]](#ref12)[[28]](#ref28)[[29]](#ref29)[[33]](#ref33)</sup>

1. Prone jackknife
2. Parasacral incision from coccyx to anal verge
3. Posterior-midline (6 o'clock) division of external and internal sphincter complex
4. Direct exposure of the anterior rectal wall and fistula
5. Tract excision; urethral closure; rectal closure
6. Anatomic re-approximation of the divided sphincter complex in labeled layers
7. Optional dartos / gluteal-fat interposition<sup>[[24]](#ref24)[[28]](#ref28)</sup>

**Outcomes:**

| Series | N | Success | Continence |
|---|---|---|---|
| van der Graaf 2025 | 12 total; **8 nonirradiated first fistula repairs** | All eight in that subgroup closed | Five had colostomy reversal, with LARS scores 0–20; median 5.1 years applies to the eight-patient subgroup<sup>[[10]](#ref10)</sup> |
| Dafnis 2018 | 20 | **18/20** closed | 13 had stoma reversal and were reported continent; median follow-up 84.7 months<sup>[[29]](#ref29)</sup> |
| McKibben 2018 | 17 | **16/17** closed | Among ten questionnaire respondents, two had rare fecal incontinence; mean Wexner 1.4/20<sup>[[28]](#ref28)</sup> |
| Falavolti 2013 | 39 | Overall over 50%; approximately 90% with one previous operation | The abstract does not justify a specific success rate after two failed fistula repairs<sup>[[27]](#ref27)</sup> |
| Dal Moro 2011 | 14 | All initially closed; one Crohn-associated recurrence at 11 years | Twenty years describes the experience period, not each patient's follow-up<sup>[[33]](#ref33)</sup> |

**Where York–Mason fits:** selected, usually nonirradiated RUFs suitable for closure through a posterior exposure. Radiation warrants particular caution and may favor another route with vascularized interposition, but is not an absolute prohibition. The two irradiated patients in the 2025 report cannot establish a rule based on radiation timing. Fecal continence is not guaranteed. In a 2026 multicenter retrospective comparison (57 patients; York-Mason 27, transperineal gracilis 30), gracilis repair had higher first-attempt success (77% versus 44%) and overall success (94% versus 70%), and complex fistula morphology predicted York-Mason failure; the groups were not randomized and follow-up differed (median 53 versus 27 months).<sup>[[10]](#ref10)[[34]](#ref34)[[41]](#ref41)</sup>

---

### C. Minimally invasive approaches

- **MITAR** (through a Parks' retractor): 12 selected patients, all closed at median 21 months; one early complication. Fistulas larger than 1.5 cm, sepsis and fecaluria were excluded; radiation-induced fistulas were not studied<sup>[[25]](#ref25)</sup>
- **Robotic TAMIS:** two selected nonirradiated patients, both with diverting loop ileostomy, had no recurrence at at least 15 months. This is feasibility evidence<sup>[[35]](#ref35)</sup>
- **Robotic or laparoscopic transabdominal repair:** a selected 15-patient series included nine robotic and six laparoscopic operations; all closed at 12 months, with nine postoperative complications reported. Separate reports address simultaneous fistula and outlet-stricture reconstruction<sup>[[30]](#ref30)[[36]](#ref36)</sup>
- **Transanal endoscopic surgery (TEO/TEM):** one RUF series achieved closure in only 2/8 repairs. All four mesh recipients and two of four non-mesh recipients recurred; this uncontrolled comparison does not prove that mesh caused failure<sup>[[37]](#ref37)</sup>

---

### 4. Radiation/ablation-induced RUF — the difficult subset

Radiation and ablation RUFs differ fundamentally and need a different mental model:<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[4]](#ref4)[[17]](#ref17)[[20]](#ref20)[[34]](#ref34)[[38]](#ref38)</sup>

- Higher concurrent urethral stricture / BNC (26% versus 14%)
- Higher post-repair urinary incontinence (35% versus 16%)
- Higher permanent fecal diversion (31–86% versus 0–3%)
- Higher permanent urinary diversion (up to 93% versus 6% in one series)
- **Consider vascularized interposition and tissue quality carefully.** The cited 17% versus 87% comparison was primary-repair success in irradiated or ablated (1 of 6) versus nonirradiated (13 of 15) patients, not repair without versus with a flap<sup>[[20]](#ref20)</sup>
- **Salvage prostatectomy** may be required when a discrete prostate remains<sup>[[17]](#ref17)[[26]](#ref26)</sup>
- **Proctectomy with coloanal pull-through** (Turnbull–Cutait) for severe rectal injury<sup>[[39]](#ref39)[[40]](#ref40)</sup>
- **Permanent dual diversion** (fecal and urinary) should be discussed early as a legitimate primary option, required in about 50% of radiation or ablation patients in one multi-institutional series<sup>[[38]](#ref38)</sup>

## Concurrent Posterior Urethral Reconstruction

Posterior urethral stenosis is present in about 18% of RUF patients and complicates repair. In a Cleveland Clinic series of 23 patients, simultaneous posterior urethroplasty and RUF repair achieved 87% fistula closure; 78% of urethroplasty was anastomotic and 22% used BMG. Postoperative urinary incontinence in 61%, with 30% ultimately needing artificial urinary sphincter, but no isolated stricture recurrences requiring instrumentation.<sup>[[19]](#ref19)</sup>

**Concurrent posterior urethral stenosis does not automatically exclude restorative surgery**.<sup>[[19]](#ref19)</sup>

## Long-Term Functional Outcomes

Even after successful closure, functional sequelae are common and must be discussed preoperatively.<sup>[[4]](#ref4)[[18]](#ref18)[[19]](#ref19)[[20]](#ref20)[[28]](#ref28)</sup>

| Outcome | Rate |
|---|---|
| Post-repair urinary incontinence | 16% (non-irradiated) → 61% (in concurrent urethroplasty cohorts) |
| Stoma reversal / bowel continuity restored | Non-irradiated 91–94%; irradiated 55–65% (10 of 12 diverted patients in the Sbizzera mixed cohort)<sup>[[32]](#ref32)[[34]](#ref34)[[22]](#ref22)</sup> |
| Eventual AUS placement | ~30% in concurrent-urethroplasty series |
| Permanent fecal diversion | 0–3% (non-irradiated); 31–86% (irradiated) |
| Permanent urinary diversion | 6–20% (non-irradiated); up to 93% (irradiated, severe) |
| Fecal continence after York-Mason | Often preserved in selected series; Wexner is scored /20 and St Mark’s /24 and should not be combined |
| Patient satisfaction | High (mean 9/10) despite incontinence |
| Decision regret | Negligible (median 0/100) |

A 2026 long-term outcomes study (median follow-up 50 months) reported 96% 5-year recurrence-free survival (75% for any disease-related re-intervention-free survival) after open RUF repair in 29 mostly non-irradiated men, with restored voiding function, mild fecal incontinence, high patient satisfaction, and negligible decisional regret, though moderate urinary incontinence persisted in some.<sup>[[18]](#ref18)</sup>

## Algorithm Summary

1. **Confirm diagnosis.** Cystoscopy, proctoscopy, VCUG, MRI; biopsy suspicious tissue when recurrence is a concern
2. **Characterize.** Size, location, etiology, concurrent stricture / BNC, tissue quality, cavitation
3. **Conservative trial** for small fistula without fecaluria (catheter ± SP tube, bowel rest)
4. **Plan fecal diversion individually**, particularly with sepsis, substantial contamination or complex reconstruction
5. **Definitive repair**
   - Non-irradiated, small or simple: York-Mason (or MITAR in selected cases)
   - Non-irradiated, larger or complex: transperineal gracilis, with or without BMG
   - **Irradiated / ablation-induced:** consider vascularized interposition and the route needed for reconstruction; add BMG only when the urinary defect requires it. Salvage prostatectomy or permanent diversion depends on tissue destruction, function and goals
6. **Failed repair / devastated pelvis:** permanent fecal and/or urinary diversion; pelvic exenteration as last resort<sup>[[11]](#ref11)[[38]](#ref38)</sup>

---

## Operative Principles

- The first repair is the best repair<sup>[[21]](#ref21)[[27]](#ref27)</sup>
- **Etiology dictates complexity.** Non-irradiated and irradiated or ablation-induced RUFs are different operations with different expectations
- **Vascularized tissue interposition** (gracilis, omentum, dartos) is often valuable in irradiated or complex repairs; select it according to tissue viability, dead space and the reconstruction rather than treating every fistula identically
- **Multidisciplinary planning** with colorectal surgery is the rule
- **Treat concurrent urethral pathology simultaneously** when feasible<sup>[[19]](#ref19)</sup>
- **Counsel about post-repair incontinence and possible AUS** as part of the preoperative conversation
- **Permanent diversion is not failure.** It is the right operation for the right radiation or ablation patient<sup>[[20]](#ref20)[[38]](#ref38)</sup>
