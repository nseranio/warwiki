# Vesicovaginal Fistula

A vesicovaginal fistula (VVF) is an abnormal epithelialized communication between the bladder and the vagina, resulting in continuous urinary incontinence per vaginum. It is the most common acquired genitourinary fistula in the developed world.

## Etiology

| Cause | Notes |
|---|---|
| Gynecologic surgery | Most common cause in developed countries; hysterectomy accounts for ~75% of cases |
| Obstetric injury | Leading cause globally; prolonged obstructed labor causing pressure necrosis |
| Pelvic radiation | Delayed presentation; tissue ischemia and poor healing capacity |
| Malignancy | Direct tumor invasion or post-treatment necrosis |
| Trauma | Penetrating injury, foreign body |

## Presentation

- Continuous, painless urinary leakage per vaginum, typically beginning 1–2 weeks post-operatively
- Leakage is often positional and worse supine
- Recurrent UTIs, vaginal irritation, skin excoriation
- Reduced or absent voiding per urethra (if fistula is large)

## Evaluation

### Office
- Tampon test / dye test: instill methylene blue or dilute betadine into the bladder; blue staining of the proximal tampon confirms VVF (distal staining suggests urethrovaginal fistula)
- Vaginoscopy: direct visualization

### Imaging & Endoscopy
- **Cystoscopy**: identifies fistula location relative to ureteral orifices; assess for concurrent ureteral involvement
- **CT urogram or fistulogram**: delineates anatomy, rules out ureterovaginal fistula
- **MRI pelvis**: preferred in radiation or recurrent cases to assess tissue quality and extent

### Additional Workup
- Urine culture prior to repair
- Urodynamics if concurrent voiding dysfunction suspected
- Consider EUA if fistula not clearly visualized in office

## Classification

- **Juxta-urethral**: within 1.5 cm of ureteral orifice — increases complexity, may require ureteral reimplantation
- **Simple**: single, small (<0.5 cm), non-irradiated, first repair
- **Complex**: large, multiple, irradiated, recurrent, or involving the ureter

## Management

### Conservative
- Small (<3 mm), early-recognized fistulas may close with prolonged catheter drainage (4–6 weeks) and anti-spasmodics
- Success rate low; most require surgical repair

### Timing of Repair
- **Early repair** (within days): appropriate for iatrogenic injury recognized intraoperatively or immediately post-operatively with healthy tissue
- **Delayed repair** (≥3 months): traditionally favored to allow edema and inflammation to resolve; most common approach
- Radiation fistulas: delay longer (6–12 months); tissue quality is paramount

### Surgical Repair

#### Approach Selection
| Approach | Indication |
|---|---|
| Transvaginal | Small to moderate, non-irradiated, good vaginal access |
| Transabdominal (O'Conor) | Large, complex, juxta-ureteral, recurrent |
| Robotic/laparoscopic | Surgeon preference; equivalent outcomes to open in experienced hands |

#### Principles (Latzko / Layered Closure)
1. Wide excision of fistula tract and surrounding fibrosis
2. Tension-free, multi-layer, water-tight closure with non-overlapping suture lines
3. Interposition of well-vascularized tissue (Martius flap, peritoneal flap, omental pedicle) in complex or irradiated cases
4. Urethral catheter drainage for 10–14 days post-operatively

## Key Points

- Ureteral orifice proximity must be assessed at cystoscopy prior to every repair
- Martius flap (labial fat pad) is the workhorse interposition for transvaginal repair
- Radiation fistulas almost always require vascularized tissue interposition; consider omentum or gracilis
- Success rates >90% for first-time repair of simple VVF; drops with each recurrence
