# Ureterovaginal Fistula

A ureterovaginal fistula (UVF) is an abnormal communication between the ureter and the vagina, most commonly resulting from inadvertent ureteral injury during pelvic surgery. It presents similarly to VVF but requires a distinct diagnostic and surgical approach.

## Etiology

- **Gynecologic surgery**: hysterectomy is the most common cause; injury typically occurs at the level of the cardinal ligament or ureterovesical junction
- **Pelvic radiation**: delayed ischemic necrosis
- **Malignancy**: direct invasion or post-resection
- **Obstetric injury**: less common than with VVF

## Presentation

- Continuous urinary leakage per vaginum beginning 5–14 days post-operatively (delayed presentation as devascularized ureter sloughs)
- Unlike VVF, the patient often continues to void normally per urethra (contralateral kidney functioning)
- Flank pain or hydronephrosis may be present if obstruction coexists
- Fever, urinoma, or urosepsis in unrecognized cases

## Evaluation

- **Tampon test with IV indigo carmine or oral phenazopyridine**: blue or orange staining of tampon distinguishes UVF from VVF — ureteral injury produces staining without bladder dye instillation
- **CT urogram**: investigation of choice; demonstrates hydroureteronephrosis, contrast extravasation, and fistula location
- **Retrograde pyelogram**: definitive anatomic delineation
- **Cystoscopy with ureteral catheterization**: assess ureteral orifice patency; attempt retrograde stent placement
- **Renal scintigraphy (MAG3)**: assess differential renal function if nephrectomy is being considered in severe cases

## Classification

- **Location**: upper, mid, or distal ureter (distal most common)
- **Mechanism**: ligation/devascularization vs. transection vs. thermal injury
- **Associated obstruction**: partial vs. complete

## Management

### Initial (Temporizing)
- Attempt **retrograde ureteral stent** placement (if ureteral orifice accessible and injury amenable); allows fistula to heal in ~30–50% of cases
- If retrograde fails: **percutaneous nephrostomy** to decompress the upper tract and allow inflammation to resolve prior to definitive repair

### Definitive Surgical Repair
- Timing: after 6–12 weeks of drainage to allow tissue recovery
- Approach dictated by level of injury and degree of ureteral loss:

| Injury Level | Preferred Repair |
|---|---|
| Distal ureter | Ureteroneocystostomy ± psoas hitch |
| Mid ureter | Ureteroureterostomy or Boari flap |
| Long segment loss | Ileal ureter; renal autotransplantation |
| Poor tissue / radiation | Interposition graft; vascularized tissue |

- Principles: spatulated, tension-free, stented anastomosis; retroperitoneal drainage

## Key Points

- Always rule out concurrent VVF or contralateral ureteral injury
- Retrograde stenting should be attempted before committing to open repair
- Ureteral vascularity (not just continuity) determines viability — a ureter that looks intact may still slough
- Psoas hitch dramatically increases the length of bladder that can be mobilized for a tension-free reimplant
