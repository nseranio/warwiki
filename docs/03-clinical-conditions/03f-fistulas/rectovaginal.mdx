# Rectovaginal Fistula

A rectovaginal fistula (RVF) is an epithelialized tract between the rectum and vagina, resulting in passage of stool, flatus, and/or purulent material through the vagina. Though primarily managed by colorectal surgery, functional urologists frequently encounter RVF in the context of pelvic reconstruction, obstetric injury repair, and complex multi-compartment fistula cases.

## Etiology

| Cause | Notes |
|---|---|
| Obstetric injury | Most common; 4th-degree laceration, prolonged labor, instrumental delivery |
| Inflammatory bowel disease | Crohn's disease; associated with poor tissue healing |
| Pelvic radiation | Delayed; often associated with concurrent urinary fistula |
| Surgical injury | Low anterior resection, episiotomy repair, hysterectomy |
| Malignancy | Cervical, rectal, or vaginal cancer; direct invasion |
| Infection | Bartholin abscess, diverticulitis, perirectal abscess |

## Presentation

- Passage of flatus or feces per vaginum
- Foul-smelling vaginal discharge
- Recurrent vaginitis or UTIs
- Perineal pain or dyspareunia
- Symptoms may be subtle (flatus only) or debilitating (frank fecal incontinence)

## Classification

**By location:**
- **Low RVF**: at or just above the dentate line; approached transperineally or transvaginally
- **Mid RVF**: mid-vaginal septum; most common
- **High RVF**: at or near vaginal apex; typically requires transabdominal approach

**By complexity:**
- **Simple**: small (&lt;2.5 cm), low, single, no prior repair, healthy tissue
- **Complex**: large, high, irradiated, recurrent, or IBD-associated

## Evaluation

- **Physical examination**: EUA with probing of the tract; vaginoscopy and proctoscopy
- **MRI pelvis**: best modality for defining tract anatomy, sphincter integrity, and adjacent structures
- **Endoanal ultrasound**: assess sphincter complex — critical if fecal incontinence coexists
- **Anorectal manometry**: baseline sphincter function prior to repair
- **Contrast enema or fistulogram**: delineate complex tracts
- **Colonoscopy**: rule out IBD or malignancy

## Management

### Bowel Preparation & Optimization
- Mechanical bowel prep and antibiotics pre-operatively
- Nutritional optimization, particularly in IBD or radiation cases
- Consider fecal diversion (loop colostomy or ileostomy) prior to repair in complex cases

### Conservative
- Small, asymptomatic fistulas (especially in the immediate post-partum period) may close spontaneously with conservative measures; allow 3–6 months
- Fibrin glue or plug: limited role; may be considered for simple low fistulas

### Surgical Repair

#### Approach by Location
| Location | Preferred Approach |
|---|---|
| Low/mid (simple) | Transperineal or transvaginal layered closure ± sphincteroplasty |
| Low (recurrent) | Martius flap interposition; advancement flap |
| High | Transabdominal; laparoscopic or robotic |
| Irradiated / Complex | Gracilis muscle flap or omental interposition |

#### Key Surgical Principles
1. Complete excision of fistula tract
2. Tension-free, multi-layer closure with non-overlapping suture lines
3. Repair any concurrent sphincter defect (overlapping sphincteroplasty)
4. Vascularized tissue interposition mandatory in irradiated, Crohn's, or recurrent cases
5. Proximal fecal diversion improves healing rates in complex repair

## Key Points

- Always assess sphincter integrity — an unrepaired defect will doom the repair
- Crohn's-associated RVF requires IBD to be in remission before surgical repair; biologic agents should be optimized pre-operatively
- Radiation RVF has the worst outcomes; gracilis muscle flap provides the most robust vascularized tissue for perineal reconstruction
- Fecal diversion reduces but does not eliminate repair failure
