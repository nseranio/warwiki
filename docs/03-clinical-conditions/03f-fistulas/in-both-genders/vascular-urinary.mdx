# Vascular-Urinary Fistula

**Vascular-urinary fistula** covers two clinically distinct entities: the **arterioureteral fistula (AUF / UAF)** — a communication between an artery (most often the iliac) and the ureter — and the **renal arteriovenous fistula (AVF)** — a communication between a renal artery branch and a renal vein. AUF is the more clinically urgent: rare but potentially lethal, with rising incidence driven by chronic ureteral stents and pelvic oncologic surgery / radiation.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[2]](#ref2)</sup>

See also: [The Ureters](/docs/foundations/anatomy-physiology/urinary-tract/ureter-anatomy-physiology), [Pelvic Vascular Anatomy](/docs/foundations/anatomy-physiology/pelvis-support/pelvic-vascular-anatomy), [Fistulas landing page](/docs/clinical-conditions/03f-fistulas).

## Part 1 — Arterioureteral Fistula (AUF)

### Definition

An arterioureteral fistula is an abnormal communication between an artery — most commonly the iliac — and the ureter. It has been described as a "wolf in sheep's clothing" because of its deceptive presentation with intermittent hematuria that can rapidly escalate to life-threatening hemorrhage.<sup>[[3]](#ref3)</sup>

### Epidemiology

- Estimated nationwide incidence in the Netherlands ~**3.5 cases / year**.<sup>[[4]](#ref4)</sup>
- Mean age **66 years** (range 29–90).<sup>[[5]](#ref5)</sup>
- **Female predominance ~59%**, reflecting the higher prevalence of pelvic malignancies (cervical, uterine) requiring surgery and radiation in women.<sup>[[5]](#ref5)</sup>
- Reported cases have risen substantially over the past two decades, attributed to growing use of chronic indwelling ureteral stents and improved survival of pelvic-cancer patients.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[2]](#ref2)</sup>

### Classification

| Class | Description |
|---|---|
| **Primary AUF** | Rare; direct erosion of a native (typically aneurysmal) iliac artery into the ureter without prior surgery. Most often associated with aortoiliac aneurysmal disease.<sup>[[6]](#ref6)</sup> |
| **Secondary AUF** | The vast majority; occurs in patients with prior pelvic surgery, radiation, vascular grafting, and / or chronic ureteral stenting. The dominant form in contemporary practice.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[6]](#ref6)</sup> |

### Etiology and Risk Factors

| Risk factor | Prevalence among AUF patients |
|---|---|
| Chronic indwelling ureteral stent | **72–80%**<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[2]](#ref2)</sup> |
| History of pelvic surgery | **~87%**<sup>[[2]](#ref2)</sup> |
| Pelvic malignancy | **67–70%**<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[2]](#ref2)</sup> |
| Pelvic radiotherapy | **45–88%**<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[2]](#ref2)[[3]](#ref3)</sup> |
| Prior aortoiliac vascular surgery | Variable (especially primary AUF)<sup>[[4]](#ref4)[[5]](#ref5)</sup> |
| Ileal-conduit urinary diversion | Important subset<sup>[[6]](#ref6)[[7]](#ref7)</sup> |

The archetypal AUF patient has a history of pelvic cancer (cervical, colorectal, bladder), prior surgery and radiation, and a chronic indwelling ureteral stent for malignant or post-treatment ureteral obstruction.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[2]](#ref2)</sup>

### Pathophysiology

The mechanism converges on the anatomic crossing of the ureter and iliac artery:<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[5]](#ref5)[[6]](#ref6)</sup>

1. **Anatomic proximity** — the ureter crosses anterior to the common iliac bifurcation; **47.7%** of AUF occur at the common iliac.<sup>[[5]](#ref5)</sup>
2. **Loss of protective tissue planes** — pelvic surgery and radiation destroy the fibrofatty layer separating ureter from artery.
3. **Chronic ureteral stent irritation** — the rigid stent exerts continuous mechanical pressure at the crossing point and prevents the ureter from moving away from the artery.
4. **Radiation-induced arterial wall injury** — endarteritis obliterans weakens the arterial wall and predisposes to pseudoaneurysm and fistulization.
5. **Vascular graft erosion** — synthetic graft material from prior aortoiliac reconstruction can erode into the adjacent ureter.<sup>[[6]](#ref6)[[9]](#ref9)[[10]](#ref10)</sup>

A **rigid foreign body** (stent) pressing against a **weakened arterial wall** in a **scarred, devascularized tissue bed** creates the conditions for fistula formation.

### Location

| Artery | Frequency<sup>[[5]](#ref5)</sup> |
|---|---|
| Common iliac | **47.7%** |
| External iliac | **35.7%** |
| Internal iliac | **10.6%** |
| Aorta | Rare |
| Other (femoral, renal) | Rare |

### Clinical Presentation

The hallmark is **hematuria** with a characteristic pattern:<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[2]](#ref2)[[3]](#ref3)[[5]](#ref5)</sup>

- **Hematuria** in **98.7–99%**
  - **Massive, life-threatening** in **76%**, often preceded by intermittent microhematuria or self-limited gross hematuria — **"herald bleeds"**
  - **Intermittent microhematuria** as the sole presentation in **9%**<sup>[[4]](#ref4)</sup>
  - Bleeding frequently occurs **at the time of stent exchange** — **42%** (11/26) in one series<sup>[[2]](#ref2)</sup>
- **Hemorrhagic shock** — vasopressors (**46%**), transfusion (**92%**)<sup>[[2]](#ref2)</sup>
- **Flank pain** **42%**<sup>[[2]](#ref2)</sup>
- **Bladder tamponade** from clot retention

The intermittent pattern is the diagnostic clue — the fistula may be temporarily sealed by clot or by the ureteral stent, leading to a deceptive period of resolution before catastrophic rebleeding. Any "herald bleed" in an at-risk patient should raise suspicion for AUF.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[3]](#ref3)[[11]](#ref11)</sup>

### Diagnosis

No single test is highly sensitive; **clinical suspicion is the most important factor**. The diagnosis was made only at laparotomy in **36%** of patients in one series.<sup>[[3]](#ref3)</sup>

| Test | Sensitivity | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| **Conventional angiography** | 58–66% | Most sensitive single test; allows simultaneous treatment<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[2]](#ref2)[[3]](#ref3)</sup> |
| **Provocative angiography** | Higher | Stent / guidewire manipulation during angiography to disrupt sealing clot; demonstrated the fistula in **100%** when standard angiography was negative<sup>[[12]](#ref12)[[13]](#ref13)</sup> |
| **CT angiography** | 33–36% | Reasonable first imaging study; shows extravasation, pseudoaneurysm, hydronephrosis<sup>[[2]](#ref2)[[6]](#ref6)</sup> |
| **Retrograde / antegrade pyelography** | Variable | May show contrast entering the vascular system<sup>[[7]](#ref7)[[8]](#ref8)</sup> |
| **Ureteroscopy** | Variable | Direct visualization; may provoke bleeding at the fistula site<sup>[[9]](#ref9)</sup> |
| **Intraoperative diagnosis** | — | At laparotomy in 36% of cases<sup>[[3]](#ref3)</sup> |

**Recommended workflow:**<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[2]](#ref2)[[5]](#ref5)[[11]](#ref11)</sup>

1. High clinical suspicion in any at-risk patient with hematuria.
2. **CT angiography** as the first imaging study for availability and adjunctive findings, despite its modest direct-fistula sensitivity (~33%).<sup>[[5]](#ref5)</sup>
3. **Interventional angiography** promptly when AUF is suspected — both the most sensitive test and the immediate treatment modality. CT angiography may be skipped when suspicion is high.<sup>[[2]](#ref2)</sup>
4. **Provocative angiography** when standard angiography is non-diagnostic.<sup>[[12]](#ref12)[[13]](#ref13)</sup>

### Management

AUF is a **surgical / interventional emergency** requiring a coordinated urology / vascular surgery / interventional radiology response.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[2]](#ref2)[[14]](#ref14)</sup>

#### 1. Endovascular treatment (preferred initial approach)

Covered stent-graft placement is now first-line for most AUF, particularly in unstable or high-comorbidity patients.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[2]](#ref2)[[8]](#ref8)[[14]](#ref14)</sup>

- **Technical success 91–100%**<sup>[[2]](#ref2)[[8]](#ref8)[[15]](#ref15)</sup>
- Deployment of a covered stent-graft (Viabahn, iCast) across the fistula in the iliac artery, often with internal-iliac exclusion (coil or plug) to prevent retrograde flow<sup>[[8]](#ref8)[[14]](#ref14)</sup>
- Avoids laparotomy in a hostile pelvis

**Endovascular complications:**

- **Stent-graft infection** — the most significant concern; **14–50%** require reintervention. Particularly high in ileal-conduit patients given chronic colonization.<sup>[[7]](#ref7)[[14]](#ref14)</sup>
- **Recurrent hematuria** — **24–36%** at 1–2 years; the hematuria recurrence-free rate at 2 years was only **40.6%** in one long-term series.<sup>[[15]](#ref15)</sup>
- **Stent thrombosis** with lower-limb ischemia<sup>[[16]](#ref16)[[17]](#ref17)</sup>
- **Stent migration** — rare but dramatic; bladder migration has been reported<sup>[[17]](#ref17)</sup>
- **Lower-extremity morbidity** — ulceration, ischemia, amputation in **53%** of patients in one series despite anticoagulation<sup>[[16]](#ref16)</sup>

**Long-term endovascular outcomes:**<sup>[[8]](#ref8)[[15]](#ref15)</sup>

- Freedom from recurrent massive hematuria at 3 years: **76%**
- Primary patency at 3 years: **81%**; secondary patency **92%**
- Freedom from stent-graft infection at 3 years: 100% in one series, but **14.3%** infection rate in ileal-conduit patients<sup>[[7]](#ref7)[[8]](#ref8)</sup>

#### 2. Open surgical repair

Indicated in specific scenarios:<sup>[[8]](#ref8)[[14]](#ref14)[[18]](#ref18)</sup>

- Preexisting vascular reconstruction (e.g., aortobifemoral graft) — endovascular stenting within a prior graft is technically challenging and infection-prone
- Manifest infection — abscess, sepsis, infected endograft
- Enteric contamination — concurrent enteric fistula
- Failed endovascular treatment — recurrent bleeding or stent-graft infection after initial endovascular repair

**Techniques:**

- **Arterial ligation with extra-anatomic bypass** (femoral-femoral crossover graft) — classic; avoids operating in the irradiated, scarred pelvis<sup>[[6]](#ref6)[[12]](#ref12)</sup>
- **Direct arterial repair** — primary repair or interposition graft; technically challenging in a hostile pelvis<sup>[[8]](#ref8)</sup>
- **Arterial embolization with extra-anatomic bypass** — percutaneous coil embolization of the common iliac followed by surgical fem-fem bypass; avoids laparotomy<sup>[[12]](#ref12)</sup>
- **Ureteral management** — nephrectomy, ureteral ligation, or PCN as needed<sup>[[8]](#ref8)[[16]](#ref16)</sup>

**Open-repair complications:**<sup>[[8]](#ref8)[[16]](#ref16)</sup>

- Enterocutaneous fistula in **37.5%** (3/8) in one series
- Wound infection in **25%**
- Overall complication rate: **63% open vs. 36% endovascular**

#### 3. Hybrid approaches

Initial endovascular stent-graft for hemorrhage control followed by planned open reconstruction is increasingly used.<sup>[[18]](#ref18)</sup>

### Mortality and Prognosis

- **AUF-specific mortality** has fallen from **~19% before 2000** to **~7% after 2000**, paralleling adoption of endovascular stent-grafts.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup>
- **In-hospital mortality 7.7–10%** in contemporary series.<sup>[[2]](#ref2)[[16]](#ref16)</sup>
- **Overall mortality 47%** at mean 15.5-month follow-up — driven by underlying advanced malignancy.<sup>[[16]](#ref16)</sup>
- **5-year survival 42%** vs. **93%** for the general population (p &lt; 0.05).<sup>[[8]](#ref8)</sup>
- **Recurrent AUF** in **12.5–15%** regardless of treatment modality.<sup>[[2]](#ref2)</sup>
- Patients with ureteral complications after aortoiliac reconstruction were **4.4× more likely** to develop graft complications (p &lt; 0.05).<sup>[[10]](#ref10)</sup>

## Part 2 — Renal Arteriovenous Fistula (AVF)

### Definition

A renal AVF is an abnormal communication between a renal artery branch and a renal vein, bypassing the capillary bed. It is distinct from AUF in that the abnormality is intrarenal rather than ureteral.<sup>[[19]](#ref19)[[20]](#ref20)</sup>

### Classification

**Congenital arteriovenous malformations (AVMs):**<sup>[[19]](#ref19)[[20]](#ref20)[[21]](#ref21)[[22]](#ref22)</sup>

- Rare (~20% of renal vascular malformations)
- Three subtypes: **cirsoid** (multiple coiled vessels with a nidus — most common congenital type), **angiomatous / cavernous** (small, multiple communications resembling a hemangioma), and **aneurysmal** (single high-flow communication with an aneurysmal draining vein)<sup>[[23]](#ref23)</sup>

**Acquired (iatrogenic / traumatic) AVFs:**<sup>[[19]](#ref19)</sup>

- Far more common than congenital AVMs
- Single direct artery–vein communication without a nidus
- Causes:
  - **Percutaneous renal biopsy** — most common; AVF detected in **14.4%** of biopsies on routine Doppler, but **95% are asymptomatic**<sup>[[24]](#ref24)</sup>
  - **PCNL and other percutaneous renal procedures**<sup>[[25]](#ref25)</sup>
  - **Partial nephrectomy** (open or laparoscopic)<sup>[[25]](#ref25)</sup>
  - **Penetrating** or **blunt trauma**

### Clinical Presentation

**Congenital AVMs:**<sup>[[20]](#ref20)[[21]](#ref21)[[23]](#ref23)</sup>

- Hematuria — most common (gross **67%**, microscopic **67%**)
- **Refractory hypertension** in **50%** (DBP ≥ 90 mmHg despite ≥ 3 medications)
- **Flank pain 67%**
- **High-output cardiac state** in **25%** with large fistulae
- **Flank bruit** audible in some cases

**Acquired (post-biopsy) AVFs:**<sup>[[24]](#ref24)</sup>

- **95% asymptomatic** — detected only on routine Doppler
- **80%** are &lt; 1 cm
- **46.6%** close spontaneously within 30 days (range 3–151 days); larger AVFs (1–2 cm) take a mean of **52 days** to close
- Symptomatic forms can present with persistent hematuria, hypertension, or rarely high-output heart failure<sup>[[20]](#ref20)[[26]](#ref26)</sup>

### Diagnosis

- **Doppler ultrasound** — initial screening; high-velocity, low-resistance arterial flow with arterialized venous flow at the site. Routine post-biopsy Doppler is recommended.<sup>[[24]](#ref24)[[27]](#ref27)</sup>
- **CT angiography** — feeding artery, draining vein, nidus (if present); useful for treatment planning<sup>[[22]](#ref22)</sup>
- **DSA renal arteriography** — gold standard; demonstrates early venous filling and the fistula anatomy, and allows simultaneous embolization.<sup>[[20]](#ref20)[[21]](#ref21)</sup>
- **MR angiography** — alternative; useful for complex AVMs.<sup>[[19]](#ref19)</sup>

### Management

**Asymptomatic post-biopsy AVFs:**

Conservative observation, with follow-up Doppler at **6 months** to detect non-resolving lesions.<sup>[[24]](#ref24)[[27]](#ref27)[[28]](#ref28)</sup>

**Symptomatic or persistent AVFs — transarterial embolization (TAE):**<sup>[[23]](#ref23)[[25]](#ref25)[[29]](#ref29)</sup>

- **Technical success 100%** in multiple series
- **Clinical success 88–100%**
- Embolic agents: coils (most common), n-butylcyanoacrylate glue, Amplatzer plugs, detachable balloons, or combinations
- Renal parenchymal loss limited to &lt; ~10–15%
- Recurrence rare — none at mean 55-month follow-up in one series<sup>[[23]](#ref23)</sup>
- Postembolization syndrome (self-limited fever, pain) in **75%**; no embolic-agent migration in most series

**Surgery (nephrectomy or vessel ligation):**<sup>[[20]](#ref20)</sup>

- Reserved for large, complex AVMs not amenable to embolization, failed embolization, or AVMs with associated renal arterial disease (aneurysm). Largely supplanted by embolization.

## AUF vs. Renal AVF — Key Differences

| Feature | Arterioureteral Fistula (AUF) | Renal Arteriovenous Fistula (AVF) |
|---|---|---|
| **Communication** | Artery (iliac) ↔ ureter | Renal artery ↔ renal vein |
| **Most common cause** | Chronic ureteral stent + pelvic cancer / radiation | Percutaneous renal biopsy |
| **Incidence** | ~3.5 / year (Netherlands) | 14.4% post-biopsy (mostly asymptomatic) |
| **Presentation** | Massive hematuria, hemorrhagic shock | Usually asymptomatic; hematuria, hypertension if symptomatic |
| **Mortality** | 7–19% (AUF-specific) | Very low (~1%) |
| **Diagnosis** | Angiography (~62% sensitivity) | Doppler US → angiography |
| **Treatment** | Endovascular stent-graft or open surgery | Transarterial embolization |
| **Spontaneous resolution** | No | 46.6% (post-biopsy) |

## Special Considerations

### AUF after aortoiliac vascular surgery

Ureteral complications occur in approximately **1.4%** of patients after aortoiliac reconstruction — hydronephrosis, ureteral leak, and AUF.<sup>[[10]](#ref10)</sup> They are markers for graft complications: patients with ureteral complications had a **55% rate** of graft complications (anastomotic aneurysm, thrombosis, infection, aortoenteric fistula) vs. **12%** without ureteral complications (p &lt; 0.05).<sup>[[10]](#ref10)</sup>

### AUF in ileal-conduit patients

Chronic bacterial colonization of the conduit raises the risk of **stent-graft infection** after endovascular repair (**14.3%**), and infection is the leading indication for reintervention. Combined endovascular and endoureteral approaches showed similar outcomes to open surgery (UAF-related mortality 7.1% vs. 13.3%; complication rates 28.6% vs. 26.7%).<sup>[[7]](#ref7)</sup>

### Prevention

No established prevention strategies, but the following are reasonable in at-risk patients:<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[2]](#ref2)[[6]](#ref6)</sup>

- Minimize the duration of chronic ureteral stenting when possible
- Consider softer, more flexible stent materials
- Maintain a low threshold for angiography in any at-risk patient with even minor hematuria
- Multidisciplinary discussion before stent exchanges in high-risk patients — **42%** of AUF presentations occur during stent exchange.<sup>[[2]](#ref2)</sup>
