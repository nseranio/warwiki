# Pyeloenteric Fistula

A pyeloenteric fistula is a rare abnormal communication between the renal pelvis (pyelo-) and a segment of the gastrointestinal tract (enteric). Pyeloduodenal fistulas are well described, but a review of 158 reported kidney-to-bowel fistulas found 93 renocolic and 55 renoduodenal cases.<sup>[[18]](#ref18)</sup> Fistulae to the colon (pyelocolic or renocolic) are also well described, and some surgical series treat them as the most frequent nephroenteric fistula.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[2]](#ref2)[[14]](#ref14)</sup> It is the upper-tract counterpart to the more familiar enterovesical fistulas of the bladder, and often arises from chronic infected obstruction or calculus disease; traumatic, iatrogenic and malignant causes also occur.<sup>[[13]](#ref13)</sup>

For operative selection, see the [Fistula Repair (All Patients) database](/docs/surgical-techniques/04h-fistula-repair/all-patients-fistula).

See also: [The Kidneys](/docs/foundations/anatomy-physiology/urinary-tract/renal-anatomy-physiology), [Fistulas landing page](/docs/clinical-conditions/03f-fistulas).

## Definition and Classification

A pyeloenteric fistula falls under the broader category of uroenteric fistulae, which are classified by the urinary-tract organ of origin and the GI-tract segment involved.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup> Specific subtypes include:

- **Pyeloduodenal fistula** is a well-described small-bowel subtype. In the 2000–2023 review, every spontaneous case involved the right kidney, adjacent to the second portion of the duodenum.<sup>[[2]](#ref2)[[3]](#ref3)[[13]](#ref13)</sup>
- **Renocolic and pyelocolonic fistulas** connect the kidney and colon; a renocolic tract may arise from renal parenchyma or the collecting system.<sup>[[19]](#ref19)</sup> The cited cases were associated with chronic obstructive stone disease and pyonephrosis, xanthogranulomatous pyelonephritis, renal tuberculosis, trauma, or renal tumor ablation; their reported operations included nephrectomy with bowel repair or resection.<sup>[[14]](#ref14)[[15]](#ref15)[[16]](#ref16)[[17]](#ref17)</sup>
- **Pyeloileal or other small-bowel fistulae.** Exceedingly rare

Congenital fistulae are extremely rare; the vast majority are acquired.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup>

---

## Etiology

The fistula most commonly arises from a chronic inflammatory process originating in the kidney:<sup>[[2]](#ref2)[[3]](#ref3)</sup>

- **Renal calculi with chronic pyelonephritis** are a leading reported cause. In a 25-case review covering 2000–2023, calculi and pyonephrosis occurred in 81% and 76% of spontaneous cases, respectively.<sup>[[13]](#ref13)</sup> Staghorn calculi and recurrent infections can drive perirenal abscess formation and erosion into adjacent bowel.<sup>[[2]](#ref2)[[3]](#ref3)[[4]](#ref4)</sup>
- **Renal or perirenal abscess.** Can erode through Gerota's fascia into the duodenum or colon<sup>[[5]](#ref5)</sup>
- **Iatrogenic / traumatic.** Described after percutaneous nephrolithotomy (PCNL), renal trauma and thermal ablation of renal tumors. Traumatic cases were 4 of 25 in the 2000–2023 review, a minority mechanism.<sup>[[5]](#ref5)[[13]](#ref13)[[14]](#ref14)[[16]](#ref16)</sup>
- **Malignancy.** Rare; reported with renal cell carcinoma and transitional cell carcinoma of the renal pelvis<sup>[[6]](#ref6)</sup>
- **Inflammatory bowel disease.** Crohn's disease can produce urinary fistulas; the cited Crohn cohort predominantly involved the bladder, not the renal pelvis<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[7]](#ref7)</sup>
- **Xanthogranulomatous pyelonephritis.** Destructive granulomatous process that can extend beyond the kidney into adjacent viscera; renocolic fistula is a reported complication<sup>[[17]](#ref17)</sup>
- **Renal tuberculosis.** Reported as the underlying cause of a renocolic fistula found only on histology after nephrectomy with colectomy<sup>[[15]](#ref15)</sup>

---

## Pathogenesis

The typical sequence is chronic renal infection or obstruction (most often from calculi), then perirenal inflammation and abscess, followed by extension through Gerota's fascia into the adjacent bowel wall. The right kidney's intimate relationship to the second portion of the duodenum explains the strong right-sided predominance of pyeloduodenal fistulae.<sup>[[2]](#ref2)[[3]](#ref3)</sup>

---

## Clinical Presentation

Symptoms are nonspecific and overlap with those of the underlying renal disease, which makes the diagnosis easy to miss:

- **Fever**, often recurrent or persistent (67% in the recent pyeloduodenal case review)<sup>[[13]](#ref13)</sup>
- **Flank pain** (57% in the pyeloduodenal case review)<sup>[[13]](#ref13)</sup>; abdominal discomfort has also been reported<sup>[[8]](#ref8)</sup>
- **Recurrent urinary tract infections or urosepsis**<sup>[[8]](#ref8)</sup>
- **Gastrointestinal symptoms.** Nausea, vomiting, diarrhea
- **Pneumaturia or fecaluria.** The hallmark of enterovesical fistulae and uncommon with a pyeloenteric tract; when present with a renocolic fistula they suggest a wide communication. Pyuria is expected with the underlying infected obstruction and is not specific
- **Hematuria**
- **Reported passage of urinary calculi per rectum.** An unusual clue requiring imaging confirmation; nonspecific debris is not diagnostic
- **Anemia** and weight loss in chronic cases<sup>[[4]](#ref4)</sup>
- **Sepsis.** A renocolic fistula has presented with septicemia and organ dysfunction<sup>[[15]](#ref15)</sup>

---

## Evaluation

The diagnosis is frequently incidental, made during imaging or procedures performed for the underlying renal disease.<sup>[[4]](#ref4)[[8]](#ref8)</sup>

| Modality | Role |
|---|---|
| **CT abdomen / pelvis with contrast** | Increasingly the primary diagnostic tool; CT urography or antegrade pyelography made the diagnosis in about 80% of the recent pyeloduodenal cases combined;<sup>[[13]](#ref13)</sup> CT demonstrates the fistulous tract, perinephric inflammation, gas in the collecting system, and associated calculi or abscess<sup>[[4]](#ref4)[[9]](#ref9)</sup> |
| **Retrograde pyelography** | Historically the most effective study for demonstrating the tract<sup>[[3]](#ref3)</sup> |
| **Antegrade pyelography / nephrostography** | Contrast injection through a percutaneous nephrostomy tube can opacify the bowel and confirm the fistula<sup>[[4]](#ref4)[[8]](#ref8)</sup> |
| **Upper GI series / barium studies** | May show contrast entering the collecting system. Barium enema helped define a renocolic fistula<sup>[[15]](#ref15)</sup> |
| **Fistulography** | Useful for follow-up and confirmation of closure<sup>[[8]](#ref8)</sup> |
| **Cystoscopy** | More relevant for lower-tract uroenteric fistulae<sup>[[7]](#ref7)</sup> |

## Management

Management depends on the underlying etiology, the patient's overall condition, and the complexity of the fistula.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[10]](#ref10)</sup>

### Surgical management

- **Nephrectomy with closure of the bowel defect** is frequently used for a nonfunctioning, severely infected kidney. Assess residual renal function, source control and the possibility of renal preservation before choosing an ablative operation<sup>[[2]](#ref2)[[3]](#ref3)[[4]](#ref4)</sup>
- In a ten-patient referral series of complex entero-urinary fistulas, urological and gastrointestinal surgeons undertook reconstruction jointly.<sup>[[10]](#ref10)</sup>
- For complex entero-urinary fistulae, a staged approach has been described: (1) proximal defunctioning and drainage to control sepsis, (2) nutritional optimization with TPN, and (3) delayed definitive reconstruction once the patient is stable<sup>[[10]](#ref10)</sup>

### Conservative and minimally invasive alternatives

- **Ureteral stenting** (internal or percutaneous) was proposed as an alternative to nephrectomy in a two-case report with literature review, by diverting urine and allowing the tract to heal<sup>[[11]](#ref11)</sup>
- **Endoscopic clipping and ligation.** Successful closure of a pyeloduodenal fistula using endoclips and an endoloop has been reported in a patient unfit for surgery<sup>[[8]](#ref8)</sup>
- **Percutaneous fulguration** of the fistulous tract has been described as a nephron-sparing option<sup>[[12]](#ref12)</sup>
- **Medical support and septic obstruction.** For sepsis with an obstructing stone, the 2026 EAU guideline gives Strong recommendations to start antibiotics immediately, urgently decompress the collecting system by ureteral stent or nephrostomy, and delay definitive stone treatment until sepsis resolves.<sup>[[20]](#ref20)</sup> Nutritional support and treatment of underlying inflammatory disease are management components.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup> In a Crohn urinary-fistula cohort, antibiotics and/or immunosuppressants were tried in four of 78 patients and 70 underwent surgery; 88% of tracts involved the bladder, so the cohort does not establish nonsurgical closure of a renal-pelvis tract.<sup>[[7]](#ref7)</sup>

### Key principles

Adequate nutrition, eradication of sepsis, urinary-tract diversion, GI-tract diversion when needed, and treatment of the underlying cause anchor the management plan.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup>

---

## Outcomes

The historical 28-case review supported nephrectomy with duodenal closure for severely diseased kidneys.<sup>[[3]](#ref3)</sup> A 2025 review identified 25 cases from 24 reports published in 2000–2023; more than half underwent nephrectomy. These uncontrolled reports describe management choices and cannot establish comparative superiority, a reliable recurrence rate, or a universal requirement for nephrectomy.<sup>[[13]](#ref13)</sup> Nephrectomy was accompanied by parenteral nutrition in 28% of these cases, and the review's authors describe duodenal closure with somatostatin and parenteral nutrition as the most frequent approach.<sup>[[13]](#ref13)</sup> No mortality rate can be derived from these reports. A separate 10-patient series reported successful staged treatment of mixed complex entero-urinary fistulas, not ten pyeloduodenal fistulas; all ten were treated successfully, with functional restoration in four and/or gastrointestinal and urological diversion in six, no postoperative deaths, and a mean of 5 months to reconstruction, in patients referred to a national intestinal failure unit.<sup>[[10]](#ref10)</sup> Case reports describe endoscopic clipping in a patient unfit for surgery and percutaneous fulguration (no recurrent fistula on imaging at 18 weeks); a separate three-case series reported nephrectomy with resection of involved bowel for one pyeloduodenal and two pyelocolic fistulas.<sup>[[8]](#ref8)[[12]](#ref12)[[14]](#ref14)</sup>
