# Ureterocolonic Fistula

A ureterocolonic fistula is a rare abnormal communication between the ureter and the colon. It falls within the broader category of uroenteric fistulae and is usually acquired, arising from inflammatory, iatrogenic, neoplastic, or radiation-related processes.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[2]](#ref2)[[3]](#ref3)</sup> Left-sided involvement predominated in a small historical review of diverticulitis-related cases, reflecting the proximity of the sigmoid colon; this is not a population estimate for all causes.<sup>[[5]](#ref5)</sup>

For operative selection, see the [Fistula Repair (All Patients) database](/docs/surgical-techniques/04h-fistula-repair/all-patients-fistula).

See also: [The Ureters](/docs/foundations/anatomy-physiology/urinary-tract/ureter-anatomy-physiology), [Fistulas landing page](/docs/clinical-conditions/03f-fistulas), [Pyeloenteric Fistula](/docs/clinical-conditions/03f-fistulas/all-patients/pyeloenteric).

---

## Definition and Classification

The fistula is classified by the urinary-tract organ of origin (ureter) and the GI segment involved (colon). It is distinct from:

- **Enterovesical fistula** (bowel-to-bladder). The most common uroenteric fistula overall
- **Pyeloenteric fistula** (renal pelvis–to–bowel)
- **Ureteroileal fistula** (ureter-to-ileum). Exceedingly rare, with fewer than 10 reported cases in the older literature<sup>[[4]](#ref4)</sup>

---

## Etiology

| Mechanism | Notes |
|---|---|
| **Obstructing ureteral calculi** | Historically the most common spontaneous cause. Chronic stone impaction drives periureteral inflammation, abscess, and erosion into the adjacent colon.<sup>[[2]](#ref2)[[5]](#ref5)[[6]](#ref6)</sup> |
| **Colonic diverticular disease** | Recurrent sigmoid diverticulitis can erode into the left ureter. The historical demographic estimates come from only a handful of published cases.<sup>[[5]](#ref5)</sup> |
| **Malignancy** | Colorectal carcinoma, urothelial carcinoma of the ureter, or locally advanced pelvic malignancies producing direct invasion or necrotic fistulization.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[2]](#ref2)</sup> |
| **Inflammatory bowel disease** | In a cohort of **78 patients with Crohn-related urinary fistulas**, 88% involved the bladder and 1% the ureter; these are distributions within that cohort, not the causes of all uroenteric fistulas.<sup>[[7]](#ref7)</sup> |
| **Xanthogranulomatous pyelonephritis** | Destructive granulomatous process can extend along the ureter into adjacent bowel.<sup>[[8]](#ref8)</sup> |
| **Iatrogenic — colorectal / gynecologic surgery** | Most common iatrogenic cause; unrecognized intraoperative ureteral injury during colectomy, anterior resection, or hysterectomy. Ureteral injury during colectomy is more likely with diverticular disease (OR 2.1) and T4 malignancy (OR 1.8) in the cohort cited by the WSES guideline; those odds ratios describe injury risk, not fistula risk. Prior radiotherapy and re-operative surgery also make dissection harder.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[2]](#ref2)[[9]](#ref9)</sup> |
| **Anastomotic leak** | Colorectal anastomotic leak eroding into the adjacent ureter — reported after laparoscopic anterior resection for sigmoid cancer.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup> |
| **Extracorporeal shock-wave lithotripsy** | Rare; reported at a stone-fragment impaction site after piezoelectric ESWL.<sup>[[8]](#ref8)</sup> |
| **Pelvic radiotherapy** | Delayed radiation injury can cause fistulization months to years later. The cited prostate-radiotherapy meta-analysis measures fistulas at multiple sites and does **not** establish the incidence of ureterocolonic fistula.<sup>[[10]](#ref10)[[11]](#ref11)</sup> |

---

## Pathogenesis

- **From urologic disease:** obstructing calculus, then periureteral inflammation and abscess, then erosion through the ureteral wall into the adjacent colon
- **From colonic disease:** diverticulitis or colonic malignancy, then pericolic inflammation, then erosion into the adjacent ureter
- **From iatrogenic injury:** unrecognized ureteral injury, then urine leak, then periureteral abscess, then fistulization to the bowel anastomosis or adjacent colon<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[2]](#ref2)</sup>
- **From radiation:** chronic ischemic vasculitis of ureteral and colonic walls, then tissue necrosis, then delayed fistulization months to years later<sup>[[10]](#ref10)</sup>

## Clinical Presentation

Symptoms are nonspecific and insidious; diagnostic delay of up to 10 years has been reported.<sup>[[5]](#ref5)[[12]](#ref12)</sup> Urologic symptoms tend to dominate over GI symptoms:

- **Recurrent urinary tract infections.** Prominent in the small historical diverticulitis review<sup>[[5]](#ref5)</sup>
- **Fecaluria**<sup>[[5]](#ref5)</sup>
- **Abdominal pain**<sup>[[5]](#ref5)</sup>
- **Flank pain**<sup>[[5]](#ref5)</sup>
- **Pneumaturia.** Passage of gas during urination
- **Watery diarrhea.** May be the presenting symptom in iatrogenic cases as urine drains into the colon<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup>
- **Fever and sepsis.** Ascending infection of an obstructed or refluxing system; fever is prominent when the fistula follows surgery<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[2]](#ref2)</sup>
- **Hematuria**
- **Hyperchloremic metabolic acidosis.** When a substantial urine volume drains into the colon, colonic reabsorption of chloride and ammonium can cause hyperchloremic acidosis with hypokalemia, as in ureterosigmoidostomy. Case-level extrapolation only; check electrolytes in a patient with a high-volume fistula.<sup>[[21]](#ref21)</sup>
- **Progressive ipsilateral renal-function loss** from chronic obstruction and infection<sup>[[2]](#ref2)</sup>

## Evaluation

Small historical case collections report detection proportions without validated sensitivity estimates.<sup>[[5]](#ref5)</sup>

### Imaging

| Modality | Role |
|---|---|
| **CT urography (nephrographic + 5–20-min excretory phase)** | Can show ureteral contrast extravasation into colon, periureteral inflammation, hydronephrosis, calculi, or abscess. WSES calls nephrographic and excretory CT urography the diagnostic gold standard for postoperative iatrogenic urinary-tract injury after emergency digestive surgery (strong recommendation, moderate-quality evidence, GRADE 1B).<sup>[[9]](#ref9)[[13]](#ref13)</sup> |
| **Retrograde pyelography / ureterography** | Can directly demonstrate contrast flowing from ureter into colon.<sup>[[5]](#ref5)</sup> |
| **Antegrade nephrostogram** | Contrast injection through a percutaneous nephrostomy can opacify the fistula and confirm the communication.<sup>[[6]](#ref6)</sup> |
| **Barium enema** | A historical test; it opacified the fistula in 3 of 4 cases in the small diverticular collection. Avoid it in acute diverticulitis because of perforation risk; contrast-enhanced CT is the recommended test there.<sup>[[5]](#ref5)[[14]](#ref14)</sup> |
| **CT colonography** | Emerging modality with potential utility in colonic fistulae.<sup>[[15]](#ref15)</sup> |
| **Intravenous pyelogram (IVP)** | Historical study that may show obstruction; largely superseded by CT urography.<sup>[[5]](#ref5)</sup> |

### Other diagnostics

- **Cystoscopy.** Efflux of turbid or feculent urine from the affected ureteral orifice
- **Colonoscopy.** May identify the colonic opening or underlying pathology and help exclude malignancy when indicated. Do not use it to diagnose suspected acute diverticulitis because of perforation risk; after complicated diverticulitis managed nonoperatively, perform an indicated examination 6–8 weeks after symptom resolution.<sup>[[14]](#ref14)</sup>
- **Renal scintigraphy (DMSA / MAG3).** Quantifies differential renal function; reassess after relief of reversible obstruction when feasible, and consider contralateral function and repair options before nephroureterectomy.<sup>[[2]](#ref2)[[6]](#ref6)</sup>

## Management

Treatment depends on etiology, timing of diagnosis, residual renal function on the affected side, and the patient's overall condition.<sup>[[3]](#ref3)[[16]](#ref16)</sup>

### General principles<sup>[[3]](#ref3)[[16]](#ref16)</sup>

1. Adequate nutrition (TPN if needed)
2. Control sepsis. With an infected obstructing stone, start antibiotics immediately, urgently decompress the collecting system with a stent or nephrostomy, and delay definitive stone treatment until sepsis resolves; the 2026 EAU guideline rates these steps Strong.<sup>[[25]](#ref25)</sup>
3. Urinary-tract diversion
4. GI-tract diversion when necessary
5. Treatment of the underlying cause
6. Definitive surgical reconstruction when needed, once the patient is optimized.<sup>[[16]](#ref16)[[22]](#ref22)</sup>

### Conservative / minimally invasive management

For a selected early postoperative fistula from partial iatrogenic ureteral injury, retrograde or antegrade stenting may be tried first; WSES recommends these approaches for partial transection after emergency digestive surgery (strong recommendation, low-quality evidence, GRADE 1C). Complete or extensive injury favors repair; confirm closure after diversion and monitor for delayed stricture.<sup>[[9]](#ref9)</sup>

- **Percutaneous nephrostomy and antegrade ureteral stenting.** Diverts urine and allows spontaneous closure. In a historical series of 50 patients with partial ureteral dehiscence or fistula, stenting was achieved in 41 and preserved renal function in 30 (60% overall).<sup>[[17]](#ref17)</sup> This mixed cohort was not a ureterocolonic-specific comparative trial. One stone-associated ureterocolonic fistula was treated with antegrade stenting and early clinical improvement, but sustained fistula closure was not established before the patient died days later from an unrelated cardiac event.<sup>[[17]](#ref17)[[6]](#ref6)</sup> In a 25-patient mixed ureteral-fistula series (ileal, colonic, sigmoid, duodenal, pancreatic, and fallopian-tube tracts, including transplant ureters), nephrostomy and double-J stenting closed eight fistulas over 7–16 weeks; three radiologic ureteroneocystostomies and one pyelocalicostomy closed four more (12/25 definitive).<sup>[[22]](#ref22)</sup> Stents or nephrostomy reduced fistula flow in eight additional patients.<sup>[[22]](#ref22)</sup>
- **Retrograde ureteral stenting.** Alternative when the fistula can be traversed cystoscopically; the American College of Surgeons trauma guideline recommends retrograde stenting or PCN for delayed-presentation ureteral injuries (written for traumatic injury, not fistula specifically).<sup>[[18]](#ref18)</sup>
- **Antegrade stenting and nephrostomy.** In one early postoperative case after anterior resection for sigmoid cancer, CT showed a distal ureter-to-rectum tract; after retrograde access failed, nephrostomy and antegrade stenting achieved closure without reoperation.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup>
- In a 40-patient mixed upper-tract fistula series, percutaneous treatment met a composite endpoint of fistula healing, normal renal function, and no secondary stenosis at six months in 28/40 (70%); results were 9/9 after endourologic procedures and 7/8 after ureterointestinal anastomoses.<sup>[[19]](#ref19)</sup>

### Definitive surgical management

Required for most spontaneous fistulae and for complex or refractory iatrogenic cases.

- **Bowel resection with an individualized continuity plan.** Resection may be needed for colonic pathology, including diverticulitis or malignancy; anastomosis or diversion depends on operative findings and patient condition.<sup>[[3]](#ref3)</sup> For left-sided diverticulitis complicated by fistula, including a coloureteral tract, the 2026 ASCRS guideline recommends elective colectomy (strong recommendation, low-quality evidence) with an individualized discussion of symptoms and operative risk.<sup>[[24]](#ref24)</sup> Some historical diverticular cases required no ureteral reconstruction, but assess ureteral viability, obstruction and the residual defect rather than assuming that bowel repair alone is sufficient.<sup>[[5]](#ref5)</sup>
- **Nephroureterectomy.** Consider for a nonfunctioning or unsalvageable infected kidney, after assessing differential function and reconstruction options.<sup>[[2]](#ref2)[[12]](#ref12)</sup>
- **Ureteral repair.** When the kidney is salvageable, reconstruction depends on the location and length of the defect:<sup>[[9]](#ref9)[[18]](#ref18)</sup>
  - **Ureteroureterostomy** for upper- and mid-ureteral injuries
  - **[Ureteroneocystostomy](/docs/surgical-techniques/04d-upper-tract-reconstruction/ureteral-reimplantation)** for distal injuries
  - **[Boari flap or psoas hitch](/docs/surgical-techniques/04d-upper-tract-reconstruction/boari-flap-psoas-hitch)** for longer defects
  - All repairs stented with a double-J, tension-free, and externally drained
- **Staged management for complex entero-urinary fistulae.** Three phases:<sup>[[16]](#ref16)</sup>
  1. **Acute:** proximal defunctioning and distal drainage of both GI and urinary tracts; eradication of sepsis
  2. **Recovery:** TPN, organ support, radiologic planning
  3. **Reconstructive:** joint urologic and GI surgical repair once nutritionally replete and stable

  In ten patients referred after failed treatment elsewhere for complex entero-urinary fistulas, the authors reported successful treatment in all; four had functional restoration and six required gastrointestinal and/or urinary diversion. There were no postoperative deaths, and mean time to reconstruction was 5 months (range 1–20 months).<sup>[[16]](#ref16)</sup>

- **Radiation-associated fistulae.** Poor tissue vascularity can complicate repair. Large or refractory fistulae may require urinary and/or fecal diversion in the cited prostate-radiation and mixed pelvic-radiation cohorts.<sup>[[20]](#ref20)[[10]](#ref10)</sup>

## Prevention

For iatrogenic ureterocolonic fistula during colorectal and pelvic surgery:

- **Prophylactic ureteral stent placement.** Utilization rose from 6.7% to 16.3% between 2000 and 2013 in colectomies for diverticulitis.<sup>[[9]](#ref9)</sup> The American Society of Colon and Rectal Surgeons (2014) gave a weak, low-quality-evidence recommendation for placement "at the discretion of the surgeon," with stronger justification for morbid obesity, prior radiation, abnormal anatomy, and re-operative surgery.<sup>[[9]](#ref9)</sup> The ASCRS textbook chapter on diverticular disease notes that routine stenting is associated with longer operative time, longer stay and higher cost, that comparisons are confounded because higher-risk patients receive stents, and that a selective approach appears appropriate.<sup>[[23]](#ref23)</sup> The 2026 EAU trauma guideline weakly recommends prophylactic stents for high-risk cases and notes that they may aid detection but do not prevent ureteral injury.<sup>[[26]](#ref26)</sup> A meta-analysis of 17 retrospective colorectal cohorts (104,162 patients) found no statistically significant injury difference; stenting was associated with more UTI and hematuria.<sup>[[27]](#ref27)</sup>
- **Careful surgical technique.** Particularly during dissection between Toldt's and Gerota's fascia and during ligation of the inferior mesenteric vessels, the highest-risk phases for ureteral injury.<sup>[[9]](#ref9)</sup>
- **Intraoperative ureteral identification.** Direct inspection, methylene blue, or cystoscopic visualization of ureteral efflux may help when injury is suspected.<sup>[[18]](#ref18)</sup> For a stable, properly positioned patient with equipment and expertise during emergency digestive surgery, WSES identifies cystoscopy with retrograde pyelography as the diagnostic gold standard (weak recommendation, low-quality evidence, GRADE 2C).<sup>[[9]](#ref9)</sup>

---

## Outcomes

- **Successful surgery is reported** in the small historical diverticulitis case collection; its reported 100% cure rate is not a generalizable prognosis.<sup>[[5]](#ref5)</sup>
- **One early-diagnosed postoperative case** closed after nephrostomy and antegrade stenting without reoperation; CT showed no leak at one month and the patient was asymptomatic at six months.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup>
- **Delayed diagnosis** (months to years) drives progressive renal-function loss and may necessitate nephroureterectomy.<sup>[[2]](#ref2)[[12]](#ref12)</sup>
- **Radiation-associated fistulae** may require urinary and/or fecal diversion after unsuccessful conservative treatment; the cited post-prostate-radiation cohort involved mostly rectourinary tracts and cannot establish a ureterocolonic-specific prognosis.<sup>[[20]](#ref20)</sup>
