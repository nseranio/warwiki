# Rectourethral Fistula

A rectourethral fistula (RUF) is an abnormal communication between the rectum and the urethra. It is a rare but surgically demanding condition encountered almost exclusively in men, most commonly as a complication of prostate cancer treatment. Management requires close collaboration between urologic reconstruction and colorectal surgery.

## Etiology

| Cause | Notes |
|---|---|
| Post-prostatectomy | Most common in the developed world; incidence ~0.1–2% after radical prostatectomy |
| Radiation therapy | External beam, brachytherapy, or combined; often delayed onset (months–years) |
| HIFU / cryotherapy | Ablative prostate therapies; increasing incidence as salvage treatments rise |
| Trauma | Perineal trauma, pelvic fracture |
| Congenital | Rare; typically presents in childhood |
| IBD / Infection | Perirectal abscess, Crohn's disease |

## Presentation

- **Pneumaturia**: passage of air through the urethra — often the earliest symptom
- **Fecaluria**: passage of feculent material per urethra
- **Urine per rectum**: urinary stream diverted into rectum
- Recurrent UTIs, urosepsis
- Pelvic and perineal pain
- Dysuria, hematuria

## Evaluation

- **Retrograde urethrogram (RUG)**: first-line imaging; defines urethral anatomy and fistula location
- **Cystoscopy**: identifies fistula within the urethra; assess for associated stricture or bladder neck stenosis
- **Proctoscopy / rigid sigmoidoscopy**: visualize rectal opening
- **CT pelvis with contrast**: assess for pelvic abscess, residual disease, or associated pathology
- **MRI pelvis**: best for soft tissue characterization, radiation changes, sphincter anatomy
- **Antegrade cystogram**: assess bladder neck and proximal urethra if retrograde study incomplete
- **Anorectal manometry + endoanal US**: evaluate sphincter complex prior to repair

## Classification

**By etiology:**
- Post-surgical (non-irradiated)
- Post-radiation or post-ablative therapy (irradiated)

**By size:**
- Small (<1 cm) vs. large (≥1 cm)

**Irradiated RUF is the critical distinction** — tissue quality, healing potential, and repair strategy differ fundamentally from non-irradiated cases.

## Management

### Initial Steps
- **Fecal diversion**: loop colostomy or ileostomy — mandatory in most cases to reduce contamination and allow tissue recovery
- **Urinary diversion**: suprapubic catheter preferred; allows urethral rest
- Nutritional support; infection control
- Allow 3–6 months of diversion before definitive repair

### Definitive Repair

#### Non-Irradiated RUF
| Approach | Details |
|---|---|
| Transperineal (York Mason, posterior sagittal) | Gold standard; excellent exposure; allows layered closure with gracilis interposition |
| Transabdominal | Large or high fistulas; combined with cystoprostatectomy if needed |
| Transanal / endoscopic | Small fistulas; limited data; not recommended as primary approach for large defects |

**Transperineal repair principles:**
1. Prone jackknife position
2. Complete fistula excision
3. Separate urethral and rectal closures in layers
4. **Gracilis muscle flap interposition** — the procedure of choice for tissue interposition in perineal RUF
5. Leave urethral catheter and suprapubic tube post-operatively

#### Irradiated RUF
- Far more challenging; tissue is fibrotic, avascular, and poorly healing
- Most cases require **vascularized muscle flap** (gracilis) as an obligatory step, not optional
- Consider **omental pedicle** flap via laparotomy in selected cases
- Combined urinary and fecal diversion often becomes permanent in severe radiation injury
- **Total pelvic exenteration** may be necessary for refractory cases, particularly if associated with recurrent malignancy

## Outcomes

| Scenario | Success Rate |
|---|---|
| Non-irradiated, first repair | ~85–95% |
| Irradiated, with gracilis | ~60–80% |
| Recurrent or post-ablative | Variable; lower |

## Key Points

- Fecal and urinary diversion before repair is not optional — it is the foundation of success
- Gracilis muscle interposition is the cornerstone of perineal RUF repair
- Irradiated tissue requires aggressive debridement and mandatory flap coverage
- Always evaluate for recurrent prostate cancer before planning repair — treat malignancy first
- Urethral stricture frequently coexists and must be addressed concurrently
