# Apical Prolapse

**Apical pelvic organ prolapse** is descent of the vaginal apex, either the uterus/cervix in a patient with uterus in situ or the vaginal cuff after hysterectomy.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[2]](#ref2)</sup> It is also described as **upper vaginal prolapse** and includes uterine prolapse, post-hysterectomy vaginal vault prolapse, and enterocele when small bowel herniates into the upper vagina.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[3]](#ref3)</sup>

Apical support is the load-bearing center of vaginal support. For the reconstructive urologist and urogynecologist, missed apical descent is a setup for recurrent anterior or posterior compartment prolapse; many apparent cystoceles and rectoceles are partly apex-driven. The operative question is therefore not simply "which wall is bulging?" but whether Level I support has failed and what repair best matches the patient's anatomy, sexual goals, uterine preferences, comorbidity, and recurrence risk.

See also: [POP Overview](/docs/clinical-conditions/03c-pelvic-support/pelvic-organ-prolapse), [Anterior Compartment Prolapse](/docs/clinical-conditions/03c-pelvic-support/anterior-compartment), [Female Pelvic Examination](/docs/evaluation/history-physical/female-pelvic-examination), [The Vagina](/docs/foundations/anatomy-physiology/genitalia/vaginal-anatomy), [The Uterus](/docs/foundations/anatomy-physiology/genitalia/uterus), [Uterosacral Ligament Suspension](/docs/surgical-techniques/04g-prolapse-repair/uterosacral-ligament-suspension), [Sacrospinous Ligament Fixation](/docs/surgical-techniques/04g-prolapse-repair/sacrospinous-ligament-fixation), [Sacrocolpopexy](/docs/surgical-techniques/04g-prolapse-repair/sacrocolpopexy), and [Le Fort Colpocleisis](/docs/surgical-techniques/04g-prolapse-repair/le-fort-colpocleisis).

## Definition and Terminology

| Term | Meaning | Operative relevance |
|---|---|---|
| **Apical prolapse** | Descent of the cervix, uterus, or vaginal cuff | Requires Level I support repair |
| **Uterine prolapse** | Descent of the uterus and cervix | Treat with hysterectomy + apical suspension or uterine-sparing hysteropexy |
| **Vaginal vault / cuff prolapse** | Descent of the vaginal apex after hysterectomy | Requires vault suspension; hysterectomy alone is not a prolapse repair |
| **Enterocele** | Herniation of small bowel / peritoneal sac into the vaginal apex | Repair requires sac reduction / obliteration and apical support |
| **Hysteropexy** | Uterine-preserving apical suspension | Option when uterine preservation is desired and uterine pathology is absent |
| **Colpocleisis** | Obliterative closure of the vaginal canal | Durable option when future vaginal intercourse is not desired |

POP-Q describes apical support through **point C** (cervix or vaginal cuff) and **point D** (posterior fornix, when the cervix is present). Total vaginal length is measured with prolapse reduced.<sup>[[4]](#ref4)</sup> Definitions of apical support loss vary across studies, ranging from point C below -1 cm to descent beyond +1 cm or more than 50% of total vaginal length.<sup>[[3]](#ref3)</sup>

## Epidemiology

POP is identified on examination in **40-60%** of parous women, but symptom-based prevalence is much lower because many anatomic findings are not bothersome.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[5]](#ref5)</sup> About **3%** of women report vaginal bulge symptoms, and roughly **10%** undergo surgery for prolapse or urinary incontinence during their lifetime.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[6]](#ref6)</sup>

Apical support loss is often underrecognized because the most visible bulge may be anterior or posterior. Most patients presenting for prolapse surgery have multicompartment disease, and apical descent frequently contributes to recurrence after isolated anterior or posterior repair.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[3]](#ref3)</sup>

Surgical burden is substantial. Contemporary U.S. estimates cite roughly **300,000 prolapse operations annually**, and large cohort data show ongoing long-term reoperation risk after apical repair.<sup>[[6]](#ref6)[[7]](#ref7)</sup>

## Anatomy and Pathophysiology

### DeLancey Support Levels

Apical prolapse is the clinical failure of **Level I** support. The cardinal-uterosacral complex suspends the cervix and upper vagina from the sacrum and lateral pelvic sidewall, while Level II and III supports stabilize the mid-vagina and distal vagina.<sup>[[2]](#ref2)[[5]](#ref5)</sup>

| Level | Support structure | Failure pattern |
|---|---|---|
| **Level I** | Cardinal-uterosacral complex; upper vagina / cervix | Uterine descent, cuff prolapse, enterocele, transverse anterior defect |
| **Level II** | Pubocervical and rectovaginal fibromuscular attachments to the pelvic sidewall | Cystocele, rectocele, lateral wall defects |
| **Level III** | Perineal membrane, distal vagina, perineal body | Distal urethrocele, perineal descent, introital widening |

Apical descent can make anterior or posterior wall prolapse appear worse. Reducing the apex during examination is often the fastest way to determine how much of the visible compartment defect is apex-driven.

### Risk Factors

| Risk factor | Effect / association |
|---|---|
| **Vaginal delivery** | OR 2.65 for first vaginal delivery in pooled analyses |
| **Forceps delivery** | OR 2.51; marker of levator and fascial injury |
| **Parity** | Established risk factor |
| **Age** | OR 1.34 per decade |
| **BMI / obesity** | OR 1.75 per 5 kg/m2 increment |
| **Birthweight** | OR 1.04 per 100 g increment |
| **Levator ani defect** | OR 3.99; strong anatomic predictor |
| **Cesarean delivery** | Protective association |
| **Connective tissue susceptibility** | ESR1, FBLN5, PGR, and COL1A1 polymorphisms associated with POP risk in genetic studies |

Risk factors for recurrence after surgery include preoperative stage III-IV prolapse, younger age at vaginal prolapse surgery, obesity, levator avulsion, enlarged levator hiatus, and failure to restore apical support.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[8]](#ref8)</sup>

## Clinical Presentation

### Symptoms

The most specific symptom is **vaginal bulge**. Patients may also describe heaviness, pelvic pressure, a dragging sensation, low back discomfort, or the need to manually reduce the prolapse to void or defecate.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[9]](#ref9)</sup>

Associated symptoms include:

- **Urinary:** SUI, occult SUI, urgency/frequency, incomplete emptying, recurrent UTI, or need to splint to void.
- **Bowel:** constipation, obstructed defecation, enterocele-related pressure, fecal incontinence, or splinting.
- **Sexual:** dyspareunia, vaginal laxity, avoidance, body-image concerns, or coital incontinence.

Symptoms usually emerge when the leading edge reaches the hymen or passes it, but symptom severity and POP-Q stage correlate imperfectly.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[9]](#ref9)</sup>

### Physical Examination

Apical prolapse evaluation requires a dynamic POP-Q examination.

Core steps:

1. Examine supine with split speculum during maximal Valsalva and cough.
2. Repeat standing if symptoms are not reproduced.
3. Record **C**, **D** if cervix present, **tvl**, **gh**, **pb**, and compartment points Aa/Ba and Ap/Bp.
4. Reduce the apex and reassess anterior/posterior wall descent.
5. Assess uterine/cervical status, prior hysterectomy scar, vaginal length, atrophy/GSM, levator tone, and perineal body support.
6. Perform cough stress testing with prolapse reduced when surgery is being considered.

| POP-Q point | Meaning |
|---|---|
| **C** | Most distal cervix or vaginal cuff |
| **D** | Posterior fornix when cervix is present; omitted after hysterectomy |
| **TVL** | Total vaginal length with prolapse reduced |
| **GH / PB** | Genital hiatus and perineal body; large hiatus predicts support failure and recurrence |

### Adjunctive Testing

| Test | Indication |
|---|---|
| **PVR** | Prolapse beyond hymen, voiding symptoms, recurrent UTI, or preoperative assessment |
| **Urinalysis / culture** | Urgency, dysuria, recurrent UTI, or hematuria |
| **Cough stress test with reduction** | Significant anterior/apical prolapse before repair |
| **Urodynamics** | Unclear SUI, occult SUI counseling, elevated PVR, voiding dysfunction, prior failed repair |
| **Dynamic pelvic MRI / defecography** | Recurrent or multicompartment prolapse, suspected enterocele/sigmoidocele, obstructed defecation |
| **Endometrial / cervical evaluation** | Abnormal bleeding, uterus-preserving plan, colpocleisis with uterus in situ, or inadequate screening |

## Treatment

### Conservative Management

Observation is appropriate for asymptomatic apical prolapse. Treatment is indicated when bulge, pressure, urinary dysfunction, bowel dysfunction, sexual dysfunction, or skin exposure symptoms are bothersome enough that the patient wants intervention.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[10]](#ref10)</sup>

Conservative options:

- [Pelvic floor physical therapy](/docs/surgical-techniques/04f-incontinence-procedures/procedures/pelvic-floor-pt) to reduce symptom bother and improve coexisting SUI/OAB or defecatory coordination.
- Pessary fitting, including ring, Gellhorn, cube, or space-occupying devices depending on stage and anatomy.
- [Chronic constipation](/docs/clinical-conditions/03c-pelvic-support/chronic-constipation) treatment to reduce repetitive Valsalva load.
- Topical estrogen for hypoestrogenic tissue irritation or pessary tolerance, recognizing that estrogen is not a definitive prolapse treatment.<sup>[[11]](#ref11)</sup>

Pessaries should be offered to women considering treatment, particularly those who prefer to avoid surgery, are poor surgical candidates, or desire future pregnancy.<sup>[[12]](#ref12)[[13]](#ref13)</sup>

### Vaginal Native Tissue Apical Repair

The two standard native-tissue vaginal apical repairs are uterosacral ligament suspension and sacrospinous ligament fixation.

| Procedure | Mechanism | Key risks / tradeoffs |
|---|---|---|
| **Uterosacral ligament suspension (USLS)** | Bilateral suspension of the apex to the uterosacral ligaments near the ischial spine | Ureteral kinking/injury; cystoscopy mandatory |
| **Sacrospinous ligament fixation (SSLF)** | Apex attached to the sacrospinous ligament, usually right-sided | Buttock pain, neurovascular risk, posterior vaginal axis shift, anterior recurrence risk |
| **Iliococcygeus suspension** | Apex suspended to iliococcygeus fascia | Alternative when USLS/SSLF anatomy is unfavorable |
| **McCall culdoplasty / uterosacral plication** | Vaginal hysterectomy adjunct to obliterate cul-de-sac and support apex | Prevents enterocele / vault prolapse when done well |

The OPTIMAL randomized trial found similar 2-year success for USLS and SSLF (**64.5% vs 63.1%**). At 5 years, many patients met strict composite failure definitions, but symptoms remained improved and retreatment rates were low, around 10%.<sup>[[14]](#ref14)</sup> This is the core counseling point: native tissue apical surgery improves symptoms, but POP is a chronic support disorder and anatomic perfection is not guaranteed.

### Abdominal / Laparoscopic / Robotic Sacrocolpopexy

**Sacrocolpopexy** suspends the vaginal apex to the anterior longitudinal ligament of the sacrum using type I polypropylene mesh. It can be performed open, laparoscopically, or robotically and remains the benchmark durable reconstructive repair for apical prolapse.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[15]](#ref15)</sup>

Compared with vaginal procedures, sacrocolpopexy is associated with lower risk of awareness of prolapse, recurrent prolapse on examination, repeat surgery for prolapse, and postoperative SUI in Cochrane review data.<sup>[[15]](#ref15)</sup>

| Approach | Practical tradeoffs |
|---|---|
| **Open abdominal** | Durable long-term data; more blood loss and longer recovery |
| **Laparoscopic** | Less blood loss and shorter stay than open; lower cost and shorter operative time than robotic in many series |
| **Robotic** | Ergonomic suturing and common contemporary approach; longer operative time and higher cost than pure laparoscopy without clear outcome superiority |

Candidates who often favor sacrocolpopexy include younger patients, stage III-IV prolapse, prior failed vaginal repair, shortened vaginal length, high recurrence-risk anatomy, and patients already requiring abdominal surgery.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[15]](#ref15)</sup>

### Mesh Complications After Sacrocolpopexy

Mesh exposure after sacrocolpopexy is less common than historic transvaginal mesh complications but remains a real long-term risk. Reported exposure rates vary by follow-up duration, hysterectomy status, mesh weight, and ascertainment method.<sup>[[16]](#ref16)[[17]](#ref17)[[18]](#ref18)[[19]](#ref19)</sup>

| Finding | Pattern |
|---|---|
| **Minimally invasive sacrocolpopexy review** | Mesh exposure around 3.5% in pooled narrative estimates |
| **CARE 7-year follow-up** | Mesh erosion probability approximately 10.5% by 7 years |
| **Mesh weight** | Heavier-weight mesh has higher graft-related complications than lightweight mesh in long-term data |
| **Concomitant total hysterectomy** | Higher exposure risk than supracervical hysterectomy or hysteropexy in many datasets |

Use macroporous monofilament type I polypropylene when synthetic mesh is used. Biologic grafts have lower anatomic cure rates than synthetic mesh in sacrocolpopexy and are not routine substitutes for durability.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[18]](#ref18)</sup>

### Uterine Preservation: Hysteropexy

Hysteropexy is a viable alternative to hysterectomy for uterine prolapse when the patient desires uterine preservation and has no uterine/cervical pathology requiring removal.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[20]](#ref20)[[21]](#ref21)</sup>

| Approach | Examples |
|---|---|
| **Vaginal native tissue** | Sacrospinous hysteropexy, uterosacral hysteropexy, Manchester-Fothergill |
| **Abdominal / laparoscopic / robotic** | Sacral hysteropexy, mesh hysteropexy, uterosacral shortening |

Systematic reviews show similar short-term prolapse outcomes for uterine preservation and hysterectomy-based repair, with hysteropexy often associated with shorter operative time, less blood loss, shorter hospitalization, and lower mesh exposure when mesh is used.<sup>[[20]](#ref20)[[21]](#ref21)</sup> In the SAVE U randomized follow-up, sacrospinous hysteropexy had lower apical failure than vaginal hysterectomy with uterosacral suspension at 5 years (**1% vs 7.8%**) and higher composite success.<sup>[[22]](#ref22)</sup>

Reasons to favor hysterectomy include abnormal bleeding, cervical dysplasia, endometrial pathology, patient preference, inability to ensure future surveillance, or desire to eliminate future uterine procedures. Reasons to favor hysteropexy include uterine preference, fertility potential, shorter operation, lower blood loss, and avoidance of hysterectomy-specific morbidity.

### Obliterative Surgery: Colpocleisis

Colpocleisis is the most durable option for advanced apical prolapse in patients who do not desire future vaginal intercourse.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[23]](#ref23)[[24]](#ref24)</sup>

| Procedure | Use |
|---|---|
| **Le Fort partial colpocleisis** | Uterus in situ; anterior and posterior vaginal walls approximated with lateral drainage channels |
| **Total colpocleisis / colpectomy** | Post-hysterectomy vault prolapse or when the uterus is absent |

Advantages include short operative time, ability to use regional or local anesthesia in selected patients, low recurrence, and high satisfaction. Preoperative counseling must explicitly document permanent loss of vaginal penetrative function. If the uterus is retained, cervical screening and endometrial evaluation must be appropriate before vaginal closure.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[23]](#ref23)</sup>

## Concomitant Stress Urinary Incontinence

Apical prolapse can mask SUI by kinking the urethra, especially when anterior descent coexists. Evaluate occult SUI with prolapse reduction before apical surgery.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[25]](#ref25)</sup>

| Situation | Counseling |
|---|---|
| **Overt SUI** | Offer concomitant continence procedure or staged strategy |
| **Occult SUI** | Concomitant sling/Burch lowers postoperative SUI but increases voiding dysfunction and UTI risk |
| **No SUI with reduction** | De novo SUI can still occur; prophylactic continence surgery is not routine for every patient |
| **Sacrocolpopexy** | Burch colposuspension or sling can be considered depending on SUI phenotype and mesh preference |

See [Stress Urinary Incontinence (Female)](/docs/clinical-conditions/03a-storage-incontinence/sui-female), [Mixed Urinary Incontinence](/docs/clinical-conditions/03a-storage-incontinence/mixed-incontinence), and the [Female SUI Treatment Database](/docs/surgical-techniques/04f-incontinence-procedures/female-sui/female-stress-incontinence-database).

## Long-Term Outcomes and Reoperation

Long-term durability varies by procedure and by how failure is defined. Anatomic recurrence, symptomatic recurrence, retreatment, and patient satisfaction are not interchangeable endpoints.

| Procedure family | Durability pattern |
|---|---|
| **Colpocleisis** | Lowest recurrence and reoperation risk in appropriately selected patients; obliterative |
| **Sacrocolpopexy** | Most durable reconstructive approach; mesh exposure is the key long-term tradeoff |
| **USLS** | Effective native tissue repair; ureteral risk and recurrent support failure require counseling |
| **SSLF** | Effective native tissue repair; posterior axis shift may contribute to anterior recurrence |
| **Hysteropexy** | Comparable short-term outcomes to hysterectomy-based repair in selected patients; long-term surveillance still evolving |

Large cohort data in older women show lower 7-year reoperation for recurrence after colpocleisis and sacrocolpopexy than after some vaginal native tissue apical repairs, while patient-reported outcomes may remain similar across procedures.<sup>[[7]](#ref7)[[26]](#ref26)[[27]](#ref27)</sup>

## Key Recommendations

- Treat the apex as the central load-bearing compartment in POP surgery.
- Use POP-Q **C**, **D**, **TVL**, **GH**, and compartment points to define anatomy before treatment.
- Reduce the apex during examination to determine whether anterior or posterior descent is apex-driven.
- Offer pessary and pelvic floor therapy for symptomatic patients who prefer conservative care or are not surgical candidates.
- Perform an apical suspension at hysterectomy for uterine prolapse; hysterectomy alone is not a suspension.
- USLS and SSLF are both reasonable native tissue repairs; counsel on different risk profiles.
- Sacrocolpopexy offers the most durable reconstructive apical repair but introduces mesh-specific risk.
- Hysteropexy is a valid option in appropriately selected patients desiring uterine preservation.
- Colpocleisis should be discussed early with patients who have advanced prolapse, high comorbidity, and no desire for future vaginal intercourse.
- Evaluate occult SUI with prolapse reduction before apical repair.
