# Posterior Compartment Prolapse

**Posterior compartment pelvic organ prolapse** is descent of the posterior vaginal wall, usually from rectovaginal support failure, allowing the rectum, peritoneal cul-de-sac, small bowel, or deficient perineal body to protrude toward the vaginal lumen.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[2]](#ref2)</sup> The most common posterior defect is a **rectocele**. Enterocele and perineal deficiency commonly coexist and can be missed if the exam focuses only on the visible posterior wall bulge.

Posterior prolapse is rarely an isolated mechanical problem. Symptoms often sit at the intersection of vaginal support, stool consistency, pelvic-floor coordination, rectal emptying, anal sphincter function, and apical support. The practical clinical question is therefore: is the patient bothered by a vaginal bulge, by obstructed defecation, by sexual dysfunction, or by a mixed functional-anatomic disorder that needs pelvic-floor therapy, bowel management, colorectal evaluation, and selective surgery?

See also: [POP Overview](/docs/clinical-conditions/03c-pelvic-support/pelvic-organ-prolapse), [Anterior Compartment Prolapse](/docs/clinical-conditions/03c-pelvic-support/anterior-compartment), [Apical Prolapse](/docs/clinical-conditions/03c-pelvic-support/apical-prolapse), [Female Pelvic Examination](/docs/evaluation/history-physical/female-pelvic-examination), [Chronic Constipation](/docs/clinical-conditions/03i-defecatory-disorders/chronic-constipation), [Defecatory Dysfunction](/docs/clinical-conditions/03i-defecatory-disorders/defecatory-dysfunction), [Fecal Incontinence](/docs/clinical-conditions/03i-defecatory-disorders/fecal-incontinence), [Posterior Colporrhaphy](/docs/surgical-techniques/04g-prolapse-repair/posterior-colporrhaphy), and [Perineorrhaphy](/docs/surgical-techniques/04g-prolapse-repair/perineorrhaphy).

## Definition and Terminology

| Term | Meaning | Clinical relevance |
|---|---|---|
| **Posterior compartment prolapse** | Descent of the posterior vaginal wall | POP-Q point **Bp** captures the leading posterior wall point |
| **Rectocele** | Bulging of the anterior rectal wall into the posterior vaginal wall | Most common posterior defect; may cause bulge, splinting, stool trapping, or obstructed defecation |
| **Enterocele** | Peritoneal sac, often with small bowel, descending between rectum and vagina | Often apex-related; may mimic or coexist with rectocele |
| **Perineal deficiency** | Weakness, shortening, scarring, or disruption of the perineal body | Widened genital hiatus, gaping introitus, distal rectocele, stool trapping |
| **Posterior rectocele** | Posterior rectal wall outpouching | Rare; not visible on vaginal exam and usually diagnosed radiographically |
| **Obstructed defecation syndrome (ODS)** | Difficulty evacuating stool despite urge and effort | May be structural, functional, or both; rectocele repair helps selected patients only |

POP-Q uses **point Bp**, the most distal point of the posterior vaginal wall, to measure posterior compartment descent.<sup>[[3]](#ref3)[[4]](#ref4)</sup> Point **Ap** describes the fixed posterior wall point 3 cm proximal to the hymen; **GH** and **PB** are essential because a widened genital hiatus or deficient perineal body changes repair planning.

## Epidemiology

POP is seen on examination in **40-60%** of parous women, but many findings are asymptomatic.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[5]](#ref5)</sup> Posterior prolapse commonly coexists with anterior and apical prolapse, so compartment-specific symptoms and exam findings should be interpreted in the context of the whole pelvic support system.

Rectocele prevalence depends heavily on definition and population. Among women with obstructed defecation syndrome, a rectocele of at least **2 cm** is reported in about **37-42%**; among women with POP, imaging-defined "true rectocele" has been reported around **23%**.<sup>[[6]](#ref6)[[7]](#ref7)</sup>

The presence of a rectocele does not prove causality. Rectoceles can be seen in nulliparous women and in asymptomatic women, while many patients with ODS have pelvic-floor dyssynergia, constipation, rectal intussusception, or other anorectal disorders that will not improve with vaginal wall repair alone.<sup>[[6]](#ref6)[[8]](#ref8)</sup>

## Anatomy and Pathophysiology

### Posterior Support

Posterior support is provided by the posterior vaginal fibromuscular layer / rectovaginal septum, attachments to the pelvic sidewall and levator ani, the perineal body, and Level I apical support.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[6]](#ref6)</sup> A defect or attenuation in this support allows the anterior rectal wall to bow forward during Valsalva.

Some authors debate whether a discrete histologic "rectovaginal fascia" exists as a separate layer. Operatively, the key concept remains useful: the surgeon restores posterior vaginal wall support by plicating or reattaching the fibromuscular layer while avoiding levator over-tightening and rectal injury.

### Defect Patterns

| Defect | Pattern | Exam / imaging clues |
|---|---|---|
| **Midline rectocele** | Central attenuation of posterior support | Smooth posterior bulge; stool trapping; often treated with midline plication |
| **Transverse / apical posterior defect** | Separation near the vaginal apex or cul-de-sac | Enterocele, high posterior wall descent, apical descent on POP-Q |
| **Distal perineal defect** | Perineal body deficiency | Short PB, widened GH, gaping introitus, distal stool trapping |
| **Enterocele / sigmoidocele** | Peritoneal sac descends between rectum and vagina | May be occult; rectovaginal exam, defecography, or MRI can help |
| **Functional outlet disorder** | Dyssynergic pelvic-floor contraction or nonrelaxing puborectalis | ODS symptoms out of proportion to anatomy; abnormal balloon expulsion / anorectal testing |

### Risk Factors

| Risk factor | Relevance |
|---|---|
| **Vaginal delivery / parity** | Increases prevalence and size of rectocele; obstetric trauma may injure perineal body and levator complex |
| **Age** | Progressive connective tissue and neuromuscular support decline |
| **Obesity** | Chronic increased abdominal pressure and recurrence risk |
| **Chronic straining** | Constipation, heavy lifting, chronic cough, and repetitive Valsalva load repairs and native support |
| **Levator ani injury / enlarged hiatus** | Independent risk for POP and obstructed defecation symptoms |
| **Apical prolapse** | Apex-driven posterior descent or enterocele can masquerade as isolated rectocele |
| **Prior pelvic surgery** | Scarring, altered axis, and uncorrected apical support can influence recurrence |

## Clinical Presentation

### Prolapse Symptoms

The most specific POP symptom is **vaginal bulge**. Patients may also report pelvic pressure, heaviness, dragging, vaginal tissue rubbing, or difficulty with intercourse.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[9]](#ref9)</sup>

Posterior prolapse becomes clinically meaningful when anatomy matches bother. A small rectocele found incidentally does not require treatment; a larger rectocele with stool trapping, bulge, and splinting is a different disease state.

### Defecatory Symptoms

| Symptom | Association with posterior prolapse |
|---|---|
| **Splinting / digitation** | Strongest practical clue for symptomatic rectocele; patients may press the posterior vagina or perineum to evacuate |
| **Straining** | Common but nonspecific; also reflects constipation and dyssynergia |
| **Incomplete evacuation** | May reflect stool trapping in rectocele or functional outlet obstruction |
| **Pain with defecation** | Suggests fissure, pelvic-floor spasm, levator pain, or difficult evacuation |
| **Fecal urgency / liquid FI** | Can coexist; evaluate stool form and sphincter function |
| **Constipation** | Reported in 30-70% of rectocele patients but is not proof that the rectocele is causal |

Defecatory symptoms most linked to posterior wall anatomy, especially splinting, straining, and incomplete evacuation, are the symptoms most likely to improve after posterior repair.<sup>[[10]](#ref10)[[11]](#ref11)</sup> Posterior prolapse does not necessarily cause new defecatory symptoms in asymptomatic women, but it may increase persistence or progression of existing symptoms.<sup>[[11]](#ref11)</sup>

### Sexual Function

Symptomatic prolapse is associated with worse sexual function than asymptomatic anatomic prolapse.<sup>[[12]](#ref12)[[13]](#ref13)</sup> Posterior prolapse can cause dyspareunia, avoidance because of bulge or body-image concerns, decreased arousal, difficulty with orgasm, and fear of incontinence. Posterior repair often improves bother-related sexual symptoms, but over-plication and levatorplasty increase risk of de novo dyspareunia.

## Diagnostic Evaluation

### Physical Examination

Use a dynamic [female pelvic examination](/docs/evaluation/history-physical/female-pelvic-examination) with split speculum isolation.

Core steps:

1. Retract the anterior wall with the anterior blade or split speculum.
2. Ask for maximal Valsalva and cough; repeat standing if symptoms are not reproduced.
3. Record POP-Q **Ap**, **Bp**, **C**, **D** when applicable, **TVL**, **GH**, and **PB**.
4. Assess perineal body length, scarring, gaping introitus, levator tone, and resting/squeeze function.
5. Perform rectovaginal examination to distinguish rectocele, enterocele, perineocele, stool trapping, and rectovaginal septum integrity.
6. Reduce the apex to determine whether posterior descent is apex-driven.

### Adjunctive Testing

| Test | Use |
|---|---|
| **PVR / urinalysis** | If concomitant urinary symptoms, advanced POP, recurrent UTI, or surgery planned |
| **Defecography** | Complex ODS, suspected stool trapping, intussusception, enterocele, sigmoidocele, or recurrent posterior repair |
| **MR defecography** | Multicompartment pelvic-floor disorder, suspected enterocele, or need to distinguish rectocele from cul-de-sac hernia |
| **Translabial ultrasound** | Dynamic anatomic assessment of rectocele depth, levator avulsion, hiatal area, and enterocele |
| **Anorectal physiology testing** | ODS out of proportion to anatomy, suspected dyssynergia, fecal incontinence, or colorectal co-management |

Clinically meaningful rectocele features on imaging include preferential filling during defecation, failure to empty / contrast trapping, and larger size. A threshold of **>2 cm** is often used for definition, while **>5 cm** plus trapping and bothersome symptoms is more persuasive when considering surgery for ODS.<sup>[[6]](#ref6)[[8]](#ref8)[[14]](#ref14)</sup>

## Treatment

### Conservative Management

Observation is appropriate for asymptomatic posterior prolapse. Many rectoceles found on exam or imaging do not need intervention.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[15]](#ref15)</sup>

Conservative treatment targets both support and bowel mechanics:

- Fiber, osmotic laxatives, hydration, stool-form optimization, and avoidance of repetitive straining.
- [Pelvic floor physical therapy](/docs/surgical-techniques/04f-incontinence-procedures/procedures/pelvic-floor-pt) for pelvic-floor coordination, levator overactivity, dyspareunia, ODS, and coexisting urinary symptoms.
- Biofeedback therapy when dyssynergic defecation or nonrelaxing puborectalis is suspected.
- Vaginal pessary for symptomatic prolapse in patients who want nonsurgical treatment or are poor surgical candidates.
- Treat [chronic constipation](/docs/clinical-conditions/03i-defecatory-disorders/chronic-constipation) and [fecal incontinence](/docs/clinical-conditions/03i-defecatory-disorders/fecal-incontinence) mechanisms before labeling symptoms as surgical rectocele failure.

Women with defecatory dysfunction and coexistent rectocele should generally start with conservative bowel and pelvic-floor management, especially when bulge is not the dominant symptom.<sup>[[8]](#ref8)[[14]](#ref14)</sup>

### Indications for Surgery

Surgery is considered for bothersome posterior vaginal bulge, pressure, sexual dysfunction, or defecatory dysfunction that persists despite appropriate conservative treatment.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[8]](#ref8)[[14]](#ref14)</sup>

Stronger selection features for rectocele repair in ODS include:

- bothersome vaginal bulge or protrusion,
- need for vaginal or perineal splinting,
- large rectocele, especially **>5 cm**,
- trapping / non-emptying on defecography,
- failure of bowel optimization and biofeedback,
- exclusion or treatment of dyssynergia, intussusception, rectal prolapse, and severe sphincter dysfunction.

### Posterior Colporrhaphy and Perineorrhaphy

[Posterior colporrhaphy](/docs/surgical-techniques/04g-prolapse-repair/posterior-colporrhaphy) is the standard transvaginal native-tissue repair for symptomatic rectocele. It uses midline plication of posterior vaginal fibromuscular tissue after separating the vaginal epithelium from the rectum. [Perineorrhaphy](/docs/surgical-techniques/04g-prolapse-repair/perineorrhaphy) reconstructs the perineal body when distal support and the genital hiatus are deficient.<sup>[[2]](#ref2)[[16]](#ref16)</sup>

| Step | Principle |
|---|---|
| Posterior vaginal incision | Midline incision from distal posterior wall toward the apex as needed |
| Rectovaginal dissection | Develop safe plane while avoiding rectal entry |
| Fascial / fibromuscular plication | Midline delayed-absorbable plication; avoid excessive narrowing |
| Enterocele repair if present | Reduce sac and restore apical / cul-de-sac support |
| Perineorrhaphy if needed | Reapproximate perineal muscles and normalize GH/PB without over-tightening |
| Rectal exam | Confirm no rectal suture or injury before closure is complete |

Midline fascial plication is generally preferred over levatorplasty. Levatorplasty can narrow the introitus but carries higher dyspareunia risk and is not routine for posterior compartment prolapse.<sup>[[2]](#ref2)[[16]](#ref16)</sup>

Reported outcomes vary by definition and follow-up. Transvaginal rectocele repair improves vaginal bulge in most appropriately selected patients and improves ODS symptoms in a majority, but symptom recurrence can increase over time.<sup>[[16]](#ref16)[[17]](#ref17)</sup>

### Surgical Approach Comparison

| Approach | Role | Main tradeoffs |
|---|---|---|
| **Transvaginal native tissue repair** | Standard approach for symptomatic posterior vaginal wall prolapse | Best anatomic posterior repair data; dyspareunia risk if over-tightened |
| **Transanal repair** | Selected colorectal use when rectal mucosal pathology dominates | Inferior anatomic posterior wall outcomes; rectal morbidity; less improvement in ODS |
| **Transperineal repair** | Distal rectocele / perineal body deficiency; limited data | Useful for perineal reconstruction; less standardized evidence base |
| **Ventral mesh rectopexy** | Rectal intussusception, rectal prolapse, complex ODS with colorectal indication | Abdominal mesh procedure; colorectal collaboration; longer operative time |

The transvaginal approach is superior to the transanal approach for posterior vaginal wall prolapse repair in available comparative evidence.<sup>[[2]](#ref2)[[16]](#ref16)[[18]](#ref18)</sup> Stapled transanal rectal resection (STARR) is not recommended for routine rectocele repair because of complication concerns and better alternatives in selected patients.<sup>[[8]](#ref8)</sup>

### Mesh and Biologic Grafts

Synthetic mesh and biologic grafts do **not** improve routine posterior vaginal wall repair outcomes enough to justify use and may increase complications.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[18]](#ref18)[[19]](#ref19)</sup> ACOG recommends that synthetic mesh or biologic grafts should not be placed routinely through posterior vaginal wall incisions for primary posterior wall prolapse repair.

This is separate from abdominal mesh procedures performed for selected colorectal or apical indications, such as ventral mesh rectopexy or sacrocolpopexy, where risk-benefit counseling is different.

### Ventral Mesh Rectopexy

Laparoscopic ventral mesh rectopexy suspends the anterior rectal wall to the sacral promontory and is primarily a colorectal operation for rectal prolapse or internal intussusception with ODS. Compared with posterior colporrhaphy in selected anterior rectocele cohorts, it may provide better defecographic correction and improvements in constipation, sexual symptoms, and quality of life, at the cost of longer operative time and mesh-specific risk.<sup>[[20]](#ref20)</sup>

Ventral rectopexy should not be viewed as the default operation for a vaginal rectocele. It is best considered when rectal prolapse / intussusception, enterocele, or complex posterior pelvic-floor disease drives symptoms and colorectal collaboration is appropriate.

## Sexual Function Outcomes

Posterior prolapse surgery often improves sexual function when the preoperative driver is bulge, avoidance, stool trapping, or body-image concern.<sup>[[12]](#ref12)[[13]](#ref13)</sup> However, posterior repair has a recognized risk of de novo dyspareunia, especially with levatorplasty, aggressive introitus narrowing, untreated atrophy/GSM, or pelvic-floor hypertonicity.

Counseling should explicitly cover:

- baseline dyspareunia and pelvic-floor tenderness,
- desire for future penetrative vaginal intercourse,
- planned degree of perineorrhaphy / introital narrowing,
- GSM treatment when indicated,
- expectation that bowel and sexual symptoms may not track perfectly with anatomic success.

---

## Long-Term Outcomes and Recurrence

Posterior repair outcomes depend on endpoint. Anatomic success, bulge resolution, splinting improvement, constipation improvement, dyspareunia, and reoperation are different outcomes.

| Outcome domain | Pattern |
|---|---|
| **Bulge** | Usually improves substantially after posterior colporrhaphy in selected patients |
| **Splinting / stool trapping** | More likely to improve when rectocele is large and traps stool |
| **Global constipation** | Less predictable; often depends on stool form, transit, and dyssynergia |
| **Dyspareunia** | May improve if prolapse-related; may worsen if repair is over-tightened |
| **Recurrence** | Increases with longer follow-up and uncorrected apical / hiatal support failure |

Defecatory symptoms associated most strongly with degree of posterior wall descent tend to improve most after surgery.<sup>[[10]](#ref10)</sup> Symptoms can progressively deteriorate over time, so long-term follow-up and continued bowel management matter even after a technically successful repair.<sup>[[17]](#ref17)</sup>

## Key Recommendations

- Treat only symptomatic posterior prolapse; incidental rectocele on exam or imaging does not mandate repair.
- Use POP-Q **Ap**, **Bp**, **GH**, **PB**, **C/D**, and rectovaginal exam to define anatomy.
- Look for apical prolapse and enterocele before calling a defect "isolated rectocele."
- Treat constipation, stool form, and pelvic-floor dyssynergia before surgery for ODS-predominant symptoms.
- Favor transvaginal native-tissue posterior colporrhaphy for symptomatic posterior vaginal wall prolapse.
- Add perineorrhaphy when the perineal body is deficient or the genital hiatus is widened.
- Avoid routine levatorplasty because of dyspareunia risk.
- Do not routinely use synthetic mesh or biologic grafts through posterior vaginal wall incisions.
- Consider colorectal collaboration for rectal prolapse, intussusception, complex ODS, fecal incontinence, or failed posterior repairs.
