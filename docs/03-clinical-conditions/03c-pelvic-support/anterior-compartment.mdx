# Anterior Compartment Prolapse

**Anterior compartment pelvic organ prolapse** is descent of the anterior vaginal wall, allowing the bladder and sometimes the urethra to approach or pass the hymen.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[2]](#ref2)</sup> It is the most common form of pelvic organ prolapse, and recurrent anterior prolapse is more likely after native-tissue repair than after biological graft or mesh augmentation.<sup>[[3]](#ref3)</sup>

Identifying a cystocele does not answer the operative question, which is whether the descent is driven by central fibromuscular attenuation, lateral detachment from the arcus tendineus fascia pelvis (ATFP), apical loss of Level I support, an enlarged levator hiatus, or a combination. Different drivers demand different repairs.

See also: [POP Overview](/docs/clinical-conditions/03c-pelvic-support/pelvic-organ-prolapse) for the DeLancey framework, POP-Q staging, pessary management, and mesh-regulatory history. This page covers what is distinctive about the anterior compartment. For operative options and comparative outcomes, see the [Prolapse Repair atlas](/docs/surgical-techniques/04g-prolapse-repair).

---

## Definition and Terminology

POP-Q uses **point Ba** — the most distal point on the anterior vaginal wall — rather than an organ-based label, because examination alone may not prove which organ is behind the epithelium.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[5]](#ref5)</sup>

| Term | Meaning | Surgical implication |
|---|---|---|
| **Anterior vaginal wall prolapse** | Descent of the anterior vaginal wall on POP-Q | Preferred descriptive term |
| **Cystocele** | Bladder descent behind the anterior vaginal wall | Common shorthand; implies an organ assumption |
| **Urethrocele** | Descent / hypermobility of the urethra | Evaluate with urethral mobility and stress testing |
| **Cystourethrocele** | Combined bladder base and urethral descent | Often coexists with SUI in mild-to-moderate prolapse |
| **Central defect** | Midline attenuation of the pubocervical fibromuscular layer | Classic anterior colporrhaphy target |
| **Paravaginal (lateral) defect** | Detachment of anterior vaginal wall from the ATFP | May require lateral reattachment, not midline plication |
| **Apical contribution** | Descent of the cervix or cuff that carries the anterior wall down | Apical suspension required for durable repair |

After radical cystectomy, an anterior enterocele occupying the vesicovaginal space has been described as a rare cause of new anterior vaginal bulging in case reports and a case series; it belongs in the differential of a post-cystectomy patient with a new anterior bulge rather than being assumed to be a recurrent cystocele.<sup>[[19]](#ref19)</sup>

## Compartment-Specific Pathophysiology

Anterior wall integrity depends on all three DeLancey levels (see [POP Overview](/docs/clinical-conditions/03c-pelvic-support/pelvic-organ-prolapse#anatomy-and-levels-of-support-delancey)), but the distinctive anterior-compartment finding is that anterior descent is rarely a pure failure of any one support. MRI-based 3D modeling shows that anterior wall descent follows a **collinear triad** of apical location, paravaginal location, and levator hiatus size, which together explain up to 83% of the variation in cystocele size on imaging.<sup>[[4]](#ref4)</sup>

In this MRI study, apical location and levator hiatus size were associated with cystocele size; isolated anterior colporrhaphy outcomes were not studied.<sup>[[4]](#ref4)</sup>

### Types of anterior wall defect

| Defect | Clinical clues | Repair implication |
|---|---|---|
| **Central / midline** | Smooth central bulge; lateral sulci preserved | Anterior colporrhaphy adequate **if** apex is supported |
| **Suspected paravaginal / lateral** | Loss of a lateral sulcus or asymmetric descent raises suspicion; examination alone is inconsistent for diagnosis | Assess apical and overall support; consider paravaginal repair when lateral detachment is convincing<sup>[[2]](#ref2)</sup> |
| **Apical contribution** | Cervix or cuff descends with the anterior wall; Ba improves when the apex is reduced | Assess whether apical descent is clinically significant and restore support when it is deficient<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup> |
| **Distal / urethrocele** | Urethral hypermobility, distal descent, gaping hiatus | Address urethral mobility; evaluate SUI |
| **Combined** | Most common clinical pattern | Repair must target the dominant failure, not just the visible bulge |

### Risk factors

Generic POP risk factors apply (see [parent](/docs/clinical-conditions/03c-pelvic-support/pelvic-organ-prolapse#pathophysiology-and-risk-factors)). **Distinctive for the anterior compartment:**

- **Levator ani defect** and an enlarged hiatus are among the strongest risk factors for primary prolapse (pooled OR 3.99 for levator defect); higher preoperative prolapse stage (pooled OR 2.68) and younger age predict recurrence after native-tissue repair<sup>[[6]](#ref6)</sup>
- **Apical descent** travels with the anterior wall: on dynamic MRI, apical support explained about half of the variation in bladder-base position (r = 0.73), and finite-element modeling produces a larger cystocele with apical than with paravaginal impairment<sup>[[9]](#ref9)[[14]](#ref14)</sup>. Uncorrected apical support is therefore a leading modifiable cause of recurrent cystocele

## Clinical Presentation — Urinary Focus

The anterior compartment is the one where urinary symptoms drive the conversation.

| Symptom | Relationship to anterior prolapse |
|---|---|
| **Obstructive voiding** (hesitancy, weak stream, intermittency, incomplete emptying) | Urethral kinking against the levator hiatus; more common once prolapse passes the hymen |
| **Elevated PVR** | Among 184 women with POP in a 655-woman uroflowmetry cohort, 30% had PVR &gt;100 mL.<sup>[[22]](#ref22)</sup> |
| **Stress urinary incontinence** | Coexists with mild-to-moderate prolapse; may be **masked** by advanced prolapse (urethral kinking) |
| **Occult (masked) SUI** | Leakage appears only after prolapse is reduced; assess before surgery in women with significant anterior or apical prolapse.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup> |
| **Urgency / OAB** | Common but variable; may or may not improve after repair |
| **Recurrent UTI** | Inefficient emptying from mechanical obstruction |

**Romanzi's urodynamic series (60 women):** bladder outlet obstruction was found in 72% of those with grade 3–4 cystocele (18 of 25 women) versus 6% with grade 1–2 (2 of 35), and it normalized in 94% (17 of 18) after ring-pessary reduction.<sup>[[7]](#ref7)</sup> Consider detrusor underactivity and other outlet causes when symptoms, reduction testing and PVR do not agree.

In a separate, smaller retrospective series of 24 women with anterior vaginal wall prolapse and urinary retention (PVR ≥100 mL) who had a preoperative pessary trial, pessary reduction predicted postoperative cure of retention (PVR &lt;100 mL at 3 months) with 89% sensitivity, 80% specificity and a 79% overall cure rate (19 of 24).<sup>[[20]](#ref20)</sup>

## Compartment-Specific Evaluation

Generic exam and adjunctive-testing framework lives in the [parent article](/docs/clinical-conditions/03c-pelvic-support/pelvic-organ-prolapse#evaluation). The anterior-specific maneuvers that matter:

1. **Split speculum** (posterior blade retracts posterior wall) to isolate anterior descent.
2. **Valsalva or cough**; if symptoms are not reproduced supine, examine standing.
3. **Apex-reduction test.** Manually reduce the apex with ring forceps, swab, or speculum. If Ba improves with apical reduction, apical support contributes to anterior descent; assess the clinically relevant apical and residual anterior defects when planning repair.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[23]](#ref23)</sup>
4. **Urethral mobility** and **cough stress test** with prolapse reduced.
5. **Post-void residual.** Measure in every patient with prolapse beyond the hymen or voiding symptoms.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup>

### Occult SUI — the key pre-op decision point

Women with significant anterior or apical prolapse should have a stress test with the prolapse reduced (pessary, ring forceps, speculum, or swab) before surgery.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[15]](#ref15)</sup> In patients having both prolapse repair and SUI surgery, AUA/SUFU 2023 says clinicians may use a midurethral sling, pubovaginal sling, or Burch colposuspension (Conditional Recommendation; Grade C). For women without demonstrable SUI, it discusses prophylactic anti-incontinence surgery as a shared decision after counseling about benefit and added morbidity.<sup>[[15]](#ref15)</sup> Repairing the prolapse relieves urethral kinking and can unmask SUI. Counseling points:

- In women with occult SUI, a concomitant midurethral sling probably lowers postoperative SUI (RR 0.38; about 34% with prolapse surgery alone versus 10–22% with a sling) and further continence surgery.<sup>[[8]](#ref8)</sup> Sling-related adverse effects remain poorly defined; EAU 2026 notes a tendency toward more voiding problems and more serious events (bladder perforation, urethral injury, tape exposure) with combined surgery. Delaying the sling and operating only if SUI persists is a reasonable alternative.<sup>[[8]](#ref8)[[16]](#ref16)</sup>
- In women without symptomatic or occult SUI, NICE NG123 advises against anti-incontinence surgery solely to prevent leakage, and EAU 2026 makes a Strong recommendation against adding it to abdominal prolapse surgery. AUA/SUFU 2023 permits consideration of a concomitant procedure after counseling about benefit and morbidity.<sup>[[15]](#ref15)[[16]](#ref16)[[17]](#ref17)</sup>
- The decision is driven by preoperative leakage severity with reduction, patient preference, and anatomy.

## Compartment-Specific Surgical Management

Generic POP surgical principles, sacrocolpopexy, colpocleisis, and FDA transvaginal-mesh history are covered in the [parent article's Surgical Management section](/docs/clinical-conditions/03c-pelvic-support/pelvic-organ-prolapse#surgical-management). What follows is anterior-specific.

### Anterior colporrhaphy

Plication of the anterior vaginal fibromuscular layer in the midline after vaginal-wall dissection.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[3]](#ref3)</sup> ACOG and AUGS describe it as effective for most anterior prolapse; many women with anterior prolapse also have apical prolapse, and concurrent apical resupport reduces recurrent prolapse surgery.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup>

| Outcome | Pattern |
|---|---|
| Composite success at 1 year | Depends on the threshold. In the Altman RCT, composite success (POP-Q stage 0–1 and no bulge symptoms) was 34.5% after colporrhaphy; more lenient definitions (for example, anterior wall above the hymen) give higher figures<sup>[[11]](#ref11)</sup> |
| Versus biological graft | At 1–2 years, recurrent anterior prolapse is more likely after native repair (RR 1.53; illustrative absolute risks: if 21% recur after graft, 24–40% would recur after native repair); awareness of prolapse and repeat surgery do not differ significantly<sup>[[3]](#ref3)</sup> |
| Subjective success | Typically higher than anatomic — many women are not bothered by mild recurrence |
| Morbidity | Native-tissue anterior repair has no transvaginal POP mesh exposure from the repair itself; anterior-colporrhaphy sutures can compromise a ureter. In a retrospective cystoscopy series of 526 mixed procedures, these sutures prompted seven intraoperative management changes (2.0% of anterior colporrhaphies).<sup>[[24]](#ref24)</sup> |
| Failure pattern | Recurrent anterior descent, usually from uncorrected apical or lateral support |

### Paravaginal repair

Reattaches the lateral anterior vaginal wall to the ATFP by a vaginal, abdominal, laparoscopic, or robotic route. In one small RCT (70 women), abdominal paravaginal repair and anterior colporrhaphy with absorbable mesh had similar 2-year objective failure (40% versus 32%, not significant), with most failures asymptomatic.<sup>[[10]](#ref10)</sup> Physical examination detects paravaginal defects inconsistently; ultrasound and MRI help, and pure lateral defects without a central component are uncommon.<sup>[[2]](#ref2)</sup> Paravaginal repair is most defensible when lateral sulcal detachment is convincing, the central defect is not dominant, apical support is also restored, and the surgeon has specific experience with the technique.

### Apical suspension — not optional

Apical location is one of the strongest predictors of cystocele size, and anterior recurrence is common when apical descent is ignored. Apical support must be addressed whenever it is deficient; options (uterosacral ligament suspension, sacrospinous ligament fixation, sacrocolpopexy, or uterine-sparing hysteropexy) are discussed in the [parent article](/docs/clinical-conditions/03c-pelvic-support/pelvic-organ-prolapse#surgical-management) and in [Apical Prolapse](/docs/clinical-conditions/03c-pelvic-support/apical-prolapse).<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[4]](#ref4)</sup> In the OPTIMAL trial of 374 women randomized to vaginal apical repair, all but three received a midurethral sling; corrected 2-year composite prolapse success was 64.5% (100/155) after uterosacral suspension and 63.1% (94/149) after sacrospinous fixation, with no significant difference.<sup>[[18]](#ref18)</sup> This composite endpoint is not isolated anterior-compartment success or long-term durability.<sup>[[18]](#ref18)</sup>

### Transvaginal mesh — anterior-specific context

The April 2026 Cochrane update (41 RCTs, 4,531 women) finds that, at 1–2 years, native-tissue anterior repair probably leads to more prolapse awareness (RR 1.77), repeat prolapse surgery (RR 2.17) and, with lower certainty, recurrence (RR 3.21) than permanent transvaginal mesh, with little or no difference in SUI surgery or dyspareunia. The authors advise caution with the mesh kits still marketed elsewhere,<sup>[[3]](#ref3)</sup> and NICE NG123 recommends anterior repair without mesh.<sup>[[17]](#ref17)</sup> This efficacy comparison does not settle the overall benefit–harm decision or make a withdrawn product category available again. Compare mesh complications, sexual symptoms and repeat surgery as separate outcomes.<sup>[[3]](#ref3)</sup>

In the Altman 2011 NEJM RCT, transvaginal permanent mesh improved anterior anatomic success (POP-Q stage 0–1: 82.3% versus 47.5%) and composite success (anatomic success plus no bulge symptoms: 60.8% versus 34.5%) at 1 year compared with native tissue, but had more bladder perforation (3.5% versus 0.5%), new SUI (12.3% versus 6.3%), mesh-exposure reintervention (6/186, 3.2%), and longer operations with more bleeding.<sup>[[11]](#ref11)</sup> The April 2019 FDA order required manufacturers to stop selling and distributing mesh intended for transvaginal POP repair (including anterior-wall products) and applies to this product category; it does not apply to midurethral slings or to abdominal sacrocolpopexy mesh. See the [parent article](/docs/clinical-conditions/03c-pelvic-support/pelvic-organ-prolapse#transvaginal-mesh--fda-actions-and-current-status) for the full regulatory timeline.

### Severe cystourethrocele

For grade IV cystourethrocele with urethral hypermobility and coexisting SUI, a combined repair is often required. Raz's classic vaginal series (51 women with grade IV cystocele) paired anterior colporrhaphy for the central defect with needle suspension of the bladder base and neck for the paravaginal herniation, and 46 of 51 needed both regardless of preoperative SUI; apical and other vaginal defects were repaired at the same operation.<sup>[[12]](#ref12)</sup> In a separate cohort of 148 women having pelvic reconstructive surgery for a cystocele reaching or beyond the hymen, a concomitant suburethral sling was independently associated with less cystocele recurrence at 1 year (19% versus 42% without a sling; adjusted OR 0.29), a signal that a concomitant continence procedure may also support the anterior repair and not only treat incontinence.<sup>[[21]](#ref21)</sup>

## Outcomes and Follow-Up

Anterior-specific post-op priorities:

- **Voiding function.** PVR at first follow-up if preoperative retention, advanced prolapse, or concurrent sling
- **Postoperative SUI.** For women with occult SUI undergoing vaginal prolapse repair, the 2026 Cochrane review estimates postoperative subjective SUI in about 34% after prolapse surgery alone.<sup>[[8]](#ref8)</sup>
- **Recurrent UTI.** Should resolve with obstruction relief; persistence prompts re-evaluation
- **Anatomic surveillance.** Aa, Ba, C/D, gh; recurrent descent is most commonly apical-driven even when it looks anterior

In a prospective series of 74 women with stage II or greater anterior prolapse, 61/74 (82%) reported marked LUTS improvement at 6 months after surgery; 41/74 also had an anti-incontinence procedure, so the result is not an isolated prolapse-repair effect.<sup>[[13]](#ref13)</sup> Detrusor underactivity accompanied persistent voiding symptoms, and preoperative detrusor overactivity and PVR were poor predictors of outcome, so persistent urgency or voiding symptoms may need separate evaluation and management.<sup>[[13]](#ref13)</sup>

## Key Recommendations

- Describe the defect in POP-Q language (Ba), and not only as "cystocele."
- Classify the defect as central, lateral, apical-driven, distal, or combined. Most are combined.
- **Reduce the apex on exam.** If Ba improves, apical support contributes to anterior descent; restore apical support when the apical defect is clinically significant.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup>
- Measure PVR if prolapse extends beyond the hymen or voiding symptoms exist.
- Evaluate occult SUI with prolapse reduction before any repair.
- **Avoid isolated anterior repair when the apex is significantly descended**; restore apical support at the same operation.
- Offer pessary and pelvic-floor therapy first where appropriate (see [parent](/docs/clinical-conditions/03c-pelvic-support/pelvic-organ-prolapse#nonsurgical-management)).
- Do not use transvaginal mesh as routine primary anterior repair.
