# Anterior Compartment Prolapse

**Anterior compartment pelvic organ prolapse** is descent of the anterior vaginal wall, allowing the bladder and sometimes the urethra to descend toward or beyond the hymen.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[2]](#ref2)</sup> It is the most common compartment involved in pelvic organ prolapse and the compartment most prone to recurrence after isolated repair.<sup>[[3]](#ref3)[[4]](#ref4)</sup>

For the reconstructive urologist and urogynecologist, anterior prolapse is rarely just a "cystocele." The operative question is whether the visible anterior wall descent is driven by central fibromuscular attenuation, lateral detachment from the arcus tendineus fascia pelvis, apical loss of Level I support, levator hiatus enlargement, or a combination of these failures.

See also: [POP Overview](/docs/clinical-conditions/03c-pelvic-support/pelvic-organ-prolapse), [Female Pelvic Examination](/docs/evaluation/history-physical/female-pelvic-examination), [The Vagina](/docs/foundations/anatomy-physiology/genitalia/vaginal-anatomy), [The Bladder](/docs/foundations/anatomy-physiology/urinary-tract/bladder-anatomy-physiology), [The Female Urethra](/docs/foundations/anatomy-physiology/urinary-tract/female-urethra), [Anterior Colporrhaphy](/docs/surgical-techniques/04g-prolapse-repair/anterior-colporrhaphy), and [Vaginal Paravaginal Repair](/docs/surgical-techniques/04g-prolapse-repair/vaginal-paravaginal-repair).

## Definition and Terminology

The POP-Q system describes anterior compartment descent using **point Ba**, the most distal point on the anterior vaginal wall. This avoids assuming that the organ behind the prolapsed epithelium is always bladder, because clinical examination alone may not distinguish bladder, urethra, enteric contents, or combined defects with certainty.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[5]](#ref5)</sup>

| Term | Meaning | Surgical implication |
|---|---|---|
| **Anterior vaginal wall prolapse** | Descent of the anterior vaginal wall on POP-Q examination | Preferred descriptive term |
| **Cystocele** | Bladder descent behind the anterior vaginal wall | Common shorthand; can imply bladder-specific anatomy that may not be proven |
| **Urethrocele** | Descent / hypermobility of the urethra | Evaluate with urethral mobility and stress testing |
| **Cystourethrocele** | Combined bladder base and urethral descent | Often coexists with SUI in mild to moderate prolapse |
| **Paravaginal defect** | Lateral detachment of the anterior vaginal wall from the ATFP / pelvic sidewall | May require lateral reattachment rather than midline plication alone |
| **Central defect** | Midline attenuation of the anterior vaginal fibromuscular layer | Classic anterior colporrhaphy target |
| **Apical contribution** | Descent of the cervix or vaginal cuff that carries the anterior wall down | Requires apical suspension to avoid recurrent anterior prolapse |

## Epidemiology

POP is seen on examination in **40-60%** of parous women, but many anatomic findings are asymptomatic.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[6]](#ref6)</sup> The anterior compartment is the most commonly affected vaginal site and is the most common site of recurrent prolapse after surgery.<sup>[[3]](#ref3)[[4]](#ref4)</sup>

Annual prolapse surgery rates in the United States are commonly estimated around **10-30 per 10,000 women**, and roughly **10%** of women undergo surgery for prolapse or urinary incontinence during their lifetime.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[7]](#ref7)</sup>

Symptoms correlate imperfectly with anatomy. Bulge symptoms usually emerge when the leading edge reaches or passes the hymenal ring, whereas many women with lesser anterior wall descent on examination have no meaningful bother.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[8]](#ref8)</sup>

## Anatomy and Pathophysiology

### DeLancey Support Levels

Anterior wall support depends on all three DeLancey levels:<sup>[[2]](#ref2)[[9]](#ref9)</sup>

| Level | Anterior compartment role | Failure pattern |
|---|---|---|
| **Level I** | Cardinal-uterosacral complex suspends the cervix / upper vagina | Apical descent, transverse anterior defect, enterocele, recurrent cystocele if uncorrected |
| **Level II** | Pubocervical fibromuscular layer attaches laterally to the arcus tendineus fascia pelvis | Central cystocele or lateral paravaginal defect |
| **Level III** | Distal vagina fuses with urethra, perineal membrane, and perineal body | Distal urethrocele, gaping introitus, perineal deficiency |

### Types of Anterior Wall Defects

| Defect type | Description | Clinical clues | Operative consequence |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Central / midline defect** | Midline attenuation or separation of the anterior fibromuscular layer | Smooth central bulge; lateral sulci may be preserved | Anterior colporrhaphy may be adequate if apex is supported |
| **Paravaginal / lateral defect** | Detachment of anterior vaginal wall from ATFP | Loss of lateral sulcus; lateral wall descent; asymmetric defect possible | Consider paravaginal repair or apical repair that restores lateral support |
| **Apical defect** | Loss of Level I support dragging the anterior wall down | Cervix or cuff descends with anterior wall; high Ba often improves when apex is reduced | Apical suspension is mandatory for durable repair |
| **Distal defect / urethrocele** | Level III deficiency near urethra and perineal membrane | Urethral hypermobility, distal anterior wall descent, gaping hiatus | Evaluate SUI and urethral mobility |
| **Combined defect** | Multiple support failures coexist | Most common clinical pattern | Repair must target the dominant failure, not just the visible bulge |

MRI-based modeling has shown that anterior wall descent is strongly explained by a **collinear triad** of apical location, paravaginal location, and levator hiatus size, accounting for up to **83%** of variation in cystocele size.<sup>[[4]](#ref4)</sup> That is the anatomic reason isolated anterior colporrhaphy fails when the apex and hiatus are ignored.

### Risk Factors

Risk factors for primary prolapse overlap across compartments but matter in anterior prolapse because recurrence is common.

| Risk factor | Effect / association |
|---|---|
| **Vaginal delivery and parity** | Established risk factors |
| **Forceps delivery / obstetric trauma** | Increases levator and fascial injury risk |
| **Birthweight** | OR 1.04 per 100 g increment in meta-analysis |
| **Age** | OR 1.34 per decade |
| **BMI / obesity** | OR 1.75 per 5 kg/m2 increment |
| **Levator ani defect** | OR 3.99; one of the strongest anatomic predictors |
| **Enlarged levator hiatus** | Strongly associated with cystocele size and recurrence |
| **Cesarean delivery** | Protective association in pooled data |
| **Chronic constipation / straining** | Raises repetitive load on anterior and apical repairs |

Risk factors for recurrence include preoperative stage III-IV prolapse, younger age at vaginal prolapse surgery, obesity, levator avulsion, and failure to correct apical support.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[6]](#ref6)</sup>

## Clinical Presentation

The symptom most specific to anterior compartment prolapse is **vaginal bulge**. Pelvic pressure, heaviness, dragging sensation, low back discomfort, and the need to manually reduce the prolapse to void or defecate can occur, but they are less specific.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[8]](#ref8)</sup>

### Urinary Symptoms

| Symptom | Relationship to anterior prolapse |
|---|---|
| **Obstructive voiding** | More common when prolapse extends beyond the hymen; urethral kinking can cause hesitancy, weak stream, intermittency, and elevated PVR |
| **Incomplete emptying** | Up to 30% of women with stage III-IV prolapse have PVR &gt;100 mL in some series |
| **Stress urinary incontinence** | May coexist with mild / moderate prolapse; may be masked by advanced prolapse |
| **Occult SUI** | Leakage appears only after prolapse reduction; essential to identify before repair |
| **Urgency / OAB** | Common but variable; may improve after repair in some patients but should not be assumed to be prolapse-driven |

Women with advanced prolapse are often **less likely** to report SUI and **more likely** to report voiding obstruction because the prolapse mechanically kinks or compresses the urethra.<sup>[[8]](#ref8)[[10]](#ref10)</sup> In Romanzi's urodynamic series, **58%** of women with grade 3-4 cystocele had bladder outlet obstruction, and obstruction normalized in **94%** after pessary reduction.<sup>[[10]](#ref10)</sup>

## Evaluation

### Physical Examination

Anterior compartment evaluation is dynamic. A small supine bulge may underestimate the functional defect.

Core steps:

1. Use a **split speculum** or posterior blade to retract the posterior wall.
2. Ask for maximal Valsalva and cough; repeat several times.
3. If the patient's symptoms are not reproduced supine, repeat the examination standing.
4. Record POP-Q values, especially **Aa**, **Ba**, **C**, **D** when uterus is present, **gh**, **pb**, and **tvl**.
5. Reduce the apex manually or with a speculum to estimate how much anterior descent is apical-driven.
6. Assess urethral mobility, cough stress test, vaginal atrophy/GSM, levator tone, levator tenderness, and perineal body integrity.

POP-Q stage is determined by the most distal point of prolapse:<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[5]](#ref5)</sup>

| Stage | Definition |
|---|---|
| **0** | No prolapse |
| **I** | Leading edge more than 1 cm above hymen |
| **II** | Leading edge from 1 cm above to 1 cm below hymen |
| **III** | Leading edge more than 1 cm beyond hymen but not complete eversion |
| **IV** | Complete or near-complete vaginal eversion |

### Adjunctive Testing

Most patients do not need extensive testing before conservative management. Testing becomes important when surgery is planned or when symptoms suggest obstruction, occult SUI, recurrent UTI, or complex posterior compartment disease.

| Test | Indication |
|---|---|
| **PVR** | Prolapse beyond hymen, obstructive voiding symptoms, recurrent UTI, or before surgery |
| **Urinalysis / culture** | Urgency, dysuria, recurrent UTI, hematuria, or LUTS |
| **Cough stress test with prolapse reduced** | Significant anterior or apical prolapse before surgery |
| **Urodynamics** | Unclear incontinence phenotype, voiding dysfunction, elevated PVR, prior failed surgery, or high-stakes decision about concomitant sling |
| **Dynamic pelvic MRI / defecography** | Complex multicompartment prolapse, recurrent repair, suspected enterocele/sigmoidocele, or obstructed defecation not explained by examination |
| **Cystoscopy** | Hematuria, suspected bladder pathology, prior mesh exposure/erosion, or planned complex anterior repair when bladder injury risk is high |

### Occult Stress Urinary Incontinence

All women with significant anterior or apical prolapse should be evaluated for occult SUI by cough stress testing or urodynamics **with the prolapse reduced**.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[11]](#ref11)</sup> Reduction can be performed with a pessary, speculum, swab, or ring forceps depending on the office workflow.

The counseling point is simple: repairing the prolapse can unmask SUI by relieving urethral kinking. A concomitant continence procedure reduces postoperative SUI but increases risks of voiding dysfunction, UTI, and mesh/sling-specific complications.

## Treatment

### Conservative Management

Observation is appropriate for asymptomatic anterior prolapse. Treatment is indicated when bulge, pressure, voiding dysfunction, sexual dysfunction, or bowel symptoms are bothersome enough that the patient wants intervention.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup>

Conservative measures:

- Weight reduction when obesity is contributing.
- Treat [chronic constipation](/docs/clinical-conditions/03c-pelvic-support/chronic-constipation) with fiber, osmotic laxatives, and defecatory-disorder therapy when indicated.
- Avoid repetitive heavy Valsalva loading when possible.
- Use [pelvic floor physical therapy](/docs/surgical-techniques/04f-incontinence-procedures/procedures/pelvic-floor-pt) to improve symptom control, pelvic floor coordination, and coexisting SUI/OAB patterns.
- Offer a pessary to all women considering treatment, especially those who prefer to avoid surgery, are poor surgical candidates, or desire future pregnancy.<sup>[[2]](#ref2)[[12]](#ref12)</sup>

Pessary fitting is successful in many women. Ring pessaries often work well for stage II and selected stage III anterior prolapse; advanced stage IV prolapse more often requires space-occupying devices such as a Gellhorn.<sup>[[12]](#ref12)</sup> Vaginal estrogen does not reliably reverse prolapse, but it can improve GSM-related irritation and pessary tolerance in hypoestrogenic tissue.<sup>[[13]](#ref13)</sup>

### Native Tissue Anterior Repair

**Anterior colporrhaphy** plicates the anterior vaginal fibromuscular layer in the midline after vaginal epithelial dissection. It is most suited to central anterior wall defects and is commonly combined with apical suspension when Level I support is deficient.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[3]](#ref3)</sup>

Outcomes depend heavily on the definition used:

| Outcome | Pattern |
|---|---|
| **Anatomic success** | Lower than mesh-augmented repair; reported 1-year success often 34.5-64% depending on threshold |
| **Subjective success** | Often higher than anatomic success; many women are not bothered by mild recurrent descent |
| **Morbidity** | Lower than transvaginal mesh; avoids mesh exposure and mesh pain syndromes |
| **Failure pattern** | Recurrent anterior wall descent, often from uncorrected apical or lateral support failure |

The key principle is that anterior repair should not be planned in isolation. If the apex descends, the apex gets repaired.

### Paravaginal Repair

Paravaginal repair attempts to reattach the lateral anterior vaginal wall to the ATFP. It may be performed vaginally, abdominally, laparoscopically, or robotically, but true lateral defect diagnosis is difficult on office examination and clinical defects are frequently combined rather than pure.<sup>[[9]](#ref9)[[14]](#ref14)</sup>

Paravaginal repair is most defensible when:

- Lateral sulcal detachment is convincing.
- The central defect is not the dominant problem.
- Apical support is also restored.
- The surgeon has specific experience with the approach.

### Apical Suspension

Apical support is central to anterior compartment durability. Apical location is one of the strongest predictors of cystocele size, and anterior recurrence is common when apical descent is ignored.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[4]](#ref4)</sup>

Apical options include:

| Procedure | Role |
|---|---|
| **Uterosacral ligament suspension** | Native-tissue vaginal apical repair; requires cystoscopy because ureteral kinking is a key risk |
| **Sacrospinous ligament fixation** | Native-tissue vaginal apical repair; may shift the vaginal axis posteriorly and can contribute to anterior recurrence |
| **Sacrocolpopexy** | Abdominal / laparoscopic / robotic mesh-based apical repair; lower recurrence than vaginal native tissue in selected patients |
| **Uterine-sparing hysteropexy** | Option when uterine preservation is desired and uterine pathology is absent |

The OPTIMAL trial found uterosacral ligament suspension and sacrospinous ligament fixation similarly effective for apical support at 2 years, so procedure selection depends on anatomy, risk profile, concomitant defects, and surgeon expertise.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup>

### Mesh and Grafts

Transvaginal permanent mesh improves anatomic outcomes for anterior prolapse but increases morbidity, including exposure, bladder injury, de novo SUI, pain, dyspareunia, and repeat surgery.<sup>[[15]](#ref15)[[16]](#ref16)</sup> In the United States, the FDA ordered manufacturers to stop selling and distributing transvaginal mesh products for POP in April 2019; that order did not apply to midurethral slings for SUI or to abdominal mesh used for sacrocolpopexy.<sup>[[17]](#ref17)</sup>

Biologic grafts have not shown enough durable benefit to justify routine use in anterior or posterior repair.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[16]](#ref16)</sup>

## Concomitant Stress Urinary Incontinence

Anterior prolapse and SUI interact in both directions: mild anterior descent may coexist with urethral hypermobility, while advanced prolapse may mask SUI by kinking the urethra.

| Preoperative finding | Counseling / option |
|---|---|
| **Overt SUI** | Discuss concomitant midurethral sling, autologous fascial sling, Burch at sacrocolpopexy, or staged treatment depending on anatomy and mesh preferences |
| **Occult SUI only** | Counsel that postoperative SUI risk is increased; offer concomitant continence surgery or staged approach |
| **No SUI with reduction** | De novo SUI can still occur, but prophylactic continence surgery is not routine for every patient |
| **Voiding dysfunction / elevated PVR** | Avoid reflex sling placement until obstruction, detrusor function, and risk of postoperative retention are understood |

See [Stress Urinary Incontinence (Female)](/docs/clinical-conditions/03a-storage-incontinence/sui-female), [Mixed Urinary Incontinence](/docs/clinical-conditions/03a-storage-incontinence/mixed-incontinence), and the [Female SUI treatment database](/docs/surgical-techniques/04f-incontinence-procedures/female-sui/female-stress-incontinence-database).

## Outcomes and Follow-Up

Follow-up should assess both anatomy and function:

- Bulge resolution and patient-defined bother.
- POP-Q points Aa, Ba, C/D, gh, pb, and tvl.
- New or persistent SUI, urgency, recurrent UTI, and voiding symptoms.
- PVR if preoperative retention, advanced prolapse, or postoperative voiding dysfunction existed.
- Dyspareunia, vaginal narrowing, pain, or mesh/graft exposure when applicable.
- Constipation, splinting, and obstructed defecation when posterior or apical disease coexists.

Urinary symptoms often improve after prolapse repair, especially voiding obstruction and elevated PVR, but urgency and mixed incontinence may persist and require separate treatment.<sup>[[18]](#ref18)[[19]](#ref19)</sup>

---

## Key Recommendations

- Use POP-Q language: **anterior wall prolapse with Ba measurement**, not just "cystocele."
- Identify whether the defect is central, lateral, apical, distal, or combined.
- Reduce the apex during examination; if Ba improves, the anterior defect is apical-driven.
- Check PVR when prolapse is beyond the hymen or voiding symptoms are present.
- Evaluate occult SUI with prolapse reduction before surgery.
- Offer pessary and pelvic floor therapy before surgery when appropriate.
- Do not perform isolated anterior repair when clinically significant apical prolapse is present.
- Avoid routine transvaginal mesh for primary anterior repair; abdominal sacrocolpopexy mesh and midurethral sling mesh are separate regulatory and risk categories.
