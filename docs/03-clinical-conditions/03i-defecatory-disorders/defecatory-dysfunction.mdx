# Defecatory Dysfunction

**Defecatory dysfunction** or **obstructed defecation** is the inability to satisfactorily expel stool because of inadequate rectal propulsive forces, increased outlet resistance, or both.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[2]](#ref2)</sup> It is the most common cause of medically refractory chronic constipation and is underrecognized because the symptoms overlap entirely with those of slow-transit and normal-transit constipation. The diagnostic decision point is physiologic, made on anorectal manometry with balloon expulsion, with defecography as a second-line test.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[3]](#ref3)</sup>

See the [subsection landing](/docs/clinical-conditions/03i-defecatory-disorders) for how defecatory dysfunction sits alongside chronic constipation and fecal incontinence.

---

## Epidemiology

For generic chronic-constipation prevalence, see [Chronic Constipation — Epidemiology](/docs/clinical-conditions/03i-defecatory-disorders/chronic-constipation#epidemiology). The DD-specific numbers are:

- Functional defecation disorders account for 33–50% of chronic-constipation referrals to pelvic-floor testing.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[4]](#ref4)</sup>
- In a meta-analysis of 79 studies (7,581 patients referred for physiologic testing), the median prevalence of any single abnormal finding associated with dyssynergic defecation was 37%; estimates depend on the test used (48% dyssynergic pattern on manometry; 15% absent anorectal-angle opening on defecography).<sup>[[20]](#ref20)</sup> In one tertiary motility-center series of 230 patients, 22% had dyssynergic defecation and 55% had slow transit, with 13% having both.<sup>[[21]](#ref21)</sup>
- Up to 50% of patients with defecatory disorders also have delayed colonic transit, which may be secondary to pelvic-floor dysfunction rather than a primary motility disease.<sup>[[2]](#ref2)</sup>

---

## Pathophysiology

Three mechanisms, usually in combination:<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[2]](#ref2)</sup>

1. **Inadequate rectal propulsion.** Insufficient intra-abdominal or rectal pressure during attempted defecation
2. **Increased outlet resistance.** Paradoxical contraction or failure of relaxation of the external anal sphincter and/or puborectalis during defecation; high anal resting pressure ("anismus")
3. **Structural or sensory contributors.** Rectocele, rectal intussusception, excessive perineal descent, and rectal hyposensitivity that reduces the urge to defecate

**Dyssynergia** may involve learned maladaptive coordination, but the broader syndrome includes structural, sensory and neurologic contributors; it should not be reduced to a behavioral diagnosis in every patient. Repeated straining can weaken pelvic-floor support and increase the risk of excessive perineal descent, rectal intussusception, solitary rectal ulcer, and pudendal neuropathy. Pudendal neuropathy may contribute to sphincter-related fecal incontinence; stool retention or impaction can separately cause overflow leakage.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[2]](#ref2)[[6]](#ref6)</sup>

## Classification

### Rome IV — Functional Defecation Disorders

The historical Rome IV framework requires qualifying functional constipation or IBS-C symptoms, plus impaired evacuation on two of three test categories: abnormal BET, abnormal evacuation pattern on manometry or surface EMG, and impaired evacuation on imaging. Symptoms must satisfy the relevant duration and frequency criteria; two isolated abnormal tests are insufficient.<sup>[[7]](#ref7)</sup>

| Subtype | Definition |
|---|---|
| **Dyssynergic defecation** | Paradoxical contraction or inadequate relaxation of the pelvic floor muscles during attempted defecation **with adequate** propulsive force |
| **Inadequate defecatory propulsion** | Insufficient propulsive force during attempted defecation, with or without sphincter / pelvic-floor dyssynergia |

### Rome V (2026) update

Rome V lists dyssynergic defecation separately and adds rectal hypo- and hypersensitivity disorders. Rome V requires one or more difficult-evacuation symptoms with at least 25% of bowel movements over the past 3 months (onset at least 6 months earlier) plus impaired evacuation on one of BET, manometry, or defecography; surface EMG is no longer a diagnostic criterion. This broadens recognition of a possible disorder, while concordant testing and the clinical picture remain important when results are borderline or surgery is considered. The older Rome IV two-test rule below is identified explicitly rather than presented as the only current framework.<sup>[[3]](#ref3)[[18]](#ref18)</sup>

### High-resolution manometry patterns of dyssynergia

Four descriptive patterns; an isolated laboratory pattern does not establish clinically relevant dyssynergia:<sup>[[8]](#ref8)</sup>

| Type | Rectal push | Anal response |
|---|---|---|
| **I** | Adequate | Paradoxical contraction |
| **II** | Inadequate | Paradoxical contraction |
| **III** | Adequate | Impaired / incomplete relaxation |
| **IV** | Inadequate | Impaired relaxation |

### London Protocol framework

The London classification organizes anorectal physiology with manometry with BET into: (1) rectoanal inhibitory reflex disorders (absent in Hirschsprung disease); (2) anal tone and contractility disorders; (3) rectoanal coordination disorders (the dyssynergia group); (4) rectal sensory disorders (hypersensitivity versus hyposensitivity). These test categories are distinct from Rome symptom-based diagnostic categories.<sup>[[16]](#ref16)</sup>

## Clinical Presentation

Symptoms are indistinguishable from other constipation types, hence the diagnostic reliance on physiology. Classic features:<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup>

- Excessive straining
- Sensation of anorectal blockage
- Manual maneuvers (digitation, perineal or vaginal splinting)
- Sensation of incomplete evacuation
- Enema or suppository dependence
- Hard stools and reduced frequency

### Digital rectal exam

The underused, high-yield bedside maneuver. In a tertiary referral cohort of 209 patients with chronic constipation (191 men, 18 women), DRE identified dyssynergia with 75% sensitivity, 87% specificity, and 97% PPV against physiologic criteria. The high PPV reflects this high-prevalence cohort and should not be assumed in other populations; a normal DRE does not exclude dyssynergia.<sup>[[22]](#ref22)</sup>

Assess at rest, with squeeze, and during simulated defecation:

- Anal tone at rest
- Voluntary squeeze (external sphincter and puborectalis integrity)
- **Push or bear down.** The sphincter should relax and the puborectalis should release the anorectal angle; paradoxical contraction or no relaxation suggests dyssynergia
- Palpable stool, rectocele (push anteriorly), excessive perineal descent, prolapse on strain

## Diagnostic Evaluation

The first-line combination is anorectal manometry with a balloon expulsion test; defecography is useful for discordant results or suspected structural disease.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[2]](#ref2)</sup>

| Test | What it measures | Use |
|---|---|---|
| **Balloon expulsion test (BET)** | Time to expel a rectal balloon, often filled with 50 mL water; use device/protocol-specific normal limits | Useful, reproducible screen; ASCRS 2024 cites about 50% sensitivity and high specificity, whereas a 15-study meta-analysis estimated 70% sensitivity and 77% specificity in chronic-constipation cohorts. Performance varies by population and protocol; a normal result does not exclude an evacuation disorder.<sup>[[3]](#ref3)[[16]](#ref16)[[29]](#ref29)</sup> |
| **High-resolution anorectal manometry (HRM)** | Resting / squeeze / push pressures, rectoanal inhibitory reflex, rectal sensation | Classifies dyssynergia pattern (I–IV); quantifies the rectoanal gradient during push<sup>[[3]](#ref3)</sup> |
| **Defecography (fluoroscopic or MR)** | Structural anatomy and functional evacuation — rectocele trapping, intussusception, enterocele, perineal descent | Second-line when ARM/BET discordant, or when structural disease suspected<sup>[[9]](#ref9)</sup> |

**Study-specific probabilities of impaired evacuation on defecography among the 158 of 474 patients selected for imaging**, not universal diagnostic cutoffs:<sup>[[10]](#ref10)</sup>

- **Both BET and rectoanal gradient abnormal:** approximately 75% probability of impaired evacuation
- **One abnormal:** approximately 45% probability
- **Both normal:** approximately 14% probability; persistent suspicion may still warrant imaging
- Rome IV requires qualifying symptoms and two abnormal test categories; see the Rome V update above.<sup>[[7]](#ref7)</sup>

Defecography can identify rectocele (anterior wall), enterocele (cul-de-sac hernia), and intussusception, and measure perineal descent. These findings inform decisions about biofeedback, rectocele repair, or rectopexy alongside symptoms, examination, and physiology.<sup>[[11]](#ref11)[[16]](#ref16)</sup>

## Management

### First: lifestyle and bowel-management baseline

Optimize bowel-management basics while arranging biofeedback for symptomatic dyssynergia; laxative trials need not precede referral.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[12]](#ref12)[[16]](#ref16)[[19]](#ref19)</sup>

- Hydration and stool-form optimization (Bristol target approximately 4)
- Routine post-meal toileting to exploit the gastrocolonic reflex; footstool for squat posture
- Stopping constipating medications where possible
- Fiber or an osmotic laxative for stool-form management when appropriate, with stimulant rescue as needed; these measures can accompany biofeedback.<sup>[[16]](#ref16)[[19]](#ref19)</sup>

The full pharmacologic algorithm (AGA/ACG 2023 stepwise management, secretagogues, 5-HT4 agonists) is covered in [Chronic Constipation](/docs/clinical-conditions/03i-defecatory-disorders/chronic-constipation). The DD-specific point is that up to 50% of defecatory-dysfunction patients have coexistent slow transit, but this does not change the order of operations. Treat the outlet first. Slow transit may improve after evacuation is restored. In patients with coexisting slow transit or IBS-C, laxatives or secretagogues may relieve bowel symptoms alongside biofeedback; these drugs have not been shown to correct dyssynergia.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[2]](#ref2)[[30]](#ref30)</sup>

### Second: anorectal biofeedback therapy (the cornerstone)

For dyssynergic defecation, anorectal biofeedback is first-line treatment, alongside stool-form optimization and management of coexisting disorders.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[5]](#ref5)[[13]](#ref13)</sup> The ASCRS 2024 guideline gives this a strong recommendation on moderate-quality evidence.<sup>[[16]](#ref16)</sup>

Goals:

- Coordinate abdominal push with pelvic-floor or sphincter relaxation (not simultaneous contraction)
- Improve diaphragmatic push mechanics
- Rectal sensory retraining for hyposensitivity
- Balloon-expulsion retraining

**Delivery:** EMG- or manometry-guided feedback can extend over weeks to months; bowel-trained therapists tailor the modality and visit count to response. The 2026 Pelvic Floor Consortium generally expected 4–6 sessions before symptom change, without endorsing a fixed course.<sup>[[19]](#ref19)</sup> Periodic reinforcement after initial training helps sustain the effect. ASCRS 2024 cites a systematic review of 7 randomized trials (413 patients) in which EMG biofeedback outperformed non-EMG biofeedback (OR 6.74), although the trials were heterogeneous and the 2026 consensus below finds insufficient evidence to favor one technique.<sup>[[16]](#ref16)</sup>

**Evidence:**

- **Chiarioni 2006 RCT** (109 patients with dyssynergia who had failed fiber plus enemas or suppositories): at 6 months, major improvement in 43 of 54 (80%) after five weekly biofeedback sessions versus 12 of 55 (22%) on polyethylene glycol plus counseling (P &lt; .001); biofeedback benefit persisted at 12 and 24 months, while the PEG comparator was assessed only through 12 months.<sup>[[23]](#ref23)</sup>
- **Rao 2007 RCT** (77 patients, 3 months): manometric biofeedback was superior to both sham feedback and standard therapy for correcting dyssynergia, defecation index and balloon expulsion time, with more complete spontaneous bowel movements and less digital maneuvering. Sham comparison shows the effect is not attention alone.<sup>[[24]](#ref24)</sup>
- Across RCTs, major improvement is reported in roughly 70–80% of biofeedback patients, and long-term follow-up studies find 55–82% maintain improvement.<sup>[[5]](#ref5)</sup>
- General strengthening alone is not equivalent to coordinated evacuation training. The 2026 Pelvic Floor Consortium consensus calls biofeedback a best practice for obstructed defecation, supported by multiple randomized trials, and finds insufficient evidence to favor one biofeedback technique over another, so the pelvic floor physical therapist chooses the modality (statement 7).<sup>[[19]](#ref19)</sup>

**Predictors of response:**<sup>[[14]](#ref14)</sup> absence of baseline manual maneuvers, reported awareness that "anal muscles are causing the straining," and demonstrable coordination deficit on augmented DRE.

### Third: botulinum toxin

For biofeedback non-responders with a nonrelaxing puborectalis:<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[15]](#ref15)</sup>

- Published onabotulinumtoxinA regimens vary; 100 U into the puborectalis ± external sphincter is one studied protocol, not an established universal dose
- Symptom response is highly variable across studies (29–100%) and is temporary, typically waning within 3 months. ASCRS 2024 gives a conditional recommendation on low-quality evidence for nonrelaxing puborectalis.<sup>[[15]](#ref15)[[16]](#ref16)</sup> In one observational cohort of 31 biofeedback non-responders, 100 U into the puborectalis and external sphincter followed by biofeedback allowed 23 (74.2%) to expel the balloon.<sup>[[16]](#ref16)</sup>
- In the 2020 review (11 studies, 248 patients, three RCTs), adverse effects ranged from 0% to 70%, most often flatus and fecal incontinence.<sup>[[15]](#ref15)</sup> A 2023 meta-analysis found more incontinence than with biofeedback; pooled symptom improvement was 66.4% in the short term and 38.2% beyond 12 months.<sup>[[27]](#ref27)</sup>
- Combination with post-injection biofeedback may extend durability
- The overall evidence base is low quality (heterogeneous doses, outcomes, and designs)

### Surgical considerations

Surgery addresses structural contributors; it does not fix dyssynergia.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[16]](#ref16)</sup>

| Pathology | Surgical option | Key caveat |
|---|---|---|
| **Rectocele** — symptomatic outlet dysfunction with a clinically significant rectocele on vaginal examination; imaging can clarify stool trapping or coexisting abnormalities | **[Transvaginal posterior repair](/docs/surgical-techniques/04g-prolapse-repair/posterior-colporrhaphy)** or another individualized approach; no universal &gt;5-cm threshold or guaranteed functional result | Address coexisting dyssynergia before surgery; anatomic correction alone may not resolve bowel symptoms. ASCRS 2024 conditionally recommends repair on moderate-quality evidence and reports variable success of 50% to 100% with no clearly superior route. A 2026 IUGA opinion supports native-tissue transvaginal repair as a first-line surgical option for selected women with stage 2 or greater clinically evident rectocele and obstructed defecation (strong recommendation, moderate-quality evidence).<sup>[[16]](#ref16)[[28]](#ref28)</sup> |
| **Rectal intussusception** | Ventral mesh rectopexy, Delorme | ASCRS 2024 allows repair only for severe obstructed defecation after failed nonoperative treatment (conditional, low-quality evidence).<sup>[[16]](#ref16)</sup> Anatomy may be corrected without symptom improvement. The 2026 Pelvic Floor Consortium consensus recommends offering pelvic floor PT before surgery for high-grade intussusception with obstructed defecation and discourages surgery for low-grade intussusception unless all other therapies have failed.<sup>[[19]](#ref19)</sup> In a single-operator randomized trial of 112 elderly patients with obstructed defecation from structural abnormalities (nonrelaxing puborectalis excluded), laparoscopic ventral rectopexy and STARR each gave &gt;50% improvement in obstructed-defecation score at 6 months (73% vs 82%, NS), but symptom recurrence at a mean 41 months was 7% vs 24% (P = 0.04)<sup>[[25]](#ref25)</sup> |
| **Full-thickness rectal prolapse** | Abdominal rectopexy ± resection | Referral to colorectal surgery. ASCRS 2017 states that prolapse cannot be corrected nonoperatively. It advises transabdominal rectal fixation in patients with acceptable risk (weak recommendation, moderate-quality evidence, 2B), and allows sigmoid resection with suture rectopexy when constipation is present (strong, moderate, 1B). Posterior mobilization without rectopexy is not recommended (strong, moderate, 1B)<sup>[[26]](#ref26)</sup> |
| **STARR** (stapled transanal rectal resection) | **Not recommended for rectocele or internal intussusception** (ASCRS 2024: strong recommendation, moderate-quality evidence)<sup>[[16]](#ref16)</sup> | Complication rates of 10–78% across series: bleeding, infection, pain, fecal urgency, incontinence, rectovaginal fistula, anastomotic leak, urinary retention<sup>[[16]](#ref16)</sup> |
| **Sacral neuromodulation** for constipation | Not established treatment for dyssynergic evacuation | Evidence in selected slow-transit constipation concerns a different phenotype and must not be extrapolated to outlet dysfunction |

## Refractory defecatory dysfunction

The AGA 2026 update advises assessment before labeling chronic constipation refractory. It does not require every refractory patient to have dyssynergia:<sup>[[17]](#ref17)</sup>

1. Most patients should undergo ARM with BET
2. Complete pelvic-floor biofeedback when indicated
3. Review secondary causes and adequate medical trials; document transit and consider defecography as appropriate

Persistent symptoms then prompt mechanism-specific reassessment. Colectomy is considered only for confirmed refractory slow transit without an ongoing defecatory disorder.

## Pelvic Floor Physical Therapy Consensus (2026)

A consensus of the ASCRS, ICS, IUGA, AUGS and APTA Pelvic Floor Consortium produced 15 statements, each with more than 70% agreement. The recommendations mainly reflect expert consensus because high-quality comparative evidence is limited, and they assume access to a therapist with bowel-specific training.<sup>[[19]](#ref19)</sup>

- **Referral.** Prompt referral to a properly trained pelvic floor physical therapist is recommended for obstructed defecation after other pathology and barriers to active participation are excluded (statement 1). A formal diagnosis by rectal testing or imaging is not required before the initial evaluation (statement 3). Test findings can serve as treatment targets and can prioritize referrals when access is limited (statement 4).
- **Course of therapy.** Goals are set with the patient, and no consensus was reached on a set number of visits. The experts agreed that 4 to 6 sessions are generally needed to see a change in symptoms, and that reevaluation or further testing is unlikely to alter management before at least 8 sessions (statement 5).
- **Trauma-informed care.** Every pelvic floor examination should be trauma-informed. When known or suspected trauma could make testing distressing, referral before anorectal manometry or defecography is considered appropriate (statement 6).
- **Rectocele.** Women with posterior vaginal wall prolapse, obstructed defecation and increased pelvic floor tone or dyssynergia should have pelvic floor PT before rectocele surgery (statement 11). When tone is decreased, PT can be offered before or after surgery (statement 12).
- **External rectal prolapse.** Timely surgery is the recommended first-line treatment; PT is considered after surgery for persistent symptoms and before surgery only if it does not delay it (statement 13).
- **Internal prolapse.** For high-grade intussusception with obstructed defecation, PT is offered before surgery and again afterward if symptoms persist (statement 14). Low-grade intussusception and solitary rectal ulcer syndrome are treated with PT from a bowel-trained therapist, and surgery is strongly discouraged unless all other therapies have failed (statement 15).


## Pearls for the Reconstructive Pelvic Surgeon

- A careful DRE with simulated defecation is the single most informative office maneuver. Do it before offering any posterior-compartment surgery.
- When rectocele and dyssynergia coexist, address coordination before deciding whether a residual structural problem warrants repair.
- "Pelvic floor therapy" is not a monolith. Anorectal biofeedback specifically trains evacuation coordination; a PFPT referral should state the diagnosis and treatment goals.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup>
- Slow transit often improves after the outlet is restored. Resist the urge to escalate prokinetics before the outlet has been properly addressed.
- Overflow fecal incontinence can result from stool retention or impaction; evaluate constipation and possible outlet dysfunction while addressing the leakage. See [Fecal Incontinence](/docs/clinical-conditions/03i-defecatory-disorders/fecal-incontinence) for the phenotype split and management.<sup>[[2]](#ref2)</sup>
