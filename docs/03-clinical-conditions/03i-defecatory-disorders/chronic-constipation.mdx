# Chronic Constipation

Chronic constipation affects approximately 15% of adults worldwide and is among the most common reasons for outpatient gastrointestinal and pelvic floor referral, with substantial effects on quality of life, healthcare utilization, and direct and indirect cost.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[2]](#ref2)</sup> Symptoms extend beyond infrequent defecation to include straining, sensation of incomplete evacuation, anorectal blockage, and the need for manual maneuvers. For the reconstructive pelvic surgeon, chronic constipation sits at the crossroads of gastroenterology and pelvic floor medicine; it coexists with pelvic organ prolapse, obstructed defecation, and fecal incontinence, and its recognition and treatment are frequently prerequisite to successful pelvic reconstruction.

---

## Epidemiology

- **Prevalence**: about 14% of community adults have chronic idiopathic constipation; prevalence is higher in women (female-versus-male odds ratio 2.22), older adults, and lower-socioeconomic populations.<sup>[[2]](#ref2)</sup>
- **Age**: Prevalence increases with age and lower socioeconomic status.<sup>[[2]](#ref2)</sup>
- **Healthcare utilization**: One of the most frequent GI diagnoses in ambulatory practice and a common reason for referral to gastroenterologists and colorectal surgeons.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup>
- **Overlap**: Chronic constipation and IBS-C overlap substantially; the odds of chronic idiopathic constipation were about eightfold higher in people who also reported IBS (OR 7.98).<sup>[[2]](#ref2)</sup> A large share of patients who fail over-the-counter therapy have a defecatory disorder on physiologic testing.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[6]](#ref6)</sup>

---

## Classification

Chronic constipation is first partitioned into primary (functional) and secondary forms. Primary constipation is further subdivided by pathophysiology.

| Category | Subtype | Defining features |
|---|---|---|
| **Primary (functional)** | Normal-transit constipation (NTC) | Normal colonic transit; symptom-based; most common subtype |
| | Slow-transit constipation (STC) | Delayed colonic transit on scintigraphy or radiopaque markers; high-amplitude propagating contractions may be reduced on manometry.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup> |
| | [**Defecatory (evacuation) disorders**](/docs/clinical-conditions/03i-defecatory-disorders/defecatory-dysfunction) | Impaired rectal evacuation from dyssynergia, inadequate propulsion, or structural outlet obstruction. **See the dedicated article** for physiologic diagnosis (ARM and BET, with defecography when indicated), biofeedback as first-line for symptomatic pelvic floor dyssynergia, botulinum toxin, and structural surgery.<sup>[[5]](#ref5)</sup> |
| **Secondary** | Medications | Opioids ([see opioid-induced constipation below](#opioid-induced-constipation)), anticholinergics, calcium-channel blockers, iron, calcium, 5-HT3 antagonists, antipsychotics |
| | Metabolic / endocrine | Hypothyroidism, hypercalcemia, hypokalemia, diabetes, pregnancy |
| | Neurologic | Parkinson disease, multiple sclerosis, spinal cord injury, autonomic neuropathy |
| | Structural / anatomic | Colorectal cancer, strictures, anal stenosis, large rectocele, full-thickness intussusception |

Defecatory disorders frequently coexist with slow-transit constipation. Treat the outlet first; secretagogues and prokinetics have reduced efficacy when outlet obstruction is unaddressed, and slow transit often improves once evacuation is restored.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[6]](#ref6)</sup>

## Diagnosis

### Symptom criteria and the Rome V update

The Rome IV functional-constipation criteria require symptoms for the last 3 months with onset at least 6 months prior, and the presence of ≥2 of the following:<sup>[[3]](#ref3)</sup>

1. Straining during &gt;25% of defecations<sup>[[3]](#ref3)</sup>
2. Lumpy or hard stools (Bristol 1–2) in &gt;25% of defecations<sup>[[3]](#ref3)</sup>
3. Sensation of incomplete evacuation in &gt;25% of defecations<sup>[[3]](#ref3)</sup>
4. Sensation of anorectal obstruction or blockage in &gt;25% of defecations<sup>[[3]](#ref3)</sup>
5. Manual maneuvers to facilitate &gt;25% of defecations (digital evacuation, splinting of the pelvic floor or posterior vaginal wall)<sup>[[3]](#ref3)</sup>
6. Fewer than three spontaneous bowel movements per week

Plus:

- Loose stools are rarely present without the use of laxatives
- Insufficient criteria for IBS

**Rome V (2026)** now uses the term chronic constipation and updates the broader bowel and anorectal classification. Symptom questionnaires support characterization but cannot diagnose dyssynergia or rectal sensory disorders without physiologic assessment; see the [defecatory-dysfunction update](/docs/clinical-conditions/03i-defecatory-disorders/defecatory-dysfunction#rome-v-2026-update).<sup>[[11]](#ref11)[[12]](#ref12)</sup>

### Initial Evaluation

A focused history should characterize stool frequency, stool form (Bristol Stool Form Scale), straining, sense of incomplete evacuation, manual maneuvers, duration, and triggers; and should review alarm features: hematochezia, unintentional weight loss, iron-deficiency anemia, new or progressive constipation in an older adult, rapid progression, or first-degree family history of colorectal cancer or inflammatory bowel disease.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[5]](#ref5)</sup>

A medication review is mandatory; medications are a common, reversible contributor.

**Physical examination** includes abdominal exam and a digital rectal examination with dynamic maneuvers (squeeze, simulated defecation). Paradoxical contraction of the puborectalis or failure of perineal descent on bearing down strongly suggests a defecatory disorder.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[6]](#ref6)</sup>

| Test | Indication |
|---|---|
| **Basic labs** (CBC, TSH, calcium, glucose, creatinine) | Selected patients with short history, alarm features, or suspected metabolic cause |
| **Colonoscopy** | Alarm features, or due for age-appropriate colorectal cancer screening |
| **Anorectal manometry + balloon expulsion test** | Refractory constipation, suspicion for defecatory disorder, or pre-surgical assessment. **Workflow and interpretation** → see [Defecatory Dysfunction](/docs/clinical-conditions/03i-defecatory-disorders/defecatory-dysfunction) |
| **Colonic transit** (radiopaque markers or scintigraphy) | Refractory constipation **after** excluding or treating an outlet disorder; guides subtype and surgical candidacy |
| **Defecography** (fluoroscopic or MR) | Suspected structural outlet obstruction — rectocele, intussusception, enterocele, perineal descent |

**Evaluation sequence.** ASCRS 2024 recommends evaluating for outlet dysfunction in patients who fail diet, fiber and osmotic laxatives (conditional recommendation, low-quality evidence), using anorectal physiology testing or dynamic imaging (fluoroscopic or MR defecography, dynamic ultrasound). It recommends measuring colonic motility and transit before any surgical intervention (strong recommendation, low-quality evidence). In refractory patients, anorectal manometry with balloon expulsion testing comes first, and transit testing is interpreted after outlet obstruction has been excluded or treated.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[5]](#ref5)</sup> The balloon expulsion test is highly specific but only about 50% sensitive, and radiopaque markers remain the most widely used transit test.<sup>[[5]](#ref5)</sup>

## Management

Management proceeds in a stepwise fashion: lifestyle and fiber, then osmotic laxatives, then stimulants as rescue or an individualized adjunct, then prescription secretagogues or prokinetics for OTC failures, with biofeedback promptly when a defecatory disorder is identified, then surgery in carefully selected refractory cases.<sup>[[4]](#ref4)[[5]](#ref5)</sup>

### Lifestyle and Dietary Measures

- **Diet, fluid and fiber.** ASCRS 2024 makes dietary change, adequate fluid and fiber supplementation the initial step (strong recommendation, low-quality evidence). Fiber helps least in slow transit and outlet obstruction: about 80% of patients with slow transit and 63% with outlet obstruction may not respond adequately, versus about 85% improvement in patients without an underlying pathologic finding (as reported by ASCRS).<sup>[[5]](#ref5)</sup>
- **Toileting routine.** Post-prandial attempts leveraging the gastrocolic response; knees-above-hips positioning (e.g., footstool); avoidance of prolonged straining.
- **Hydration.** Adequate fluid intake, particularly with fiber.
- **Physical activity.** Modest evidence for benefit; reasonable first-line adjunct.

### Pharmacologic Therapy — AGA/ACG 2023 Recommendations

The 2023 AGA–ACG guideline on pharmacologic management of chronic idiopathic constipation provides graded recommendations for adult chronic idiopathic constipation.<sup>[[4]](#ref4)</sup>

| Class | Agent | Recommendation | Certainty | Key Implementation Notes |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Fiber** | Psyllium | Conditional (suggest) | Low | First-line for patients with low dietary fiber intake; encourage hydration; flatulence is common; evidence for bran and inulin is very limited<sup>[[4]](#ref4)</sup> |
| **Osmotic** | PEG | **Strong (recommend)** | Moderate | Durable response at 6 months; well tolerated; combine with fiber as needed |
| | Magnesium oxide | Conditional (suggest) | Very low | Start low, titrate; avoid in renal insufficiency (hypermagnesemia risk) |
| | Lactulose | Conditional (suggest) | Very low | Reserve for OTC failures; bloating and flatulence are dose-limiting |
| **Stimulant** | Bisacodyl / sodium picosulfate | **Strong (recommend)** | Moderate | Short-term (≤4 wk) or rescue therapy; start low to limit cramping and diarrhea. Sodium picosulfate is not marketed in the United States |
| | Senna | Conditional (suggest) | Low | Trials used higher doses than are common in practice; start low and titrate |
| **Secretagogue** | Lubiprostone | Conditional (suggest) | Low | For OTC failures; label dose for CIC is 24 mcg twice daily with food and water (may lower nausea risk); contraindicated in known or suspected mechanical GI obstruction<sup>[[4]](#ref4)</sup> |
| | Linaclotide | **Strong (recommend)** | Moderate | For OTC failures; label CIC dose 145 mcg daily (72 mcg if tolerability is limiting), on an empty stomach; diarrhea is the main adverse effect; contraindicated under age 2 years and in mechanical obstruction<sup>[[4]](#ref4)[[31]](#ref31)</sup> |
| | Plecanatide | **Strong (recommend)** | Moderate | For OTC failures; label CIC dose 3 mg daily; diarrhea is a principal adverse effect; contraindicated under age 6 years and in mechanical obstruction; avoid use from age 6 to under 18 years; no head-to-head equivalence with linaclotide implied<sup>[[4]](#ref4)[[30]](#ref30)</sup> |
| **5-HT4 agonist** | Prucalopride | **Strong (recommend)** | Moderate | For OTC failures; label dose 2 mg daily (1 mg daily if creatinine clearance is below 30 mL/min). Contraindicated in intestinal perforation or obstruction, obstructive ileus, severe intestinal inflammatory conditions such as Crohn disease or ulcerative colitis, and toxic megacolon/megarectum. Monitor for suicidal thoughts or behavior and new or worsening depression; stop immediately for these or unusual mood changes, although causation is unestablished. Headache, abdominal pain, nausea and diarrhea are the most common AEs.<sup>[[20]](#ref20)</sup> |

ASCRS 2024 rates osmotic laxatives as appropriate first-line medical therapy (strong recommendation, moderate-quality evidence), with bisacodyl or other stimulants for rescue or second-line use.<sup>[[5]](#ref5)</sup> It cites limited randomized data for anthraquinone stimulants (senna, cascara, aloe) in chronic constipation.

**Practical sequencing.** A reasonable first-line regimen for most adults is fiber (psyllium) plus PEG, with bisacodyl or sodium picosulfate reserved for rescue. Patients who fail or cannot tolerate OTC therapy should advance to a secretagogue (linaclotide, plecanatide, or lubiprostone) or the 5-HT4 agonist prucalopride; choice is driven largely by payer formulary, comorbidity (e.g., avoid magnesium oxide in CKD), and side-effect profile.<sup>[[4]](#ref4)</sup>

**Tenapanor** (an NHE3 inhibitor, 50 mg twice daily) is US-labeled for IBS-C, not chronic idiopathic constipation, and is not part of the 2023 CIC guideline.

<a id="opioid-induced-constipation"></a>

### Opioid-Induced Constipation

Opioid-induced constipation (OIC) is the commonest medication-related secondary cause and is managed on a separate track. Stimulant and osmotic laxatives are first-line; peripherally acting mu-opioid receptor antagonists (PAMORAs) are reserved for an inadequate laxative response.<sup>[[14]](#ref14)</sup> Lubiprostone (24 mcg twice daily) is also labeled for OIC in chronic non-cancer pain.

| PAMORA | Label notes |
|---|---|
| **Naloxegol** (Movantik) | Contraindicated in patients with known or suspected GI obstruction or at risk of recurrent obstruction, and with strong CYP3A4 inhibitors<sup>[[24]](#ref24)</sup> |
| **Naldemedine** (Symproic) | Contraindicated in known or suspected GI obstruction or risk of recurrent obstruction<sup>[[25]](#ref25)</sup> |
| **Methylnaltrexone** (Relistor; tablet and injection) | Contraindicated in known or suspected mechanical GI obstruction or increased risk of recurrent obstruction<sup>[[26]](#ref26)</sup> |

Perforation is the reason for the obstruction contraindication. Agent-specific PAMORA labels warn about impaired GI-wall integrity, including GI malignancy, GI surgery, diverticular disease, or Crohn disease; review the chosen label and discontinue treatment for severe, persistent, or worsening abdominal pain.<sup>[[24]](#ref24)[[25]](#ref25)[[26]](#ref26)</sup>

### Defecatory Disorders — handled separately

Anorectal biofeedback may be used for defecatory disorders with dyssynergia or inadequate propulsion identified on ARM and BET; ASCRS 2024 specifically considers it first-line for symptomatic pelvic floor dyssynergia (strong recommendation, moderate-quality evidence).<sup>[[5]](#ref5)[[10]](#ref10)</sup> In a randomized trial of 109 patients with normal-transit constipation due to dyssynergia who had not responded to fiber plus enemas or suppositories, five weekly biofeedback sessions gave major improvement in 80% at 6 months versus 22% with polyethylene glycol plus counseling, with benefit sustained to 24 months in the biofeedback arm (the PEG group was assessed through 12 months).<sup>[[15]](#ref15)</sup> A sham-controlled trial (n = 77) showed greater correction of dyssynergia and more complete spontaneous bowel movements with biofeedback than with sham or standard therapy.<sup>[[16]](#ref16)</sup> Its one-year extension enrolled 26/44 eligible patients; 13/13 biofeedback and 7/13 standard-care patients completed follow-up, with sustained improvement among biofeedback completers.<sup>[[17]](#ref17)</sup> The full diagnostic workflow and treatment algorithm (biofeedback, botulinum toxin, selected structural surgery) lives on the [Defecatory Dysfunction](/docs/clinical-conditions/03i-defecatory-disorders/defecatory-dysfunction) page.<sup>[[6]](#ref6)[[10]](#ref10)</sup>

The relevant point for constipation management: treat confirmed dyssynergia while continuing appropriate stool-form treatment. Slow-transit constipation commonly improves once evacuation is restored.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[6]](#ref6)</sup>

### Surgical Considerations

Surgery for chronic constipation is reserved for carefully selected refractory patients after exhaustive medical and pelvic floor therapy, and mandates objective physiologic confirmation of the constipation subtype.<sup>[[5]](#ref5)</sup> ASCRS 2024 grades the operations as follows, all from low-quality evidence except rectocele repair and STARR:<sup>[[5]](#ref5)</sup>

| ASCRS 2024 statement | Strength | Evidence |
|---|---|---|
| Total abdominal colectomy with ileorectal anastomosis may benefit isolated refractory slow-transit constipation | Conditional | Low |
| Rectocele repair may be considered for significant outlet dysfunction after concomitant functional causes such as a nonrelaxing puborectalis are addressed | Conditional | Moderate |
| STARR is not recommended for rectocele or internal intussusception because of high complication rates | Strong | Moderate |
| Repair of rectal intussusception may be considered for severe obstructed defecation after nonoperative treatment fails | Conditional | Low |
| Fecal diversion may be considered for intractable constipation refractory to other treatment | Conditional | Low |

| Indication | Operation | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| Medically refractory slow-transit constipation; no ongoing defecatory disorder (normal ARM/BET or corrected dyssynergia); assess severe symptomatic upper-gut delay | **Total abdominal colectomy with ileorectal anastomosis (TAC-IRA)** | High symptom satisfaction but meaningful rates of persistent abdominal pain, bloating, small-bowel obstruction, and diarrhea; clinically significant upper-gut dysmotility is a relative contraindication.<sup>[[13]](#ref13)</sup> |
| Symptomatic, reducible **rectocele** with trapping on defecography and splinting on history | Transvaginal / transanal / transperineal **[rectocele repair](/docs/surgical-techniques/04g-prolapse-repair/posterior-colporrhaphy)** | Best outcomes when obstructed defecation is demonstrably mechanical and dyssynergia has been corrected |
| Symptomatic, high-grade **rectal intussusception or external rectal prolapse** | **Ventral mesh rectopexy** (or abdominal/perineal alternatives) | Consider combined repair only when the apical prolapse independently warrants treatment |
| Enterocele / sigmoidocele contributing to obstructed defecation | **Enterocele repair**, often as part of apical suspension | Address concurrent apical defects |

**Sacral neuromodulation** is sometimes offered before colectomy. Evidence is mixed. In an open-label randomized trial of refractory idiopathic slow-transit constipation without outlet obstruction, 22/41 assigned to sacral neuromodulation versus 1/26 to personalized conservative care averaged at least three defecations per week at six months.<sup>[[21]](#ref21)</sup> A 2025 meta-analysis of five RCTs (187 patients) found no statistically significant pooled advantage over sham or conservative controls (OR 1.92, 95% CI 0.68–5.42).<sup>[[22]](#ref22)</sup> In the earlier tined-lead test study, a reduction of at least 0.5 in constipation symptom score at six months occurred in 60% of discriminate versus 57% of indiscriminate test responders (P = 0.76); investigators suggested a placebo response. Infection occurred in 10/45 patients during the six-week test phase, and the study ended early.<sup>[[18]](#ref18)</sup>

**Do not operate without objective workup.** Colectomy should be considered only for documented slow transit with no ongoing defecatory disorder (normal ARM/BET or corrected dyssynergia). Evaluate severe, symptomatic gastric or small-bowel dysmotility; clinically significant upper-gut dysmotility is a relative contraindication.<sup>[[5]](#ref5)[[13]](#ref13)</sup> Outcomes of colectomy performed in unselected patients are poor.<sup>[[5]](#ref5)</sup>

### Refractory constipation: AGA 2026

Before labeling constipation refractory, reassess secondary causes and complete anorectal testing and biofeedback when indicated. For patients who remain refractory, the AGA 2026 expert update considers a vibrating capsule a reasonable adjunct.<sup>[[13]](#ref13)</sup> In a separate eight-week sham-controlled randomized trial of chronic constipation (n = 312), at least one additional complete spontaneous bowel movement per week for at least six of eight weeks occurred in 39.3% with the capsule versus 22.1% with sham.<sup>[[28]](#ref28)</sup> Confirm transit off therapy and, ideally, on maximal therapy. Colectomy requires documented slow transit without an ongoing defecatory disorder, regional gut-transit assessment and psychological evaluation. Pain- or bloating-dominant illness and untreated severe psychiatric disease are relative contraindications.<sup>[[13]](#ref13)</sup>

## Outcomes and Follow-Up

- **PEG.** In a six-month randomized trial (n = 304), PEG 17 g daily relieved modified Rome criteria for at least half of treatment weeks in 52% of patients versus 11% on placebo.<sup>[[4]](#ref4)[[27]](#ref27)</sup>
- **Linaclotide** 9-of-12-week CSBM response (≥3 per week and ≥1 above baseline) was 16–21% (145 µg) and 19–21% (290 µg) versus 3–6% with placebo across two 12-week trials.<sup>[[8]](#ref8)</sup> Plecanatide durable response was 20.1% (3 mg) versus 12.8% with placebo.<sup>[[9]](#ref9)</sup>
- **Prucalopride** 2 mg produced three or more spontaneous complete bowel movements per week in 30.9% versus 12.0% with placebo over 12 weeks; headache and abdominal pain were the commonest adverse events.<sup>[[7]](#ref7)</sup>
- **Biofeedback for dyssynergia** yielded adequate relief in 70% at 3 months in one controlled trial and major improvement in 80% at 6 months in another; the latter reported 81.5% major improvement at 24 months, while a separate one-year extension followed 13 biofeedback completers.<sup>[[10]](#ref10)[[15]](#ref15)[[17]](#ref17)</sup>
- **Colectomy for STC.** ASCRS reports clinical improvement in 50–100% after total colectomy with ileorectal anastomosis, whereas segmental colectomy has failed in up to 100%.<sup>[[5]](#ref5)</sup> Satisfaction is high in carefully selected series, but long-term morbidity is substantial. Among 42 of 102 original subtotal-colectomy patients available at a mean 15.9-year follow-up, half reported fewer than four bowel movements daily, 21% reported severe fecal incontinence, and eight had converted to ileostomy.<sup>[[23]](#ref23)</sup> In a separate survey, 75 of 112 subtotal-colectomy patients returned questionnaires; among respondents, 46% reported diarrhea at least some of the time, yet 93% would have the operation again.<sup>[[5]](#ref5)[[29]](#ref29)</sup> In a postal survey at a mean 15.3 years after total colectomy with ileorectal anastomosis, 32/71 contacted patients (45%) responded.<sup>[[19]](#ref19)</sup> Most respondents were satisfied, yet 72% reported abdominal pain, 53% fecal incontinence and 44% a later admission for bowel obstruction.<sup>[[5]](#ref5)[[19]](#ref19)</sup>

Follow-up intervals are typically 4–8 weeks during initial titration and 3–6 months once a stable regimen is established. Patients on long-term laxatives should be reassessed periodically for efficacy and for the emergence of alarm features.
