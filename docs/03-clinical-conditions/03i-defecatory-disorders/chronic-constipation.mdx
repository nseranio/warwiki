# Chronic Constipation

Chronic constipation affects approximately 15% of adults worldwide and is among the most common reasons for outpatient gastrointestinal and pelvic floor referral, with substantial effects on quality of life, healthcare utilization, and direct and indirect cost.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[2]](#ref2)</sup> Symptoms extend beyond infrequent defecation to include straining, sensation of incomplete evacuation, anorectal blockage, and the need for manual maneuvers. For the reconstructive pelvic surgeon, chronic constipation sits at the crossroads of gastroenterology and pelvic floor medicine — it coexists with pelvic organ prolapse, obstructed defecation, and fecal incontinence, and its recognition and treatment are frequently prerequisite to successful pelvic reconstruction.

---

## Epidemiology

- **Prevalence**: ~14–15% of adults globally; higher in women (~2:1 female-to-male), older adults, and lower-socioeconomic populations.<sup>[[2]](#ref2)</sup>
- **Age**: Prevalence increases with age and lower socioeconomic status.<sup>[[2]](#ref2)</sup>
- **Healthcare utilization**: One of the most frequent GI diagnoses in ambulatory practice and a common reason for referral to gastroenterologists and colorectal surgeons.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup>
- **Overlap**: Chronic constipation and IBS-C overlap substantially; constipation is about eight times more likely in people who also report IBS (OR 7.98).<sup>[[2]](#ref2)</sup> A large share of patients who fail over-the-counter therapy have a defecatory disorder on physiologic testing.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[6]](#ref6)</sup>

---

## Classification

Chronic constipation is first partitioned into **primary (functional)** and **secondary** forms. Primary constipation is further subdivided by pathophysiology.

| Category | Subtype | Defining features |
|---|---|---|
| **Primary (functional)** | Normal-transit constipation (NTC) | Normal colonic transit; symptom-based; most common subtype |
| | Slow-transit constipation (STC) | Delayed colonic transit on scintigraphy or radiopaque markers; reduced high-amplitude propagating contractions |
| | [**Defecatory (evacuation) disorders**](/docs/clinical-conditions/03i-defecatory-disorders/defecatory-dysfunction) | Impaired rectal evacuation from dyssynergia, inadequate propulsion, or structural outlet obstruction. **See the dedicated article** for physiologic diagnosis (ARM + BET + defecography), biofeedback as first-line, botulinum toxin, and structural surgery. |
| **Secondary** | Medications | Opioids ([see opioid-induced constipation below](#opioid-induced-constipation)), anticholinergics, calcium-channel blockers, iron, calcium, 5-HT3 antagonists, antipsychotics |
| | Metabolic / endocrine | Hypothyroidism, hypercalcemia, hypokalemia, diabetes, pregnancy |
| | Neurologic | Parkinson disease, multiple sclerosis, spinal cord injury, autonomic neuropathy |
| | Structural / anatomic | Colorectal cancer, strictures, anal stenosis, large rectocele, full-thickness intussusception |

Defecatory disorders frequently coexist with slow-transit constipation. **Treat the outlet first** — secretagogues and prokinetics have reduced efficacy when outlet obstruction is unaddressed, and slow transit often improves once evacuation is restored.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[6]](#ref6)</sup>

## Diagnosis

### Symptom criteria and the Rome V update

The **Rome IV functional-constipation criteria** require symptoms for the last **3 months** with onset at least **6 months** prior, and the presence of **≥2** of the following:<sup>[[3]](#ref3)</sup>

1. Straining during &gt;25% of defecations
2. Lumpy or hard stools (Bristol 1–2) in &gt;25% of defecations
3. Sensation of incomplete evacuation in &gt;25% of defecations
4. Sensation of anorectal obstruction or blockage in &gt;25% of defecations
5. Manual maneuvers to facilitate &gt;25% of defecations (digital evacuation, splinting of the pelvic floor or posterior vaginal wall)
6. Fewer than three spontaneous bowel movements per week

Plus:

- Loose stools are rarely present without the use of laxatives
- Insufficient criteria for IBS

**Rome V (2026)** now uses the term **chronic constipation** and updates the broader bowel/anorectal classification. Symptom questionnaires support characterization but cannot diagnose dyssynergia or rectal sensory disorders without physiologic assessment; see the [defecatory-dysfunction update](/docs/clinical-conditions/03i-defecatory-disorders/defecatory-dysfunction#rome-v-2026-update).<sup>[[11]](#ref11)[[12]](#ref12)</sup>

### Initial Evaluation

A focused **history** should characterize stool frequency, stool form (Bristol Stool Form Scale), straining, sense of incomplete evacuation, manual maneuvers, duration, and triggers; and should review **alarm features**: hematochezia, unintentional weight loss, iron-deficiency anemia, new or progressive constipation in an older adult, rapid progression, or first-degree family history of colorectal cancer or inflammatory bowel disease.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[5]](#ref5)</sup>

A **medication review** is mandatory — medications are a common, reversible contributor.

**Physical examination** includes abdominal exam and a **digital rectal examination** with dynamic maneuvers (squeeze, simulated defecation). Paradoxical contraction of the puborectalis or failure of perineal descent on bearing down strongly suggests a defecatory disorder.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[6]](#ref6)</sup>

| Test | Indication |
|---|---|
| **Basic labs** (CBC, TSH, calcium, glucose, creatinine) | Selected patients with short history, alarm features, or suspected metabolic cause |
| **Colonoscopy** | Alarm features, or due for age-appropriate colorectal cancer screening |
| **Anorectal manometry + balloon expulsion test** | Refractory constipation, suspicion for defecatory disorder, or pre-surgical assessment. **Workflow and interpretation** → see [Defecatory Dysfunction](/docs/clinical-conditions/03i-defecatory-disorders/defecatory-dysfunction) |
| **Colonic transit** (radiopaque markers or scintigraphy) | Refractory constipation **after** excluding or treating an outlet disorder; guides subtype and surgical candidacy |
| **Defecography** (fluoroscopic or MR) | Suspected structural outlet obstruction — rectocele, intussusception, enterocele, perineal descent |

**Evaluation sequence.** In refractory patients, **anorectal manometry with balloon expulsion testing comes first**; colonic transit testing is performed only after outlet obstruction has been excluded or treated.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[5]](#ref5)</sup>

## Management

Management proceeds in a stepwise fashion: lifestyle and fiber → osmotic laxatives → stimulants as rescue or an individualized adjunct → prescription secretagogues/prokinetics for OTC failures, with biofeedback promptly when a defecatory disorder is identified → surgery in carefully selected refractory cases.<sup>[[4]](#ref4)[[5]](#ref5)</sup>

### Lifestyle and Dietary Measures

- **Toileting routine** — post-prandial attempts leveraging the gastrocolic response; knees-above-hips positioning (e.g., footstool); avoidance of prolonged straining.
- **Hydration** — adequate fluid intake, particularly with fiber.
- **Physical activity** — modest evidence for benefit; reasonable first-line adjunct.

### Pharmacologic Therapy — AGA/ACG 2023 Recommendations

The 2023 AGA–ACG guideline on pharmacologic management of chronic idiopathic constipation provides graded recommendations for adult chronic idiopathic constipation.<sup>[[4]](#ref4)</sup>

| Class | Agent | Recommendation | Certainty | Key Implementation Notes |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Fiber** | Psyllium | Conditional (suggest) | Low | First-line for patients with low dietary fiber intake; encourage hydration; flatulence is common; evidence for bran and inulin is very limited |
| **Osmotic** | PEG | **Strong (recommend)** | Moderate | Durable response at 6 months; well tolerated; combine with fiber as needed |
| | Magnesium oxide | Conditional (suggest) | Very low | Start low, titrate; avoid in renal insufficiency (hypermagnesemia risk) |
| | Lactulose | Conditional (suggest) | Very low | Reserve for OTC failures; bloating and flatulence are dose-limiting |
| **Stimulant** | Bisacodyl / sodium picosulfate | **Strong (recommend)** | Moderate | Short-term (≤4 wk) or rescue therapy; start low to limit cramping and diarrhea. Sodium picosulfate is not marketed in the United States |
| | Senna | Conditional (suggest) | Low | Trials used higher doses than are common in practice; start low and titrate |
| **Secretagogue** | Lubiprostone | Conditional (suggest) | Low | For OTC failures; label dose for CIC is 24 mcg twice daily with food and water (reduces nausea); contraindicated in known or suspected mechanical GI obstruction |
| | Linaclotide | **Strong (recommend)** | Moderate | For OTC failures; label CIC dose 145 mcg daily (72 mcg if tolerability is limiting), on an empty stomach; diarrhea is the main adverse effect; contraindicated under age 2 years and in mechanical obstruction |
| | Plecanatide | **Strong (recommend)** | Moderate | For OTC failures; label CIC dose 3 mg daily; diarrhea is a principal adverse effect; contraindicated under age 6 years and in mechanical obstruction; no head-to-head equivalence with linaclotide implied |
| **5-HT4 agonist** | Prucalopride | **Strong (recommend)** | Moderate | For OTC failures; label dose 2 mg daily (1 mg daily if creatinine clearance is below 30 mL/min); headache, abdominal pain, nausea and diarrhea are the most common AEs |

**Practical sequencing.** A reasonable first-line regimen for most adults is **fiber (psyllium) + PEG**, with bisacodyl or sodium picosulfate reserved for rescue. Patients who fail or cannot tolerate OTC therapy should advance to a **secretagogue (linaclotide, plecanatide, or lubiprostone)** or the **5-HT4 agonist prucalopride**; choice is driven largely by payer formulary, comorbidity (e.g., avoid magnesium oxide in CKD), and side-effect profile.<sup>[[4]](#ref4)</sup>

**Tenapanor** (an NHE3 inhibitor, 50 mg twice daily) is US-labeled for IBS-C, not chronic idiopathic constipation, and is not part of the 2023 CIC guideline.

<a id="opioid-induced-constipation"></a>

### Opioid-Induced Constipation

Opioid-induced constipation (OIC) is the commonest medication-related secondary cause and is managed on a separate track. Stimulant and osmotic laxatives are first-line; peripherally acting mu-opioid receptor antagonists (PAMORAs) are reserved for an inadequate laxative response.<sup>[[14]](#ref14)</sup> Lubiprostone (24 mcg twice daily) is also labeled for OIC in chronic non-cancer pain.

| PAMORA | Label notes |
|---|---|
| **Naloxegol** (Movantik) | Contraindicated in known or suspected GI obstruction and with strong CYP3A4 inhibitors |
| **Naldemedine** (Symproic) | Contraindicated in known or suspected GI obstruction or risk of recurrent obstruction |
| **Methylnaltrexone** (Relistor; tablet and injection) | Contraindicated in known or suspected GI obstruction or risk of recurrent obstruction |

Perforation is the reason for the obstruction contraindication. Consider it in patients with prior pelvic or bowel surgery, Crohn disease, or pelvic malignancy.

### Defecatory Disorders — handled separately

Patients with dyssynergia or inadequate propulsion confirmed on ARM + BET are treated with **anorectal biofeedback as first-line** — not further pharmacology escalation. In a randomized trial of 109 patients with dyssynergia who had failed fiber and enemas, five weekly biofeedback sessions gave major improvement in 80% at 6 months versus 22% with polyethylene glycol plus counseling, with benefit sustained to 24 months.<sup>[[15]](#ref15)</sup> A sham-controlled trial (n = 77) showed correction of dyssynergia and more complete spontaneous bowel movements with biofeedback than with sham or standard therapy, and a 1-year follow-up of the trial (n = 26) showed persistence.<sup>[[16]](#ref16)[[17]](#ref17)</sup> The full diagnostic workflow and treatment algorithm (biofeedback, botulinum toxin, selected structural surgery) lives on the **[Defecatory Dysfunction](/docs/clinical-conditions/03i-defecatory-disorders/defecatory-dysfunction)** page.<sup>[[6]](#ref6)[[10]](#ref10)</sup>

The relevant point for constipation management: **treat confirmed dyssynergia while continuing appropriate stool-form treatment**. Slow-transit constipation commonly improves once evacuation is restored.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[6]](#ref6)</sup>

### Surgical Considerations

Surgery for chronic constipation is reserved for **carefully selected refractory patients** after exhaustive medical and pelvic floor therapy, and mandates objective physiologic confirmation of the constipation subtype.<sup>[[5]](#ref5)</sup>

| Indication | Operation | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| Medically refractory slow-transit constipation; normal ARM/BET; no generalized GI dysmotility | **Total abdominal colectomy with ileorectal anastomosis (TAC-IRA)** | High symptom satisfaction but meaningful rates of persistent abdominal pain, bloating, small-bowel obstruction, and diarrhea; exclude upper GI dysmotility preoperatively |
| Symptomatic, reducible **rectocele** with trapping on defecography and splinting on history | Transvaginal / transanal / transperineal **[rectocele repair](/docs/surgical-techniques/04g-prolapse-repair/posterior-colporrhaphy)** | Best outcomes when obstructed defecation is demonstrably mechanical and dyssynergia has been corrected |
| Symptomatic, high-grade **rectal intussusception or external rectal prolapse** | **Ventral mesh rectopexy** (or abdominal/perineal alternatives) | Consider combined repair only when the apical prolapse independently warrants treatment |
| Enterocele / sigmoidocele contributing to obstructed defecation | **Enterocele repair**, often as part of apical suspension | Address concurrent apical defects |

**Sacral neuromodulation** is sometimes offered before colectomy. Evidence is weak: in a sham-controlled crossover trial of tined-lead testing (n = 45), test-phase response did not predict 6-month benefit (60% vs 57%, P = 0.76), suggesting a strong placebo response, and the study was stopped early for infection during prolonged lead testing.<sup>[[18]](#ref18)</sup>

**Do not operate without objective workup.** Colectomy should not be offered for chronic constipation without documented slow transit, an excluded defecatory disorder (normal ARM/BET or corrected dyssynergia), and exclusion of generalized GI dysmotility. Outcomes of colectomy performed in unselected patients are poor.<sup>[[5]](#ref5)</sup>

### Refractory constipation: AGA 2026

Before labeling constipation refractory, reassess secondary causes and complete anorectal testing and biofeedback when indicated. Confirm transit off therapy and, ideally, on maximal therapy. Colectomy requires documented slow transit without an ongoing defecatory disorder, regional gut-transit assessment and psychological evaluation. Pain/bloating-dominant illness and untreated severe psychiatric disease are relative contraindications.<sup>[[13]](#ref13)</sup>

## Outcomes and Follow-Up

- **PEG** produces durable response at 6 months in most patients; tachyphylaxis is uncommon.<sup>[[4]](#ref4)</sup>
- **Linaclotide** durable CSBM response was 16–21% (145 µg) and 19–21% (290 µg) versus 3–6% with placebo across two 12-week trials.<sup>[[8]](#ref8)</sup> **Plecanatide** durable response was 20.1% (3 mg) versus 12.8% with placebo.<sup>[[9]](#ref9)</sup>
- **Prucalopride** 2 mg produced three or more spontaneous complete bowel movements per week in 30.9% versus 12.0% with placebo over 12 weeks; headache and abdominal pain were the commonest adverse events.<sup>[[7]](#ref7)</sup>
- **Biofeedback for dyssynergia** produces major improvement in roughly 70–80% of appropriately selected patients in controlled trials, with response durable at ≥1 year.<sup>[[10]](#ref10)[[15]](#ref15)[[17]](#ref17)</sup>
- **Colectomy for STC** — satisfaction is high in carefully selected series, but long-term morbidity is substantial. In a 15-year survey of 32 responders after total colectomy with ileorectal anastomosis, most were satisfied, yet 72% had abdominal pain, 53% fecal incontinence and 44% a later admission for bowel obstruction.<sup>[[5]](#ref5)[[19]](#ref19)</sup>

Follow-up intervals are typically 4–8 weeks during initial titration and 3–6 months once a stable regimen is established. Patients on long-term laxatives should be reassessed periodically for efficacy and for the emergence of alarm features.
