# Stress Urinary Incontinence (Female)

**Stress urinary incontinence (SUI) is the involuntary leakage of urine with physical exertion, coughing, sneezing, or any activity that raises intra-abdominal pressure.** In the 2017–2018 US NHANES cycle, 45.9% of adult women reported stress leakage at least once in the preceding year.<sup>[[13]](#ref13)</sup> This broad symptom-based estimate is not the prevalence of bothersome, treatment-seeking SUI.<sup>[[13]](#ref13)</sup> A 2022–2023 US cohort (RISE FOR HEALTH, n = 3,000) found an overall lower urinary tract symptom prevalence of 79%, with urgency and incontinence carrying the greatest bother, yet only 7.1% of all 3,000 participants reported treatment for LUTS — a persistent treatment-seeking gap rather than a change in the underlying prevalence estimate.<sup>[[18]](#ref18)</sup> SUI causes significant physical, emotional, and social distress and is a leading driver of gynecologic and urogynecologic referral.

> **ACP Guideline:** The American College of Physicians recommends PFMT for stress UI, bladder training for urgency UI, and PFMT combined with bladder training for mixed UI.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup>

This page covers the clinical framework and diagnostic workup. For procedural selection and comparative treatment options, see the [Female SUI treatment database](/docs/surgical-techniques/04f-incontinence-procedures/female-sui/female-stress-incontinence-database).

## Pathophysiology

SUI occurs when intra-abdominal pressure transiently exceeds urethral closure pressure, allowing urine to escape.<sup>[[2]](#ref2)</sup><sup>[[3]](#ref3)</sup> Two primary mechanisms account for most cases:

**Urethral hypermobility**
Loss of pelvic floor muscular support or vaginal connective tissue prevents the urethra and bladder neck from assuming a stable closed position in response to pressure increases. Weakness of support structures and collagen-dependent tissue damage are central. This is the predominant mechanism in most post-partum and post-menopausal patients. Levator ani avulsion is a plausible contributor to this loss of support, but a systematic review and meta-analysis of seven studies (2,388 women) found no significant association between levator avulsion and SUI (pooled OR 0.87, 95% CI 0.56–1.34, adjusted OR 0.72, 95% CI 0.40–1.30) — a contrast with avulsion's established link to prolapse, and a reminder that stress leakage is multifactorial rather than driven by a single anatomic lesion.<sup>[[19]](#ref19)</sup>

![The DeLancey hammock hypothesis: a cough compresses the urethra against a supported anterior vaginal wall when lateral attachments are intact, but leaks when the hammock is lax](/img/diagrams/continence-hammock.svg)

*DeLancey's hammock hypothesis. The urethra rests on a supportive layer (anterior vaginal wall + endopelvic fascia) anchored laterally to the **arcus tendineus** and **levator ani**. With intact support, a cough compresses the urethra against this **stable backboard** and the lumen closes (continent). When the lateral (paravaginal) attachments fail, the hammock sags, the urethra becomes hypermobile, and the cough no longer compresses it — **stress leakage**. This is why a midurethral sling restores continence: it re-creates the suburethral backboard. (Original WARWIKI schematic)*

**Intrinsic sphincter deficiency (ISD)**
Loss of intrinsic urethral mucosal and muscular tone results in poor urethral coaptation and can reduce resting urethral closure pressure. AUA/SUFU notes that ISD is often defined by a leak point pressure &lt;60 cmH₂O or a maximal urethral closure pressure &lt;20 cmH₂O.<sup>[[10]](#ref10)</sup> EAU 2026 advises against using either pressure test to grade SUI severity (Strong).<sup>[[15]](#ref15)</sup> ISD produces more severe leakage, often with minimal provocation, and is associated with prior urethral surgery, radiation, and neurological injury.

In practice, many patients exhibit a combination of both mechanisms.

Blaivas and Olsson (1988) proposed a classification of stress incontinence based on the nature of vesical neck descent and the integrity of the intrinsic sphincteric mechanism, and used it to select the operation in 72 patients. With a minimum follow-up of 18 months, 94% were cured of stress incontinence, but 14 developed frequency and urgency persisting at least 6 months, 13 of them after a pubovaginal sling.<sup>[[25]](#ref25)</sup>

## Risk Factors

| Risk Factor | Notes |
|---|---|
| **Vaginal delivery** | ~2× risk vs cesarean; risk increases with parity and instrumental delivery |
| **Increasing parity** | Cumulative effect with each vaginal birth |
| **Obesity / elevated BMI** | Chronic elevated intra-abdominal pressure; weight loss reduces severity |
| **Age and menopause** | Estrogen deficiency reduces urethral mucosal coaptation; prevalence peaks 40–60 years |
| **White race** | Higher prevalence than Black or Hispanic women |
| **Pelvic surgery** | Hysterectomy disrupts endopelvic fascia and autonomic innervation |
| **Conditions raising IAP** | Chronic cough, constipation, heavy lifting, high-impact exercise |
| **Smoking** | Chronic cough; collagen effects |
| **Connective tissue disorders** | Joint hypermobility syndrome / hypermobile Ehlers-Danlos syndrome is associated with more than twice the risk of pelvic organ prolapse and high rates of stress and urgency incontinence; affected women often present younger and carry added perioperative considerations (tissue fragility, impaired wound healing)<sup>[[21]](#ref21)</sup> |

In an anonymous survey of 271 identical twin pairs (mean age 47.1 years), analyzed to account for shared genetics within pairs, vaginal delivery was associated with self-reported stress incontinence relative to cesarean delivery (OR 2.28, 95% CI 1.14–4.55), as were age, parity and obesity.<sup>[[26]](#ref26)</sup> In a case-control study of women aged 39 to 65 years without prolapse (213 with moderate or severe SUI and 213 age-matched controls with none or mild), lifetime strenuous activity was not associated with SUI (OR 1.11, 95% CI 0.99–1.25), whereas greater strenuous activity during the teen years modestly increased the odds (OR 1.37 per 7 additional hours a week, 95% CI 1.09–1.71).<sup>[[27]](#ref27)</sup>

### SUI During Pregnancy

SUI is the most common type of urinary incontinence during pregnancy. Symptoms may improve after delivery, but pregnancy-associated leakage is linked to a higher risk of postpartum incontinence. A systematic review of 28 studies found a mean prevalence of 41% (range 18.6% to 60%) that increases with gestational age. The proposed mechanism is the growing uterus and fetal weight adding pressure on the pelvic floor muscles through pregnancy, combined with pregnancy-related hormonal change, reducing pelvic floor muscle strength and its supportive and sphincteric function and leading to bladder neck and urethral mobility. Higher prenatal BMI, older maternal age and multigravidity are associated with a higher risk. Pelvic floor muscle exercise is a safe and effective treatment during pregnancy, without significant adverse effects.<sup>[[16]](#ref16)</sup>

## Diagnostic Evaluation

### History

The Women’s Preventive Services Initiative recommends annual screening for urinary incontinence, including its impact on activities and quality of life, with further evaluation when indicated.<sup>[[9]](#ref9)</sup>

Elements to assess:

- **Leakage pattern:** Predictable with cough, sneeze, Valsalva, exercise, position change
- **Severity:** Frequency (daily, weekly), volume (small drops versus soaking), pad use and type
- **Degree of bother:** Drives treatment urgency and goal-setting
- **Coexisting symptoms:** Urgency, urgency incontinence, incomplete emptying, prolapse symptoms, fecal incontinence, dyspareunia
- **Sexual activity:** Ask directly about urine loss during sexual activity, including whether it occurs with penetration, orgasm, or both; coital incontinence is common but underreported and does not by itself localize the leakage mechanism
- **Obstetric and surgical history:** Deliveries, episiotomies, prior anti-incontinence surgery
- **Medication review:** Diuretics, α-blockers, anticholinergics, caffeine, alcohol

### Physical Examination

| Finding | Significance |
|---|---|
| Vulvovaginal atrophy | Common in postmenopausal women; may worsen symptoms |
| Skin maceration / excoriation | Severity of leakage; hygiene counseling |
| Pelvic organ prolapse | May mask or worsen SUI; assess all three compartments. In a continent woman planning prolapse surgery, perform a prolapse-reduction stress test for occult SUI and counsel about the risks and benefits of concomitant continence surgery versus prolapse repair alone.<sup>[[15]](#ref15)</sup> |
| Pelvic floor muscle strength | Baseline for PFMT; assess ability to contract voluntarily |
| Urethral hypermobility | Cotton swab (Q-tip) test — rotation &gt;30° from horizontal indicates hypermobility |
| Urethral diverticulum / fistula | Rule out anatomical causes of leakage |

### Urinalysis

Obtain urinalysis to assess for infection, hematuria, glycosuria, and pyuria; investigate abnormal findings before attributing leakage solely to SUI.<sup>[[2]](#ref2)</sup><sup>[[3]](#ref3)</sup><sup>[[10]](#ref10)</sup>

### Voiding Diary

A fluid intake and voiding diary kept for at least three days identifies modifiable factors (total intake, caffeine, nocturnal patterns) and quantifies leakage frequency when history is insufficient.<sup>[[3]](#ref3)[[15]](#ref15)</sup>

### Cough Stress Test

With the bladder comfortably full (not immediately after voiding), ask the patient to cough or Valsalva in standing or lithotomy position. Immediate urethral leakage synchronous with the cough confirms SUI. Positive predictive value 78–97%.<sup>[[2]](#ref2)</sup><sup>[[3]](#ref3)</sup>

### Postvoid Residual

Performed by catheterization or bladder ultrasound. Important before surgical planning to exclude incomplete emptying.

### Urodynamic Testing

**Urodynamics may be omitted for an uncomplicated index patient with clearly demonstrated SUI.** ValUE supports office evaluation alone in its selected population; its PVR eligibility threshold describes trial enrollment. A single PVR does not by itself establish or exclude emptying dysfunction. Consider additional evaluation, including urodynamics when it can change management, for:<sup>[[10]](#ref10)[[11]](#ref11)</sup>

- Mixed incontinence with significant urgency component
- Prior anti-incontinence surgery (failed or new evaluation)
- Neurogenic lower urinary tract dysfunction
- Elevated PVR or voiding dysfunction
- Discordance between symptoms and examination findings

A secondary analysis of a 597-woman midurethral-sling trial population (valid VLPP n = 372; MUCP n = 539) found that urethral-function urodynamic measures (VLPP, MUCP) had little or no correlation with subjective or objective incontinence-severity measures, even though VLPP and MUCP correlated moderately with each other (r = 0.36, P &lt; 0.001); patient-reported severity measures, in turn, correlated only weakly to moderately with objective measures such as pad weight and 3-day diary episode frequency (r = −0.06 to 0.45). This study-specific finding supports keeping patient-reported and objective outcomes distinct when interpreting SUI severity; it does not mean urodynamic testing has no diagnostic role, only that urethral pressure measures do not track how bothered a patient reports being.<sup>[[22]](#ref22)</sup>

## Treatment

### Step 1 — Conservative (First-Line)

#### Pelvic Floor Muscle Training (PFMT)

The cornerstone of first-line management and the ACP-recommended initial treatment for all women with SUI.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup> PFMT involves repeated voluntary pelvic floor contractions taught and supervised by a trained physiotherapist or pelvic health specialist. NICE NG123 offers a supervised trial of at least 3 months as first-line treatment for stress or mixed incontinence, with at least 8 contractions three times daily, continued if beneficial.<sup>[[14]](#ref14)</sup>

Evidence:
- PFMT improves patient-reported cure or improvement and condition-specific quality of life versus control; effects depend on the population and outcome assessed.<sup>[[7]](#ref7)</sup>
- Adding PFMT to another active treatment is a separate comparison: the 2015 review found insufficient evidence for a general additional benefit across those combinations.<sup>[[4]](#ref4)</sup>
- About half of women with stress-predominant incontinence are satisfied 1 year after starting PFMT; ACOG adds that training is most effective when started under supervision and that its effect wanes over time<sup>[[6]](#ref6)</sup>
- More intensive programs with adherence support are more effective than unsupervised instruction<sup>[[7]](#ref7)</sup>
- Agree on the patient’s goals and distinguish complete dryness, meaningful symptom reduction and satisfaction; the cited study used a ≥50% episode reduction as its outcome definition.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup>

Adjunctive physical therapy modalities:

| Modality | Evidence |
|---|---|
| **Biofeedback** | May help teach or monitor a contraction; do not promise additional long-term benefit for every patient |
| **Vaginal cones** | Weighted resistance training; modest benefit |
| **Electrical stimulation** | Evidence is strongest for urgency incontinence; data for SUI are limited. NICE advises against routine use with PFMT but supports considering it, with or without biofeedback, for women who cannot actively contract the pelvic floor<sup>[[14]](#ref14)</sup> |
| **Intravaginal pessaries** | Incontinence ring/dish type; improves QoL; may be combined with PFMT |

#### Lifestyle Modifications

| Modification | Rationale |
|---|---|
| **Weight loss** | Moderate weight loss improves UI in overweight and obese women (ACOG Level A; in the 6-month trial ACOG cites, a mean 7.8 kg loss, about 8% of baseline weight, gave 47% vs 28% fewer weekly incontinence episodes); NICE advises weight loss for women with incontinence and a BMI above 30<sup>[[6]](#ref6)[[7]](#ref7)[[14]](#ref14)</sup> |
| **Fluid management** | Individualize intake to clinical needs and the diary; avoid both excess intake and dehydration |
| **Caffeine reduction** | For coexisting OAB, EAU 2026 advises that reducing caffeine may improve urgency and frequency, but not incontinence (Strong).<sup>[[15]](#ref15)</sup> In a case-control study of women with urinary incontinence, intake >400 mg/day was associated with detrusor instability after adjustment for age and smoking (OR 2.4, 95% CI 1.1–6.5); this does not establish causality or benefit for stress leakage.<sup>[[20]](#ref20)</sup> |
| **Reduce nocturnal fluids** | For nocturia-associated symptoms |
| **Frequent voiding** | Reduce bladder volume at times of activity |
| **Constipation management** | Reduces chronic Valsalva and pelvic floor strain |

### Surgical options

Offer surgical counselling when desired, including before conservative treatment fails; discuss conservative and operative choices, individual risks and goals.<sup>[[10]](#ref10)</sup>

#### Midurethral Slings (Synthetic Mesh)

Midurethral slings have substantial efficacy evidence, but “success” depends on its definition. In TOMUS, retropubic and transobturator slings met the prespecified equivalence criterion for objective 12-month success; subjective success did not meet equivalence. Discuss retention or voiding dysfunction, pain or neurologic symptoms, mesh complications and retreatment alongside expected benefit.<sup>[[12]](#ref12)</sup>

Comparisons with fascial sling, Burch and bulking require procedure-specific evidence and patient selection; these operations should not be described as universally equivalent.<sup>[[10]](#ref10)</sup>

> **Note on mesh:** The FDA's 2011 safety communication and 2019 stop-selling order concerned transvaginal mesh for prolapse; they did not prohibit midurethral slings for SUI, which remain FDA-cleared devices. Mesh-related concerns have increased patient hesitancy; informed discussion is needed.<sup>[[8]](#ref8)</sup>

Types of midurethral slings:

| Type | Approach | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| **Retropubic (TVT)** | Behind pubic bone | Highest long-term data (EAU advises women that long-term outcomes are better than transobturator); bladder perforation in 15/298 (5.0%) retropubic recipients in TOMUS<sup>[[12]](#ref12)[[15]](#ref15)</sup> |
| **Transobturator (TOT/TVT-O)** | Lateral obturator | Lower bladder injury risk; higher groin/thigh pain rate |
| **Single-incision mini-sling** | Anchored midurethral | Shorter procedure; long-term data still maturing. EAU notes non-inferior patient-reported success for Ajust and Altis at 36 months but more mesh exposure, repeat SUI surgery and dyspareunia than conventional MUS<sup>[[15]](#ref15)</sup> |

#### Autologous Fascial Sling

Pubovaginal sling using rectus fascia or fascia lata. Established evidence; a non-mesh surgical option when mesh is contraindicated or the patient declines synthetic materials. Higher voiding dysfunction rate than midurethral slings; durable long-term results.<sup>[[8]](#ref8)</sup><sup>[[10]](#ref10)</sup>

#### Burch Colposuspension

Open or laparoscopic retropubic colposuspension uses paravaginal sutures to Cooper's ligament. It is an established alternative to a sling; efficacy, adverse effects and durability should be discussed using the specific comparison and follow-up interval rather than assuming equivalence. Concomitant continence surgery at abdominal sacrocolpopexy is a separate shared decision, balancing postoperative leakage against added complications.<sup>[[6]](#ref6)[[10]](#ref10)</sup>

#### Urethral Bulking Agents

Periurethral or transurethral injection of bulking material (polyacrylamide hydrogel, calcium hydroxylapatite, dextranomer/hyaluronic acid, which falls under the EAU caution on hyaluronic acid agents noted below). Office-based, minimally invasive option. Lower cure rates than slings; useful in patients unfit for anesthesia, prior mesh failure, ISD-predominant disease, or as temporizing measure.<sup>[[8]](#ref8)</sup> EAU 2026 offers bulking to women who request a lower-risk procedure (Strong), counseling that efficacy is lower than other operations and repeat injections are likely; it advises against autologous fat and hyaluronic acid agents because of higher adverse-event risk (Strong) and notes that the periurethral route may carry more retention than the transurethral route.<sup>[[15]](#ref15)</sup>

See: [Urethral Bulking Agents](/docs/surgical-techniques/04f-incontinence-procedures/procedures/urethral-bulking-agents)

#### Artificial Urinary Sphincter

Reserved for severe, complicated SUI, particularly ISD after prior failed surgery or radiation. High-quality data limited for this indication in women: EAU 2026 rates the evidence level 3 and lists the AUS among options for complicated SUI (Weak), warning of a high risk of complications, mechanical failure or explantation even in expert centres (Strong); see [AUS article](/docs/surgical-techniques/04f-incontinence-procedures/procedures/artificial-urinary-sphincter).<sup>[[15]](#ref15)</sup>

### Pharmacotherapy

Medical therapies for SUI are generally not recommended. Current evidence-based pharmacological treatments primarily address urgency incontinence (antimuscarinics, β3-agonists) and not SUI.<sup>[[14]](#ref14)[[15]](#ref15)</sup>

*Duloxetine* (SNRI, not FDA-approved for SUI in the US) has modest evidence for symptom improvement but significant side effects limiting use; not in routine clinical practice in North America. EAU 2026 offers duloxetine, where licensed, only to selected women unresponsive to conservative care who wish to avoid surgery (Weak), with dose titration on starting and stopping (Strong), and recommends vaginal estrogen for postmenopausal women with SUI and vulvovaginal atrophy (Strong).<sup>[[15]](#ref15)</sup> Across 17 studies (2,111 patients) examining several urinary outcomes, a 2026 meta-analysis reported lower SUI occurrence after versus before vulvovaginal estrogen treatment (pooled OR 0.12, 95% CI 0.05–0.29); 2,111 is the overall review sample, not the SUI-specific denominator.<sup>[[17]](#ref17)</sup> NICE advises against duloxetine as first-line treatment and against routine second-line use, but allows it as second-line therapy for women who prefer medication to surgery or are unsuitable for surgery, with counseling about adverse effects.<sup>[[14]](#ref14)</sup>

---

### Emerging Therapies

**Vaginal laser therapy** (CO₂ fractional laser, Er:YAG laser) has been investigated for SUI. A 2025 Cochrane review of nine studies (689 women) found very-low-certainty evidence: short-term continence may differ little from sham or control, and the small average patient-reported improvement did not meet its prespecified threshold for clinical importance. The review reported no major adverse events in these trials, but longer-term benefit and safety remain uncertain; laser therapy is not established routine SUI care.<sup>[[5]](#ref5)</sup>

## Outcomes

Outcome definitions and follow-up must accompany any percentage. In TOMUS, 12-month objective success was 80.8% after retropubic and 77.7% after transobturator sling; subjective success was 62.2% and 55.8%, respectively. Objective outcomes met the trial's equivalence criterion, while subjective outcomes did not. TOMUS outcomes apply to its studied sling groups; direct comparison with unrelated Burch, fascial-sling or bulking series is unsupported.<sup>[[12]](#ref12)</sup>

For counseling, separate symptom improvement, complete dryness, satisfaction, adverse effects and retreatment. The [selected evidence](#selected-evidence) records the principal trial comparisons; procedure pages contain the operation-specific evidence.

**Sexual function and coital incontinence.** A systematic review and meta-analysis of 21 studies of continence surgery (TVT, TVT-O/TOT, Burch, autologous fascial sling) without concurrent prolapse repair found a significant reduction in coital incontinence after surgery (OR 0.11, 95% CI 0.07–0.17); study heterogeneity and outcome-measure variation limited certainty.<sup>[[23]](#ref23)</sup> A combined secondary analysis of SISTEr and TOMUS included 924 women with a PISQ-12 questionnaire completed at one or more study visits (autologous fascial sling, Burch colposuspension, retropubic sling, or transobturator sling). Available scores improved clinically over 24 months across all four procedures, with no significant difference by procedure type; most improvement occurred within the first 12 months.<sup>[[24]](#ref24)</sup> Counsel that sexual function improved on average in these trial cohorts, while individual outcomes vary; this secondary analysis detected no statistically significant difference in 24-month PISQ-12 scores among the four operations.

## Clinical Approach Summary

A practical assessment and shared-decision checklist:<sup>[[10]](#ref10)</sup>

1. **Assess bother and QoL impact.** Drives urgency and goals of treatment
2. **Quantify leakage.** Frequency, volume, pad use, pad weight test if available
3. **Pelvic exam.** Prolapse assessment, muscle function, Q-tip test, cough stress test
4. **Urinalysis.** Rule out UTI
5. **Conservative option:** Offer supervised PFMT and relevant lifestyle measures (weight loss when indicated; caffeine reduction for coexisting OAB symptoms); surgery need not await PFMT failure.<sup>[[10]](#ref10)[[15]](#ref15)</sup>
6. **Adjuncts:** Incontinence pessary if patient prefers non-surgical option or awaits PFMT response
7. **Procedure discussion when desired:** Discuss sling, colposuspension, fascial sling and bulking according to goals; use additional testing according to complexity.

### Referral Indications

- Significant pelvic organ prolapse (concurrent repair consideration)
- Elevated PVR / voiding dysfunction
- Failed prior anti-incontinence surgery
- Mixed incontinence with dominant urgency component
- Consideration of surgical management
- Neurological comorbidity
