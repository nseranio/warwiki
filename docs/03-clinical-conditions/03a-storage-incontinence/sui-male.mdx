# Stress Urinary Incontinence (Male)

**Male stress urinary incontinence (SUI)** is involuntary urine loss with physical exertion, cough, sneeze, or Valsalva. In contemporary reconstructive practice it is overwhelmingly an incontinence-after-prostate-treatment problem: radical prostatectomy, radiation, and outlet surgery can injure the external rhabdosphincter, bladder-neck support, periurethral tissues, and pelvic innervation.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[2]](#ref2)[[3]](#ref3)</sup> The AUA/GURS/SUFU *Incontinence After Prostate Treatment* guideline anchors contemporary evaluation, counseling, and procedural selection.<sup>[[4]](#ref4)</sup>

This page covers the clinical framework: who has it, how to grade it, when to intervene. For the operative options and selection criteria, see the [Male SUI treatment database](/docs/surgical-techniques/04f-incontinence-procedures/male-sui/male-stress-incontinence-database).

---

## Epidemiology and Etiology

### Post-prostatectomy (the dominant cause)

After radical prostatectomy, reported SUI prevalence spans a wide range depending on definition, follow-up, and measurement method: 5–60% at 3–6 months, with most resolving by 12 months.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[2]](#ref2)[[5]](#ref5)</sup> Pooled data in the AUA/GURS/SUFU guideline put continence at about 90% by 6 months after robotic prostatectomy and 94% by 12 months, with only about 1% further improvement between 12 and 24 months, so leakage that persists at 12 months is unlikely to resolve on its own.<sup>[[4]](#ref4)</sup> Persistent incontinence beyond one year affects roughly 5–20% of post-RP patients in modern cohorts; in the CEASAR trial, 14–25% of men reported bothersome leakage 10 years after prostatectomy, versus 4–11% after external beam radiation.<sup>[[4]](#ref4)[[6]](#ref6)</sup> The definition drives the number: in one long-term series, 24-month continence was 83% with no or occasional pad use, 92.3% with 0–1 pad/day for occasional dribbling only, and 93.4% with 0–1 pad/day irrespective of reason.<sup>[[5]](#ref5)</sup>

Risk factors for persistent post-RP SUI:<sup>[[4]](#ref4)[[6]](#ref6)</sup>

- Advancing age
- Larger prostate size
- Shorter membranous urethral length on preoperative MRI (longer length is protective)
- Bilateral neurovascular bundle preservation (protective; men were about 26% more likely to be continent at 6 months)
- Prior or subsequent radiation therapy
- Higher BMI (associated with short-term leakage; little evidence of an effect at 1 year)
- Preoperative LUTS and baseline urethral function and surgeon experience (reported in cohorts)

For men considering salvage radiotherapy after radical prostatectomy, AUA/ASTRO/SUO 2024 calls for counseling about risks to urinary control, erectile function and bowel function in the context of risk from recurrent cancer, life expectancy, comorbidities and patient preferences (Statement 4, Clinical Principle).<sup>[[21]](#ref21)</sup>

The AUA/GURS/SUFU guideline reports similar incontinence rates after open and robotic prostatectomy and identifies bilateral neurovascular bundle preservation as the only maneuver associated with improved 12-month continence in its review.<sup>[[4]](#ref4)</sup> AUA/ASTRO recommends nerve sparing when oncologically appropriate (Statement 20, Moderate Recommendation, Grade B). Its review found favorable but variable continence associations and no randomized comparison of nerve-sparing with non-nerve-sparing prostatectomy.<sup>[[20]](#ref20)</sup>

A Cochrane review of 5 RCTs (571 randomized, 502 completing) found the Retzius-sparing (posterior) robotic approach probably improves continence within a week of catheter removal (RR 1.74, 95% CI 1.41–2.14) and may improve continence at 3 months (RR 1.33, 95% CI 1.06–1.68), with little to no difference by 12 months (RR 1.01, 95% CI 0.97–1.04); it may also increase positive surgical margins (RR 1.95, 95% CI 1.19–3.20). This affects the expected recovery trajectory and the timing of a persistent-incontinence workup, not the 12-month continence rate itself.<sup>[[15]](#ref15)</sup>

### Other causes

- **TURP / HoLEP / other BPH surgery.** Persistent SUI is uncommon (0–8.4% in the guideline review) but clinically important when apical resection, prior radiation, or sphincter injury compromises the external sphincter.<sup>[[4]](#ref4)</sup>
- **Pelvic trauma.** External urethral sphincter damage with pelvic fracture urethral injury
- **Neurogenic.** Lesions of the thoracolumbar sympathetic outflow (T11–L2) impair proximal urethral tone; sacral lesions (S2–S4) affect the rhabdosphincter; pelvic plexus destruction, cauda equina compression, and selected spinal cord lesions can similarly unmask sphincteric leakage. For NLUTD patients with SUI and acceptable bladder storage parameters, AUA/SUFU recommends slings (statement 45, moderate recommendation, Grade C) and allows an artificial urinary sphincter (statement 46, conditional recommendation, Grade C) in selected patients.<sup>[[7]](#ref7)</sup>
- **Post-cystectomy neobladder.** Continence depends on an intact external sphincter; SUI here is a distinct problem from reservoir function

## Pathophysiology

Male SUI is fundamentally an intrinsic sphincter deficiency (ISD) problem.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[2]](#ref2)</sup> After prostatectomy, three mechanisms compound:

1. **Direct rhabdosphincter injury** during apical dissection
2. **Loss of the proximal continence mechanism.** Bladder neck and prostatic urethra no longer contribute
3. **Local denervation** from pelvic plexus or pudendal-branch injury

Radiation adds progressive fibrosis of the membranous urethra, stiffens the sphincter, and over years can convert a well-compensated patient into a leaker.

Detrusor overactivity, impaired compliance, and bladder-neck contracture can coexist with sphincter deficiency and produce a mixed incontinence picture. The reconstructive consequence is practical: bladder-only management rarely resolves leakage with a sphincteric driver, and sling or AUS planning should follow treatment of bladder neck contracture or anastomotic stenosis, infection, and urgency-predominant symptoms. Detrusor overactivity and low capacity have not been shown to worsen sling or AUS outcomes, but high storage pressures or poor compliance should be treated or followed with upper tract surveillance after implant.<sup>[[4]](#ref4)[[6]](#ref6)</sup>

## Diagnosis and Evaluation

### Initial workup

- **History.** Onset relative to surgery, pad count (pads-per-day, PPD), pad weight, activities that provoke leakage, nocturnal versus daytime distribution, prior radiation, comorbidities, medications
- **Sexual activity.** Ask directly about urine loss at orgasm (climacturia) in men with post-prostatectomy incontinence. In a study of 84 men continent and potent at least 1 year after bladder-neck-sparing radical prostatectomy, 24 (28.6%) reported climacturia; on video-urodynamic sub-study, functional urethral length was significantly shorter in men with climacturia (P = 0.02) and time to continence recovery was significantly longer (P = 0.05). Climacturia is a distinct symptom from persistent stress leakage during daily activity and does not by itself indicate treatment failure.<sup>[[18]](#ref18)</sup>
- **Validated instruments.** ICIQ-SF, UDI-6, IPSS for mixed LUTS<sup>[[4]](#ref4)</sup>
- **3-day voiding diary** with pad weights
- **Physical exam.** Standing cough stress test, perineal sensation, anal tone, DRE (post-RP anatomy)
- **Urinalysis and culture** to rule out UTI
- **Post-void residual.** Bladder US or catheter
- **Pad test.** Pad count correlates reasonably with pad weight, but a formal 1-hour or 24-hour pad test is useful when severity is uncertain<sup>[[4]](#ref4)</sup>

See [Assessment Tools & Questionnaires](/docs/evaluation/history-physical/assessment-tools) for instrument detail.

### Specialized evaluation

Before an SUI operation, assess urethral integrity and whether further functional testing will change management:<sup>[[4]](#ref4)[[6]](#ref6)</sup>

- **Urodynamics.** Use selectively to clarify the cause of leakage, storage safety or emptying dysfunction and inform counselling; not mandatory for every patient
- **Cystourethroscopy.** Perform before SUI surgery to assess urethral and bladder pathology (AUA/GURS/SUFU Expert Opinion), including contracture, anastomotic stenosis, stricture, lesions or stones; treat symptomatic bladder neck contracture or anastomotic stenosis before sling or AUS.<sup>[[4]](#ref4)</sup>
- **MRI** (selected): membranous urethral length if planning anatomic sling

Red flags deserving expedited referral: gross hematuria, new obstructive symptoms, recurrent UTIs, suspected fistula.

Two catheter-related pitfalls can affect the filling-cystometry portion of the study:

- **The filling catheter can itself mask leakage across a tight outlet.** In a 60-man protocol study, all men eventually demonstrated urodynamic stress incontinence, but 21 (35%) leaked only after the urethral (transducer) catheter was removed; among men who leaked both with and without the catheter, abdominal leak-point pressure was 86.3 versus 67 cmH₂O, respectively (P = 0.002); men who leaked only after catheter removal had higher leak-point pressures in a separate comparison (P &lt; 0.001). In a man with convincing clinical stress leakage but no leak on catheter-based testing, consider repeating a cough or Valsalva stress test after removing the filling catheter (retaining the rectal pressure line) rather than concluding leakage is absent.<sup>[[16]](#ref16)</sup>
- **Severe leakage can itself prevent adequate filling.** In a series of 166 post-prostatectomy men undergoing video-urodynamics, 30 (18%) had filling cystometry interrupted by leakage severe enough to prevent reaching cystometric capacity; applying a temporary occlusive penile clamp during filling allowed the study to be completed in each case without altering pressure recording. These men had lower maximum urethral closure pressure, lower volume at strong desire to void, and lower cystometric capacity than the overall post-prostatectomy incontinence population studied. This is a selected testing technique for men whose severe leakage otherwise truncates the study, not a routine part of UDS or a long-term continence measure; the clamp should not occlude the filling line, and rapid emptying can follow its release.<sup>[[17]](#ref17)</sup>

### Severity grading

Pad count and weight help describe severity, but these practical bands are not validated universal thresholds. Pad type, activity and patient bother also matter; the guideline notes that sling studies generally defined severe leakage as more than 500 g/day or more than 5 pads/day. Patient-reported pad use can understate severity, and in one series, 34% of men reporting mild-to-moderate SUI leaked enough on a standing cough test to be graded severe (more often with prior SUI surgery or radiation).<sup>[[4]](#ref4)[[8]](#ref8)</sup> A separate 104-man series using the Male Stress Incontinence Grading Scale reported a strong correlation between standing cough test grade and 24-hour pad weight (Spearman ρ = 0.68), with mean pad weights of roughly 57 g, 117 g, 223 g, 385 g and 513 g for grades 0 through 4 respectively (reported leakage not seen on exam; delayed drops; early drops without a stream; delayed stream; early, persistent stream). Treat these as correlations from one retrospective cohort, not validated treatment thresholds.<sup>[[19]](#ref19)</sup>

| Severity | Pads/day | 24-h pad weight | Typical surgical option |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Mild** | 1–2 PPD | &lt;100 g | Observation, sling or AUS after counselling |
| **Moderate** | 3–4 PPD (2 PPD overlaps the guideline’s mild band) | 100–500 g | Sling or AUS (patient preference; prior radiation favors AUS) |
| **Severe** | ≥5 PPD or continuous leak | &gt;500 g | **AUS**; male sling should not be routine (AUA/GURS/SUFU Moderate Recommendation, Grade C)<sup>[[4]](#ref4)</sup> |

Radiation history shifts selection toward AUS at any severity because radiated tissue is a poor sling bed and a high-risk erosion environment.

## Natural History and Timing of Intervention

Post-RP SUI follows a predictable recovery curve. The AUA/GURS/SUFU guideline and contemporary series support:<sup>[[4]](#ref4)[[5]](#ref5)[[6]](#ref6)</sup>

- **Before and early after surgery:** pelvic floor exercises or training may be offered before prostatectomy (started 3–4 weeks ahead in the trials) and should be offered after catheter removal; document the recovery trajectory.<sup>[[4]](#ref4)[[9]](#ref9)</sup>
- **From 6 months:** surgery may be offered for bothersome SUI that is not improving despite conservative treatment (AUA/GURS/SUFU Conditional Recommendation, Grade C).
- **At 1 year:** offer surgical treatment for bothersome persistent SUI despite conservative care (AUA/GURS/SUFU Strong Recommendation, Grade B); continued observation remains a patient choice.

A minority of men with severe persistent early SUI (e.g., continuous leakage at 6 months with no improvement trajectory) warrant earlier formal evaluation. The guideline emphasizes shared decision-making and bounds rather than rigid timelines.<sup>[[4]](#ref4)</sup>

## Management

### Conservative (first 6–12 months)

- **Pelvic floor muscle exercises / training (PFME/PFMT).** Offer after catheter removal; the guideline evidence indicates earlier return to continence, but similar continence rates at 12 months. The 2023 Cochrane review rates the overall evidence as uncertain, limited by small trials with methodological flaws and non-standardized PFMT protocols<sup>[[4]](#ref4)[[9]](#ref9)</sup>
- **Lifestyle.** Fluid and caffeine management, weight loss, smoking cessation
- **Biofeedback-augmented PFMT.** Cochrane found low-certainty, conflicting results (greater subjective cure in one trial, lower objective cure in two), so it has no proven advantage over PFMT alone<sup>[[9]](#ref9)</sup>
- **Electrical stimulation** (surface, perineal, or anal stimulation): evidence is insufficient; the 2023 Cochrane review found no trials of stimulation against control reporting its outcomes. Use as an adjunct, not a substitute for supervised PFMT<sup>[[9]](#ref9)[[10]](#ref10)</sup>
- **Penile compression (clamp).** A containment option alone or with pads for selected men, including longer-term use. Release it at least every two hours, never wear it overnight, and avoid it when sensation, dexterity or memory is impaired or urgency symptoms are substantial; prolonged pressure can injure skin and urethra.<sup>[[4]](#ref4)</sup>

There is no FDA-approved drug specifically for male SUI. Duloxetine has been used off label with tolerability limitations; bladder medications target coexisting urgency and do not repair sphincter injury. See the [duloxetine hub](/docs/foundations/pharmacology/neuropathic-pelvic-pain/snris) for its evidence and prescribing status.<sup>[[6]](#ref6)</sup>

### Surgical options — overview

Treatment selection integrates severity, radiation history, urethral and cognitive factors, and patient preference. For detailed selection criteria and outcomes see the [Male SUI treatment database](/docs/surgical-techniques/04f-incontinence-procedures/male-sui/male-stress-incontinence-database). These are choices, not a mandatory escalation sequence:

| Option | Best candidate | Key consideration |
|---|---|---|
| [**Urethral bulking**](/docs/surgical-techniques/04f-incontinence-procedures/procedures/urethral-bulking-agents) | Mild, patient declining sling/AUS | Low efficacy; cure is rare, and repeat treatment may be needed |
| [**Male urethral sling**](/docs/surgical-techniques/04f-incontinence-procedures/procedures/male-urethral-slings) | Mild to moderate, no radiation, intact urethra | Passive, no device to operate; best outcomes in low pad-weight patients |
| [**Periurethral balloons (ProACT)**](/docs/surgical-techniques/04f-incontinence-procedures/procedures/proact-balloons) | Non-radiated patients with mild to severe SUI, where available | Postoperative titration is the main draw; AUA 2024 gives a conditional recommendation, and evidence and availability vary by region |
| [**Artificial urinary sphincter (AUS)**](/docs/surgical-techniques/04f-incontinence-procedures/procedures/artificial-urinary-sphincter) | Mild through severe; particularly radiation or higher severity | Most predictable and reliable option in the guideline; effectiveness can decline over time and reoperation is common for mechanical malfunction, erosion, infection or atrophy; patient must be able to operate the pump<sup>[[4]](#ref4)</sup> |

Historical autologous fascial slings and Burch-style suspensions have been largely replaced by the options above, though the principle of compression or support under the bulbar urethra persists.<sup>[[2]](#ref2)[[11]](#ref11)</sup>

## Outcomes

| Intervention | Key outcome | Reference |
|---|---|---|
| **PFMT** | The AUA guideline reports earlier return to continence after prostatectomy but similar 12-month incontinence rates (RR 0.85, 95% CI 0.60–1.22, from its cited older review); the 2023 Cochrane review judged the value of conservative interventions uncertain across heterogeneous trials | AUA/GURS/SUFU 2024; Johnson Cochrane 2023<sup>[[4]](#ref4)[[9]](#ref9)</sup> |
| **Urethral sling** | Best outcomes in non-irradiated mild-to-moderate SUI; failure risk rises with high pad weight, prior radiation, and poor urethral mobility | Sandhu 2010; Prebay 2023<sup>[[2]](#ref2)[[11]](#ref11)</sup> |
| **AUS** | Strong recommendation to discuss AUS for mild to severe post-prostate-treatment SUI; long-term continence and satisfaction are high, but the device likely loses effectiveness over time and reoperation is common | AUA/GURS/SUFU 2024; Sandhu 2010<sup>[[4]](#ref4)[[2]](#ref2)</sup> |
| **Bulking** | Low efficacy and rare cure; not a routine substitute for sling or AUS | AUA 2024 and older limited comparative evidence<sup>[[4]](#ref4)[[12]](#ref12)</sup> |

Radiation degrades outcomes across every modality; AUS in the radiated patient has higher erosion and revision rates but remains the most effective option.

## Complications to Counsel

**AUS-specific:** mechanical failure, cuff erosion (higher with radiation), infection requiring explant, atrophy requiring downsizing or reposition, need for patient dexterity to cycle the pump.

**Sling-specific:** perineal pain, inability to catheterize in retention, sling release if obstructive, mesh-related complications (rare but present).

**ProACT-specific:** the AUA/GURS/SUFU guideline reports a mean all-cause explantation rate of 27% (range 7–55%) across studies, including erosion, infection, balloon migration and balloon failure; counsel about revision and complication burden.<sup>[[4]](#ref4)</sup>

**Bulking-specific:** migration, repeat injections, limited durability.

**Shared:** urinary retention, UTI, de novo or persistent overactive bladder symptoms.
