# Ileal Conduit

The **ileal conduit** is the most commonly performed urinary diversion following radical cystectomy, although its use varies by population and center.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[2]](#ref2)</sup> It is an **incontinent diversion** in which a 15–20 cm segment of distal ileum serves as a passive conduit from the ureters to an external collection device via an abdominal stoma.<sup>[[3]](#ref3)</sup> It is intended to drain continuously, rather than act as a storage reservoir; kinking or obstruction can still impair drainage.

Popularized by Eugene Bricker after World War II, it became the standard of care for incontinent urinary diversion in the 1950s once effective adhesive stoma appliances became available, and has remained the most frequently performed urinary diversion worldwide for more than 70 years.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[4]](#ref4)</sup>

---

## Indications

Ileal conduit is an established option after cystectomy for cancer and for selected refractory benign lower-tract disease. Choice depends on comorbidity, renal/hepatic function, available bowel, support for stoma care, and patient preference, and not on age or one creatinine cutoff alone.<sup>[[5]](#ref5)[[6]](#ref6)</sup>

It is often suitable when a continent reconstruction's catheterization or training demands are impractical. In multiple sclerosis, a French multicenter retrospective cohort of 211 selected patients reported improved urinary quality of life and fewer UTI hospitalizations after diversion, with early complications in 42%; this remains major surgery.<sup>[[7]](#ref7)</sup>

Severe renal or hepatic impairment limits continent bowel reservoirs, but creatinine &gt;2 mg/dL is not a universal exclusion threshold for every diversion. Ileal conduit still carries an absorptive/metabolic burden. Active bowel disease, short bowel, or damaged/irradiated ileum may preclude use of the proposed segment and require another reconstruction. Cutaneous ureterostomy may be considered in frail patients when avoiding bowel is a priority.<sup>[[6]](#ref6)</sup>

## Surgical Technique

### Key steps

1. Isolate a 15–20 cm segment of distal ileum and preserve its mesenteric blood supply.
2. Restore bowel continuity (ileoileal anastomosis).
3. Construct the ureteroileal anastomosis (Bricker or Wallace; see below).
4. Mature the stoma through the abdominal wall, typically in the right lower quadrant. See [Stoma Site Marking](/docs/foundations/surgical-principles/stoma-site-marking) for the preoperative WOC-nurse marking workflow.

In an obese patient with a short, bulky mesentery and a thick abdominal wall, a Turnbull-type loop stoma has been described as an alternative to an end-loop nipple stoma: the conduit end is left closed, the loop is brought through the stoma without tension or twist, opened transversely about 3 cm from the closed end, and matured over a temporary supporting rod with 2–3 cm of bowel exteriorized without tension.<sup>[[34]](#ref34)</sup>

### Ureteroileal anastomosis — Bricker vs Wallace

| Technique | Construction | Practical considerations |
|---|---|---|
| **Bricker** | Separate end-to-side ureteral anastomoses to the conduit | Allows separate treatment of unequal ureteral lengths. |
| **Wallace** | Spatulated ureters joined into a shared plate and then to the conduit | A stricture at the common outlet can affect both upper tracts. |

These are technical distinctions, not a universal ranking of stricture risk. Comparative cohorts have different selection and follow-up, and results conflict.<sup>[[8]](#ref8)[[9]](#ref9)[[10]](#ref10)</sup>

A shared Wallace plate means one anastomotic stricture can obstruct both kidneys. From Martínez-Gómez C, et al. Comparative findings vary by study and patient selection; technique choice should not rest on a single small cohort.<sup>[[8]](#ref8)[[9]](#ref9)[[10]](#ref10)</sup> A 96-patient retrospective robotic cohort found no statistically significant differences in strictures, complications, or readmissions; it does not establish equivalence.<sup>[[11]](#ref11)</sup> A single-center series of 137 patients, in which one surgeon performed all Bricker anastomoses and a second performed all Wallace anastomoses, found a significantly higher stricture rate with Bricker (25.3% versus 7.7%, p = 0.001); however, follow-up was more than twice as long in the Bricker group (36.5 versus 17 months), and technique was not independent of surgeon, so this comparison does not settle the question either.<sup>[[32]](#ref32)</sup>

The Ren 2025 Bricker–Wallace comparison carries a June 2026 publisher expression of concern about reference reliability, which is not a retraction. It is not used here to support a technique preference.<sup>[[12]](#ref12)</sup>

### Distal Ureter: Perfusion and Reach

In the retrospective Das 2024 cohort (366 patients; 711 anastomoses), 35 patients developed strictures affecting 40 anastomoses. Greater distal resection was associated with lower stricture odds, adjusted OR 0.73 (95% CI 0.58–0.92). Resection lengths were 2.3 versus 1.65 cm in non-stricturing versus stricturing anastomoses. This association does not establish an optimal resection length or prove that additional resection itself prevents strictures.<sup>[[13]](#ref13)</sup>

An earlier 463-patient cohort found no independent association between resection length and stricture. Preserve adventitial blood supply and construct a well-perfused, tension-free anastomosis; do not sacrifice reach simply to meet a length target. Perfusion assessment supplements, rather than replaces, those principles.<sup>[[14]](#ref14)[[15]](#ref15)</sup>

### Core technical principles (irrespective of technique)

- Spatulate the distal end of each ureter to widen the anastomotic lumen.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[9]](#ref9)</sup>
- **Minimize periureteral dissection.** Preserve adventitial blood supply; never strip the ureter.<sup>[[15]](#ref15)</sup>
- Construct a tension-free mucosa-to-mucosa anastomosis with fine absorbable suture (4-0 or 5-0 polyglactin), running or interrupted.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[9]](#ref9)</sup>
- Temporary ureteral stents are commonly used across the anastomoses. Select the device and removal timing according to drainage, healing, and the operative protocol; 10–14 days is not a universal rule.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup>
- For a ureter with a history of pelvic radiation, excising additional proximal ureteral tissue before the anastomosis has been described as a way to reduce stricture risk; this is an attributed practice preference, not a validated resection length.<sup>[[35]](#ref35)</sup>

### Positioning, Catheter and Drain Management

Conduit construction is performed in whatever position the antecedent radical cystectomy or extirpative procedure already used — supine for an open approach, or steep Trendelenburg with a split-leg/low-lithotomy setup for a robotic or laparoscopic approach — since the conduit is built at the end of the same operation rather than in a separate setup. Stoma-site marking is done preoperatively regardless of approach; see [Stoma Site Marking](/docs/foundations/surgical-principles/stoma-site-marking).

A urethral (Foley) catheter drains the cystectomy bed until it is removed with the specimen or shortly after, and a stoma appliance replaces any conduit "catheter." At the conduit itself, temporary ureteral stents and a closed suction pelvic drain are typical. Traditional practice stented the ureteroileal anastomoses and kept a pelvic drain for roughly two weeks, but this is shifting toward earlier removal: in a 178-patient single-center cohort, decreasing routine stent duration from about 15.5 to 5 days did not increase ureteroenteric stricture at a median 9.8-month follow-up, and was associated with fewer 90-day readmissions and fewer total urinary tract infections.<sup>[[29]](#ref29)</sup> A separate 228-patient cohort undergoing cystectomy with ileal conduit similarly found that removing stents by postoperative day 5 (cutting total antibiotic exposure from about 22 to 8 days) did not increase 30-day UTI, urine leak (defined by drain-fluid creatinine at least twice serum creatinine), or readmission compared with later removal.<sup>[[30]](#ref30)</sup> No single stent or drain duration is universal; use drain output/creatinine and imaging, not the calendar alone, to guide removal.<sup>[[29]](#ref29)[[30]](#ref30)</sup> These duration comparisons all stented every patient; the separate question of stenting versus no stenting at all has its own evidence. A systematic review and meta-analysis (11 studies) found no significant difference in ureteroenteric anastomotic leak between stented and unstented patients (pooled leak 1% stented versus 2% unstented). Comparative meta-analysis of 4 studies found a numerically higher stricture incidence with stenting that did not reach significance (OR 1.64, 95% CI 0.88–3.05, p = 0.12; pooled single-proportion stricture rate 7% stented versus 3% unstented), so stenting is not established to prevent leak or stricture at this anastomosis.<sup>[[33]](#ref33)</sup>

### VTE Prophylaxis

Ileal conduit for bladder cancer is nearly always performed with radical cystectomy, and that combined operation has a specific, graded recommendation rather than only a general risk framework: the AUA/ASCO/SUO 2024 guideline states that perioperative pharmacologic VTE prophylaxis should be given to patients undergoing radical cystectomy (Statement 16, Strong Recommendation; Evidence Level: Grade B), combined with mechanical prophylaxis, with up to four weeks of postoperative coverage considered potentially beneficial though optimal duration is undefined.<sup>[[28]](#ref28)</sup> See the [VTE Prophylaxis](/docs/foundations/pharmacology/perioperative-eras/vte-prophylaxis) hub for dosing, timing, and the wider extended-prophylaxis evidence for major pelvic cancer surgery. This recommendation is specific to the cancer operation; it does not automatically apply to ileal conduit performed for benign indications, where the general risk framework (age, obesity, prior VTE, malignancy, prolonged operation or immobility) on that page applies instead.

## Complications

Complications accumulate with time. In a historical series of 131 patients surviving at least five years, 87 (66%) had late conduit-related complications; among those surviving beyond 15 years, the reported rate was 94%. These survivor-cohort estimates are not an individual patient forecast.<sup>[[16]](#ref16)</sup>

### Early (within 90 days)<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup>

- Urine leak / urinoma
- Urinary obstruction
- Postoperative fluid collections (hematoma, lymphocele, abscess)
- Fistula formation
- Wound infection

### Late<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[16]](#ref16)[[17]](#ref17)</sup>

| Complication | Rate |
|---|---|
| **Ureteroileal anastomotic stricture** | 5–14% |
| **Stomal complications (including stenosis)** | About 15–24% in the cited cohorts |
| **Renal/upper-tract functional or morphological complications** | About 20–27%; definitions vary |
| **Urolithiasis** | 9–15% (38% after 15 yr) |
| Symptomatic UTI / pyelonephritis | 23% |
| Bowel complications | 20–24% |
| Ureteroarterial fistula | Rare but life-threatening |

In the Mayo Clinic series (Shimko 2011, n = 1,057; ileal or colonic conduits), 643 patients (60.8%) had diversion-related complications. The reported mean of 2.3 complications was among those 643 patients, not all 1,057. Distribution: bowel 20.3%, renal 20.2%, infectious 16.5%, stomal 15.4%, urolithiasis 15.3%.<sup>[[17]](#ref17)</sup>

### Bowel anastomotic leak and enterocutaneous fistula

Leak at the ileoileal bowel anastomosis has been reported in about 1–5% of ileal-conduit patients; cited risk factors include poor nutrition, ischemia, prior intestinal surgery, chemotherapy or radiation, steroid use, anastomotic tension, and distal obstruction. A severe leak may require exploration with bowel resection and re-anastomosis, with proximal fecal diversion if needed. Enterocutaneous fistula after diversion is uncommon (&lt;5%), can present like a wound infection around postoperative days 4–7, and is managed initially with infection and source control, drainage, fluid/electrolyte replacement, and nutritional support; spontaneous closure after six weeks of adequate nutrition without infection is considered unlikely, though ongoing drainage volume, sepsis risk, and the option of long-term nonoperative care still guide further management.<sup>[[35]](#ref35)</sup> Acid-suppressive therapy and, in selected cases, a somatostatin analog to reduce enteric secretions have been described as adjuncts for enterocutaneous fistula, though the cited evidence for somatostatin comes from small-bowel fistula series rather than a conduit-specific comparison.<sup>[[37]](#ref37)</sup>

### Conduit and stomal necrosis

Progressive darkening and retraction of the conduit at the abdominal-wall opening should raise concern for ischemic necrosis, distinct from the transient maroon, dusky venous congestion and edema that is common above the fascia in the first several days. Viability can be assessed above and below the fascia by endoscopy, by bedside transillumination of the lumen through a transparent appliance or a test tube passed into the stoma, or by flexible cystoscopy if the bedside assessment is indeterminate; superficial mucosal injury may simply slough with improved stoma care, but confirmed full-thickness necrosis is a surgical emergency requiring urgent exploration, inspection of the conduit and adjacent small bowel, and excision and replacement of nonviable bowel. Sepsis, metabolic acidosis, hyperphosphatemia, or shock can accompany extensive nonviable bowel. True necrosis of the conduit itself is rare; more often the finding is limited to the superficial stoma above the fascia.<sup>[[35]](#ref35)</sup>

### Stomal variceal bleeding

Bleeding stomal varices are a rare late complication, most often associated with portal hypertension from hepatic dysfunction, typically arising within a few years of surgery. Local compression, suture ligation, or mucocutaneous disconnection may not achieve durable control while portal pressure remains elevated; case reports describe successful transjugular intrahepatic portosystemic shunt (TIPS) or angiographic embolization with sclerosis for refractory bleeding, though TIPS itself carries a substantial risk of hepatic encephalopathy in patients with more advanced liver disease. A patient with a bleeding stoma and known or suspected portal hypertension warrants hepatology/interventional-radiology involvement rather than repeated local measures alone.<sup>[[38]](#ref38)[[39]](#ref39)</sup>

## Metabolic Consequences

Intestinal diversions cause characteristic metabolic derangements:<sup>[[18]](#ref18)[[19]](#ref19)</sup>

- Hyperchloremic metabolic acidosis results from ammonium chloride absorption and bicarbonate wasting and may require oral bicarbonate supplementation.
- The risk of vitamin B12 deficiency depends on the ileal segment used and underlying absorption. Terminal ileum is preserved where feasible; the AUA/ASCO/SUO guideline discussion notes that preserving the most distal 15 cm of ileum in a conduit can reduce problems with B12, fat-soluble vitamin and bile salt absorption. The EAU bladder cancer guideline states that B12 should be measured annually after bowel diversion (guideline text, not a graded recommendation).<sup>[[20]](#ref20)</sup> The AUA/ASCO/SUO guideline (Statement 31, Expert Opinion) advises checking B12 in follow-up because deficiency risk is increased after resection of more than 60 cm of ileum or use of the terminal ileum.<sup>[[28]](#ref28)</sup>
- One retrospective comparative cohort reported metabolic abnormalities in 21% of its ileal-conduit group; this is not a universal rate.<sup>[[19]](#ref19)</sup>

For full pharmacologic management see [Vitamin B12 supplementation](/docs/foundations/pharmacology/urinary-diversion-specific/vitamin-b12-supplementation), [Urinary acidifiers & alkalinizers](/docs/foundations/pharmacology/urinary-diversion-specific/urinary-acidifiers), and [Mucus management](/docs/foundations/pharmacology/urinary-diversion-specific/mucus-management).

## Quality of Life — vs Other Diversions

Quality-of-life comparisons between ileal conduit and neobladder depend on the domain and the population:

- Meta-analyses generally show marginally better QoL with neobladder for physical, role and social functioning and global health, but neobladder patients experience more urinary symptoms (incontinence, need for self-cath).<sup>[[21]](#ref21)[[22]](#ref22)[[23]](#ref23)</sup>
- When matched for age and comorbidities, global health is often similar; ileal-conduit patients report better long-term urinary function.<sup>[[23]](#ref23)[[24]](#ref24)[[25]](#ref25)</sup>
- In women, a meta-analysis of four studies (283 patients) detected no significant overall QoL difference; wide confidence intervals and observational evidence do not prove equivalence.<sup>[[26]](#ref26)</sup>
- Patient acceptance can be good, including in older patients, but counseling should address appliance care, leakage, body image, and available support.<sup>[[5]](#ref5)</sup>
- In a prospective single-center cohort of 411 patients (205 ileal conduit, 206 continent diversion) followed for 2 years, sexual function was low before surgery with limited recovery across both diversion types, while body image scores worsened after surgery specifically among ileal-conduit patients (about a 10-point decline on a 100-point scale), a pattern not seen with continent diversion.<sup>[[36]](#ref36)</sup>

### Comparison with Other Diversions

| Feature | Ileal conduit | Orthotopic neobladder | Continent cutaneous reservoir |
|---|---|---|---|
| Emptying | Continuous drainage to an appliance | Timed urethral emptying; catheterization may be needed<sup>[[40]](#ref40)</sup> | Scheduled catheterization through a stoma |
| Main practical burden | Appliance/stoma care | Continence training, nocturnal leakage, retention<sup>[[40]](#ref40)</sup> | Reliable lifelong catheter access and mucus care |
| Shared concerns | Renal deterioration, obstruction, infection, stones, metabolic effects | Same, plus storage/emptying problems | Same, plus channel/continence-mechanism problems |

There is no randomized comparison establishing one diversion as best overall. Differences in age, comorbidity, selection, and follow-up complicate renal and quality-of-life comparisons.<sup>[[23]](#ref23)[[24]](#ref24)[[25]](#ref25)[[6]](#ref6)</sup> In the 281-patient Nieuwenhuijzen cohort, late complications were lower after ileal conduit, largely because of fewer uncomplicated UTIs; late major complications did not differ significantly.<sup>[[19]](#ref19)</sup>

## Long-Term Surveillance

Lifelong surveillance is needed given the high cumulative complication burden:<sup>[[16]](#ref16)[[27]](#ref27)</sup>

- Renal function (serum creatinine, eGFR)
- Upper-tract imaging for hydronephrosis and urolithiasis
- Annual serum vitamin B12 under EAU guidance.<sup>[[20]](#ref20)</sup> NCCN post-cystectomy follow-up specifies annual testing after the first year based on clinical judgment (category 2A).<sup>[[40]](#ref40)</sup>
- Metabolic panel for acidosis
- Stoma assessment by a wound or ostomy nurse
- Cancer surveillance per primary-disease protocol

In a Medicare claims cohort of 1,565 patients, renal failure/impairment was estimated in more than 16% after urinary diversion. Obstruction and renal impairment were more common in the conduit group, illustrating why a universal claim of “least renal decline” is inappropriate.<sup>[[27]](#ref27)</sup> Functional follow-up remains necessary even after oncologic surveillance becomes less frequent.

### Loopography

Loopography (conduitography) is the contrast-study analog of a VCUG for a urinary conduit. Indications include upper-tract evaluation after diversion (hematuria, obstruction, renal insufficiency), assessment of the bowel segment itself (obstruction, leak, infection, pain), and, in a patient who cannot safely receive IV contrast, a low-systemic-absorption option for upper-tract evaluation. Technique: a plain film is obtained first; a 14–16 Fr Foley catheter is passed past the fascia with the balloon inflated and pulled back against the fascia to distend the conduit; contrast is instilled and frontal and oblique films obtained (a refluxing anastomosis gives a full upper-tract view); a post-drainage film follows, and the catheter can be removed before drainage specifically to assess for obstruction within the conduit itself. Because most intestinal diversions are colonized, periprocedural antibiotics should be considered.<sup>[[31]](#ref31)</sup>
