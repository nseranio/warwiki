# Penn Pouch

The **Penn pouch** is an ileocecal continent cutaneous reservoir associated with the Mitrofanoff appendiceal channel. Two published operative case descriptions use the appendix as the catheterizable continence mechanism, with the stoma at the umbilicus in both.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[2]](#ref2)</sup> It belongs to the broader ileocecal pouch family described in the historical imaging literature.<sup>[[3]](#ref3)</sup>

Webster and Bertram described a different reconstruction in seven patients: an intussuscepted ileocecal valve stabilized with a Marlex collar. That mechanism is not the appendiceal Penn channel, and its outcomes do not apply to it.<sup>[[4]](#ref4)</sup> Cecal segments in that series were usually hyperactive and were controlled with anticholinergic medication.<sup>[[4]](#ref4)</sup>

## Reservoir and Channel Design

The ileocecal bowel segment forms the storage reservoir. A vascularized appendix provides a narrow catheterizable channel, with a tunneled flap-valve relationship to the reservoir wall. In the published Penn cases, the appendix was reversed and its cecal end brought to the umbilicus. Ureteral implantation and the appendiceal tunnel are separate structures.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[2]](#ref2)</sup>

| Structure | Purpose | Operative consideration |
|---|---|---|
| Ileocecal reservoir | Urinary storage | Preserve the bowel's vascular supply and create adequate capacity and compliance; a named pouch does not guarantee low storage pressure |
| Appendiceal channel | Continent access for intermittent catheterization | Preserve the appendiceal mesentery, ensure sufficient reach and an accessible stoma, and avoid a tight or angulated tunnel |
| Ureteral implantation | Drainage of the upper urinary tract into the reservoir | Maintain perfusion and a tension-free course; evaluate for obstruction during follow-up |
| Cutaneous stoma | Repeated catheter access | Site according to abdominal contour, reach and ability to catheterize; the umbilicus is an option, not an obligation |

For detailed channel construction and alternatives when the appendix is absent or unsuitable, see [Principles of Continent Catheterizable Channels](/docs/surgical-techniques/04b-bladder-reconstruction/catheterizable-channels). If the reservoir or channel is modified, the operative record should list the actual bowel segments, orientation, continence mechanism and implantation sites, because the pouch name does not specify them.

## Selection

This diversion requires reliable lifelong intermittent catheterization and follow-up. Selection depends on renal function, bowel suitability, reservoir requirements, channel reach, dexterity or dependable catheterization assistance, and the patient's preferences. Pediatric reconstruction also needs a plan for growth, transition and independent self-care where possible.

The published Penn case reports describe selected children with severe congenital urinary anomalies. They illustrate an available reconstruction; they do not establish a preferred diversion for every child with a small or absent bladder.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[2]](#ref2)</sup> See [Urinary Diversion Principles](/docs/surgical-techniques/04c-urinary-diversion/urinary-diversion-principles) for the wider choice between conduit, cutaneous reservoir and other reconstruction.

## Evidence and Outcomes

Dedicated Penn evidence is limited. Two accessible operative case reports describe successful catheterization and continence during approximately four years of follow-up, but individual successes cannot estimate a population's complication risk or demonstrate superiority.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[2]](#ref2)</sup>

The following sources describe related operations, and their denominators should remain separate:

| Source | What was actually studied |
|---|---|
| Webster and Bertram 1986<sup>[[4]](#ref4)</sup> | Seven patients with a stapled intussuscepted ileocecal valve, including three augmentations and four bladder replacements; this is not evidence for appendiceal Penn-channel outcomes |
| Lowe and Woodside 1990<sup>[[5]](#ref5)</sup> | Five urodynamically assessed patients with an intussuscepted ileocecal valve. Incontinence was minimal, filling and peristaltic pressures were low, nipple pressure exceeded reservoir pressure in 4 of 5, and no reflux was found. One patient leaked when reservoir pressure transiently exceeded nipple pressure at capacity, and this resolved with a shorter catheterization interval. These pressures do not describe an appendiceal channel |
| Carroll et al. 1989<sup>[[6]](#ref6)</sup> | Fourteen patients with a plicated terminal-ileal efferent segment, another distinct continence mechanism. Twelve were completely continent, two had mild nocturnal incontinence, mean reservoir volume was 675 mL, and mean reservoir contraction pressure was 24 cm H₂O (maximum 47) |
| Gerharz et al. 1997<sup>[[7]](#ref7)</sup> | Appendix (n = 96) versus intussuscepted ileal nipple (n = 106) in Mainz I reservoirs. Appendico-umbilical stenosis occurred in 15.6% of appendix stomas, and 12.3% of ileal nipples needed a second operation. Stones were removed in 2.8% of ileal-nipple patients and none in the appendix group. These are not Penn-specific revision rates |
| Duckett and Lotfi 1993<sup>[[8]](#ref8)</sup> | Appendicovesicostomy (Mitrofanoff principle) and variations in 41 children, with a mean follow-up of 3.2 years. Continence was 100% and uncomplicated catheterization 93%. Stones formed in 32%, attributed to mucus and bacteriuria. This series used bladder remnants or colon segments, not the Penn pouch |

## Long-term Care

Maintain a regular, individualized catheterization schedule and teach recognition of difficult access, leakage and incomplete emptying. Do not force a catheter through a resistant channel; inability to drain a full reservoir requires prompt specialist assessment. Reservoir overdistension, channel stenosis, stones, mucus obstruction, urinary infection and perforation remain possible despite initially good continence.

Monitor renal function, electrolytes and bicarbonate, upper-tract imaging, and nutritional consequences according to the bowel segments and renal reserve. Ileal resection can affect vitamin B12 handling; colonic and ileal urine exposure can cause metabolic acidosis. One Penn case report explicitly described mild persistent acidosis despite stable renal function.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup> Dosing, irrigation and replacement protocols are in the linked pharmacology pages.
