# Colon Conduit Urinary Diversion

A **colon conduit** is an incontinent diversion in which an isolated colonic segment drains urine from the ureters into an abdominal appliance. Transverse, descending or sigmoid colon is chosen according to tissue quality, vascular supply, ureteral reach and the need for fecal diversion. It is an alternative to the [ileal conduit](/docs/surgical-techniques/04c-urinary-diversion/ileal-conduit) when ileum is unsuitable, and no colonic segment is universally preferred.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[2]](#ref2)</sup>

## Selection and segment choice

| Situation | Planning considerations |
|---|---|
| Prior pelvic radiation | Review the actual field and bowel injury. Healthy transverse colon may be available above the treated area, but colon is not intrinsically radiation-resistant |
| Existing or planned colostomy | A suitable colonic segment can provide the urinary conduit with a separate proximal fecal stoma, avoiding an additional bowel-to-bowel anastomosis |
| Limited small-bowel reserve | Preserve remaining absorptive bowel when feasible; assess nutritional status and the effect of excluding colon as well |
| Inflammatory bowel disease or prior colonic surgery | Establish whether the proposed segment and its blood supply are healthy; small-bowel Crohn's disease does not automatically make colon suitable |
| Renal or hepatic impairment | Urine-exposed colon still produces a metabolic burden; assess severity and alternatives rather than assuming a colon conduit avoids acidosis |

The historical transverse-conduit literature supports feasibility after radiation but not a comparative reduction in complications.<sup>[[3]](#ref3)[[4]](#ref4)[[2]](#ref2)[[5]](#ref5)</sup>

An isolated colon conduit does not use the anal sphincter for continence. Anal-function prerequisites belong to ureterosigmoidostomy and staged ureterocolocolostomy. Nieh's staged ureterocolocolostomy, for example, lists a poor prognosis, previously irradiated rectosigmoid, fecal incontinence or poor sphincter tone, inflammatory large-bowel disease, inadequate ureteral length and a strong family history of colon cancer as contraindications.<sup>[[18]](#ref18)</sup>

### Segments

- **Transverse colon** is used most often after pelvic radiation because it lies above the pelvic field and the ureters can be dissected well above the irradiated tissue. Schmidt described eight patients treated with high-dose external radiotherapy, and Beckley reported satisfactory results in the last 30 patients of a series using an end-to-end (Wallace) refluxing ureterocolic anastomosis.<sup>[[3]](#ref3)[[15]](#ref15)</sup>
- **Sigmoid and descending colon** are used when a colostomy is present or planned. The distal colon becomes the conduit and no bowel anastomosis is needed. Davis described this in 14 patients with preexisting or imminent colostomy, and Alemozaffar reported 41 patients with distal-colon conduits for malignancy, fistula or neurogenic bladder.<sup>[[6]](#ref6)[[4]](#ref4)</sup>
- **Children.** Hendren described a nonrefluxing colon conduit for temporary or permanent diversion in children.<sup>[[14]](#ref14)</sup>

## Operative framework

1. **Select viable bowel and a preserved vascular pedicle.** The pedicle is the middle colic artery for transverse colon and inferior mesenteric branches for sigmoid or descending colon. Determine the length needed for tension-free ureteral implantation and stoma reach. Prior radiation, scarring, mesenteric injury or active bowel disease may make a proposed segment unsuitable.
2. **Plan both bowel ends.** An isolated segment ordinarily requires restoration of intestinal continuity. When fecal diversion is also required, an appropriately designed proximal colostomy can avoid that bowel-to-bowel anastomosis.
3. **Preserve ureteral perfusion and avoid tension or angulation.** Refluxing implantation can be separate or use a common ureteral plate, so refluxing is not synonymous with Wallace. An end-to-end refluxing ureterocolic anastomosis allows later retrograde conduitography to evaluate the urothelium.<sup>[[15]](#ref15)</sup> A nonrefluxing submucosal tunnel is feasible in the thick colonic wall but requires adequate ureteral caliber and freedom from compression.
4. **Mature a well-perfused, accessible stoma.** Confirm unobstructed drainage and a feasible appliance surface. Coordinate the siting of any fecal stoma.

These are shared principles, not a fixed-length recipe. Theva's five-patient series of sigmoid conduit with end colostomy reported operative times of 5 to 11 hours and one Clavien III complication.<sup>[[5]](#ref5)</sup> See the [colon shuffle](/docs/surgical-techniques/04c-urinary-diversion/colon-shuffle) for colostomy conversion and [upper-tract reconstruction principles](/docs/surgical-techniques/04d-upper-tract-reconstruction/upper-tract-reconstruction-principles) for ureteral handling.<sup>[[6]](#ref6)[[7]](#ref7)[[5]](#ref5)</sup>

The Cochrane review (search to October 2011) included five small trials. One trial of conduits fashioned from ileum or colon found no difference in upper urinary tract infection or uretero-intestinal stenosis, and no difference in renal scarring was found between antirefluxing and freely refluxing anastomoses. The review concluded that the evidence was insufficient to identify a best technique.<sup>[[8]](#ref8)</sup>

Dagen reported two cases of severe stenosis of the ureter within the submucosal tunnel of a nonrefluxing colon conduit and noted that, with long-term follow-up, the incidence was 8 to 10% of patients and might exceed that of refluxing conduits. This is a 1980 estimate, not a contemporary pooled rate. Techniques described to prevent stenosis are preservation of periureteral adventitia, careful formation and closure of the submucosal tunnel, a tension-free anastomosis, and tapering the ureters adequately but not excessively. Correction may require lysis of adhesions, a refluxing ureterocolic anastomosis, transureteroureterostomy, transureteropyelostomy, ureteral replacement with small bowel or nephroureterectomy.<sup>[[7]](#ref7)</sup>

In Morales's 46 colon conduits (39 transverse, 7 sigmoid), the advantages reported were minimal stomal stenosis, little residual urine, less electrolyte disturbance and availability for high and low diversions. The series moved from widened refluxing anastomoses to antireflux procedures after the dangers of reflux were recognized.<sup>[[16]](#ref16)</sup>

## Contemporary outcomes

Hebert's 2026 retrospective study included 179 patients from four institutions, treated during 1990–2022. Prior radiation and abdominal surgery were common; the cohort combined colon conduits with anastomosis, with colostomy and with colostomy switch.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup>

| Outcome | Reported result |
|---|---|
| High-grade complications within 30 days | 28.5% |
| High-grade complications during days 30–90 | 14.5% |
| Mortality within 90 days | 4.5% |
| Reintervention within 90 days | 30.2% |
| Later ureteral stent or nephrostomy requirement | 16.8%; this is not synonymous with anastomotic stricture |

Source: Hebert 2026.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup> Preoperative albumin ≥3.2 g/dL was associated with fewer 30–90-day high-grade complications (HR 0.18). A high-grade complication within 30 days was associated with a secondary high-grade complication between days 30 and 90 (HR 2.85). These are observational associations, not proof that treating to a threshold prevents complications. Concurrent colonic anastomosis was not associated with worse 30-day outcomes, which does not establish equivalence of the operative strategies.

### Comparisons with ileal conduit

Hagemans retrospectively compared 214 ileal and 45 colon conduits after rectal-cancer exenteration. Ileus occurred in 21% versus 7%; wound infection in 14% versus 31%, although the wound association did not persist in adjusted analysis. Nine ileal-conduit patients had an ileo-ileal anastomotic leak. Avoiding that anastomosis avoids a leak at that particular site, not all bowel leaks, fistulas or major complications. Other selected outcomes were not statistically different; nonrandomized selection limits comparative conclusions.<sup>[[9]](#ref9)</sup>

Pycha's prospective, nonrandomized comparison of 130 high-risk patients included 55 ileal conduits, 34 colon conduits and 41 ureterocutaneostomies (median follow-up 16 months). Major diversion-related complications occurred in 18.1% of ileal conduits and 5.8% of colon conduits, and late surgical reintervention in 20% and 5.8%, whereas diversion-unrelated perioperative complications were 18.1% and 26.4%. Baseline comorbidity was higher in the colon group (35.2% versus 12.7%), so the groups are not directly comparable.<sup>[[10]](#ref10)</sup>

## Metabolic and late complications

Urine exposure to ileum or colon can cause hyperchloremic metabolic acidosis, vitamin B12 deficiency, osteoporosis, fat malabsorption, urinary calculi and ammoniagenic encephalopathy.<sup>[[11]](#ref11)</sup> Severity depends on renal reserve, bowel surface and contact time, and drainage. Reabsorbed ammonium and sulfate inhibit renal calcium and magnesium reabsorption, as shown in patients with intestinal diversions and in a rat model.<sup>[[17]](#ref17)</sup> Alkali and electrolyte treatment follows measured abnormalities and the [pharmacology guidance](/docs/foundations/pharmacology/urinary-diversion-specific/urinary-acidifiers).<sup>[[2]](#ref2)</sup>

Alemozaffar's 41-patient distal-colon series reported, at ≥90 days, hypokalemia in 39%, hyperchloremia in 24.4% and metabolic acidosis in 34.1%. It also recorded ureterocolonic strictures in 4/41. These are one cohort's time-specific outcomes, not universal colon-conduit rates. Early ileus, bowel obstruction and an enterocutaneous fistula still occurred despite avoiding small-bowel anastomosis.<sup>[[4]](#ref4)</sup>

Shimko's long-term report combined ileal and colon conduits: 643 of 1,057 patients (60.8%) developed diversion-related complications, with a mean of 2.3 per patient. The 15.5-year median follow-up applied to the 213 survivors, not the entire cohort. The study supports prolonged follow-up but cannot supply colon-only complication probabilities. Complications by domain were:<sup>[[12]](#ref12)</sup>

| Domain | Patients affected |
|---|---|
| Bowel | 20.3% |
| Renal | 20.2% |
| Infectious | 16.5% |
| Stomal | 15.4% |
| Urolithiasis | 15.3% |
| Metabolic | 12.8% |
| Structural | 11.5% |

Monitor renal function, electrolytes/acid–base status, upper-tract drainage, infection symptoms and stoma function. B12 risk also depends on prior ileal resection, nutrition and other causes. The EAU guideline advises annual B12 measurement after bowel diversion. New hydronephrosis warrants assessment for obstruction even when a nonrefluxing implantation was used.<sup>[[7]](#ref7)[[13]](#ref13)</sup>
