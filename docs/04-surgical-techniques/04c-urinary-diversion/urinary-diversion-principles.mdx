# Principles of Urinary Diversion

Urinary diversion is the set of reconstructive operations used when the native bladder is removed, bypassed, or no longer suitable for safe storage and emptying. In modern practice, the major families are ileal conduit, orthotopic neobladder, continent cutaneous diversion<sup>[[50]](#ref50)</sup>, and cutaneous ureterostomy.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[2]](#ref2)[[3]](#ref3)[[4]](#ref4)</sup>

The principles that unify these operations are simpler than the number of named procedures suggests: build a low-pressure reservoir when continence is desired, protect the upper tracts at the ureteroenteric junction, choose bowel according to physiology and patient context, match the continence mechanism to what the patient can actually manage, and commit to lifelong metabolic and structural surveillance.<sup>[[5]](#ref5)[[6]](#ref6)[[7]](#ref7)</sup>

For the named diversion library, use the database on the [Urinary Diversion landing page](/docs/surgical-techniques/04c-urinary-diversion).

---

## 1. Diversion Type Must Match the Clinical Goal

Before discussing bowel segments or anastomoses, the surgeon has to decide what problem the diversion is solving:

- **simple continuous drainage** with the lowest operative burden,
- **continent orthotopic storage with urethral emptying**,
- **continent cutaneous storage with catheterizable emptying**,
- or the shortest, safest operation possible in a high-risk patient.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[2]](#ref2)[[3]](#ref3)[[4]](#ref4)</sup>

That decision usually places the patient into one of four buckets:

| Diversion family | Primary design goal |
|---|---|
| **Ileal conduit** | Simple dependable incontinent drainage |
| **Orthotopic neobladder** | Continence per native urethra |
| **Continent cutaneous diversion** | Continence without a urethral outlet |
| **Cutaneous ureterostomy** | Avoid an intestinal segment; preserve ureteral drainage and stomal patency |

This first choice matters because each family carries different requirements for sphincter integrity, manual dexterity, bowel use, metabolic tolerance, and follow-up burden.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[2]](#ref2)[[8]](#ref8)</sup>

---

## 2. Detubularization Is the Core Reservoir Principle

For most contemporary continent bowel reservoirs, **detubularization** opens the selected segment (usually along its antimesenteric border) to reduce coordinated bowel contractions. Individual reservoir designs differ; detubularization does not guarantee absent contractions or safe storage in every patient.<sup>[[5]](#ref5)[[6]](#ref6)</sup>

Why it matters:

- intact bowel behaves like bowel and generates coordinated high-pressure contractions,
- detubularized bowel behaves more like a compliant pouch,
- and reservoir pressure falls while capacity rises.<sup>[[5]](#ref5)[[6]](#ref6)</sup>

Schmidbauer’s work and Hinman’s classic physiologic framework established why this is true: detubularization improves compliance, reduces organized peristaltic pressure spikes, and creates a geometry that supports low-pressure storage.<sup>[[5]](#ref5)[[6]](#ref6)</sup>

This principle is fundamental to orthotopic neobladder and continent cutaneous reservoirs. It is less central to ileal conduit and cutaneous ureterostomy, which are not trying to create storage.

For hand-sewn bowel-stump closure, see the [Parker-Kerr stitch](/docs/foundations/surgical-skills/parker-kerr-stitch) and [Bowel Anastomosis](/docs/foundations/surgical-principles/bowel-anastomosis) technique pages.

---

## 3. Spherical Reconfiguration Maximizes Volume While Minimizing Pressure

Detubularization alone is not enough. The bowel has to be reconfigured into a rounded, near-spherical reservoir rather than left as a long tube or a poorly folded sac.<sup>[[6]](#ref6)[[9]](#ref9)[[10]](#ref10)</sup>

A rounded reservoir increases volume for the available bowel surface. Laplace’s relationship describes pressure, radius and wall tension for an idealized geometry; it does not hold the actual tissue tension constant as the reservoir fills. Bowel compliance, contraction and emptying remain important, and final storage safety requires functional assessment.<sup>[[6]](#ref6)[[10]](#ref10)</sup>

This principle explains why continent urinary diversion and bladder augmentation share so much reservoir design logic. In both settings, the surgeon is trying to create the same thing:

a low-pressure, high-capacity reservoir that does not transmit harmful pressure to the kidneys.

## 4. Bowel Segment Selection Is a Physiologic Choice

The choice of bowel segment determines both technical handling and long-term metabolic behavior.<sup>[[2]](#ref2)[[7]](#ref7)[[11]](#ref11)</sup>

### Practical segment logic

| Segment | Main advantage | Main limitation |
|---|---|---|
| **Ileum** | Technically versatile, mobile mesentery, most familiar for conduit and neobladder | Hyperchloremic metabolic acidosis; terminal ileum loss risks B12 deficiency<sup>[[7]](#ref7)[[11]](#ref11)</sup> |
| **Right colon / ileocecal segment** | Useful for continent cutaneous reservoirs; ileocecal valve may contribute to **outlet continence**, while ureteral antireflux protection is a separate construction | Larger-caliber bowel with its own metabolic profile and handling constraints<sup>[[12]](#ref12)[[13]](#ref13)</sup> |
| **Transverse colon** | Can be helpful when prior pelvic radiation makes distal bowel less attractive | More complex mobilization; not first-line in most routine diversions<sup>[[2]](#ref2)[[14]](#ref14)</sup> |
| **Jejunum** | Rare historical use only | Severe electrolyte derangements make it a poor routine diversion segment<sup>[[7]](#ref7)</sup> |
| **Stomach** | Rare historical niche | Alkalosis and hematuria-dysuria syndrome limit modern use<sup>[[7]](#ref7)</sup> |

Ileum is commonly used, but segment choice depends on the actual reconstruction, bowel health, prior resection or radiation, mesenteric reach, and renal and metabolic tolerance.

Transverse colon conduit has been described in irradiated pelves because it uses non-irradiated bowel and ureter. In a series of 30 patients treated with at least 65 Gy, there were no anastomotic leaks, the complication rate was 37% and the reoperation rate was 20%; among those with renal failure, postoperative serum creatinine was normal or improved in 83%. This is one selected historical series, not a comparative demonstration of superiority over another diversion after high-dose pelvic radiation.<sup>[[14]](#ref14)</sup>

## 5. The Ureteroenteric Anastomosis Is the Achilles Heel

In bowel-based diversions, the ureteroenteric anastomosis is an important site of obstruction and renal risk.<sup>[[3]](#ref3)[[15]](#ref15)[[16]](#ref16)</sup> The central tradeoff is familiar:

- an antireflux construction may add obstruction risk,
- refluxing implantation requires a low-pressure, freely draining system; reflux alone does not establish renal injury.

### The main design tension

| Strategy | Strength | Weakness |
|---|---|---|
| **Refluxing implantation** | Simpler, lower stricture burden in many series | Allows free reflux |
| **Formal anti-reflux implantation** | Better reflux control | Often higher stricture burden |
| **Studer afferent limb** | Separates ureteral implantation from the storage reservoir without a formal tunnel | Does not guarantee absence of reflux; drainage, perfusion and pressure remain important |

In 953 open ileal neobladders, the 10-year ureteroenteric stricture rate with undilated ureters was 2.4% for the refluxing Wallace technique and 14.2% for the nonrefluxing Le Duc technique. With preoperatively obstructed ureters the rates were 7.6% and 35.5%. Preoperative ureteral dilation was a specific risk factor for stricture with either technique.<sup>[[16]](#ref16)</sup>

Regardless of the named technique, the enduring surgical principles are the same:

- preserve ureteral blood supply,
- avoid excessive ureteral devascularization,
- spatulate adequately,
- create a tension-free watertight anastomosis,
- and stent temporarily when appropriate.<sup>[[3]](#ref3)[[15]](#ref15)[[16]](#ref16)[[17]](#ref17)</sup>

Perfusion assessment can help select viable tissue. Yeaman 2024 prospectively assessed 55 SPY-guided cases against 277 historical controls: patient-level strictures were 1/55 versus 31/277. Median follow-up was 17.5 versus 58.6 months, respectively. The association is favorable, but unequal follow-up and historical controls limit causal conclusions.<sup>[[17]](#ref17)</sup> A larger 2025 meta-analysis found fewer strictures with indocyanine-green-guided resection across 11 studies (1,339 patients), but graded certainty low; a separate four-study meta-analysis (732 patients) found no clear stricture difference.<sup>[[51]](#ref51)[[52]](#ref52)</sup>

The ureter is resected to viable tissue while preserving tension-free reach. Das 2024 found an association between greater distal resection and fewer strictures; Richards 2015 did not. Neither study establishes a universal resection target. See [Ileal Conduit — Distal Ureter: Perfusion and Reach](/docs/surgical-techniques/04c-urinary-diversion/ileal-conduit#distal-ureter-perfusion-and-reach).<sup>[[18]](#ref18)[[19]](#ref19)</sup>

For the management of established ureteroenteric anastomotic strictures (endoscopic, open, robotic, ICG-guided revision, ileal bypass), see [Ureteroenteric Anastomotic Stricture Repair](/docs/surgical-techniques/04d-upper-tract-reconstruction/reimplantation/ureteroenteric-anastomotic-revision); the repair operations are described in the upper tract reconstruction section.

## 6. Continence Mechanism Determines How the Patient Will Live With the Diversion

The continence mechanism differs fundamentally across diversion families.

### Orthotopic neobladder

Continence depends on the native urethral sphincter complex, reservoir behavior and emptying. Patients usually use timed voiding with pelvic-floor relaxation and abdominal pressure; some require intermittent catheterization, including patients initially expected to void spontaneously.<sup>[[15]](#ref15)[[20]](#ref20)[[21]](#ref21)[[50]](#ref50)</sup>

Evaluate urethral preservation before choosing an orthotopic neobladder. EAU makes a strong recommendation against orthotopic diversion when an invasive tumor is in the urethra or at the level of urethral dissection. Multifocal CIS, non-muscle-invasive prostatic urethral disease, or bladder-neck involvement should not automatically be equated with mandatory urethrectomy; exact extent, margins and surveillance determine candidacy. The AUA/ASCO/SUO guideline requires that a negative urethral margin be verified in every patient receiving an orthotopic diversion (Statement 14, Clinical Principle). Its discussion of Statement 11 states that women who do not receive a neobladder should undergo urethrectomy to reduce the likelihood of a positive margin or recurrence. See [Urethrectomy](/docs/surgical-techniques/04a-urethral-reconstruction/urethrectomy).<sup>[[22]](#ref22)[[35]](#ref35)</sup>

NCCN describes prostatic-duct CIS and a positive urethral margin as relative contraindications to urethral drainage. The AUA requirement to verify a negative urethral margin remains a distinct guideline position.<sup>[[50]](#ref50)[[35]](#ref35)</sup>

Gynecologic organ preservation at cystectomy is a functional, reconstructive-consequence consideration in women being evaluated for an orthotopic neobladder, not a primary oncologic recommendation. Preserving the uterus and vagina can maintain support for a neobladder and urethro-ileal alignment. In a retrospective series of women who had undergone hysterectomy, 7/19 (37%) required intermittent catheterization; neocystocele and urethral kinking were associated with retention, but the series did not compare organ-sparing procedures.<sup>[[36]](#ref36)[[22]](#ref22)</sup> Sparing the anterior vaginal wall also limits injury to the neurovascular bundles that run along the lateral vaginal walls, which has been associated with better-preserved sexual function.<sup>[[37]](#ref37)</sup> NCCN recommends preservation of the uterus, vagina or ovaries when feasible in appropriately selected patients (category 2A), without limiting eligibility to young, sexually active women.<sup>[[50]](#ref50)</sup> In a single-center series, hydronephrosis, a palpable mass and positive lymph nodes were associated with pathologic reproductive-organ involvement at cystectomy.<sup>[[38]](#ref38)</sup> The AUA/ASCO/SUO guideline allows gynecologic organ sparing to be considered in selected patients with early-stage disease.<sup>[[35]](#ref35)</sup> The 2026 EAU guideline strongly recommends sexual organ-preserving techniques in eligible women when the area to be preserved is free of tumor.<sup>[[22]](#ref22)</sup> Abnormal cervical screening, high-risk cervical intraepithelial neoplasia, or vaginal bleeding that could represent tumor involvement argue against organ preservation.

Vaginal complications after cystectomy are under-recognized. In a Medicare sample of 481 women 65 years or older undergoing cystectomy for any indication (83% with an incontinent conduit or catheterizable-channel diversion, not predominantly neobladder), 19.5% had at least one vaginal complication on record within 2 years, most often vaginal cuff dehiscence (10.2%); over the full study period, 21.2% of women were diagnosed with a vaginal complication and 26% of those received an intervention. A separate systematic review found neobladder-vaginal fistula, usually along the anterior vaginal wall at the neobladder-urethral anastomosis, in 3–6% of patients at higher-volume centers. These figures describe older, largely non-neobladder or mixed cohorts and do not estimate risk for a contemporary organ-sparing orthotopic reconstruction specifically.<sup>[[42]](#ref42)[[43]](#ref43)</sup>

The analogous functional, reconstructive-consequence consideration in men is nerve-sparing cystectomy, using the same neurovascular-bundle-preservation technique lateral to the prostatic capsule described for radical prostatectomy, to support erectile function recovery and continence with an orthotopic neobladder.<sup>[[39]](#ref39)</sup> Some centers have described prostate- or prostate-capsule-sparing cystectomy for the same functional goals, but this should be considered only in highly selected men because of the risk of missing incidental prostate adenocarcinoma or urothelial carcinoma involving the prostate: incidental prostate cancer has been found in up to a quarter of cystoprostatectomy specimens in some series, and urothelial involvement of the prostate carries its own oncologic implications.<sup>[[40]](#ref40)[[41]](#ref41)</sup> The current AUA/ASCO/SUO muscle-invasive bladder cancer guideline frames sexual-function-preserving technique, like gynecologic organ preservation in women, as something to discuss with appropriately selected, motivated patients rather than a default approach.<sup>[[35]](#ref35)</sup>

Prior radical prostatectomy for prostate cancer does not rule out a later orthotopic neobladder, but the vesicourethral-junction dissection can be difficult; intraoperative flexible cystoscopy can help identify the prior anastomosis and confirm removal of bladder tissue.<sup>[[44]](#ref44)</sup> In a single-center series of 24 men who underwent neobladder reconstruction after prior prostatectomy, 11 of 19 evaluable patients (57.9%) who had good continence (0–1 pad/day) after prostatectomy regained that level of control within a year of cystectomy; patients with poor or fair continence after prostatectomy more often needed an artificial urinary sphincter, and adjuvant radiotherapy after prostatectomy appeared to worsen the chance of regaining good continence. This selected, retrospective series does not by itself isolate the effect of the cystoscopy maneuver.<sup>[[45]](#ref45)</sup>

When a woman's catheterization ability is uncertain because of obesity or difficult anatomy and the diversion under consideration may require intermittent catheterization, having her demonstrate self-catheterization before the final decision is a practical assessment used by some surgeons, though it has not been formally validated.<sup>[[44]](#ref44)</sup>

For a man being considered for conversion from an existing cutaneous diversion to an orthotopic reservoir, preoperative urethroscopy has been described to assess urethral patency, visualize the membranous urethra, assess sphincter coaptation, and identify a retained prostatic apex or verumontanum; a retained apex may allow a more proximal urethral anastomosis, while its absence increases the chance that the patient will need an artificial urinary sphincter. If prostatic tissue remains, ongoing PSA monitoring and examination for prostate cancer surveillance is advised. This guidance comes from small, selected undiversion experience, not a validated selection rule.<sup>[[47]](#ref47)</sup> In one single-center cohort, 51 second or third urinary diversions were required in 48 of 1,614 cystectomy patients (3.2%) over a mean interval of 57 months; the need for repeat diversion was far less common after a cancer-related primary indication (1.8%) than after a non-oncologic indication (25%), and the series recorded 4 perioperative deaths and 1 case of short bowel syndrome among the repeat-diversion patients.<sup>[[46]](#ref46)</sup>

### Continent cutaneous diversion

Continence depends on a valve mechanism:

- ileocecal valve-based systems,
- intussuscepted nipple valves,
- or catheterizable-channel flap-valve systems.<sup>[[12]](#ref12)[[23]](#ref23)[[24]](#ref24)</sup>

### Ileal conduit and cutaneous ureterostomy

These are intentionally incontinent systems. Their success is judged by dependable drainage and low complication burden, not by urine storage.

The mechanism chosen is the one the patient can realistically manage for life.

## 7. Patient Selection Is as Important as Operative Technique

There is no universally best diversion. The correct diversion is the one that matches the patient’s oncologic status, anatomy, physiology, dexterity, cognition, support system, and goals.<sup>[[2]](#ref2)[[4]](#ref4)[[15]](#ref15)[[25]](#ref25)</sup>

### Questions that should be answered before choosing diversion

1. **Can the urethra be preserved safely?**
2. **Is the sphincter reliable enough for orthotopic diversion?**
3. **Can the patient catheterize if needed?**
4. **Can the patient tolerate the bowel segment and metabolic burden?**
5. **Would a simpler incontinent diversion actually serve this patient better?**

Age, comorbidity, renal function, hepatic function, prior radiation, bowel disease, and cognitive and manual capacity all shift the answer.<sup>[[2]](#ref2)[[4]](#ref4)[[15]](#ref15)[[25]](#ref25)</sup> The AUA/ASCO/SUO guideline states that ileal conduit, continent cutaneous and orthotopic neobladder diversion should all be discussed with a patient undergoing radical cystectomy (Statement 13, Clinical Principle). Its discussion lists the absolute contraindications to continent diversion as insufficient bowel length, inadequate motor function or psychological issues that prevent self-catheterization, inadequate renal or hepatic function (for example an eGFR below 45), cancer at the urethral margin (for a neobladder) and uncorrectable significant urethral stricture disease.<sup>[[35]](#ref35)</sup> In a multicenter cohort of 6,469 cystectomies, older age, comorbidity and advanced disease were associated with cutaneous ureterostomy and with less neobladder use, and ureterostomy use rose from 2% to 22% over two decades.<sup>[[3]](#ref3)</sup> In NSQIP data, continent diversion use declined over 2008–2022.<sup>[[4]](#ref4)</sup>

## 8. Anticipate Metabolic Consequences

Whenever bowel mucosa is exposed to urine, the reconstruction creates a chronic absorptive / secretory interface that changes serum chemistry and stone risk.<sup>[[7]](#ref7)[[11]](#ref11)[[26]](#ref26)</sup>

### The major long-term issues

- **Hyperchloremic metabolic acidosis** with ileal and colonic diversions
- **Vitamin B12 deficiency risk** depending on ileal loss and underlying absorption
- **Bone demineralization** from chronic acidosis
- **Urolithiasis** from low volume, altered pH, hypocitraturia, and other metabolic changes
- **Rare ammoniagenic encephalopathy** in susceptible patients, especially with hepatic dysfunction<sup>[[7]](#ref7)[[11]](#ref11)[[26]](#ref26)[[27]](#ref27)</sup>

These risks inform the choice of diversion and require lifelong monitoring.

## 9. Stoma Planning and Perioperative Optimization Are Part of the Reconstruction

For any diversion that ends in a stoma, stoma quality is not an afterthought. Preoperative site marking and patient education are associated with fewer stoma complications and better self-care in ostomy studies. ASCRS 2022 recommends marking by a trained provider and education by a specialized provider (both strong recommendations, moderate-quality evidence), and suggests a perioperative care pathway for new ileostomies to reduce readmission (weak recommendation, moderate-quality evidence). Its scope excludes urostomies, so these are fecal-stoma data.<sup>[[28]](#ref28)[[29]](#ref29)</sup>

The principles include:

- preoperative marking by an experienced WOC nurse,
- realistic counseling about appliance care or catheterization,
- ERAS-style bowel and postoperative management (EAU makes strong recommendations against preoperative bowel preparation, for fast-track measures to shorten time to bowel recovery, and for pharmacologic VTE prophylaxis starting the first day after surgery for at least four weeks),<sup>[[34]](#ref34)</sup>
- and proactive dehydration prevention in the early postoperative period.<sup>[[28]](#ref28)[[29]](#ref29)</sup>

Site marking, counseling and pathway design are associated with how well the diversion functions in daily life.

Intraoperative fluid strategy is also part of this package. In a single-center, double-blind randomized trial of 166 patients undergoing radical cystectomy and urinary diversion, restrictive deferred crystalloid combined with preemptive norepinephrine infusion reduced in-hospital complications compared with standard crystalloid infusion (52% vs 73%, relative risk 0.70), including large reductions in gastrointestinal and cardiac complications, and shortened median hospitalization (15 vs 17 days). At one year in the orthotopic-neobladder subgroup, daytime continence was 44/51 (86%) versus 27/42 (64%); erectile-function recovery among preoperatively potent patients was 26/33 (79%) versus 11/29 (38%) in the restrictive/norepinephrine and standard-fluid groups, respectively. This is one center's regimen and dosing; it supports restrictive, vasopressor-supported intraoperative fluid management as a component of the perioperative pathway rather than a fixed universal protocol.<sup>[[48]](#ref48)[[49]](#ref49)</sup>

## 10. Plan Lifelong Functional Follow-up

All urinary diversions require indefinite follow-up because many clinically important complications are silent until they are advanced.<sup>[[11]](#ref11)[[13]](#ref13)</sup>

After cystectomy for NMIBC or MIBC, NCCN specifies electrolytes and creatinine every 3–6 months in year 1, then annually in years 2–5 (category 2A).<sup>[[50]](#ref50)</sup> The AUA/ASCO/SUO guideline advises laboratory assessment every 3–6 months for 2–3 years and then annually after MIBC treatment (Statement 31, Expert Opinion).<sup>[[35]](#ref35)</sup> See [Renal Function and Metabolic Surveillance](/docs/evaluation/laboratory-studies/renal-function-metabolic-surveillance) for the source-specific follow-up schedules.

### Core surveillance domains

- **Upper-tract imaging** for hydronephrosis, stones, or silent obstruction
- **Renal function monitoring**
- **Electrolytes and bicarbonate**
- **Annual vitamin B12 measurement after bowel diversion**, advised in the EAU follow-up text (low levels reported in 17% of patients with bowel diversion; no strength rating)<sup>[[30]](#ref30)</sup>. The AUA/ASCO/SUO guideline discussion (Statement 31, Expert Opinion) advises checking B12 in follow-up because the risk of deficiency is increased after resection of more than 60 cm of ileum or use of the terminal ileum<sup>[[35]](#ref35)</sup>. NCCN post-cystectomy follow-up specifies annual B12 testing after the first year based on clinical judgment (category 2A)<sup>[[50]](#ref50)</sup>
- **Bone-health surveillance** when chronic acidosis risk is present
- **Reservoir / conduit imaging or endoscopy** when leaks, stones, strictures, or recurrent infection are suspected<sup>[[7]](#ref7)[[11]](#ref11)[[13]](#ref13)</sup>

EAU reports diversion-related complications in 45% of patients within five years, and in 94% of a 131-patient series surviving more than 15 years.<sup>[[30]](#ref30)</sup> Follow-up intensity is individualized to the reconstruction, renal risk and clinical change. Functional follow-up continues separately from oncologic follow-up. Routine cystoscopy is not a universal cancer-screening requirement for every asymptomatic bowel reservoir; investigate concerning symptoms and follow any disease-specific surveillance plan.

## 11. Shared Decision-Making Is a Reconstructive Principle, Not a Soft Add-On

Quality-of-life studies do not support a single universally superior diversion for every patient.<sup>[[15]](#ref15)[[31]](#ref31)[[32]](#ref32)[[33]](#ref33)</sup> Some patients prioritize body image and freedom from an appliance. Others prioritize simplicity, lower revision burden, or avoidance of self-catheterization.

The best diversion fits:

- the patient’s cancer,
- the patient’s physiology,
- the patient’s capabilities,
- and the patient’s preferences.<sup>[[2]](#ref2)[[15]](#ref15)[[33]](#ref33)</sup>

## Core Principles at a Glance

1. Choose the diversion family according to the actual clinical goal.
2. Use detubularization and appropriate geometry for the intended reservoir design.
3. Reconfigure the reservoir into a rounded low-pressure shape.
4. Match bowel segment choice to technical and metabolic needs.
5. Treat the ureteroenteric anastomosis as the vulnerability of the reconstruction.
6. Choose a continence mechanism the patient can realistically manage.
7. Select patients carefully for continent diversion.
8. Anticipate and monitor metabolic sequelae lifelong.
9. Optimize stoma planning and perioperative care as part of the reconstruction.
10. Commit to lifelong surveillance of the upper tracts, renal function, and reservoir.
11. Use shared decision-making to match the diversion to the patient.

Discuss open and robotic approaches in the context of center experience, patient needs and the planned diversion. EAU strongly recommends informing patients of the advantages and disadvantages of open and robotic cystectomy and selecting centers by experience rather than technique, and makes no recommendation for intracorporeal over extracorporeal diversion.<sup>[[34]](#ref34)</sup> See the dedicated [Intracorporeal Urinary Diversion (ICUD)](/docs/surgical-techniques/04c-urinary-diversion/intracorporeal-urinary-diversion) page for technique and outcomes.<sup>[[22]](#ref22)</sup>
