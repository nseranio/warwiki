# Right Colon Pouch

**Right-colon and ileocecal reservoirs** are a family of reconstructions. Some empty through a catheterizable skin stoma; others connect to the urethra. Reservoir shape, retained ileum, outlet mechanism and ureteral implantation determine their different functional and revision risks.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[2]](#ref2)[[3]](#ref3)</sup>

See [Urinary Diversion Principles](/docs/surgical-techniques/04c-urinary-diversion/urinary-diversion-principles) for patient selection. A large-capacity colon pouch is not automatically low pressure or continent, and it may still require intermittent catheterization.

## Why the Right Colon

The cecum and ascending colon provide a large, distensible segment that becomes a low-pressure reservoir when it is detubularized. The ileocecal valve region can be reinforced or the terminal ileum tapered to form a catheterizable outlet, as in the Indiana, Florida and Miami designs. The appendix can be embedded submucosally as an outlet: in the original Mainz appendix-stoma report of 13 patients, maximum closure pressure exceeded 80 cm H₂O on pressure profiles and one stoma stenosis needed reoperation.<sup>[[4]](#ref4)</sup> The colonic wall accepts a standard submucosal tunnel for antireflux ureteral implantation.<sup>[[2]](#ref2)</sup>

## Operative Principles

Detubularization and reconfiguration aim to provide compliant storage. Preserve the ileocolic or colonic mesenteric supply and confirm that the selected segment reaches its outlet without twisting or tension. Continence depends on the pouch outlet design; the native ileocecal valve may contribute when the ileal outlet is reinforced.<sup>[[2]](#ref2)[[4]](#ref4)</sup>

Distinguish three separate constructions in the operative record:

1. **Reservoir:** bowel segment, length and configuration, including any undetubularized inlet limb.
2. **Outlet:** tapered ileum, appendiceal flap-valve channel, intussuscepted nipple, another reconstructed channel, or urethral anastomosis.
3. **Ureteral implantation:** individual or conjoined implantation, refluxing or antireflux design, and any stents.

An antireflux tunnel must preserve perfusion and avoid obstruction. Pouch name alone does not identify its implantation technique. In Wiesner's long-term Mainz I comparison, anastomotic obstruction occurred in 59/809 renal units with submucosal implantation and 3/74 renal units with an extramural tunnel; the groups were unequal and observational.<sup>[[5]](#ref5)</sup>

If a reservoir is overloaded, the catheterizable stoma should remain the lowest-resistance outlet so it can vent pressure rather than risk pouch rupture; excessive outlet resistance can also make catheterization difficult. Simmons and Campbell describe placing a large-bore cecostomy tube in a dependent pouch segment for early irrigation of mucus or clots and suturing the reservoir to the abdominal wall at the exit site, which can leave the pouch fixed to the wall and permit later access through the existing cecostomy scar in selected retention cases; this is conditional on a previously placed and fixed cecostomy, not a default puncture route for every reservoir.<sup>[[23]](#ref23)</sup>

### What the Larger Follow-up Reports Show

The Mainz experience of more than 800 patients reported 92.8% overall continence over a mean follow-up of 7.6 years. Stomal stenosis occurred in 23.5% of appendix stomas and 15.3% of ileal nipples. Stones occurred in 10.8% of reservoirs with an ileal nipple and 5.6% with an appendix stoma. Eleven patients (eight appendix, three nipple) needed reoperation for ischemic degeneration of the continence mechanism. These subgroup comparisons are retrospective, not randomized.<sup>[[7]](#ref7)</sup> In the companion ureteral report, serum creatinine was 1.6 mg/dL or lower at last follow-up in 97% of patients with submucosal tunnel implantation and 98% with an extramural tunnel.<sup>[[5]](#ref5)</sup>

The pediatric Mainz series operated on 70 patients and followed 65, assessing 118 renal units; 113 units were stable or improved. Complete continence was 97%, and revisions were needed for stoma stenosis in 23% and pouch calculi in 15%. The 113/118 upper-tract result concerns renal units; continence and the stoma and stone revisions concern the 65 followed patients.<sup>[[8]](#ref8)</sup>

The Florida pouch uses a detubularized right-colon reservoir with a tapered ileal limb reinforced at the ileocecal valve and directly anastomosed ureters. The report began with 179 patients, but its long-term analysis included 74 patients followed for a mean of 133 months; the other 105 had died or were lost, so late function reflects survivor selection and is not follow-up of all 179. Outlet incontinence occurred in 6.7%, stomal stenosis in 4% and reservoir stones in 5.4%. Ureteral obstruction occurred in 7 of 108 primary reimplantations and 4 of 14 irradiated ureters (p = 0.02). Severe acidosis developed in 5.5% and low vitamin B12 in 4%. Metabolic alterations occurred in 58% of patients converted from another diversion, compared with 6.4% of the others (p = 0.0001).<sup>[[9]](#ref9)</sup>

Using standardized complication reporting, Myers followed 53 right-colon pouch patients for a mean of 30 months: 22% had major complications (Clavien-Dindo grade 3 or higher) within 90 days, 45% were readmitted within 90 days, 53% underwent a reoperation and 32% a major reoperation during follow-up. Diabetes was associated with more complications and reoperation, and higher body mass index and prior abdominal surgery with readmission. The series was too small to establish causal risk factors.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup>

### Miami Pouch

The Miami pouch uses the terminal 15 cm of ileum and the right colon. The distal ileum is tapered over a 14 Fr catheter, the ileocecal valve is reinforced with three circumferential pursestring silk sutures, and the ureters are anastomosed to the colon without a tunnel. In the original 75-patient report (follow-up 7 to 69 months), continence was 98.6% and ureterocolonic anastomotic success was 90%. Early perioperative complications occurred in 26% and late complications (beyond six weeks) in 23%, and there were five perioperative deaths.<sup>[[21]](#ref21)</sup>

The 2021 Baboudjian series included 15 patients, with median follow-up of 39 months. Thirteen were continent and two reported nighttime leakage with catheterization intervals of 5 to 7 hours. Two patients had a Clavien III complication within 30 days. No efferent-tube stenosis or catheterization difficulty was reported. The series is too small to estimate complication rates.<sup>[[10]](#ref10)</sup>

The Salom report included 90 patients treated from 1988–2002, predominantly in an exenteration or radiation setting. “Fifteen years' experience” describes the program's accrual period rather than fifteen-year follow-up of each patient. Ninety-one percent had received prior radiation. Continence was 93%. Reservoir-related complications affected 53%, most commonly urinary infection (40%), ureteral stricture (20%) and difficult catheterization (18%), and conservative treatment resolved more than 80% of them. Postoperative death occurred in 11%. The selection and extent of pelvic surgery limit comparison with routine cystectomy cohorts.<sup>[[11]](#ref11)</sup>

### Lundiana Pouch

The Lundiana pouch combines a detubularized right-colon reservoir with a stapled ileal outlet. In a 30-patient randomized comparison (15 per outlet), leakage episodes were more common with the stapled outlet, while catheterization difficulty was more common with the intussuscepted nipple; resting outlet pressures were similar.<sup>[[25]](#ref25)</sup> A third-party follow-up of 193 patients reported 91% continence (172/188) at a median follow-up of 13 years, and 105 patients (54%) had undergone at least one reoperation, most often for uretero-intestinal stricture (28 patients, 15%). Outlet revision was needed in 16% to maintain continence and catheter access or treat stomal stenosis.<sup>[[22]](#ref22)</sup> Reoperation was more frequent after surgery for benign indications than for malignancy (60% vs 51%, p = 0.04) and in the earlier half of the study period (62% vs 47%, p = 0.03). Independent third-party review found 10 postoperative complications, 17 reoperations and 7 pyelonephritis admissions that the primary chart review had not captured. These are whole-cohort outcomes in a mixed benign-and-malignant, observational population, not results specific to women.<sup>[[22]](#ref22)</sup>

## Orthotopic Right-colon or Ileocolonic Reservoirs

Orthotopic reconstruction requires a suitable urethra or outlet and willingness to use catheterization if emptying is inadequate. Colon-based and ileal reservoirs cannot be ranked solely by continence rates from separate series.

- **Le Bag:** a stapled ileocolonic orthotopic design with several modifications; see the [dedicated page](/docs/surgical-techniques/04c-urinary-diversion/le-bag) for construction and the actual continence definitions.
- **B-bladder:** a Le Bag modification with a Studer-type ileal chimney for the ureteral anastomoses, reported in 29 patients with median follow-up of 3.4 years. Late recurrent urinary infection occurred in 17% and uretero-neobladder stricture in 3%.<sup>[[12]](#ref12)</sup>
- **D'Orazio right-colonic neobladder:** detubularized, remodeled right colon with an intact cecal segment, reported in 38 patients operated between 1993 and 2004 (the eleven years describe accrual, not follow-up of each patient). Continence came from a low-pressure reservoir of more than 600 mL and haustral contractions of the intact segment that eject urine toward the detubularized portion. Voiding used abdominal tension and mass contractions of the intact segment above 100 cm H₂O. Daytime continence was immediate in 37 of 38 (all by 30 days), nighttime continence was total and immediate in 36 of 38, with satisfactory nighttime continence in the other two, and mean maximum flow exceeded 26 mL/s. One patient needed clean intermittent catheterization. D'Orazio's single-center series had no ileal-reservoir comparator.<sup>[[13]](#ref13)</sup>

The Bedük comparison included 19 ileocecal (Mainz) and 36 ileal (Abol-Enein and Ghoneim) neobladder patients. Only 39 underwent the 12-month urodynamic evaluation. Follow-up differed and the groups were small and nonrandomized.<sup>[[14]](#ref14)</sup>

| Parameter | Ileocecal | Ileal |
|---|---|---|
| Capacity | 426 ± 34 mL | 442 ± 27 mL |
| Maximum flow | 19.6 ± 3.7 mL/s | 16 ± 6.1 mL/s |
| Residual volume | 37 ± 8.2 mL | 45 ± 7.1 mL |
| Daytime incontinence | 5.3% | 5.5% |
| Nighttime incontinence (twice weekly or more) | 21% | 8.4% |
| Ureterointestinal stenosis | 10.5% | 5.7% |
| Pyelonephritis | 15.8% | 13.8% |

Urodynamic parameters did not differ significantly.<sup>[[14]](#ref14)</sup>

The Cochrane review of intestinal reconstruction found only five randomized trials involving 355 participants, of small size and moderate or poor quality. It found no evidence that bladder replacement was better than conduit diversion after cystectomy for cancer, and no statistically significant difference in daytime or nocturnal incontinence between ileocolonic or ileocecal and ileal replacement in a two-trial meta-analysis. One small trial suggested less nocturnal incontinence with ileum, of uncertain clinical significance.<sup>[[15]](#ref15)</sup>

## Choosing the Outlet and Comparing Quality of Life

An appendiceal channel, tapered ileal limb and reconstructed neo-appendix have different reach and revision issues. Cheng's 58-patient comparison contained 33 Indiana, 15 appendico-umbilical and 10 neo-appendico-umbilical diversions, with baseline age and comorbidity and treatment differences. After a median follow-up of 21 months, the appendico-umbilical and neo-appendico-umbilical groups had more high-grade diversion-related complications than the Indiana group, and the neo-appendico group had more stoma complications (70% vs 30%). Most patients in all groups rated quality of life as good to excellent. The groups were not comparable, so the study does not rank channels.<sup>[[16]](#ref16)</sup>

A gender-matched comparison of 45 Mainz pouch I (ileal nipple) and 45 ileal-conduit pairs found no difference in stool incontinence. Mainz pouch patients more often reported increased stool frequency (53% vs 31%), softer stool (60% vs 13%) and diarrhea (62% vs 20%), and less constipation (4% vs 22%). They also reported impaired overall postoperative quality of life more often (51% vs 29%, p = 0.024). Changes in bowel habit belong in preoperative counseling. Selection also weighs catheterization burden, stoma preference and the possibility of revision.<sup>[[17]](#ref17)</sup>

## Follow-up and Late Complications

Follow storage and emptying, catheter access, continence, mucus and stone problems, renal function, electrolytes including bicarbonate, and upper-tract imaging. After cystectomy with bowel diversion, measure vitamin B12 annually, as stated in the 2026 EAU functional follow-up text.<sup>[[26]](#ref26)</sup> The frequency of investigations should reflect renal reserve, prior obstruction, biochemical abnormalities and symptoms; a fixed annual pouchogram or arterial blood gas is not necessary for every stable patient.

Ileal resection and urine-exposed bowel create risks of B12 deficiency and hyperchloremic acidosis. In the Mainz metabolic follow-up study, 94 of 458 original patients were examined after a median of nine years; B12 supplementation was recommended in 32% because levels were low-normal or low; clinical examinations found no signs of megaloblastic anemia, funicular myelosis or osteoporosis. A total of 37% were still taking sodium/potassium citrate to prevent acidosis. Bone density was measured in only 18 patients.<sup>[[18]](#ref18)</sup>

A retrospective comparison of 155 patients included only 20 ileocecal pouches, alongside 107 conduits and 28 ileal neobladders. Renal decline did not differ significantly between the diversions. The small ileocecal group limits this comparison.<sup>[[19]](#ref19)</sup>

Promptly assess new hematuria, recurrent symptomatic infection, difficult catheterization, flank pain, hydronephrosis or worsening renal function. In a German multicenter analysis of 17,758 diversions, secondary-tumor prevalence was 1.29% in orthotopic (ileo-)colonic neobladders versus 0.05% in ileal neobladders (p = 0.0001) and 0.14% in ileocecal pouches. The authors advised regular endoscopic evaluation of orthotopic (ileo-)colonic neobladders, which they judged probably at increased risk, from at least the fifth postoperative year, and endoscopy of catheterizable ileocecal pouches when hematuria, chronic urinary infection or hydronephrosis develops.<sup>[[27]](#ref27)</sup> Acute abdominal pain with reduced reservoir drainage or systemic illness raises concern for obstruction or perforation. Medication and irrigation details are maintained in the [mucus](/docs/foundations/pharmacology/urinary-diversion-specific/mucus-management), [B12](/docs/foundations/pharmacology/urinary-diversion-specific/vitamin-b12-supplementation) and [alkali](/docs/foundations/pharmacology/urinary-diversion-specific/urinary-acidifiers) companion pages.
