# Camey II Detubularized Orthotopic Ileal Neobladder

The **Camey II** is a detubularized, U-shaped ileal reservoir connected to the native urethra. Reports from the Camey group describe its use from 1987, followed by a 57-patient initial series and a 110-patient update. It developed the original tubular Camey reservoir into a configuration intended to improve storage and nighttime control.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[2]](#ref2)</sup>

The Le Duc–Camey ureteral implantation is a separate technique that has also been used in other diversions, including the Kock pouch and Hautmann's ileal neobladder.<sup>[[8]](#ref8)[[23]](#ref23)[[24]](#ref24)</sup> Selection, safe emptying, renal monitoring and cancer follow-up apply regardless of the reservoir; see [Urinary Diversion Principles](/docs/surgical-techniques/04c-urinary-diversion/urinary-diversion-principles).<sup>[[3]](#ref3)</sup>

## From Camey I to Camey II

The Camey I used intact, non-detubularized U-shaped ileum; Camey's group reviewed 84 patients over 25 years.<sup>[[22]](#ref22)</sup> Preserved bowel contractions and limited storage were the main problems. In Roehrborn's 14 men, mean pressure at capacity was 37 cm H₂O, mean capacity 362 mL and the voiding interval 2.2 hours. Nocturnal enuresis occurred in every patient, and segmental and peristaltic contraction waves persisted at 2 years. Reflux was prevented in 86% of renal units, and metabolic acidosis occurred in 43% of patients.<sup>[[4]](#ref4)</sup> Enuresis was not universal in every series. In a ten-patient urodynamic study, all but 2 neobladders had contractions at 50 to 200 mL, and the 2 men who were continent by day and night had none.<sup>[[5]](#ref5)</sup>

Detubularization interrupts coordinated tubular contractions and permits a wider reservoir. It does not eliminate smooth muscle, guarantee low pressure or compensate for an incompetent outlet. Carini's nonrandomized comparison of 35 Camey I and 29 Studer reconstructions (mean follow-up 27 and 13.6 months) found smaller capacity, higher pressure, shorter voiding intervals, near-uniform enuresis and more reflux with the Camey I, and a slight metabolic acidosis in both groups that was treated with oral alkalinization. It compared different operations and was not a randomized Camey I–II trial.<sup>[[6]](#ref6)</sup>

The 1996 Camey II report reviewed 110 patients treated during 1987–1991 and described improved capacity and nighttime control relative to the earlier Camey I experience. Historical comparisons support the design rationale but do not define outcomes for contemporary patients.<sup>[[2]](#ref2)</sup>

## Operative Framework

1. **Select a suitable ileal segment and confirm reach.** Preserve its mesenteric blood supply and ensure that the future dependent outlet reaches the urethra without tension or twisting. Reported ileal lengths differ among modifications: 30 cm for the U-shaped neobladders in Minervini's comparison, 45 cm for the Perugia Y and 70 cm for Hautmann's original W reservoir.<sup>[[19]](#ref19)[[15]](#ref15)[[24]](#ref24)</sup>
2. **Isolate the planned segment and restore bowel continuity.** Preserve appropriate terminal ileum and verify mesenteric orientation.
3. **Detubularize along the antimesenteric border.** Arrange the opened segment in a U configuration and approximate the medial edges to form the posterior plate.
4. **Implant the ureters using the selected technique.** The classic reports use Le Duc–Camey mucosal implantation; later modifications use other antireflux or refluxing anastomoses.
5. **Create the dependent urethral anastomosis and close the reservoir.** Maintain a tension-free outlet, watertight closure and reliable drainage. The closure sequence depends on the chosen modification.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[2]](#ref2)[[7]](#ref7)</sup>

The early stapled Camey II series (GIA staplers) was intended to save time and give watertight suture lines. It did not randomize stapled versus hand-sewn reservoirs or establish superiority in operative time, leakage or long-term safety.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup>

Muto's stapled modification with a serous-lined extramural tunnel included 43 men followed for a median 38 months. One reflux event and two ureteroileal strictures were reported. Neobladder construction plus ureteral implantation took about 1 hour 45 minutes, not the entire cystectomy. The authors reported a 6.9% complication rate for the reconstruction.<sup>[[7]](#ref7)</sup>

## Le Duc–Camey Ureteral Implantation

A sulcus is created in the ileal mucosa, and the spatulated ureter is laid in the sulcus and sutured to its edges. The original authors described progressive mucosal growth over the ureter as part of the antireflux mechanism. This differs from the serous-lined extramural trough formed between adjacent bowel walls. Ureteral perfusion is preserved, and angulation or a constricting implantation is avoided.<sup>[[8]](#ref8)[[9]](#ref9)</sup>

Historical reports used different populations and should remain separate:

| Report | Population and result |
|---|---|
| Le Duc, Camey and Teillac, *J Urol* 1987 | 51 patients and 97 ureters followed for 3–8 years; reported antireflux effectiveness of 85% and stenosis of 1.5%.<sup>[[8]](#ref8)</sup> |
| Same group, *Ann Urol* 1987 | A separate report described 260 implanted ureters, 85% effectiveness and stenosis below 2%. It is not the same 97-ureter denominator.<sup>[[9]](#ref9)</sup> |
| Lugagne 1997 | Retrospective series of 158 men, comprising 109 tubular and 49 detubularized reservoirs, with 313 implantations; 123 men had at least two years of follow-up. Reported anastomotic stenosis was 4.9% in that follow-up group.<sup>[[10]](#ref10)</sup> |

Lugagne observed no obstruction after two years in that cohort; all strictures were at the anastomotic site, 3.7% of patients needed ureteral reimplantation and 2% needed nephrectomy for chronic obstruction. That observation does not exclude other late complications, recurrent cancer or later deterioration, so long-term follow-up continues.<sup>[[10]](#ref10)[[11]](#ref11)</sup>

### Comparative evidence

- **Shaaban 1992 randomized 40 men** with normal preoperative upper tracts to an intussuscepted nipple or Le Duc implantation. The nipple group had no observed reflux or radiographic dilation during 6–18 months of follow-up. In the Le Duc group, 12 of 38 renal units (31%) dilated: 11 from stenosis and one from reflux. The short follow-up cannot establish long-term nipple-valve superiority.<sup>[[12]](#ref12)</sup>
- **Yamamoto 1995** applied the Camey–Le Duc technique to Kock pouches in 41 patients (78 renal units; 30 cutaneous, 11 urethral). At a mean of 23 months, 73 renal units (93.6%) had no dilatation and no patient had reflux. Dilatation was more frequent at 6 months (4 of 23 units) than at 12 months or later (1 of 55).<sup>[[23]](#ref23)</sup>
- **Minervini 1998** compared Studer (Nesbit implantation), W-shaped and U-shaped (Le Duc) ileal neobladders in 45 men. All Nesbit anastomoses refluxed at full filling, as did 15 of 50 Le Duc implantations. Double-reconfiguration reservoirs (Studer and W) had larger capacity and lower maximum pressure than the single-fold U reservoirs at 3, 6 and 12 months, with better continence and longer voiding intervals at 12 months.<sup>[[19]](#ref19)</sup>
- **Hassan 2007** compared direct Nesbit implantation in 58 patients with Le Duc in 62 patients receiving a Y-neobladder. Mean follow-up was 72 months (range 36–144). Six Le Duc patients developed a unilateral stricture versus none in the direct group; overall reflux did not differ significantly. A subgroup with dilated ureters favored Le Duc for reflux, but this nonrandomized comparison does not establish the best technique for every ureter.<sup>[[13]](#ref13)</sup>
- **USC-STAR** provides the larger randomized antireflux comparison: 484 patients randomized to Studer or T-pouch, 423 with baseline outcome data and 260 with three-year renal data. The T-pouch showed no significant renal benefit and had more diversion-related intervention. This compared two specific reservoirs; it did not test Le Duc–Camey against every refluxing anastomosis.<sup>[[14]](#ref14)</sup>

## Functional Outcomes and Their Limits

The initial 57-patient Camey II series had only 3–24 months of follow-up. There was no operative mortality and 5 perioperative complications. Reported combined day/night continence improved from 50% at three months to 90% at six months, without a stated pad definition or denominator at each visit.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup>

The **Perugia ileal neobladder** is a specified modification using 45 cm of detubularized ileum in a vertical Y shape. Its 237-patient report had a median follow-up of 64 months; only 20 patients reached ten years. The 5-year overall survival was 64% and perioperative mortality 1.6%; grade III–IV early complications occurred in 27 patients. Day/night continence was 93.5%/83.9%, and all patients emptied completely. The most frequent late complications were reflux, urolithiasis and urethral anastomotic stricture. These selected institutional outcomes do not represent the classic Camey II alone.<sup>[[15]](#ref15)</sup>

The **Fontana Y-neobladder** is another distinct reconstruction. Its 50-patient technical series reported a 15–20 minute reconstruction time and one ureteroenteric stricture (1% of renal units), with day/night continence good or satisfactory in 90%/85%. The series was uncontrolled and does not show comparative superiority.<sup>[[16]](#ref16)</sup>

### Organ-preserving cystectomy reports

Terrone's reports combined the reservoir with highly selected supra-ampullar cystectomy, preserving sexual structures. The 2004 cohort included 28 men with 26 Camey II and two Camey I reservoirs (median follow-up 90.5 months). Potency was preserved in 26 (92.8%) and antegrade ejaculation in 15 (53.5%); 16 of 27 evaluable patients had day and night continence, and one Camey I was converted to a conduit for excessive capacity, high residual urine and pyelonephritis. The 2006 update included 31 men, adding three Y reservoirs (median follow-up 95 months): 10-year cause-specific survival was 76.7%, potency was preserved in 28 (90.3%), and antegrade ejaculation in 15 (48.3%). The two reports overlap and should not be pooled, and the sexual outcomes reflect the cystectomy technique as much as the reservoir. They do not establish oncologic equivalence with standard cystectomy.<sup>[[17]](#ref17)[[18]](#ref18)</sup>

### Urodynamic interpretation

A published capacity or pressure is a measurement in a particular cohort, not a normal range for a named pouch. Continence also depends on outlet competence, filling behavior and emptying. In the 1998 U/W/Studer comparison, the reservoirs differed in ileal length (30, 40 and 60 cm) and in ureteral implantation method, so the better results with double reconfiguration cannot be attributed to folding alone.<sup>[[19]](#ref19)</sup>

Koraitim's study compared 33 intact ileocecal, 11 detubularized sigmoid and ten detubularized ileal reservoirs. With time, capacity increased in all three groups, but the intact ileocecal reservoirs showed rising pressure and persistent involuntary contractions, whereas the detubularized sigmoid and ileal reservoirs showed falling pressure and fewer contractions. The authors attributed the capacity increase to overdistension, which was greater in detubularized (109% to 112%) than in tubular (79%) segments. The study was not a direct Camey I–II comparison, and differences in bowel segment and configuration limit generalization to other neobladders.<sup>[[20]](#ref20)</sup>

## Complications and Follow-Up

Early morbidity of cystectomy and bowel reconstruction includes infection, leakage, ileus and systemic complications. The Leandri series reported no operative mortality and 5 perioperative complications in 57 patients; the Perugia series reported perioperative mortality of 1.6%, mostly grade I–II early complications and grade III–IV complications in 27 of 237 patients.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[15]](#ref15)</sup> Small historical series reporting few events do not establish a low contemporary complication rate. Late problems include ureteroenteric and urethral-outlet stricture, retention, stones, infection, mucus obstruction, metabolic acidosis and nutritional deficiency.<sup>[[10]](#ref10)[[15]](#ref15)[[11]](#ref11)</sup>

Follow-up should address:

- **Emptying and continence.** Scheduled voiding, residual urine, catheterization needs, leakage and mucus/drainage care. Detubularization does not eliminate retention or nighttime leakage.
- **Renal and upper-tract status.** Serum creatinine/eGFR, electrolytes and appropriate imaging for obstruction or other deterioration.
- **Metabolic and nutritional effects.** Bicarbonate and annual vitamin B12 measurement after bowel diversion. Acidosis occurred in 43% of Camey I patients in Roehrborn's series, and a slight acidosis treated with oral alkalinization was reported in Carini's Camey I and Studer groups.<sup>[[11]](#ref11)[[4]](#ref4)[[6]](#ref6)</sup>
- **Cancer and retained urethra:** stage- and risk-appropriate surveillance after cystectomy. The AUA/ASCO/SUO guideline states that patients should undergo laboratory assessment at three to six month intervals for two to three years and then annually (Expert Opinion), and that clinicians should monitor the urethral remnant for recurrence after cystectomy with a retained urethra (Expert Opinion).<sup>[[21]](#ref21)</sup>

See [Renal function and metabolic surveillance](/docs/evaluation/laboratory-studies/renal-function-metabolic-surveillance), [Vitamin B12 supplementation](/docs/foundations/pharmacology/urinary-diversion-specific/vitamin-b12-supplementation), [Urinary acidifiers & alkalinizers](/docs/foundations/pharmacology/urinary-diversion-specific/urinary-acidifiers), and [Mucus management](/docs/foundations/pharmacology/urinary-diversion-specific/mucus-management).

## Place Among Contemporary Reconstructions

Camey II demonstrated the functional value of detubularization. Hautmann's ileal neobladder (70 cm of ileum opened along the antimesenteric border, ureters implanted by the Le Duc–Camey method; 11 patients, pressure below 30 cm H₂O) extended the same principle.<sup>[[24]](#ref24)</sup> In Minervini's comparison, double-reconfiguration reservoirs had larger capacity and lower pressure than single-fold U-shaped reservoirs, and the Perugia and Y-neobladders are later modifications of the Camey II.<sup>[[19]](#ref19)[[15]](#ref15)[[16]](#ref16)</sup> Technique selection cannot be based on unadjusted tables combining pressure, continence and complications from unrelated series, and historical comparisons do not show that one reservoir replaced another for a single physiological reason.<sup>[[2]](#ref2)[[3]](#ref3)</sup>
