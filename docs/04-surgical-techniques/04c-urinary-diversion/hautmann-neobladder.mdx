# Hautmann W-Configuration Ileal Neobladder

The **Hautmann neobladder** is an orthotopic ileal reservoir arranged in a W configuration, detubularized and connected to the native urethra. The original 1988 publication described 11 patients and a 70 cm ileal segment opened along the antimesenteric border and fashioned into a spherical pouch, with Le Duc–Camey ureteral implantation. Videourodynamic studies showed pressures below 30 cm H₂O and no reflux. Eight of the 11 patients were dry day and night and three had grade I stress incontinence.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup> Subsequent modifications differ in bowel length, outlet construction and ureteral implantation.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[2]](#ref2)</sup>

The Ulm reports represent a large institutional experience accumulated over decades. They do not represent more than 1,000 patients each followed for 35 years: the 2011 late-complication analysis included 923 patients with a median 72 months of follow-up; the 2021 functional report selected 259 recurrence-free men with a median 121 months of follow-up.<sup>[[3]](#ref3)[[4]](#ref4)</sup>

## Selection and Planning

Discuss orthotopic, continent cutaneous and conduit options in the context of kidney/liver function, bowel suitability, cancer extent, sphincter function, the ability to empty or catheterize and patient priorities. Verify a negative urethral margin before orthotopic reconstruction. Patients must be able and willing to undertake the required self-care, including intermittent catheterization when necessary. Age alone is not an absolute contraindication.<sup>[[5]](#ref5)[[6]](#ref6)</sup>

Inadequate renal or hepatic function, insufficient usable bowel, an uncorrectable urethral stricture and inability to manage a continent diversion may preclude reconstruction. EAU treats high-dose pelvic radiotherapy, complex urethral stricture and severe sphincter-related incontinence as relative contraindications. A historical label of any urethral disease or any leakage is too broad to decide eligibility. See [Urinary Diversion Principles](/docs/surgical-techniques/04c-urinary-diversion/urinary-diversion-principles) for detailed selection.<sup>[[5]](#ref5)[[6]](#ref6)</sup>

## Anatomy and Design

- **Detubularized ileum.** Opening the bowel along the antimesenteric border interrupts coordinated tubular contractions. It does not remove smooth muscle or guarantee safe pressures.
- **Four-limb W configuration.** Adjacent edges are joined into a plate and the remaining edges are closed into a rounded reservoir.
- **Dependent urethral outlet.** The reservoir must reach the retained urethra without mesenteric tension, twisting or excessive outlet angulation.
- **Ureteral drainage.** The original report used Le Duc–Camey implantation. A refluxing chimney or a serous-lined extramural tunnel is a distinct modification; the W shape does not itself determine the reflux mechanism.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[7]](#ref7)[[8]](#ref8)</sup>

A rounded reservoir has a favorable volume-to-surface relationship. For an ideal thin-walled sphere, P = 2T/r at a given wall tension and volume varies with r³; doubling the radius gives eight times the volume. These relationships do not account for the changing wall tension, compliance, active contractions or geometry of a biological reservoir and cannot establish a safe storage pressure.

## Operative Framework

1. **Select the bowel and confirm reach.** Preserve mesenteric blood supply and choose a segment that reaches the urethra without tension. The original 70 cm design and shorter published modifications are different constructions, not mandatory lengths for every patient.
2. **Isolate the planned segment and restore bowel continuity.** Preserve appropriate terminal ileum and confirm mesenteric orientation.
3. **Detubularize the reservoir segment.** Open along the antimesenteric border; leave any deliberately planned chimney intact.
4. **Form the W plate.** Approximate adjacent limbs and suture their edges to construct the posterior reservoir wall.
5. **Construct the chosen ureteral anastomosis.** Maintain ureteral perfusion and avoid tension or kinking. Select the technique deliberately rather than combining incompatible steps from different modifications.
6. **Create the dependent urethral outlet and close the reservoir.** Achieve a tension-free pouch-to-urethra anastomosis and watertight reservoir closure. The sequence varies with approach and modification. If the pouch will not reach the urethra without tension, a described rescue sequence loosens the retractor, flattens sacral support and adjusts the table to rotate the pelvic floor upward, elevates the perineum with a finger or sponge stick, further mobilizes the cecum and descending colon if needed, and moves the outlet toward the tip of the U-shaped ileal flap; if these steps still leave tension, tubularizing the flap for a direct end-to-end anastomosis has been described, while rough traction on or incisions into the reservoir mesentery are avoided. This sequence is one surgeon's attributed technique; a quoted "about 10%" difficulty rate for this step could not be traced to a printed source and should not be repeated as a validated figure.<sup>[[31]](#ref31)</sup>
7. **Confirm drainage and leak-testing findings.** Document stents, pouch drainage and the postoperative plan. Catheter caliber and removal timing follow the actual reconstruction and the healing assessment.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[2]](#ref2)[[9]](#ref9)</sup>

### Chimney modification

An intact ileal limb provides reach for ureteral implantation and may simplify later access. Lippert–Theodorescu's three-patient report described a 5–10 cm or longer chimney when needed for a short ureter. Hollowell's 50-patient series used an 8–12 cm isoperistaltic chimney. Hollowell noted that the anterior position of the ureters and the identifiable isoperistaltic limb made open revision of the ureterointestinal anastomosis easier. These dimensions and variants should not be merged into one universal design.<sup>[[10]](#ref10)[[7]](#ref7)</sup>

Direct ureteral implantation in the chimney is refluxing. Isoperistalsis is not proof of an effective valve. Hollowell reported six strictures in 100 ureteral units and one-year good daytime/nighttime continence of 93%/86%; this was an uncontrolled series, and the four-year experience in its title is not four-year follow-up of every patient.<sup>[[7]](#ref7)</sup>

### Short-segment modification

Sevin's series used a 40 cm reservoir in 124 men operated on over ten years. Mean maximum capacity was 550 mL (range 310–720) and mean maximum pressure 26.4 cm H₂O. Day and night continence were 92% and 90% after four years. The series also reported mild-to-moderate metabolic acidosis in 58% and six deaths within 30 days (two unrelated to the diversion). It does not demonstrate that using less bowel prevents metabolic complications or makes the operation safer than other designs.<sup>[[11]](#ref11)</sup>

### Robotic intracorporeal construction

Small technical series establish feasibility. In Zhou's 40-patient single-surgeon report, functional assessment was available for 30 patients at six months and 20 at 12 months. Median follow-up was 14 months, median operative time 320 minutes (range 230–500) and estimated blood loss 300 mL (range 100–2,000). The 90-day complication rate was 45%, with high-grade complications in 10%. The 90% satisfactory daytime-continence rate at each visit is not a 40-patient complete follow-up estimate or necessarily a pad-free rate. Nighttime satisfactory continence was 76.7% at six months and 80.0% at 12 months, and one patient used clean intermittent catheterization.<sup>[[12]](#ref12)</sup>

The 2024 systematic review and atlas identified 19 studies describing nine intracorporeal configurations, but no eligible randomized or comparative cohort studies comparing configurations. Reported perioperative results were broadly similar across series, with important reporting variability; this is insufficient to establish equivalence or a preferred robotic pouch.<sup>[[13]](#ref13)</sup>

## Functional Outcomes

Continence rates depend on the definition, patient selection and the number actually assessed. Separate no pads, a safety pad, daytime control and nighttime control.

| Study population | Relevant findings and limits |
|---|---|
| Ulm 1999: 363 men, 290 functionally evaluable | Good daytime and satisfactory nighttime continence were reported by 95.9% and 95%; the adverse category was more than one pad, so these are not strict pad-free rates. Some form of intermittent catheterization was used by 3.9% of evaluable patients.<sup>[[14]](#ref14)</sup> |
| Ulm 2021: 259 men with more than five years of complete follow-up, excluding recurrence, irradiation and undiversion | Median follow-up 121 months, range 60–267. Reported day/night continence was 90%/82%, whereas pad-free rates were 71%/47%. Overall, 87% voided spontaneously and without residual urine.<sup>[[4]](#ref4)</sup> |
| W-pouch with refluxing short afferent limb: 64 patients surviving at least five years | Reported 75% daytime and 55.3% nighttime pad-free rates among those voiding spontaneously; 12.5% required intermittent or permanent catheterization. Outcomes depend on both the definition and survivor selection.<sup>[[15]](#ref15)</sup> |

Function often improves during the first postoperative year, but long-term deterioration, retention and leakage remain possible. The results in selected survivors beyond 20 years in the Ulm report do not prove continuous improvement or that every patient will eventually stop catheterizing, using pads or having mucus obstruction.<sup>[[4]](#ref4)[[16]](#ref16)</sup>

### Emptying and urodynamic assessment

The ileal reservoir retains smooth muscle but does not have a native bladder's coordinated detrusor emptying mechanism. Emptying generally requires outlet relaxation and abdominal pressure; some patients need catheterization. Assess residual urine and investigate a change in emptying rather than attributing all difficulty to adaptation.<sup>[[6]](#ref6)[[9]](#ref9)</sup>

Capacity, compliance, filling pressure, flow and residual urine must be interpreted together with symptoms and upper-tract findings. Values from separate small cohorts are not normal ranges. A 1998 nonrandomized comparison used different bowel lengths and implantation techniques in its Studer, W and U groups; it cannot isolate W-folding as the cause of superior continence or pressure at every time point.<sup>[[17]](#ref17)</sup>

## Complications and Upper-Tract Protection

Cystectomy and neobladder construction carry substantial early and late morbidity. The 2011 Ulm analysis included 923 of 1,013 neobladder patients with follow-up beyond 90 days; median follow-up was 72 months. It reported an overall long-term complication rate of 40.8%. Selected 20-year Kaplan–Meier estimates were hydronephrosis 16.9%, incisional hernia 6.4%, febrile UTI 5.7% and ileus/small-bowel obstruction 3.6%. These estimates should not be mixed with raw counts as if every patient had 20 years of observation.<sup>[[3]](#ref3)</sup>

Late problems include ureteroenteric and urethral-outlet obstruction, incomplete emptying, stones, infection, mucus obstruction, bowel complications, fistula and rare pouch perforation. New abdominal pain, inability to drain, systemic illness or upper-tract change warrants prompt evaluation.<sup>[[3]](#ref3)[[18]](#ref18)</sup>

When increased drain output raises concern for a urinary leak, comparing drain-fluid creatinine with serum creatinine is a reasonable first step; concentrations similar to serum make a major leak less likely but do not exclude one, since the peritoneum can equilibrate creatinine between spaces. Testing has been suggested when output increases or fails to improve over 48–72 hours, though no validated cutoff is established. Radiographic exclusion of a leak, such as a cystogram, is reasonable before catheter removal. For confirmed suture-line insufficiency, low-pressure drainage and reinsertion of any removed catheters or stents (with nephrostomy if needed) is the initial approach; surgical revision is described as rare.<sup>[[32]](#ref32)</sup>

### Reflux versus obstruction

A freely refluxing anastomosis and an antireflux tunnel have different tradeoffs. A short chimney, Le Duc–Camey implantation and a serous-lined extramural tunnel should not share a single blended stricture/reflux percentage. For details of the last technique, see [Mansoura Neobladder](/docs/surgical-techniques/04c-urinary-diversion/mansoura-neobladder).<sup>[[7]](#ref7)[[8]](#ref8)</sup>

Hautmann's own group moved from an antireflux ureteral anastomosis to a freely refluxing, open end-to-side implantation in short tubularized segments at each end of the W reservoir after encountering ureteroileal strictures, reporting stenosis falling from 9.5% to 1% with the change (denominators and comparison design not given at this passage).<sup>[[33]](#ref33)</sup> More broadly, an 11-study systematic review and meta-analysis across orthotopic neobladders found antireflux anastomosis associated with significantly *more* ureteroenteric stricture than direct anastomosis (OR 2.84, 95% CI 1.75–4.61), particularly when the ureteral stent was removed within 14 days, and significantly *less* vesicoureteral reflux (OR 0.24, 95% CI 0.10–0.59); reflux itself was not significantly associated with renal-function impairment in the pooled data. Treat Hautmann's own historical figures as one group's technique-associated experience and the meta-analysis as the stronger, more generalizable evidence on the stricture-versus-reflux tradeoff.<sup>[[34]](#ref34)</sup>

The main randomized evidence is USC-STAR, which compared Studer with T-pouch, not Hautmann with every alternative. Of 484 randomized patients, 423 had baseline outcome data and 260 contributed three-year renal data. Mean eGFR decline was 6.4 versus 6.6 mL/min/1.73 m² (p = 0.35); diversion-related secondary intervention was 13% versus 22%, respectively. The formal antireflux T-pouch did not show a renal benefit and required more diversion-related intervention.<sup>[[19]](#ref19)</sup>

Obstruction is a major actionable cause of deterioration. Jin's retrospective analysis selected 161 survivors of at least ten years and found obstruction independently associated with renal decline in both conduit and bladder-substitution groups. This supports surveillance and correction of obstruction, not a guarantee that reflux or other renal insults are harmless.<sup>[[20]](#ref20)</sup>

## Metabolic and Nutritional Effects

Acidosis should be monitored even when continence and emptying seem satisfactory. In the Ulm 923-patient cohort, 307 received long-term bicarbonate and 11 had episodes of severe acidosis. These are observed treatment counts in that cohort, not a universal lifetime probability.<sup>[[3]](#ref3)</sup>

A separate retrospective Studer cohort of 123 patients defined acidosis as serum bicarbonate below 22 mEq/L and found it more frequently early after surgery, with kidney impairment and diabetes among associated factors. Those percentages are not Hautmann-specific. In another 345-patient rehabilitation cohort, improved continence was associated with greater alkali requirements during early recovery; the observation supports closer early monitoring but does not establish a mechanism or a lifelong schedule.<sup>[[21]](#ref21)[[22]](#ref22)</sup>

EAU follow-up guidance advises annual vitamin B12 measurement after bowel diversion, because low levels have been reported in 17% of patients with bowel diversion (section 7.4 text; no strength rating). A low number receiving supplements in an old series does not establish a low incidence of deficiency under systematic screening. Preserving terminal ileum reduces avoidable loss of absorptive tissue but does not eliminate the need for monitoring.<sup>[[18]](#ref18)</sup>

Evaluate bone health according to acidosis, renal function and other clinical risk factors. The bone-density observation in only 20 patients in the Studer report is neither a Hautmann-specific result nor proof that osteoporosis risk is absent.<sup>[[23]](#ref23)</sup>

See [Vitamin B12 supplementation](/docs/foundations/pharmacology/urinary-diversion-specific/vitamin-b12-supplementation), [Urinary acidifiers & alkalinizers](/docs/foundations/pharmacology/urinary-diversion-specific/urinary-acidifiers), and [Mucus management](/docs/foundations/pharmacology/urinary-diversion-specific/mucus-management).

## Reconstruction in Women

Discuss both leakage and incomplete emptying, including the possibility of long-term catheterization.<sup>[[24]](#ref24)[[6]](#ref6)[[39]](#ref39)</sup> These outcomes vary with the retained outlet and pelvic support, surgical approach, patient characteristics and the definition used.<sup>[[24]](#ref24)[[6]](#ref6)</sup>

- **Historical Ulm experience.** 18 women underwent reconstruction, but only 13 were available for complete follow-up. Nine of those 13 required some intermittent catheterization. Two neobladder-vaginal fistula failures were converted to conduits and excluded. The often-quoted 70% figure is therefore a small selected series, not a mandatory risk estimate for every woman.<sup>[[25]](#ref25)</sup>
- **Neobladder-vaginal fistula incidence and prevention.** Larger contemporary female-neobladder series report an incidence around 5–10%, with higher risk when the anterior vaginal wall is excised or the patient has had prior irradiation, a range that should be kept separate from the small historical Ulm series above rather than combined with it. Described preventive measures include preserving the vagina when oncologically safe, closing an opened vaginal cuff watertight, and interposing omentum around the dependent portion of the pouch, fixed to perivaginal tissue on both sides of the urethral anastomosis, which keeps the neobladder and vaginal suture lines apart and may also suspend the reservoir floor and limit urinary retention from ileal-fold formation; a lateral cystogram before catheter removal is reasonable when early vaginal leakage is suspected. Persistent substantial incontinence beyond the first postoperative weeks should prompt pelvic examination (with intravesical methylene blue if needed) rather than an assumption of spontaneous closure; when a fistula is confirmed, transvaginal repair, abdominal exploration or conversion to a cutaneous diversion may ultimately be required. A separately described operative sequence anchors the longest feasible round-ligament remnants to the lateral vaginal opening to elevate the vaginal neofornix, then brings an omental flap through the left paracolic trough to fix to the round ligament and anterior vaginal wall, with an opening left in the flap at the urethral anastomosis to keep the vaginal closure and urethro-ileal anastomosis separated. None of these measures has been directly compared against another for fistula prevention.<sup>[[32]](#ref32)[[35]](#ref35)[[36]](#ref36)</sup>
- **Contemporary broader experience:** a 2023 study of 195 women without disease recurrence included 95 followed beyond ten years. Among those 95, chronic retention occurred in 31 (33%) and renal function deteriorated in 46 (49%). This is not an isolated classic-Hautmann cohort and demonstrates why continence alone is an incomplete outcome.<sup>[[26]](#ref26)</sup>
- **Patient-reported outcomes.** Bahlburg's prospective rehabilitation cohort enrolled 395 patients, including 38 women; one-year female continence data were available for 25 women. Sixteen reported no more than one safety pad and three reported no pads. Its separate questionnaire-severity category is a different measure, not the complement of social continence.<sup>[[27]](#ref27)</sup>

During female cystectomy, dissecting the urethra about 5 mm below the bladder neck has been described as a landmark: dissecting too close risks losing sphincteric tissue and incontinence, while leaving an excessively long urethral segment may contribute to retention. Gentle palpation of the Foley catheter balloon can help locate the bladder neck before division; a separately described technique instead digitally replaces the catheter balloon during lateral vaginal dissection, retracts it distally to identify the bladder neck, then slowly withdraws the catheter to identify the bladder-neck/urethral transition before placing a clamp and dividing sharply, with a full-thickness urethral margin taken for frozen section. These are attributed operative landmarks and maneuvers, not a validated comparative technique or a universal cutoff.<sup>[[32]](#ref32)[[37]](#ref37)[[36]](#ref36)</sup>

Urethral angulation and reservoir descent may contribute to poor emptying. Evaluate the actual outlet, residual urine, reservoir position and pelvic support; these findings do not justify assuming one mechanism in every patient. Assess associated pelvic support defects as part of the evaluation.<sup>[[28]](#ref28)[[6]](#ref6)</sup> Retention after female orthotopic substitution has been described as anatomical, functional or multifactorial, with causes including an obstructing ileal valve, loss of posterior support with neocystocele or urethral kinking, reservoir hypercapacity (from excessive ileal length or bladder-neck preservation), infection, failure to relax the pelvic floor, and inadequate abdominal straining pressure; a described basic workup is history and voiding diary, examination, urine testing, kidney/reservoir ultrasonography and post-void residual, with urethropouchoscopy, voiding pouchography, dynamic pelvic-floor MRI or urodynamics reserved for further evaluation rather than ordered routinely in every patient.<sup>[[36]](#ref36)</sup> One group reports routinely performing culdoplasty before orthotopic reconstruction, plicating the uterosacral ligaments with permanent sutures to create a support shelf for the neobladder while leaving room for two fingers beneath it to avoid compressing the sigmoid colon; that group does not routinely use an omental flap for reservoir support, a different practice from the omental-interposition approach described above for fistula prevention. Neither practice has direct comparative outcome data.<sup>[[38]](#ref38)</sup>

## Comparison with Studer and Other Designs

Lee's retrospective 130-patient comparison included 93 Studer and 37 Hautmann reconstructions. Hautmann procedures were shorter (5.3 versus 5.9 hours) and hospital stay was shorter (7.0 versus 8.3 days) in that historical cohort. Continence differences were not statistically significant. Case selection, anastomotic variations and changing discharge practices prevent treating those findings as a proven intrinsic time advantage or equivalence of function.<sup>[[29]](#ref29)</sup>

Modern intracorporeal evidence still lacks strong direct comparisons among reservoir designs. Discuss the operation a team can perform reliably, its specific emptying and surveillance demands and the patient's priorities, without implying that all named pouches have identical outcomes.<sup>[[13]](#ref13)[[5]](#ref5)</sup>

## Long-Term Surveillance

Continue functional monitoring beyond intensive cancer surveillance.<sup>[[18]](#ref18)[[30]](#ref30)</sup>

| Domain | Follow-up focus |
|---|---|
| Kidney and metabolic function | Creatinine/eGFR, electrolytes and bicarbonate. AUA/ASCO/SUO Statement 31 (Expert Opinion) advises laboratory assessment every 3–6 months for the first 2–3 years after treatment and then annually, adjusted for clinical need. A rising creatinine must not automatically be labeled age-related.<sup>[[30]](#ref30)</sup> |
| Upper tract | Imaging for obstruction, stones and other abnormalities, coordinated with cancer follow-up; investigate new dilation or renal deterioration. |
| Emptying and continence | Voiding pattern, residual urine, pad burden, catheterization technique and mucus/drainage problems. |
| Nutrition and bone | EAU advises annual B12 measurement.<sup>[[18]](#ref18)</sup> NCCN post-cystectomy follow-up specifies annual testing after the first year based on clinical judgment (category 2A).<sup>[[39]](#ref39)</sup> Additional evaluation addresses nutritional symptoms, chronic acidosis and bone-health risks. |
| Retained urethra and cancer | Risk-appropriate oncologic follow-up and monitoring of the retained urethra (Statement 32, Expert Opinion). Symptoms or higher-risk pathology guide additional evaluation rather than one universal urethral-wash schedule.<sup>[[30]](#ref30)</sup> |
