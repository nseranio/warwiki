# Parastomal Hernia After Urinary Diversion

**Parastomal hernia** after urinary diversion can impair appliance fit, cause pain or obstruction, and complicate urinary drainage. A radiologic hernia does not by itself establish a need for repair. Assessment separates an incidental imaging finding from a symptomatic hernia and defines the conduit or catheterizable-channel anatomy before treatment is chosen.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[2]](#ref2)[[3]](#ref3)</sup>

This page covers urinary stomas, including ileal conduits and [continent catheterizable channels](/docs/surgical-techniques/04b-bladder-reconstruction/catheterizable-channels). Much of the abdominal-wall literature concerns fecal stomas. The 2022 ASCRS ostomy guideline excludes urostomies, so its recommendations are not urinary-diversion recommendations.<sup>[[4]](#ref4)</sup>

## Incidence and Risk Assessment

Reported incidence depends on imaging, the hernia definition, follow-up duration and the patients available for assessment.

| Conduit cohort | Findings | Interpretation |
|---|---|---|
| Hussein 2018: 383 patients | 75 developed radiologic hernia; 23/75 were symptomatic and 11/75 received treatment. Estimated radiologic incidence was 9%, 23% and 32% at 1, 2 and 3 years. | The 31% symptomatic and 15% treated figures use all 75 hernias as the denominator, and not all operated patients. Median time to hernia was 13 months, and risk plateaued after year 3.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup> |
| Ghoreifi 2022: 361 patients | Radiologic hernia in 30% (108 patients), median time 8 months; 26% of these progressed over median follow-up of 27 months. | Progression meant radiologic upgrading or surgery; it is not an annual progression rate.<sup>[[2]](#ref2)</sup> |
| Liu 2014 | Of 516 patients, 199 with sufficient follow-up were analyzed; 58 had a hernia and 26/58 underwent repair. | Cumulative hernia risk was 12.2% at 1 year and 22.5% at 2 years. Repairs were done for discomfort (58%), obstruction or strangulation (15%), partial obstruction (15%) and other abdominal surgery (12%). Selection and follow-up differ from the other cohorts, so the repair proportion is not pooled.<sup>[[5]](#ref5)</sup> |

A 2025 systematic review pooled a clinical incidence of 14.4% across four studies and listed fascial defect of at least 24 mm, obesity and female sex as the main risk factors.<sup>[[3]](#ref3)</sup> Observational cohorts report the following independent associations:

| Cohort | Factor (effect estimate) |
|---|---|
| Hussein 2018<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup> | Fascial defect of 30 mm or more (OR 5.23), lower postoperative eGFR (OR 2.17), longer operative time (OR 1.25) |
| Ghoreifi 2022<sup>[[2]](#ref2)</sup> | Female sex (HR 1.86), diabetes (HR 1.81), COPD (HR 1.78), BMI (HR 1.07 per unit) |
| Liu 2014<sup>[[5]](#ref5)</sup> | Prior exploratory laparotomy (adjusted HR 1.98), severe obesity (adjusted HR 4.26) |

Effect sizes vary between cohorts, and none of these is a validated individual risk calculator. Modifiable risks are optimized and the patient's symptoms and operative fitness are discussed. Ghoreifi found no factor associated with hernia progression.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[2]](#ref2)[[5]](#ref5)</sup>

## Evaluation and Initial Management

- Examine the stoma and abdominal wall, including appliance seal, skin injury, reducibility and any associated midline hernia. Review the original diversion operation and prior repairs.
- Use cross-sectional imaging when needed to define the defect, hernia contents, conduit or channel course, associated ventral hernia and upper-tract abnormalities. Investigate impaired drainage or recurrent infection rather than assuming the hernia is the only cause.
- For a reducible hernia with little symptom burden, offer stoma-nurse review, appliance adjustment and a fitted support garment when helpful. Reassess worsening symptoms; a support belt does not repair the fascial defect.
- Consider elective repair for persistent pain, major appliance problems, recurrent obstruction or meaningful functional impairment after weighing recurrence and surgical morbidity. Acute pain with an irreducible swelling, vomiting or obstruction, systemic illness or threatened bowel or stoma viability requires urgent surgical assessment. The emergency approach depends on the findings.<sup>[[3]](#ref3)[[6]](#ref6)[[7]](#ref7)</sup>

## Prevention at Ileal Conduit Creation

Routine prophylactic mesh for every ileal conduit is not established. Conduit-specific randomized trials conflict, and the two more recent trials did not demonstrate benefit. They studied different materials and outcome definitions and are not a head-to-head comparison of mesh types.<sup>[[8]](#ref8)[[9]](#ref9)[[10]](#ref10)</sup>

| Trial | Population and intervention | Result and practical limit |
|---|---|---|
| Liedberg 2020 | 242 randomized at three centers; lightweight synthetic sublay mesh versus conventional construction | Clinical hernia within 24 months: 10/92 evaluable mesh patients versus 20/89 controls (11% versus 23%; p=0.06). Time-to-event analysis favored mesh, HR 0.45 (95% CI 0.24–0.86). Operating time was longer; no increase in mesh-related complications was detected.<sup>[[8]](#ref8)</sup> |
| PUBMIC 2024 | 146 randomized; FlexHD biologic mesh versus no mesh | At median 24-month follow-up, radiologic hernias occurred in 18 versus 19, and clinical hernias in 8 versus 8. The trial did not demonstrate a protective effect. No definite mesh-related adverse events were reported, which does not exclude uncommon or later harms.<sup>[[9]](#ref9)</sup> |
| Donahue 2025 phase 3 trial | 178 randomized; 137 evaluable; semiabsorbable Ultrapro mesh behind rectus and in front of the posterior sheath | At the final 24-month assessment, radiologic hernia occurred in 32/68 with mesh versus 23/69 controls (47% versus 33%); risk difference 14 percentage points (95% CI −4 to 31; p=0.14). No preventive benefit was demonstrated; this does not prove that mesh causes harm.<sup>[[10]](#ref10)</sup> |

The 2018 Cochrane review included 10 trials and 844 participants across ostomy surgery. Hernia incidence favored mesh, but certainty was low, heterogeneity substantial, and a reduction in reoperation was not demonstrated. Its search predates the three conduit trials above. The Cochrane review reported an overall hernia incidence of 22 versus 41 per 100 (RR 0.53, 95% CI 0.43–0.66), with no difference in reoperation. Likewise, a 2024 network meta-analysis mixing stoma types and techniques predates the negative 2025 trial. Neither supports a pooled ranking as guidance for all urinary stomas.<sup>[[11]](#ref11)[[12]](#ref12)</sup>

A 2023 cost-effectiveness model found prophylactic mesh cost-effective ($2,115 per QALY), sensitive to the probability of mesh infection. It depends on an assumed preventive benefit and predates the 2024 and 2025 trials.<sup>[[13]](#ref13)</sup>

Conduit construction should preserve perfusion and allow unobstructed passage without excessive fascial enlargement. Tanaka reported radiologic hernia in 3.5% with a novel construction (fascial passage limited to 2.4 cm, oblique rectus passage, separate anterior and posterior sheath fixation) versus 19.6% with the conventional procedure (p = 0.011), without mesh, in a preliminary cohort. The 2.4-cm aperture is not a caliber target for every conduit and mesentery.<sup>[[14]](#ref14)</sup>

## Planning Repair Around Urinary Anatomy

The conduit may have limited length and mobility because of its mesentery and ureteric anastomoses. Dissection must protect blood supply and avoid bowel or ureteric injury, kinking and obstruction. A concomitant ureteroenteric stricture or ventral hernia may change the reconstruction. Mesh is not normally continuously exposed to urine; urinary leakage, infection or erosion are complications to prevent and manage.<sup>[[3]](#ref3)[[6]](#ref6)[[15]](#ref15)</sup>

A urologist and an abdominal-wall surgeon should plan complex or recurrent repairs together. Select access, mesh plane and configuration according to anatomy, contamination, prior mesh and surgeon expertise. Available urinary-diversion evidence is predominantly observational; there is no universally superior repair.<sup>[[3]](#ref3)[[7]](#ref7)</sup>

| Configuration | Anatomical principle | Main limitation |
|---|---|---|
| Keyhole | Mesh has an aperture for the stoma limb. | Aperture sizing must avoid constriction while limiting recurrent herniation. |
| Sugarbaker | The stoma limb travels laterally relative to the mesh; the retromuscular variant offsets the anterior and posterior fascial openings. | Adequate length and safe lateralization are essential; do not tension or kink a short conduit to force this configuration. |
| Stoma resiting with abdominal-wall repair | Relocate the stoma when its position or surrounding tissue is unsuitable. | Both the original defect and new site need planning; relocation does not remove recurrence risk. |

These principles and their technical risks are described in operative reports and the open retromuscular trial. The configuration name does not by itself determine the mesh plane or approach.<sup>[[7]](#ref7)[[16]](#ref16)[[6]](#ref6)</sup>

### Randomized comparison of repair configuration

Maskal 2024 randomized 150 patients undergoing open retromuscular repair, including 25 ileal conduits; the remainder had fecal stomas. Patients were randomized only after both configurations were judged feasible. Two-year recurrence was 13/75 (17%) with Sugarbaker and 18/75 (24%) with keyhole; adjusted RR 0.87 (95% CI 0.42–1.69). Sugarbaker was not superior. Reoperations for recurrence were 2 versus 7 and mesh-related complications 4 versus 1, without significant differences. The study was powered for a large superiority difference and does not identify a conduit-specific winner or compare minimally invasive intraperitoneal repairs. Quality of life and decision regret did not differ between groups.<sup>[[7]](#ref7)</sup>

### Open and Minimally Invasive Repair

A 2025 meta-analysis of mixed stomas found that laparoscopic repair shortened hospital stay by about 4 days and lowered surgical site infection (RR 0.37–0.63) and mortality (RR 0.18), with no difference in recurrence. Robotic repair showed a possible reduction in operative time in limited data. Reduced mortality after minimally invasive surgery is an association with patient selection.<sup>[[21]](#ref21)</sup>

- **Xu 2021.** Four patients had robotic repair of a conduit hernia (three with the da Vinci SP). Median stay was 1 day, with no recurrence at median 18.3 months. Concurrent ureteroenteric stricture repair, panniculectomy, stoma revision and abdominal-wall reconstruction were performed in the same operation.<sup>[[15]](#ref15)</sup>
- **Dewulf 2022.** Fifteen patients had minimally invasive repair (10 robotic); median stay was 5 days, one third developed a urinary infection and one recurred at day 66. The authors provide a flow chart that chooses the technique by the presence of a concomitant midline incisional hernia.<sup>[[6]](#ref6)</sup>

### Interpreting urinary-diversion series

A French retrospective series of 51 conduit repairs reported 35% recurrence at median 15.3 months, with recurrence after keyhole repair (52%) higher than after Sugarbaker (10%, p = 0.046) and 7.8% major complications. The 2025 systematic review found the same direction (keyhole up to 52%, Sugarbaker 12.5%, overall 0–47%). Nonrandom selection prevents a causal ranking.<sup>[[17]](#ref17)[[3]](#ref3)</sup>

Roussel's 35-patient conduit series (13 keyhole, 22 Sugarbaker) reported symptomatic recurrence requiring a second operation in 8 (22.9%; 30.7% keyhole, 18.2% Sugarbaker). Urinary tract infection was the main complication, and 3 patients (8.6%) required mesh removal for infection. Four of the 8 recurrence repairs (50%) were done without synthetic mesh because of difficult adhesiolysis, and 3 patients (37.5%) needed a third operation. The authors concluded that keyhole and Sugarbaker repairs should no longer be used for ileal conduits and that new approaches are needed. These series support counseling about recurrence and revision morbidity.<sup>[[18]](#ref18)</sup>

In the 3-D funnel-mesh report, 27 of 40 operated patients took part in follow-up (median 29 months) with two recurrences (7.4%) and two stoma stenoses; one wound infection occurred, and the low recurrence rate reflects a small selected series.<sup>[[19]](#ref19)</sup> Holland's series of 97 open repairs (12.4% urostomies, mean follow-up 31.6 months) had 20.6% recurrence, with no significant difference by ostomy type. Recurrence was 42.9% after in situ suture repair, 34.8% after resiting with mesh, 17.3% after in situ mesh repair and 0% after stoma reversal (p = 0.042). By mesh plane, recurrence was 6.5% preperitoneal, 25% onlay, 36.4% intraperitoneal and 50% retrorectus (p = 0.035); these are mixed-stoma figures.<sup>[[20]](#ref20)</sup>

## Continent Catheterizable Stomas

Assess catheter passage, emptying, leakage and the continence mechanism as well as the hernia. Preserve the channel and its vascular pedicle when feasible; revise the continence mechanism if it is dysfunctional, rather than automatically reconstructing every channel during hernia repair.<sup>[[22]](#ref22)[[16]](#ref16)</sup>

Historical evidence is limited. Helal's series evaluated 21 patients (19 ileocecal reservoirs, 2 Kock pouches): 13 (61.9%) had simultaneous incontinence, 2 had difficulty catheterizing and 4 had stomal pain. Nineteen were repaired by transabdominal takedown of the anti-incontinence segment from the abdominal wall, closure through a midline incision, Marlex reinforcement when the defect exceeded 6 cm, revision of the anti-incontinence mechanism when incontinence was present, and repositioning of the stoma. Success was 89.5% at mean 23.4 months, and incontinence was corrected in all 13. This is a small historical series and not a benchmark.<sup>[[22]](#ref22)</sup>

Stout described laparoscopic repair with resiting in four patients (two continent channels, two incontinent diversions). Continence and patency were 100% at mean 2 years, one hernia recurred at 7 months, and in one patient the stoma was resited but the hernia could not be repaired laparoscopically because of dense adhesions.<sup>[[16]](#ref16)</sup>

### Resiting

Resiting has not shown an advantage over in situ repair. In Holland's mixed-stoma series, all recurrences after resiting were at the new stoma site regardless of mesh use.<sup>[[20]](#ref20)</sup> In 17,625 Medicare beneficiaries, the hazard of repeat parastomal repair was similar for resited and non-resited stomas (adjusted HR 0.93, 95% CI 0.81–1.06).<sup>[[23]](#ref23)</sup> Resiting remains an option when the stoma site or overlying skin prevents appliance fit.

## Counseling and Follow-up

Discuss recurrence, bowel or urinary injury, obstruction, infection, mesh complications and possible further surgery. Follow both abdominal-wall symptoms and urinary function. New hydronephrosis, reduced conduit output, difficult catheterization, recurrent febrile infection or substantial pain warrants reassessment.

In the 17,625 older Medicare beneficiaries undergoing elective parastomal repair, 30-day mortality was 3.8% and the five-year incidence of another parastomal or incisional hernia operation was 21.1%. This is a mixed-stoma claims cohort and not a conduit-specific recurrence rate. Reversal of a fecal stoma, a comparator in that study, is not an option for a urinary diversion.<sup>[[23]](#ref23)</sup>
