# Sigmoid Colon Neobladder

The **sigmoid (left colon) neobladder** is an orthotopic bladder substitute constructed from a detubularized segment of sigmoid colon and anastomosed to the native urethra, offered as an alternative to the more common ileal neobladders when the sigmoid is better suited to the patient's mesenteric anatomy, bowel history, or surgeon preference. It is a total bladder replacement after cystectomy, distinct from [sigmoid cystoplasty](/docs/surgical-techniques/04b-bladder-reconstruction/sigmoid-cystoplasty), which augments a retained native bladder.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[2]](#ref2)</sup>

---

## Rationale and Patient Selection

Candidacy follows the same general requirements as any orthotopic reconstruction: an oncologically appropriate urethra, adequate sphincter function, and a patient able and willing to learn abdominal straining and scheduled voiding. Counseling includes possible nighttime incontinence and retention requiring intermittent catheterization.<sup>[[8]](#ref8)</sup> See [Urinary Diversion Principles](/docs/surgical-techniques/04c-urinary-diversion/urinary-diversion-principles) for general orthotopic selection criteria. The sigmoid has been proposed over an ileal segment when the left colon offers a shorter, broader mesenteric reach to the pelvis on the sigmoid-artery pedicle, when ileal length or quality is a concern, or according to surgeon experience; no comparative trial establishes that sigmoid substitution is preferable to an ileal neobladder for any specific patient group.<sup>[[2]](#ref2)[[3]](#ref3)</sup>

---

## Operative Technique

The following sequence has been described for the sigmoid neobladder: a well-vascularized segment of left colon is isolated on its sigmoid-artery pedicle, keeping the mesenteric incision limited to protect the blood supply; the segment is detubularized along its antimesenteric (medial tenia) border and reconfigured into a dependent, low-pressure pouch; the ureters are implanted with a submucosal antireflux tunnel; and the dependent portion of the reservoir is anastomosed to the urethral stump without tension. Nicita’s separate series used direct ureteral implantation after 2005 rather than an antireflux tunnel; 12 of 17 reported ureteral strictures occurred after antireflux implantation, but method-specific denominators were not supplied.<sup>[[3]](#ref3)</sup> Preserving the ipsilateral hypogastric artery during cystectomy and limiting pelvic-floor muscle dissection have been emphasized as technical points relevant to later continence and sexual function.<sup>[[2]](#ref2)</sup> Reported segment lengths have varied; an early technique description used a minimum of 20-25 cm of sigmoid, and a separate chapter description describes 30-35 cm as the length one group of authors considers necessary for a capacious reservoir, substituting another bowel segment when that length is unavailable. These are the stated practice of individual surgeons and series, not a validated minimum.<sup>[[2]](#ref2)[[4]](#ref4)</sup> A **detaenialized** (non-detubularized) technique, in which the taenia is incised to open the sigmoid lumen without full detubularization, has also been reported as a technical variant.<sup>[[5]](#ref5)</sup>

---

## Outcomes

Most series below are retrospective, use varying definitions of continence and complications, and report separate cohorts. A 2026 systematic review of neobladder reconstruction after radical cystectomy included 17 studies (one randomized trial and 16 cohorts) and found fewer early complications and better nighttime continence with ileal than sigmoid neobladders, while sigmoid neobladders had lower postvoid residuals and catheterization rates. Heterogeneity and largely observational evidence limit certainty about the best segment for an individual patient.<sup>[[9]](#ref9)</sup>

| Series | Patients | Follow-up | Key findings |
|---|---|---|---|
| Reddy 1991<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup> | 27 | Not specified beyond 1 year | At 1 year, mean capacity 600 mL, filling and capacity pressures low (mean 12 and 16 cm H₂O); all patients continent by day, 67% continent at night without excessive voiding frequency |
| Miyake 2010<sup>[[6]](#ref6)</sup> | 82 (80 evaluated) | Mean 55 months | 73/80 voided spontaneously; daytime and nighttime continence in 69/80 and 46/80; mean maximum flow rate 18.6 mL/s, postvoid residual 24.5 mL; severe hyperchloremic metabolic acidosis in 3; five of eight SF-36 domains did not differ significantly from an age-matched Japanese reference population |
| Xu 2013 (detaenialized technique)<sup>[[5]](#ref5)</sup> | 210 | Median 48 months | 5-year daytime and nighttime complete continence 74.6% and 57.1%; younger age independently associated with continence; early complications in 31%, late complications in 21.5% |
| Nicita 2016<sup>[[3]](#ref3)</sup> | 160 enrolled; 75 evaluated at 5 years, 47 at 10 years | Mean 6.8 years (range 0.65-21.7) | Complete day-and-night continence was 34/75 (45.3%) at 5 years and 21/47 (44.7%) at 10 years; daytime-only continence was 27/75 (36%) at 5 years. Early complications occurred in 36/160 (22.5%), late complications in 40/160 (25%), and stage V chronic kidney disease in 2/160 (1.3%). In the urodynamic groups evaluated at 6 months (n=109) and 5 years (n=44), mean capacity was 306.5 and 433 mL, respectively, and mean postvoid residual was 40 and 111 mL, respectively. |
| El-Helaly 2019 (sigmoid vs ileal)<sup>[[7]](#ref7)</sup> | 52 men (25 sigmoid, 27 ileal) | Mean follow-up 37 months (range 12–51) for sigmoid and 34 months (range 13–49) for ileal | No significant difference in continence, spontaneous voiding or complication rate; night voiding frequency was lower in the ileal group. In Table 5, free-flow voiding time was shorter with sigmoid, while free-flow voided volume and maximum and closing urethral pressures were higher with ileal reconstruction. |

Daytime and day-and-night continence require separate reporting. Reddy reported daytime continence in all 27 patients but nighttime continence in 67% at 1 year; Nicita reported complete day-and-night continence in 34/75 evaluable patients (45.3%) at 5 years.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[3]](#ref3)</sup> Differences in definition, technique, follow-up and attrition prevent a single expected rate.

---

## Complications

Reported complications across these series include wound infection, ileus, pyelonephritis, ureterointestinal stricture, neobladder or enteric stones, entero-urethral stricture, metabolic acidosis, and progressive chronic kidney disease.<sup>[[3]](#ref3)[[5]](#ref5)[[6]](#ref6)</sup> As with any bowel-based orthotopic reservoir, lifelong surveillance of renal function, metabolic status, and reservoir emptying follows the general diversion follow-up framework; see [Urinary Diversion Principles](/docs/surgical-techniques/04c-urinary-diversion/urinary-diversion-principles).
