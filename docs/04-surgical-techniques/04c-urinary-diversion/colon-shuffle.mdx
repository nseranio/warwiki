# Colon Shuffle

The **colon shuffle** is an incontinent colon-conduit strategy for patients who already have a colostomy or need both urinary and fecal diversion. A suitable segment provides the urinary conduit, while proximal bowel provides a separate fecal stoma, contralateral if necessary. The term was used at the Netherlands Cancer Institute–Antoni van Leeuwenhoek Hospital, where Meijer and colleagues reported 21 patients in 2015. Davis and Noble described a related colostomy-conversion approach in 14 patients in 1992.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[2]](#ref2)</sup>

The technique avoids an additional bowel-to-bowel anastomosis when a fecal stoma is already needed, and it avoids ileal harvest. It avoids the risks of that particular anastomosis but not all bowel complications or urinary leaks.

## Clinical setting

Patients who need urinary diversion together with fecal diversion include those with locally advanced or recurrent rectal cancer requiring pelvic exenteration, locally advanced bladder cancer with rectal involvement, and radiation-induced pelvic damage such as fistula, cystitis or proctitis. Others already have a colostomy from prior surgery and later need urinary diversion. An ileal conduit in these patients requires an ileo-ileal anastomosis, which adds a site of potential leak (ileo-ileal anastomotic leakage occurred in 4% of ileal conduits after exenteration in Hagemans's series).<sup>[[5]](#ref5)</sup> In the Meijer series 90.4% of patients (19/21) had prior pelvic radiotherapy.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[2]](#ref2)[[3]](#ref3)</sup>

## Anatomy and planning

Review the existing operation, remaining bowel, vascular pedicles, ureteral reach, radiation field and proposed appliance sites with colorectal expertise. The urinary and fecal segments both need adequate perfusion and tension-free reach. An irradiated sigmoid segment or damaged mesentery may make this strategy unsuitable; colon is not inherently radiation-resistant.<sup>[[3]](#ref3)</sup>

| Setting | Reconstruction concept |
|---|---|
| **Existing end colostomy** | The distal portion of the functioning, stoma-bearing colon can be isolated for urinary drainage; the more proximal fecal bowel is redirected to another stoma |
| **Concurrent colorectal resection** | After determining the required resection, select viable remaining colon for the urinary conduit and a proximal segment for fecal diversion |
| **Limited abdominal stoma space** | Plan both appliance sites together; contralateral stomas are common, but same-side placement may be feasible |

The stoma-bearing segment in an end colostomy is not the disconnected bowel between the stoma and a Hartmann rectal stump. Nor should tumor-bearing or otherwise unsuitable distal bowel be repurposed because it would otherwise be discarded. Theva's technical report describes these reconstruction principles and includes five patients, with operative times of 5 to 11 hours and one Clavien III complication; it is an operative description, not comparative evidence of safety.<sup>[[3]](#ref3)</sup>

## Operative sequence

1. Confirm the extent of any required resection and the viability of the colon proposed for reconstruction.
2. Map and preserve the arterial supply to both planned diversions before making mesenteric divisions.
3. Isolate the urinary-conduit segment while ensuring that proximal bowel can reach its fecal stoma without tension.
4. Mobilize the ureters with their blood supply preserved; construct unobstructed, tension-free ureterocolonic anastomoses.
5. Mature the urinary and fecal stomas at accessible sites, checking perfusion, orientation, drainage and appliance fit.

In the colostomy-switch approach, the existing stoma is retained as the urinary outlet and a new proximal fecal stoma is created. No bowel anastomosis is required, and the urinary and fecal streams leave through separate stomas. Bowel length, vessel divisions and stoma location depend on the actual anatomy. See [Colon Conduit](/docs/surgical-techniques/04c-urinary-diversion/colon-conduit) for implantation and follow-up principles.<sup>[[2]](#ref2)[[3]](#ref3)</sup>

## Evidence and its limits

### Meijer 2015: named colon-shuffle cohort

The retrospective NKI-AVL series (2003–2013) included 21 patients (14 men, 7 women; mean age 61.5 years), 19 with previous pelvic radiation. Within 30 days, 52.4% (11/21) had complications. No bowel-anastomotic leakage or fecal peritonitis occurred. The abstract does not report a rate for all major complications or a comparator, and only this series has been published under the colon-shuffle name.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup>

### Comparative observational evidence

| Study | Population and finding | Interpretation |
|---|---|---|
| **Cotter 2017** | 43 patients with an existing colostomy: 10 without and 33 with bowel anastomosis. Intraoperative and early overall complications were lower in the former group | Retrospective selection; no significant difference in operative time, hospital stay or several specific complications. Not proof that the shuffle always shortens surgery or recovery |
| **Hagemans 2020** | Rectal-cancer exenteration: 214 ileal and 45 colon conduits. Ileus was 21% versus 7%; wound infection 14% versus 31%, with the wound association not persisting after adjustment | Different complication patterns, not established overall equivalence or a universally safer conduit |
| **Hebert 2026** | Four-center retrospective cohort of 179 colon conduits, including colostomy-switch patients | Broader colon-conduit evidence; pooled results are not a colon-shuffle-only estimate |

Sources: Cotter, Hagemans and Hebert.<sup>[[4]](#ref4)[[5]](#ref5)[[6]](#ref6)</sup> In Cotter, median operative time (550 versus 480 minutes) and length of stay (10 versus 25 days) did not differ significantly between the groups. In Hebert, the cohort was divided into colon conduit with colonic anastomosis, with colostomy, and colostomy switch. Albumin ≥3.2 g/dL was associated with fewer 30–90-day high-grade complications (HR 0.18), an observational association that does not show that raising albumin prevents complications. See the [colon-conduit outcome table](/docs/surgical-techniques/04c-urinary-diversion/colon-conduit) for the cohort's time-specific results.

## Difference from double-barreled wet colostomy

The colon shuffle uses separate urinary and fecal stomas. The double-barreled wet colostomy uses separate urinary and fecal limbs exiting through one abdominal stoma and appliance. Carter's design is a diverting loop colostomy with the colon divided 10 to 15 cm distal to the stoma, so that the distal segment acts as the urine limb. It provides internal stream separation and differs from the earlier wet colostomy, which mixes urine and feces upstream.<sup>[[7]](#ref7)[[8]](#ref8)</sup>

Carter's first 11-patient series reported no clinical pyelonephritis during 0.5–80 months of follow-up. Golda's 41-patient cohort reported pyelonephritis in 9.8%, ureterocolonic stenosis in 2.4% and lithiasis in the urine reservoir in 7.3%, and concluded that the risk of ascending infection must be considered. No study compares the double-barreled wet colostomy directly with the colon shuffle.<sup>[[8]](#ref8)[[9]](#ref9)</sup>

| Feature | Colon shuffle | Double-barreled wet colostomy | Davis and Noble |
|---|---|---|---|
| Stomas | Two, separate urostomy and colostomy | One, shared | Two, separate |
| Bowel anastomosis | Not required | Not required | Not required |
| Stream separation | Separate stomas | Internal, at the shared stoma | Separate stomas |
| Published series | 21 (Meijer) | 3, 11 and 41 (Carter 1989, Carter 1994, Golda) | 14 |

## Complications and follow-up

Counsel about urinary leak, ureterocolonic obstruction, infection, stones, ileus, bowel injury or fistula, stoma problems and metabolic abnormalities. Avoiding an ileal harvest does not eliminate these risks. Alemozaffar's 41-patient distal-colon series reported partial small bowel obstruction in 2 patients (4.9%) within 30 days, prolonged ileus in 8 (19.5%), an enterocutaneous fistula in 1 (2.4%) and ureterocolonic stricture in 4 (9.8%) at 90 days or later. At 90 days or later, hypokalemia occurred in 39%, hyperchloremia in 24.4% and metabolic acidosis in 34.1%.<sup>[[10]](#ref10)</sup>

A colon conduit exposes urine to intestinal mucosa and may cause hyperchloremic acidosis and hypokalemia. Assess renal function, electrolytes/acid–base status, upper-tract drainage and both stomas; apply the [diversion surveillance framework](/docs/surgical-techniques/04c-urinary-diversion/urinary-diversion-principles). Treatment doses belong in the [alkali and electrolyte pharmacology companion](/docs/foundations/pharmacology/urinary-diversion-specific/urinary-acidifiers).<sup>[[10]](#ref10)</sup>
