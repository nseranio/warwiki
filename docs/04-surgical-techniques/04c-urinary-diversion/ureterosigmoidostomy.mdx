# Ureterosigmoidostomy and Rectosigmoid Diversion

**Classical ureterosigmoidostomy** implants the ureters into the sigmoid colon, allowing urine to mix with stool and empty through the anus. Continence depends on anal function. It avoids an abdominal urinary appliance, but introduces substantial lifelong metabolic, upper-tract and colorectal surveillance needs. It is a selected reconstructive option and is not the default when a neobladder or catheterizable reservoir is unsuitable.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[2]](#ref2)</sup> Simon performed the first ureterosigmoidostomy in 1852, for bladder exstrophy. Ileal conduits and continent reservoirs later largely replaced it.<sup>[[5]](#ref5)[[3]](#ref3)</sup> Renewed interest comes from its ease, speed and applicability to minimally invasive surgery.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup>

The [Mainz pouch II](/docs/surgical-techniques/04c-urinary-diversion/mainz-pouch-ii) is a detubularized rectosigmoid modification. Its construction and procedure-specific evidence are covered on that companion page. It remains distinct from an isolated [colon conduit](/docs/surgical-techniques/04c-urinary-diversion/colon-conduit), which drains to an abdominal stoma.

## Selection and Counseling

Consider rectosigmoid diversion only after reviewing the alternatives and the patient's ability to sustain follow-up. Important considerations include:

- **Anal continence and rectosigmoid function:** assess baseline stool continence, urgency, bowel disease and prior surgery. Specialist functional testing may help when competence is uncertain; a single water-holding test should not be treated as a guarantee of postoperative continence.
- **Renal and metabolic reserve:** impaired renal function, obstruction or recurrent upper-tract infection increases concern about the acid load from urine-exposed bowel.
- **Colorectal suitability:** inflammatory bowel disease, neoplasia, significant radiation injury or an inherited colorectal cancer risk may make this route inappropriate. Assess the actual bowel and cancer risk rather than treating all family histories alike.
- **Long-term access:** reliable laboratory testing, imaging, endoscopy, treatment of acidosis and rapid evaluation of infection are needed. Avoiding appliances does not make this a low-maintenance operation for a setting without those services.
- **Patient priorities:** discuss daytime and nighttime leakage, bowel frequency, emptying routines, future conversion and cancer risk in addition to body image.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[2]](#ref2)[[3]](#ref3)[[4]](#ref4)</sup>

## Operative Principles and Modifications

Classical techniques use ureterocolonic implantation into intact bowel; tunnel-based antireflux modifications were intended to limit ascending reflux. Goodwin and Scardino favored the open-sigmoid (transcolonic) approach with a long submucosal tunnel and direct ureter-to-bowel anastomosis, and advised mandatory bowel preparation and a lifelong low-chloride diet with sodium-potassium citrate.<sup>[[5]](#ref5)</sup> Direct vision, viable ureter, preservation of blood supply and an unobstructed, tension-free anastomosis matter more than assigning a universal tunnel length. An antireflux mechanism can itself obstruct and does not eliminate infection or upper-tract deterioration.<sup>[[5]](#ref5)[[2]](#ref2)</sup>

Mainz II opens the rectosigmoid along its antimesenteric aspect and closes it side to side into a low-pressure reservoir, without colostomy, augmentation or extensive bowel surgery. The pouch is fixed to the sacral promontory to lessen ureteral kinking, and dilated ureters can be implanted. In the original report of 47 patients (mean follow-up 10 months), basal pressure with the reservoir full was 24 cm H₂O and the highest peak pressure 35 cm H₂O, and all patients were continent by day.<sup>[[6]](#ref6)</sup> Later series documented favorable pressure and continence measurements, but detubularization does not guarantee low pressure in every patient, eliminate urgency or remove the metabolic and neoplastic consequences of ureterocolonic diversion. Do not infer comparative superiority from unrelated classical and modified series.<sup>[[6]](#ref6)[[7]](#ref7)[[8]](#ref8)[[4]](#ref4)</sup>

Bowel preparation, ureteric stents, temporary drainage and postoperative emptying schedules should follow the actual reconstruction and institutional protocol. Historical instructions for mandatory mechanical preparation, fixed stent durations or lifelong sodium or potassium citrate are not universal contemporary prescriptions; electrolyte therapy requires individual renal, potassium and sodium assessment. See [Bowel preparation](/docs/foundations/pharmacology/perioperative-eras/bowel-preparation) and [Urinary acidifiers and alkalinizers](/docs/foundations/pharmacology/urinary-diversion-specific/urinary-acidifiers).<sup>[[5]](#ref5)[[9]](#ref9)</sup>

Other configurations, such as Atta's isolated detubularized pouch, have been described in small preliminary series. An inverted-U detubularized sigmoid pouch drains into the rectum while the left colon is fixed in continuity to the rectal ampulla, and ureters are implanted with a nipple technique. All 15 patients followed for 3 to 18 months passed urine (3 to 6 times daily) and solid feces (once daily) separately. That does not demonstrate elimination of carcinogenic exposure or long-term cancer protection. Such variations should not be presented as established solutions to the malignancy risk.<sup>[[10]](#ref10)</sup>

## What Long-term Outcomes Show

| Source | Population and follow-up | Findings and limits |
|---|---|---|
| **Tollefson 2010** | 51 patients selected for more than 10 years of follow-up; median 15.7 years; 40 had the operation for malignancy | 48/51 reported complete continence; 22/51 had a late complication and 19/51 required another operation. Anastomotic stricture (11/51), recurrent pyelonephritis (8), stones (5), chronic renal insufficiency (3) and severe intractable acidosis (2) occurred. No patient developed colonic malignancy during the study. These are outcomes among long-term followed patients, not an inception cohort free of survivor selection.<sup>[[2]](#ref2)</sup> |
| **Koo 1996, childhood exstrophy** | 27 patients, average 17 years after diversion | Reported daytime continence was 92% and nighttime continence 58%; 18% developed significant upper-tract changes. Two patients had retention-associated hyperammonemia and acidosis. Daytime dryness cannot stand in for night function or metabolic safety.<sup>[[3]](#ref3)</sup> |
| **Zabbo 1986, exstrophy** | 158 patients operated from 1925 to 1970; 52 reached by telephone | 34 still had a functioning ureterosigmoidostomy (30 with complete continence day and night) and 18 had required other diversion. Of patients surviving 15 or more years, 11% had colon cancer. Telephone follow-up of a minority of the original cohort limits interpretation.<sup>[[20]](#ref20)</sup> |
| **Bissada 1995** | 63 patients, median follow-up 41 months | Renal function was reported stable in 92%, while imaging deteriorated in 23% of renal units. Patient-level biochemical outcomes and renal-unit imaging outcomes measure different things.<sup>[[11]](#ref11)</sup> |
| **Mainz II series** | Different indications, denominators and generally shorter follow-up | D'elia: 102 evaluated patients (mean 46 months), day and night continence 97% and 95%, ureteric implantation stenosis 7.2% of 194 renal units, and 69% used oral alkalinizing drugs. Hadzi-Djokic: 220 patients (median 21 months), 99% continent, 52% on alkali and potassium. Obek: 50 evaluable patients (median 31 months), all continent but one, 60% on alkali, three admitted with severe acidosis and hypokalemia. Alkali treatment, obstruction, infection and occasional severe metabolic events accompany the high continence rates; see the [dedicated page](/docs/surgical-techniques/04c-urinary-diversion/mainz-pouch-ii) for analyzed denominators.<sup>[[12]](#ref12)[[13]](#ref13)[[8]](#ref8)</sup> |

The 2012 Cochrane review of intestinal urinary reconstruction found only five small trials (355 participants) across several comparisons, with limited methods and imprecise results. It did not establish a superior diversion. It also does not justify claiming that modified ureterosigmoidostomy is equivalent or superior to an ileal conduit or neobladder.<sup>[[14]](#ref14)</sup>

## Metabolic and Upper-tract Complications

Urine-exposed colon can absorb ammonium and other solutes and secrete bicarbonate, producing hyperchloremic metabolic acidosis. Severity depends on renal function, bowel exposure and emptying. In a comparison of 22 rectosigmoid-bladder and 25 classical ureterosigmoidostomy patients, acidosis occurred in 86% and 80%, and was less severe with the rectosigmoid bladder (pH 7.34 vs 7.29, p &lt; 0.01). Reducing the area of mucosa in contact with urine did not by itself prevent it.<sup>[[22]](#ref22)</sup> Infection or obstruction may precipitate deterioration. Hypokalemia can be substantial, and retention can cause hyperammonemia; new weakness, confusion, vomiting or systemic illness warrants urgent assessment.<sup>[[9]](#ref9)[[15]](#ref15)[[3]](#ref3)</sup>

Detubularization does not remove this burden. In Obek's Mainz II cohort, 50 of 60 patients were evaluable: 30 required oral alkalization, and three required hospitalization for severe acidosis and hypokalemia. Those observations should not be diluted into a claim that modified diversion causes only mild metabolic disturbance.<sup>[[8]](#ref8)</sup>

Follow serum bicarbonate, potassium and renal function; investigate symptomatic infection, hydronephrosis, stones or impaired emptying. Select alkali and potassium replacement according to laboratory results and comorbidity. Chronic acidosis also raises concern about bone health. The drug-specific regimen belongs in the [urinary alkalinizer hub](/docs/foundations/pharmacology/urinary-diversion-specific/urinary-acidifiers). Routine B12 supplementation is not inherent to a pure colonic diversion; assess any ileal resection and other B12 risks separately.<sup>[[9]](#ref9)</sup>

## Colorectal Neoplasia

Ureterocolonic anastomoses are at risk of adenoma and adenocarcinoma, often decades after diversion. A literature review gave an incidence of carcinoma of 2% to 15% with a latent period of 20 to 26 years, and polyps are more common than cancer.<sup>[[19]](#ref19)</sup> Reported risk multipliers vary with population, age, follow-up and whether adenoma is counted with cancer, and should not be combined into a single lifetime percentage. Kälble found 3 carcinomas in 30 patients examined by sigmoidoscopy, an 8.5- to 10.5-fold increase over the general population, and noted that an excess over ileal conduits was not proved. Leadbetter estimated a 500-fold increase and about a 5% lifetime risk, with a development time of 6 to 50 years that was shorter in patients older than 40 years at surgery.<sup>[[16]](#ref16)[[21]](#ref21)</sup> Mainz II retains this surveillance concern. Nitrosamine formation and inflammation are proposed contributors, not proof of a single mechanism or a preventive drug strategy.<sup>[[16]](#ref16)[[17]](#ref17)[[4]](#ref4)</sup>

Pettersson's exceptionally long childhood cohort assessed 24 patients after excluding an early postoperative death: seven developed invasive colorectal cancer and one carcinoma in situ. Mean latency to invasive cancer was 38 years, and five died from colorectal cancer. Six of the seven invasive cancers were poorly differentiated, and the risk of colorectal adenocarcinoma was 42 times that of the general Swedish population. Three cancers arose 1, 21 and 25 years after conversion to another diversion. These findings establish the importance of long-term follow-up; 7/24 is not a universal contemporary lifetime risk. Conversely, zero cancers in a smaller or shorter series does not establish safety.<sup>[[18]](#ref18)[[2]](#ref2)</sup>

### Endoscopic surveillance and anastomotic safety

Arrange a documented urology–gastroenterology plan. The 2010 BSG guidance recommends annual flexible sigmoidoscopy from the tenth anniversary, examining through and just beyond the higher ureteric implantation; this is a Grade C recommendation. Continue after conversion unless the original ureteric anastomoses are known to have been removed.<sup>[[4]](#ref4)</sup>

An older 1999 review proposed starting no later than five to six years. These are differing historical recommendations, not a newly proven single schedule. Individualize earlier or more extensive examination for symptoms, prior lesions, hereditary risk or the treating team's protocol.<sup>[[19]](#ref19)[[4]](#ref4)</sup>

A polypoid structure at a ureteric implantation should not be snared as though it were an ordinary colonic polyp. The normal anastomosis may appear polypoid, and removal can injure it and cause urinary leakage. Suspicious lesions require coordinated assessment; carefully planned biopsy or resection differs from routine snare polypectomy. Lesions clearly remote from the implantation are assessed separately.<sup>[[4]](#ref4)</sup>

Investigate bleeding, unexplained anemia, new obstruction or concerning bowel symptoms between scheduled visits. Confirmed neoplasia requires colorectal and urologic staging and a resection or diversion plan tailored to its site and extent. Difficulty attending surveillance should prompt active follow-up support and discussion of alternatives, not an automatic requirement for prophylactic bowel resection.<sup>[[19]](#ref19)[[4]](#ref4)</sup>

## Quality of Life

In a Mainz pouch II series of 83 patients, 41 completed the EORTC QLQ-C30. All scales except diarrhea were comparable to a German reference population. Daytime continence was 100%, and 63% of the Bonn subgroup could distinguish stool from urine. Only about half of the original patients were assessed (28 had died and 14 were lost to follow-up).<sup>[[23]](#ref23)</sup>

## Lifelong Follow-up

- **Urinary and bowel function:** day and night continence, emptying, stool frequency, pain and quality of life.
- **Metabolic and renal assessment:** electrolytes including bicarbonate and potassium, creatinine and eGFR, and additional acid–base testing when indicated. Increase testing during early recovery, intercurrent illness or after changing replacement therapy; avoid a rigid one-size-fits-all schedule.
- **Upper tracts:** periodic imaging, with prompt investigation of obstruction, recurrent febrile infection or declining renal function.
- **Endoscopy:** follow the documented anastomotic surveillance plan and preserve the operative record if diversion is later converted.
- **Underlying disease:** maintain any separate cancer or congenital-urology surveillance.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[2]](#ref2)[[9]](#ref9)[[4]](#ref4)</sup>
