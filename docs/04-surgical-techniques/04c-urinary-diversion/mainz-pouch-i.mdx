# Mainz Pouch I

The **Mainz pouch I** (Mixed Augmentation Ileum 'N Zecum) is a detubularized ileocecal reservoir made from cecum, ascending colon and two ileal loops. Thüroff, Alken, Riedmiller, Jacobi and Hohenfellner developed it in Mainz from 1983. It has been used for continent cutaneous diversion, orthotopic substitution and augmentation, which differ in emptying and continence mechanisms, so outcomes are reported separately below.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[2]](#ref2)[[18]](#ref18)[[19]](#ref19)</sup>

For cutaneous diversion, the outlet is a submucosally embedded appendix or an intussuscepted ileal nipple. Most published data come from two German centers (Mainz and Wuppertal), including a series of more than 800 patients with mean follow-up of 7.6 years.<sup>[[3]](#ref3)[[4]](#ref4)</sup>

## Historical Development

- **1983.** The first Mainz pouches were made for bladder augmentation and continent cutaneous diversion.<sup>[[19]](#ref19)</sup>
- **1985.** The first description reported 10 patients (4 augmentations, 6 diversions). It set out the low-pressure reservoir from cecum and two ileal loops, submucosal-tunnel ureteral implantation into the cecum, and continence by isoperistaltic ileoileal intussusception.<sup>[[18]](#ref18)</sup>
- **1986.** The *J Urol* description reported 11 augmentations and 12 diversions; the same three functional features were retained.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup>
- **1988.** The 100-patient report (34 augmentations, 15 orthotopic substitutions, 51 cutaneous diversions) recorded that stapling the nipple to the bowel wall and ileocecal valve solved nipple prolapse. The umbilicus was adopted as the stomal site for cosmetic reasons.<sup>[[2]](#ref2)</sup>
- **1990.** The in situ, submucosally embedded appendix was introduced as the continent outlet.<sup>[[9]](#ref9)</sup>
- **1995.** Seromuscular and full-thickness bowel-flap tubes were described for patients without a usable appendix.<sup>[[9]](#ref9)</sup>
- **1996.** A report of 561 patients from Mainz and Wuppertal included 60 augmentations, 61 orthotopic substitutions and 440 cutaneous diversions.<sup>[[19]](#ref19)</sup>

## Patient Selection

Selection requires suitable bowel, sufficient renal and hepatic function and a sustainable catheterization plan. A continent cutaneous reservoir may be chosen when an internal reservoir is desired but orthotopic substitution is unsuitable or unwanted. Cognition, dexterity, access to the proposed stoma and capacity for lifelong follow-up are assessed. Age alone does not determine the choice.<sup>[[5]](#ref5)[[6]](#ref6)</sup>

Oncologic suitability for an orthotopic reconstruction depends particularly on an appropriate urethral outlet and negative urethral margin. Continent cutaneous diversion remains an alternative when the urethra cannot be retained. The AUA guideline discussion lists inadequate renal or hepatic function (for example, an eGFR below 45) among the absolute contraindications to continent diversion. Renal, hepatic and bowel status are each assessed for the individual patient.<sup>[[5]](#ref5)</sup>

## Operative Principles

The original-author surgical atlas distinguishes the required bowel by the outlet selected:<sup>[[7]](#ref7)</sup>

1. **Assess the appendix before bowel division.** Its caliber, length, blood supply and reach must permit reliable catheterization.
2. **Reservoir bowel:** the appendix-stoma version uses approximately 10–12 cm of cecum/ascending colon and 20–25 cm of terminal ileum. Restore ileocolic continuity while preserving reservoir perfusion.
3. **Reconfiguration:** open the ileal loops and ascending colon for the reservoir plate. Preserve the lower cecum needed for the appendiceal continence tunnel; do not simply open every segment completely.
4. **Appendix outlet:** embed the vascularized appendix in a submucosal cecal bed, preserving its mesenteric vessels.
5. **Ileal nipple alternative:** retain an additional approximately 12 cm of ileum unopened for intussusception. The total ileum used therefore exceeds that of the appendix version.
6. **Ureters and completion:** create the selected ureteral anastomoses, avoid outlet kinking or compression, complete the pouch, and secure catheter drainage.

These dimensions come from the atlas technique and vary with the patient. The 1988 series described the same reservoir as 10–15 cm of cecum and ascending colon plus two ileal loops of equal length.<sup>[[2]](#ref2)</sup> The [Catheterizable Channels](/docs/surgical-techniques/04b-bladder-reconstruction/catheterizable-channels) page covers broader outlet planning.

### Reservoir modifications

Alcini reported 30 patients with ileocecal bladder replacement using multiple transverse teniamyotomies instead of formal detubularization. All were continent by day; at one year mean capacity was 396 mL with a mean full-filling pressure of 28 cm H₂O. This is a separate reconstruction from the detubularized Mainz pouch.<sup>[[8]](#ref8)</sup>

Bowel-flap tubes are alternatives when the appendix is absent or unsuitable. Lampel's initial report of 17 patients used a seromuscular flap tube in 11 and a full-thickness flap tube in 6. At mean follow-up of 8 months, 16 of 17 catheterized every 4 to more than 6 hours with 14–16 F catheters and were continent day and night.<sup>[[9]](#ref9)</sup>

## Continence Mechanisms and Complications

Wiesner's 2006 retrospective comparison of 401 patients was not randomized, so the groups differ in anatomy, technique and follow-up.<sup>[[4]](#ref4)</sup>

| Outcome | Ileal nipple, n = 205 | Appendix, n = 196 |
|---|---|---|
| Complete continence | 82% | 92% |
| Stomal stenosis | 34 (17%) | 63 (32%) |
| Reservoir stones | 41 (20%) | 20 (10%) |
| Reoperation for stomal incontinence | 12 | 3 |
| Reported outlet necrosis | One nipple necrosis within the incontinence-reoperation group | Four appendiceal necroses |
| Mean time to first stenosis | 43.8 months | 31.4 months |
| Mean time to first stone | 62.8 months | 47.5 months |

Overall, 144/401 patients (36%) required an intervention for a stomal complication, and 63% of stomal complications were treated endoscopically. The authors attributed the higher stenosis rate with the appendix to its smaller diameter and the higher stone rate with the nipple to metal staples. The nipple outlet produced somewhat more mucus.<sup>[[4]](#ref4)</sup>

Gerharz compared the two outlets in 193 consecutive reservoirs (96 appendix, 106 ileal nipple as reported), with mean follow-up of about 3 years. Appendix stenosis (15.6%) was corrected as a minor outpatient procedure, and two complete appendix necroses were replaced with an ileal nipple. Reoperation was needed in 13 of 106 nipples (12.3%) for necrosis (4), dislocation from the ileocecal valve (3), detachment from fascia (4) or stenosis (2). Stones occurred in 3 nipples and no appendix. The authors advised using the appendix whenever a suitable one is available.<sup>[[20]](#ref20)</sup>

The two-center series of more than 800 ileocecal reservoirs, mean follow-up 7.6 years, reported overall day-and-night continence of 92.8%. This is not an appendix-only rate. Stomal stenosis occurred in 23.5% with an appendix and 15.3% with an ileal nipple, and stones in 5.6% and 10.8%. Eleven patients (8 appendix, 3 nipple) required reoperation for ischemic degeneration of the continence mechanism. These patients overlap those in the 401-patient report.<sup>[[3]](#ref3)</sup> In the 561-patient series (mean follow-up 57 months), early and late complications occurred in 12% and 37%, and stoma failure in 11% of cutaneous diversions.<sup>[[19]](#ref19)</sup>

## Ureteral Implantation

Submucosal implantation into colon is the classic antireflux approach. Serosa-lined extramural tunnels were subsequently used, including for dilated ureters. Ureteral perfusion is preserved and tension, angulation and compression are avoided with either reconstruction.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[10]](#ref10)[[7]](#ref7)</sup>

Wiesner's 2007 retrospective study included 458 patients, with 809 renal units receiving a submucosal tunnel and 74 an extramural tunnel; median overall follow-up was 89 months.<sup>[[10]](#ref10)</sup>

| Outcome | Submucosal tunnel | Serosa-lined extramural tunnel |
|---|---|---|
| Obstruction | 59/809 renal units (7.3%) | 3/74 (4.1%) |
| Obstruction in previously dilated upper tracts | 13.9% | 3.1% |
| Obstruction in patients with neurogenic bladder | 17.1% | 7.1% |
| Obstruction-free at 1 / 5 / 10 years | 97% / 93% / 91% | 100% / 96% / 96% |
| Serum creatinine ≤ 1.6 mg/dL at last follow-up | 97% | 98% |

The extramural tunnel gave lower obstruction rates, especially with dilated upper tracts and neurogenic bladder, and the authors prefer it in those settings. Renal function remained stable with both techniques. The comparison was retrospective and the groups were very unequal in size. A normal serum creatinine does not exclude unilateral obstruction.<sup>[[10]](#ref10)</sup>

## Orthotopic and Augmentation Applications

Orthotopic Mainz reconstruction omits a cutaneous outlet and joins the reservoir to the retained urethra. Emptying relies on an appropriate outlet, pelvic-floor relaxation and abdominal pressure; some patients need intermittent catheterization.<sup>[[11]](#ref11)</sup>

Leissner's 1999 series included 108 men, 103 evaluable, with mean follow-up 42 months. Reported daytime continence was 88%, including 17% using a safety pad. At night, 34% were completely continent while sleeping through the night and another 33% used a safety pad; nighttime results should not be collapsed into a single “dry” percentage. Fifteen of 205 renal units developed ureteroenteric stenosis.<sup>[[11]](#ref11)</sup>

Augmentation retains native bladder and outlet structures, so continence depends on the native bladder neck and urethral mechanism. In the 100-patient report, 32 of 34 augmentation patients were completely dry day and night, and 3 relied on intermittent catheterization. In the 561-patient series, 93% of augmentation patients were completely continent and all but 3 voided by abdominal straining.<sup>[[2]](#ref2)[[19]](#ref19)</sup>

Bedük compared 19 Mainz and 36 Abol-Enein and Ghoneim orthotopic neobladders (median follow-up 36 and 31 months). The differences below were not tested in the abstract, and urodynamic findings did not differ significantly between the groups.<sup>[[21]](#ref21)</sup>

| Outcome | Mainz (n = 19) | Abol-Enein and Ghoneim (n = 36) |
|---|---|---|
| Ureterointestinal stenosis | 10.5% | 5.7% |
| Pouch-ureteral reflux | 7.9% | 4.2% |
| Pyelonephritis | 15.8% | 13.8% |
| Daytime incontinence | 5.3% | 5.5% |
| Nighttime incontinence (twice weekly or more) | 21% | 8.4% |
| Capacity at 12 months | 426 ± 34 mL | 442 ± 27 mL |

Santucci's urodynamic comparison of five reservoir types included 11 Mainz stomal reservoirs and 8 Mainz neobladders. Continence was 91% for the Mainz stomal reservoir and 75% for the Mainz neobladder, in a 40-patient series with mean urodynamic assessment at 9.1 months.<sup>[[22]](#ref22)</sup>

## Neurogenic and Pediatric Experience

Stein's 2005 cutaneous-diversion report included 70 children and adolescents, of whom 65 had follow-up after five deaths unrelated to diversion. At median 8.7 years, upper tracts were stable or improved in 113/118 renal units; reported continence was 97%. Revision burdens included stomal stenosis, outlet incontinence, stones and ureteral obstruction. Ureteral stenosis requiring revision occurred in 16% of renal units with a submucosal tunnel and 3% with an extramural tunnel. Revisions were also needed for outlet incontinence (9%), stoma stenosis (23%), pouch calculi (15%), stoma prolapse (2%) and symptomatic reflux (1%). These are single-institution results with continuing surveillance.<sup>[[12]](#ref12)</sup>

The earlier 2000 report covered 149 operations with follow-up in 129 patients, comprising 59 colonic conduits, 12 orthotopic reconstructions and 58 Mainz I diversions. Mean follow-up was 11.8 years. Upper tracts improved or remained stable in 97% of renal units with a colonic conduit or Mainz pouch I and in 95% with orthotopic substitution. Complete continence was achieved in 98% of those with a continent stoma. These figures pool three diversion types and are not a Mainz-pouch-only result.<sup>[[13]](#ref13)</sup> In the companion report of orthotopic reservoirs in children and adolescents, upper tracts were stable or improved in all augmentation patients and in 20 of 23 renal units after substitution.<sup>[[23]](#ref23)</sup> Reconstruction follows assessment of storage pressure, renal risk, outlet function and the patient's ability to catheterize. That ability is reassessed during transition to adulthood.

## Metabolic Outcomes

Pfitzenmaier reevaluated 94 of 458 operated patients, median follow-up nine years. B12 supplementation was recommended in 32% because levels were low-normal or low, and 37% continued citrate for acidosis prevention. No patient had megaloblastic anemia, funicular myelosis or osteoporosis. Bone density was measured in only 18 patients, and the authors suggested, without demonstrating, that systematic blood-gas follow-up with alkali may have prevented bone demineralization. Biochemical B12 deficiency was not excluded by the absence of symptoms.<sup>[[14]](#ref14)</sup>

The appendix-outlet version uses less ileum than a nipple-outlet reconstruction. Bowel length is one determinant of metabolic risk, alongside segment location, renal function, urine contact and prior bowel surgery. The 32% supplementation figure is not a B12-deficiency rate.<sup>[[14]](#ref14)[[7]](#ref7)</sup>

See [Vitamin B12 Supplementation](/docs/foundations/pharmacology/urinary-diversion-specific/vitamin-b12-supplementation), [Urinary Acidifiers and Alkalinizers](/docs/foundations/pharmacology/urinary-diversion-specific/urinary-acidifiers), and [Mucus Management](/docs/foundations/pharmacology/urinary-diversion-specific/mucus-management).

## Secondary Malignancy and Follow-up

Kälble's 2011 analysis recorded 32 secondary tumors among 17,758 diversions reported by German centers. The reported prevalence in ileocecal pouches was 0.14%; this is not a lifetime incidence estimate. The authors recommended endoscopy of catheterizable ileocecal pouches when symptoms such as hematuria, chronic urinary infection or hydronephrosis are present. The 0.14% figure did not differ significantly from ileal neobladders (0.05%, p = 0.46) but was lower than ureterosigmoidostomy (2.58%) and cystoplasty (1.58%).<sup>[[15]](#ref15)</sup>

Lifelong functional follow-up should cover catheterization and stomal access, continence, renal drainage, stones, metabolic abnormalities and bowel symptoms. After cystectomy for bladder cancer, the AUA guideline recommends laboratory assessment every 3–6 months for 2–3 years and then annually (Expert Opinion), and the EAU guideline recommends annual vitamin B12 measurement after bowel diversion. Imaging and additional testing follow the original disease and the functional risk.<sup>[[5]](#ref5)[[16]](#ref16)</sup>

Do not routinely screen or treat asymptomatic bacteriuria because an intestinal reservoir is colonized. Culture and treat for clinical indications, including established exceptions such as preparation for mucosa-breaching urological procedures.<sup>[[17]](#ref17)</sup> Difficulty passing a catheter, newly impaired drainage or suspected pouch injury requires prompt assessment and a drainage plan.
