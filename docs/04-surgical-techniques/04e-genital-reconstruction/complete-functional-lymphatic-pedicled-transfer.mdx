# Complete Functional Lymphatic-System Pedicled Transfer (Abdelfattah)

The **complete functional lymphatic-system pedicled transfer** is a single-stage operation for advanced male genital lymphedema in which fibrotic genital tissue is excised and the defect is reconstructed with a pedicled superficial circumflex iliac artery perforator (SCIP) lymphatic flap. Abdelfattah and colleagues reported 26 men treated between February 2018 and January 2022, with a mean follow-up of 44.9 months. All flaps survived, and all 26 patients reported some quality-of-life benefit. The series is uncontrolled and supports feasibility. It does not establish superiority over other excisional or lymphatic reconstructions.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup>

For disease assessment see [Genital Lymphedema](/docs/clinical-conditions/03g-genital-scrotal/genital-lymphedema) and [Giant Penoscrotal Lymphedema](/docs/clinical-conditions/03g-genital-scrotal/giant-penoscrotal-lymphedema). Related options include [3R / SCIP-LIFT](/docs/surgical-techniques/04e-genital-reconstruction/3r-scip-lft), [CHASCIP](/docs/surgical-techniques/04e-genital-reconstruction/chascip), [Debulking Scrotoplasty](/docs/surgical-techniques/04e-genital-reconstruction/debulking-scrotoplasty), [LYST](/docs/surgical-techniques/04e-genital-reconstruction/lyst), [VLNT](/docs/surgical-techniques/04e-genital-reconstruction/vascularized-lymph-node-transfer) and [LVA](/docs/surgical-techniques/04e-genital-reconstruction/lymphaticovenous-anastomosis).

## Background

Yamamoto's radical reduction and reconstruction (3R) series, published in 2022, first described SCIP lymphatic flap transfer after excision of male genital elephantiasis in seven patients, with a mean follow-up of 22.7 months.<sup>[[8]](#ref8)</sup> The Abdelfattah series is larger (26 patients) and longer (44.9 months), and it covers partial and total scrotal reconstruction as well as partial and total penile skin reconstruction.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup> The authors describe the result as a complete functional lymphatic system, meaning that the flap provides soft-tissue coverage and lymphatic tissue in one pedicled unit. That description is theirs, and it has not been shown to restore normal drainage.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup>

The concept follows the lymphatic system transfer (LYST) principle. A SCIP flap that carries afferent lymphatic vessels together with their draining nodes is thought to need less lymphangiogenesis to function than a node-only flap.<sup>[[5]](#ref5)</sup> That premise comes from a three-patient limb series.

## Selection and planning

Consider this form of reconstruction when advanced scrotal or penoscrotal disease requires removal of irreversible fibrotic tissue and a lymphatic-bearing flap can also provide coverage. Selection depends on the distribution of disease, donor anatomy, previous treatment, usable lymphatics, reconstructive needs and patient priorities. A particular genital size, ICG pattern or limb-based ISL stage does not establish which named operation is best.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[2]](#ref2)[[3]](#ref3)</sup>

Evaluate active infection, underlying cancer or other causes of obstruction, associated leg edema, venous disease and response to [conservative treatment](/docs/surgical-techniques/04e-genital-reconstruction/complex-decongestive-therapy). Previous inguinal dissection or radiation can compromise both the vascular pedicle and the lymphatic donor, so the groin is assessed before a pedicled flap is chosen.<sup>[[2]](#ref2)[[3]](#ref3)</sup>

| Tool | Role |
|---|---|
| ICG lymphography | Maps functional groin lymphatics and the extent of genital dermal backflow |
| Genital Lymphedema Score (GLS) | Symptom score from 0 to 9, used before and after surgery (see below) |
| Glasgow Benefit Inventory (GBI) | Postoperative questionnaire of perceived benefit, scored from −100 to +100; the Abdelfattah series reported it |

If nodes are harvested, reverse lymphatic mapping identifies and preserves donor-leg drainage. In a series of 84 patients who had SPECT/CT reverse mapping, drainage from the leg to at least one superficial inguinal region was seen in 38.1%, and gamma-probe-guided harvest from the contralateral side avoided donor-leg lymphedema in the 56 patients who underwent groin node transplantation. Surface landmarks alone do not define a safe harvest zone.<sup>[[3]](#ref3)[[4]](#ref4)</sup>

## Operative outline

The report describes excision of fibrotic genital tissue followed by a pedicled lymphatic SCIP flap. The flap is rotated into the defect without a microvascular anastomosis and is used for partial or total scrotal reconstruction and, where needed, penile skin reconstruction.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup> The pedicle still needs careful dissection, protection of lymphatic drainage and an inset without tension or compression.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[2]](#ref2)</sup>

1. Excise the diseased skin and subcutaneous tissue, tailored to the disease distribution. Preserve viable uninvolved tissue where appropriate, the testes and spermatic cords, the urethra and the penile neurovascular structures.<sup>[[2]](#ref2)</sup>
2. Raise the pedicled SCIP-lymphatic flap from the groin on the superficial circumflex iliac vessels, with the skin paddle sized to the defect.
3. Rotate the flap into the defect and inset it to make a neoscrotum around the testes, or to resurface penile skin. The reported reconstructions were partial scrotum in 11 patients, total scrotum in 15, total penile skin in 9 and partial penile skin in 2.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup>

The abstract does not give collector or node inclusion, the number of flaps per patient, or the skin-paddle design in each patient. Those details are in the full operative report and should be taken from it. Node-preserving 3R and node-containing LYST are different designs with different proposed mechanisms, and the label "SCIP flap" does not distinguish them.<sup>[[2]](#ref2)[[5]](#ref5)</sup>

## Assessing symptoms and benefit

The **Genital Lymphedema Score (GLS)** sums six present-or-absent symptoms. It is not three domains each graded 0 to 3.<sup>[[2]](#ref2)[[6]](#ref6)</sup>

| Symptom | Absent | Present |
|---|---:|---:|
| Heaviness | 0 | 1 |
| Tension | 0 | 1 |
| Swelling | 0 | 1 |
| Urinary difficulty due to genital edema | 0 | 2 |
| Cutaneous lymphatic cysts | 0 | 2 |
| Genital lymphorrhea | 0 | 2 |
| **Total** | **0** | **9 maximum** |

The original GLS study included 32 women with secondary lower-limb lymphedema and genital involvement and found that the score rose with ICG genital dermal-backflow stage (mean 1.2, 2.4, 4.8 and 8.0 for stages I to IV).<sup>[[6]](#ref6)</sup> Later male surgical series used it. A low postoperative GLS does not measure donor safety, sexual outcomes or long-term recurrence.

## Reported outcomes

| Outcome | Abdelfattah 2023 report |
|---|---|
| Patients | 26 men: 15 isolated scrotal, 11 penoscrotal |
| Treatment period | February 2018 to January 2022 |
| Follow-up | Mean 44.9 months |
| Scrotal coverage | Partial in 11, total in 15 |
| Penile coverage | Total skin reconstruction in 9, partial in 2 |
| Flap survival | 100% |
| GLS | Mean 6.2 before surgery and 0.05 afterward (p &lt; 0.001) |
| Cellulitis | Reduced (p &lt; 0.001); absolute episode rates are not given in the abstract |
| Glasgow Benefit Inventory | Median total score +41; all 26 reported some quality-of-life improvement |

These are uncontrolled before-and-after observations after both excision and reconstruction, so the contribution of the lymphatic transfer cannot be separated. Flap survival is not the absence of wound complications, donor morbidity or recurrence. The authors conclude that sexual function improved, but the abstract reports no validated sexual-function endpoint, complication rates, compression requirements or recurrence ascertainment.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup>

The three SCIP-based male genital series differ in design and cannot be ranked against each other.

| Feature | Abdelfattah 2023 | Yamamoto 3R 2022 | Ciudad CHASCIP 2025 |
|---|---|---|---|
| Patients | 26 | 7 | 8 |
| Mean follow-up | 44.9 months | 22.7 months | 34 months |
| Flap design | SCIP-lymphatic flap for scrotum and, when needed, penile skin | Pedicled SCIP-LFT for scrotum; SCIP pure-skin-perforator flap for penis in 3 patients | Bilateral pedicled lymphatic SCIP flaps with a skin graft |
| GLS before and after | 6.2 and 0.05 | 6.7 and 0.3 | 6.6 and 0.6 |
| Complications | Not detailed | None reported | 2 of 8 |
| Recurrence | Not reported | None at mean 22.7 months | None reported |

Sources: Abdelfattah,<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup> Yamamoto<sup>[[8]](#ref8)</sup> and Ciudad.<sup>[[9]](#ref9)</sup> Quality-of-life data from an older London series found that 19 of 21 patients who had genital reduction for primary lymphoedema would choose surgery again, and that genital reduction appeared to give greater benefit than limb reduction. That series did not use a lymphatic flap and assessed patients early.<sup>[[11]](#ref11)</sup>

## Mechanism

Transferred nodes are proposed to work through neolymphangiogenesis and by absorbing interstitial fluid. In a histologic study of 14 patients after gastroepiploic node transfer for limb lymphedema, podoplanin-positive vessel density rose from 7.92 to 11.79 vessels/mm² at 12 months (p = 0.0008).<sup>[[10]](#ref10)</sup> That study used omental nodes in limbs, so it supports the concept for node transfer but not for the genital SCIP flap specifically. Cellulitis reduction after transfer has been reported in several series, and its mechanism in this setting has not been established.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup>

## Combined genital and leg lymphedema

A separate 2020 case report described a 41-year-old man after radical prostatectomy and pelvic lymphadenectomy. Bilateral pedicled superficial inguinal node-bearing adipofascial flaps without skin (11 × 7 cm, containing afferent lymphatics, nodes and fat) were transferred, the right to the scrotal root and the left to the proximal thigh, with two-level LVA in the affected leg. At nine months, excess leg volume relative to the unaffected side decreased from 49.6% to 9.4%, scrotal swelling improved and no cellulitis was reported.<sup>[[7]](#ref7)</sup>

This describes a possible combined strategy in one selected patient. It cannot establish a standard operation after pelvic lymphadenectomy or separate the effect of the flaps from that of LVA. The combined procedure includes microsurgery even though the flaps are pedicled.<sup>[[7]](#ref7)</sup>

## Limitations

- Complication rates, compression requirements and recurrence ascertainment are not detailed in the abstract of the index series.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup>
- There is no control group, and no postoperative lymphoscintigraphy is reported.
- The series comes from one group, and no multicenter validation exists.
- A pedicled flap depends on an intact groin. After prior inguinal dissection or radiation, a free node transfer from another donor may be needed.<sup>[[2]](#ref2)[[3]](#ref3)</sup>

## Follow-up

Monitor wound and flap healing, infection, donor-leg swelling or lymphatic leakage, genital symptoms, sexual and urinary function, recurrent swelling and patient-reported benefit. Record the extent and duration of compression and other conservative care. Neither the technique name nor a favorable symptom score establishes freedom from maintenance treatment.<sup>[[2]](#ref2)[[3]](#ref3)</sup>
