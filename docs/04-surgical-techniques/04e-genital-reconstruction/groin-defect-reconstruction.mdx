# Groin Defect Reconstruction After Inguinal Lymphadenectomy

**Groin defect reconstruction** addresses the skin, soft-tissue and femoral-vessel coverage problem left after inguinal (ilioinguinal) lymph node dissection for penile, vulvar, anal or cutaneous (melanoma) cancer, independent of the oncologic indications and nodal boundaries for the dissection itself.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[2]](#ref2)</sup> The reconstructive problem varies with the extent of resection: fascial-sparing dissection preserves the fascia lata, whereas extensive skin excision during block dissection can leave the femoral vessels exposed.<sup>[[4]](#ref4)[[7]](#ref7)</sup>

---

## The Reconstructive Problem

Wound complications after inguinal lymphadenectomy are common. In a 163-patient, 237-dissection cohort from a single center, one or more wound complications occurred after 58% of procedures (10% of recorded complications graded severe), with wound infection in 43%, seroma in 24% and skin-flap problems in 16%.<sup>[[3]](#ref3)</sup> In a separate retrospective series of 421 patients undergoing 660 fascial-sparing dissections, the authors reported 50 wound infections (11.9% of 421 patients), 21 deep wound dehiscences (5.0%), and 104 lymphedema cases (24.7%).<sup>[[4]](#ref4)</sup> The earlier per-dissection and later per-patient rates have different denominators and cannot establish a reduction between cohorts. Reconstructive options are considered when skin and soft-tissue loss, wound breakdown or exposed femoral vessels exceed what local wound care and primary closure can manage. For cN3 penile cancer proceeding to consolidative resection after neoadjuvant chemotherapy, the EAU-ASCO surgical-technique section specifies preoperative assessment of the anticipated skin and soft-tissue defect and plastic-surgery consultation when appropriate.<sup>[[8]](#ref8)</sup>

A sartorius muscle flap is often used specifically to interpose muscle over exposed femoral vessels, but its use has itself been associated with a higher rate of moderate-to-severe wound complications in the same 163-patient cohort cited above (odds ratio 2.64, p = 0.04 on univariable analysis; 2.12, p = 0.06 after multivariable adjustment). This observational association does not establish that sartorius transposition causes wound complications.<sup>[[3]](#ref3)</sup> In a randomized trial of 61 women undergoing 99 inguinofemoral dissections for vulvar cancer, prophylactic sartorius transposition did not reduce postoperative wound morbidity; that trial did not test treatment of an established defect with exposed vessels.<sup>[[6]](#ref6)</sup>

---

## Sartorius Muscle Flap

The sartorius is supplied by six to eight segmental vascular pedicles along its length, grouped by a cadaveric angiographic study into a proximal major-pedicle cluster located 18 to 25 cm from the anterior superior iliac spine (ASIS), perfusing about 80% of the muscle, and a distal major-pedicle cluster 35 to 44 cm from the ASIS, perfusing about 90%; either cluster can serve as a pivot point for a local transposition flap.<sup>[[5]](#ref5)</sup>

For femoral-vessel coverage after inguinal lymphadenectomy, division of the muscle near its proximal origin at the ASIS, with limited mobilization that preserves its segmental blood supply along the thigh, allows the muscle to be rotated medially over the exposed vessels like a hinged door; the proximal 10 to 12 cm of muscle is typically sufficient for this coverage. Dissection carried medially beyond the first preserved segmental pedicle risks muscle necrosis.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup> In three patients with bulky penile-cancer nodal metastases and skin breakdown, Tabatabaei and McDougal combined sartorius vessel coverage with abdominal cutaneous advancement; all three wounds healed primarily, and postoperative radiation to one flap site caused no reported loss.<sup>[[2]](#ref2)</sup> Bilateral use was proposed but is not documented in the accessible abstract.

## Skin and Soft-Tissue Coverage

| Defect pattern | Reported option | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| **Large anterior groin skin defect** | Abdominal cutaneous advancement flap | All three reported penile-cancer wounds healed primarily; the authors suggested bilateral use and described simplicity, lower morbidity and favorable cosmesis, but the abstract documents no bilateral case or comparative test of those advantages.<sup>[[2]](#ref2)</sup> |
| **Medial groin / genitocrural skin defect** | Scrotal advancement flap | Used in men when adjacent scrotal skin laxity allows advancement to cover a medial defect; attributed operative description, not a comparative series<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup> |
| **Composite defect with exposed vessels and soft-tissue deficit** | Regional flap ([anterolateral thigh](/docs/foundations/surgical-principles/flaps/anterolateral-thigh), [tensor fasciae latae](/docs/foundations/surgical-principles/flaps/tfl), [gracilis](/docs/foundations/surgical-principles/flaps/gracilis) or [vertical rectus abdominis myocutaneous](/docs/foundations/surgical-principles/flaps/vram)); weigh abdominal donor-site hernia risk when considering VRAM<sup>[[9]](#ref9)</sup>; add sartorius transposition when separate vessel interposition is needed | In a 15-patient mixed-malignancy series (four penile cancers), pedicled TFL covered bare groin vessels after block dissection; two flaps had marginal necrosis, two patients had wound infection, and three donor sites had partial skin-graft loss. Select coverage according to defect size, depth, prior radiation and the vascular territory left intact by resection; this series specifies a viable pedicle outside the resection or planned radiation field.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[7]](#ref7)</sup> |

A 25-patient retrospective comparison of primary closure (28 groins) with elective TFL coverage (20 groins) in patients without skin involvement reported skin-flap necrosis after ilioinguinal dissection in 75% and 17% of groins, respectively; treatment selection and small subgroups limit inference.<sup>[[10]](#ref10)</sup> This study did not compare regional flaps or address established groin defects. Flap selection depends on defect size, depth, prior radiation and the vascular territory left intact by dissection.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[2]](#ref2)</sup>

## Lymphatic Complications

Lymphocele, lymphorrhea and chronic lower-extremity lymphedema are common after inguinal lymphadenectomy and are a separate problem from the acute wound-coverage issues above; the fascial-sparing series reported 104 lymphedema cases among 421 patients (24.7%), rather than 24.7% of groins.<sup>[[4]](#ref4)</sup> Evaluation and management of established lower-extremity or genital lymphedema, including compression, complex decongestive therapy and lymphatic microsurgery, are covered on [Genital Lymphedema](/docs/clinical-conditions/03g-genital-scrotal/genital-lymphedema) and on the lymphedema-specific reconstructive pages in this atlas.
