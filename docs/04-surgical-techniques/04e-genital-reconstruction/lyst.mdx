# Lymphatic System Transfer (LYST)

**LYST** deliberately transfers vascularized lymph nodes together with mapped afferent lymphatic vessels, usually within a superficial circumflex iliac artery perforator (SCIP) flap. It is an evolving specialist option for selected patients with lymphedema. Published limb cohorts are small and uncontrolled; they do not establish faster recovery, superior drainage or freedom from compression compared with other operations.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[2]](#ref2)[[3]](#ref3)</sup>

For treatment selection see [Genital Lymphedema](/docs/clinical-conditions/03g-genital-scrotal/genital-lymphedema), [Complex Decongestive Therapy](/docs/surgical-techniques/04e-genital-reconstruction/complex-decongestive-therapy), [LVA](/docs/surgical-techniques/04e-genital-reconstruction/lymphaticovenous-anastomosis) and [VLNT](/docs/surgical-techniques/04e-genital-reconstruction/vascularized-lymph-node-transfer). Genital SCIP reconstruction is discussed separately in [3R / SCIP-LIFT](/docs/surgical-techniques/04e-genital-reconstruction/3r-scip-lft), [Abdelfattah's pedicled transfer](/docs/surgical-techniques/04e-genital-reconstruction/complete-functional-lymphatic-pedicled-transfer) and [CHASCIP](/docs/surgical-techniques/04e-genital-reconstruction/chascip).

## Distinguish the procedures

| Procedure | Intended lymphatic tissue |
|---|---|
| **VLNT** | Vascularized nodes and surrounding tissue; afferent vessels may be present without being deliberately mapped into a long unit |
| **LYST** | Nodes plus deliberately mapped afferent collectors, maintained within vascularized tissue |
| **LIFT / lymphatic vessel transfer** | Vascularized collector-bearing tissue without intentional node harvest |
| **LVA** | Direct microsurgical connection between suitable recipient lymphatics and veins |

LYST aims to retain an organized drainage unit, but that unit still needs to integrate with the recipient lymphatic environment. Demonstrating dye passage within the harvested flap does not establish immediate drainage of the recipient limb. Proposed lymphangiogenic and immunologic mechanisms do not prove comparative clinical benefit.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[4]](#ref4)</sup>

Names such as "lymphatic SCIP flap" are used for different designs. The author description of genital 3R / SCIP-LIFT preserves nodes around the vascular pedicle and transfers collector-bearing tissue; it should not be automatically relabeled as node-containing LYST.<sup>[[5]](#ref5)</sup>

## Biological rationale

Transferred afferent vessels are proposed to provide pre-formed conduits and to reduce the new lymphatic growth needed before the flap functions. The 2020 and 2026 reports describe this as a presumed advantage; neither compares LYST with VLNT.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[3]](#ref3)</sup> The supporting biology comes mostly from node-transfer studies, not from LYST itself:

- **Lymphatic reconnection.** In a murine model, transferred nodes showed rapid infiltration by recipient lymphatics, putative donor-recipient connections, preserved T- and B-cell zones and increased VEGF-C in perinodal fat.<sup>[[11]](#ref11)</sup>
- **Human histology.** In 14 patients with gastroepiploic node transfer, podoplanin-positive vessel density in skin biopsies rose from 7.92 ± 1.77 to 11.79 ± 3.38 vessels/mm² at 12 months (p = 0.0008).<sup>[[12]](#ref12)</sup>
- **Cytokine profile.** In wound exudate after axillary surgery, flap transfer with lymph nodes was associated with a lower proinflammatory response than axillary dissection alone and with increased IL-10 and VEGF-C.<sup>[[13]](#ref13)</sup>

These are murine, biopsy and biomarker findings. They do not show that LYST restores immune function or drains the limb faster than VLNT.

## Selection and planning

Consider reconstructive lymphatic surgery after evaluating the cause and distribution of edema, venous disease, fibrosis, recurrent infection, functional impairment and response to appropriate conservative care. Imaging and examination should identify usable lymphatic pathways and assess the donor region. No single ICG dermal-backflow pattern or ISL stage automatically selects LYST, VLNT or LVA; ISL's physical stages primarily describe extremities.<sup>[[4]](#ref4)[[6]](#ref6)</sup>

Planning questions:

- Can suitable lymphatics support LVA, or is tissue transfer being considered because of a more extensive defect or lack of usable channels?
- Is there a safe, perfused donor unit with an appropriate vascular pedicle? Prior groin dissection, radiation or existing leg edema can change feasibility.
- Does a pedicled flap reach without compression or twisting, or is free transfer required?
- Is a separate excisional or skin-reconstruction procedure needed for irreversible genital disease?

High-frequency ultrasound can supplement ICG mapping by showing vessels and soft-tissue anatomy. Published ultrasound-planning experience supports feasibility, not a universal equipment requirement or guaranteed identification of every collector.<sup>[[4]](#ref4)[[6]](#ref6)</sup>

## Groin lymphatic anatomy

The superficial inguinal nodes form three subgroups: abdominal, medial thigh and lateral thigh. In cadaver dissections the dominant nodes draining the leg lie in the lower part of the inguinal triangle, and their efferents run medial to the common femoral artery.<sup>[[14]](#ref14)</sup>

| Subgroup or structure | Drainage and features | Relevance to harvest |
|---|---|---|
| **Abdominal (superolateral) nodes** | Lower abdominal wall, lower back, upper gluteal region and the local thigh collectors (0.3–0.5 mm, thin-walled) | Usual target nodes for a groin flap<sup>[[15]](#ref15)</sup> |
| **Medial thigh nodes** | Ventromedial thigh bundle (0.6–1 mm, thick-walled) draining to two nodes inferolateral to the saphenous bulb | Sentinel nodes of the lower leg; preserve them<sup>[[14]](#ref14)[[15]](#ref15)</sup> |
| **Lower abdominal collectors** | Above Scarpa's fascia immediately below the subdermal venules; thin-walled, 0.2–0.8 mm | Fall within the SCIP territory when the flap carries the superolateral nodes<sup>[[15]](#ref15)</sup> |

The cadaver data support limiting medial dissection to the lateral border of the femoral artery.<sup>[[15]](#ref15)</sup> This landmark does not replace reverse mapping; leg drainage reached a superficial inguinal region in 38.1% of patients in one SPECT/CT series.<sup>[[8]](#ref8)</sup>

## Donor protection

A node-harvest safe zone is not defined by surface landmarks alone. Lower-limb drainage can reach superficial inguinal nodes. Reverse lymphatic mapping identifies nodes and channels draining the donor leg so that they can be preserved; mapping of the proposed flap's afferent system serves a different purpose.<sup>[[7]](#ref7)[[8]](#ref8)</sup>

The original description of reverse mapping uses two tracers.<sup>[[7]](#ref7)</sup>

| Tracer | Injection site | Purpose |
|---|---|---|
| Technetium (first and second foot web spaces) | Foot | Locate nodes draining the leg with a gamma probe; avoid them |
| ICG (intradermal) | Lower abdomen | Trace lymphatics to the abdominal-draining nodes for harvest |

In 39 patients, ICG in the foot web spaces with Patent Blue near the skin paddle gave the same navigation of the main leg-draining nodes as technetium, and no donor-site lymphedema was reported.<sup>[[16]](#ref16)</sup>

The original free-LYST report used lower-limb technetium mapping and separate flank ICG injections. Tracer choice and injection sites must follow the specialist mapping protocol. In an 84-patient planning study, lower-limb drainage reached at least one superficial inguinal region in 38.1% of patients. No donor-leg lymphedema was observed after the 58 mapped flap harvests during the reported follow-up, but this does not establish zero lifetime risk.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[8]](#ref8)</sup>

Donor counseling should include lymphedema, lymphatic leak or seroma, wound problems and the possibility that mapping will prevent use of the planned donor. Reverse mapping reduces avoidable injury; it cannot eliminate all risk.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[6]](#ref6)</sup>

## Operative principles

The original report describes free SCIP-based LYST. These principles identify the intended operation; they are not a substitute for patient-specific flap and lymphatic mapping.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[4]](#ref4)</sup>

1. **Map before harvest.** Identify the afferent collector-bearing tissue, its nodal endpoint, arterial supply and venous outflow while preserving donor-leg drainage.
2. **Preserve the mapped unit.** Raise vascularized tissue containing the selected collectors and nodes. Flap thickness follows their actual location; aggressive thinning can remove the target lymphatics or compromise perfusion.
3. **Prepare a suitable recipient bed.** Release relevant scar when indicated and plan the flap orientation and vascular connections. Avoid a tight tunnel, pedicle torsion and pressure on the inset.
4. **Inset according to the mapped anatomy.** In the original series most of the skin paddle was de-epithelialized, with a small portion kept for monitoring, and the flap was placed in a subcutaneous tunnel in the limb. Upper-limb flaps were anastomosed to the axillary artery and vein; the lower-limb flap was connected to a medial lower-leg perforator.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup>
5. **Monitor vascular and wound recovery.** Plan compression and rehabilitation with the lymphatic team after protecting the healing flap and anastomoses.

### Pedicled SCIP-LYST (Xu and colleagues)

The 2026 report leaves the SCIP flap attached to its pedicle, which the authors state removes the flap anastomosis and shortens the operation. Its population was six patients with lower-limb lymphedema and chronic venous disease.<sup>[[3]](#ref3)</sup> Five also had a distal lymphovenous bypass, so the flap and the bypass are two levels of one reconstruction:

| Level | Operation | Intended function |
|---|---|---|
| Proximal | Pedicled SCIP-LYST | Nodes and afferent vessels at the groin |
| Distal | LVA in the lower limb | Direct lymphatic-to-venous bypass |

The authors propose that a proximal flap drains efferent to the site of venous obstruction, whereas a distal free VLNT would sit within the zone of venous hypertension. This is a stated rationale, not a demonstrated advantage. A pedicled flap avoids a free-flap vascular anastomosis but not microsurgery when a bypass is added, and a benefit specifically from the pedicled flap or from bypassing venous disease cannot be isolated from this combined series.<sup>[[3]](#ref3)</sup>

### Zone 4 lymphatic SCIP flap with DIEP breast reconstruction (Yoshimatsu)

The Zone 4 region of a lower abdominal flap overlies the SCIP territory. In seven women having DIEP reconstruction after axillary dissection, a separate lymphatic SCIP flap was raised from Zone 4 and transferred to the axilla without a further donor site. All flaps survived. No lymphedema developed in the four women treated preventively (mean follow-up 37.5 months). In the three with established lymphedema, the mean estimated volume decrease was 12.6% at 29.0 months.<sup>[[17]](#ref17)</sup> An earlier series of nine women with lymphedema having lower-abdominal flap breast reconstruction with nodes and vessels around the superficial circumflex pedicle reported some lymphoscintigraphic improvement in 5 of 6 patients and no further need for physiotherapy or compression in 3 of 9.<sup>[[18]](#ref18)</sup>

### Perforator-to-perforator SCIP node transfer (Meroni and Scaglioni)

This variant anastomoses the SCIP-based inguinal node flap to recipient perforators. It preserves major recipient vessels and places the nodes more superficially, which the authors suggest may improve drainage. In 12 patients with late stage II lymphedema (11 lower limb; 8 also had LVA), lymphoscintigraphy at 6 months confirmed transferred-node function in all, and no donor or recipient complications occurred with follow-up of at least 12 months.<sup>[[19]](#ref19)</sup> The paper describes a node transfer, so it is not LYST as defined above.

### Lymphatic flow-through SCIP flap (Scaglioni)

A pedicled SCIP flap is turned 180 degrees to match the direction of recipient lymphatics, and LVAs connect damaged recipient lymphatics to a superficial flap vein. This covers groin or thigh defects while restoring lymph flow. In four patients (three tumor excisions, one trauma; mean follow-up 8 months), one small infected seroma occurred and no lymphedema or lymphocele was reported.<sup>[[20]](#ref20)</sup> A related series of 15 sarcoma reconstructions combined perforator flaps with lymphatic procedures: infected seroma occurred in 3 and lymphedema in 2 (mean follow-up 19.6 months).<sup>[[21]](#ref21)</sup>

## Clinical evidence

| Report | Population and findings | Interpretation |
|---|---|---|
| **Original LYST, 2020** | Three women: two affected arms and one affected leg. All flaps survived; no donor complication was observed during mean 11-month follow-up. Compression resumed four weeks after surgery. | Proof of concept. No control group; continued conservative treatment and short follow-up limit conclusions.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup> |
| **Longer-term LYST, 2025** | Eight unilateral lower-limb patients; mean follow-up 39 months. The abstract reports improvement in excess-volume percentage and fewer cellulitis episodes. | Small retrospective limb cohort. It does not establish genital efficacy or superiority over VLNT/LVA.<sup>[[2]](#ref2)</sup> |
| **Pedicled LYST, 2026** | Six patients with limb lymphedema and chronic venous disease; five also had distal bypass. Mean follow-up 31 weeks; mean LLIS and L-Dex decreases were 15 points and 30 units. No immediate complications were reported. | Combined treatment, short follow-up and no comparator. "No immediate complications" is not zero complications over time.<sup>[[3]](#ref3)</sup> |
| **Zone 4 lymphatic SCIP with DIEP, 2022** | Seven women; four preventive, three with established lymphedema. | Proof of concept with no comparator.<sup>[[17]](#ref17)</sup> |
| **SCIP lymphatic vessel free flap, 2021** | Eleven patients with posttraumatic limb lymphedema or acute soft-tissue trauma with lymphatic damage; minimum follow-up 12 months; retrospective multicenter series. No flap failures. In the treatment group, mean excess-volume reduction was 63.01% and quality of life improved 51.85%; no patient in the acute-trauma (preventive) group developed lymphedema. | Small observational series of a vessel flap without nodes, not LYST proper.<sup>[[22]](#ref22)</sup> |

Genital series reporting excision plus lymphatic SCIP reconstruction use different flap designs and outcome measures. Their results cannot be pooled as LYST outcomes or compared with historical excision recurrence rates to prove a causal benefit of transferred nodes. The 26-patient Abdelfattah report is covered on its [dedicated page](/docs/surgical-techniques/04e-genital-reconstruction/complete-functional-lymphatic-pedicled-transfer).<sup>[[5]](#ref5)[[9]](#ref9)</sup>

Three genital series used pedicled lymphatic SCIP flaps after excision of fibrotic tissue in men. Each is a small uncontrolled series with a different flap design, so they cannot be pooled.

| Series | n | Follow-up | Reconstruction | Reported outcome |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| [Abdelfattah](/docs/surgical-techniques/04e-genital-reconstruction/complete-functional-lymphatic-pedicled-transfer) | 26 | mean 44.9 months | Pedicled SCIP lymphatic flap for scrotum with or without penile skin | Flap survival 100%; genital lymphedema score 6.2 to 0.05 (p &lt; 0.001); median Glasgow Benefit Inventory +41<sup>[[9]](#ref9)</sup> |
| [Yamamoto 3R](/docs/surgical-techniques/04e-genital-reconstruction/3r-scip-lft) | 7 | mean 22.7 months | Pedicled full-thickness SCIP lymphatic flap for scrotum; SCIP pure-skin flap for penis in 3 | No postoperative genital complication or recurrence; score 6.7 to 0.3; no compression applied<sup>[[23]](#ref23)</sup> |
| [Ciudad CHASCIP](/docs/surgical-techniques/04e-genital-reconstruction/chascip) | 8 | mean 34 months | Charles procedure with bilateral pedicled lymphatic SCIP flaps | Complications in 2 (25%: seroma with dehiscence; partial graft loss); no recurrence; score 6.6 to 0.6<sup>[[24]](#ref24)</sup> |

Absence of recurrence over these follow-up periods does not show that the transferred nodes caused the result; none of the series compares with excision alone.

## LYST and VLNT

No study compares LYST with VLNT directly. The table lists what separate series report for each.

| Feature | VLNT | LYST |
|---|---|---|
| Tissue transferred | Nodes with perinodal fat | Nodes, mapped afferent vessels, perinodal fat and a skin paddle |
| Pedicled option | Uncommon | Described (Xu 2026; genital series) |
| Reported limb outcomes | Prospective series (89 patients, 73% omental): 20% mean limb-volume reduction, 93% fewer cellulitis episodes and 34% off compression at 2 years.<sup>[[25]](#ref25)</sup> Series of 134 (43 groin flaps): 45.7% limb-volume change and 97.9% fewer cellulitis episodes at 24 months.<sup>[[26]](#ref26)</sup> | 8 patients: 11.2% mean excess-volume improvement and fewer cellulitis episodes at 39 months<sup>[[2]](#ref2)</sup> |
| Donor-site lymphedema | Not seen with omentum; a groin donor requires reverse mapping<sup>[[25]](#ref25)[[8]](#ref8)</sup> | None observed with mapping in the small series above |

The VLNT series mostly used non-groin donors, so their figures are not a like-for-like benchmark. In a retrospective comparison of LVA (123 patients) and VLNT (28) for lower-limb lymphedema, LVA improved faster in the first 3 months, and VLNT improved more slowly over 1 year, narrowing the gap.<sup>[[27]](#ref27)</sup> Whether LYST combines the early profile of LVA with the sustained profile of VLNT has not been tested.

A study of 31 women undergoing bilateral inguinofemoral dissection for vulvar cancer found less limb-volume increase on the side receiving a lymphatic SCIP reconstruction. The flap side was selected for reconstructive need, not randomized. This is not evidence for routine prophylactic LYST during prostate or bladder pelvic lymphadenectomy.<sup>[[10]](#ref10)</sup>

## Experimental adjuncts

Perinodal VEGF-C improved lymphatic regrowth and preserved transferred-node structure in a pig model, whereas control-treated nodes regressed and were partly replaced by fibrofatty scar.<sup>[[28]](#ref28)</sup> In mice, VEGF-C gave the best lymphangiogenic response among the VEGF-family factors tested.<sup>[[29]](#ref29)</sup> Periodic perinodal hyaluronidase injection increased lymphangiogenesis and reduced fibrosis around vascularized node flaps in rats.<sup>[[30]](#ref30)</sup> These are animal studies; no clinical trial of an adjunct with LYST has been reported.

## Limitations of the evidence

- The largest LYST-specific limb series has 8 patients, and the pedicled series has 6.<sup>[[2]](#ref2)[[3]](#ref3)</sup>
- No comparative study of LYST and VLNT exists.
- Outcome measures differ (excess-volume percentage, L-Dex, LLIS, genital lymphedema score, Glasgow Benefit Inventory), and follow-up ranges from 31 weeks to 44.9 months.
- Most series report clinical measures, not objective postoperative lymphatic-transport imaging.
- The operation requires expertise in SCIP anatomy, ICG lymphography, reverse lymphatic mapping and, for free transfer, supermicrosurgery.

## Follow-up and counseling

Document the treated limb or genital symptoms, episodes of cellulitis, objective measurements when appropriate, quality of life, compression use and both donor and recipient complications. Maintain skin care and reassess compression with the treating team; an improved score alone does not justify stopping it. Persistent or recurrent symptoms require evaluation of residual fibrosis, venous disease, lymphatic function and reconstructive complications.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[6]](#ref6)</sup>

The available evidence supports feasibility in selected patients. It does not establish a universal recovery timeline, cure, guaranteed immune restoration or superiority of node-containing transfer over other lymphatic reconstruction.
