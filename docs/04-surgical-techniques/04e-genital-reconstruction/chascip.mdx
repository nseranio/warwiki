# CHASCIP — Charles Excision and Bilateral Lymphatic SCIP Flap Transfer

**CHASCIP** is a single-stage operation for ISL stage III penoscrotal lymphedema that combines a Charles procedure (excision of the lymphedematous genital skin and subcutaneous tissue) with bilateral pedicled lymphatic superficial circumflex iliac artery perforator (SCIP) flaps. Ciudad and colleagues reported eight men in 2025.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup> It is an early reconstructive option. Comparative superiority and durable freedom from recurrence have not been established.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup>

The broader assessment and alternatives are covered in [Genital Lymphedema](/docs/clinical-conditions/03g-genital-scrotal/genital-lymphedema), [Complex Decongestive Therapy](/docs/surgical-techniques/04e-genital-reconstruction/complex-decongestive-therapy), [Debulking Scrotoplasty](/docs/surgical-techniques/04e-genital-reconstruction/debulking-scrotoplasty), and [3R / SCIP-LFT](/docs/surgical-techniques/04e-genital-reconstruction/3r-scip-lft).

## Clinical role

Excision relieves the mechanical burden of fibrotic genital enlargement, and a vascularized lymphatic flap provides coverage and may support drainage. Excision alone gives volume reduction without lymphatic reconstruction. The 3R operation uses a single pedicled SCIP flap for the scrotum, whereas CHASCIP uses a flap from each groin.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[4]](#ref4)</sup> Neither a functioning flap nor successful debulking proves normal lymphatic transport, and follow-up remains necessary.<sup>[[2]](#ref2)[[3]](#ref3)</sup>

Selection depends on symptoms, tissue fibrosis, the available skin envelope, remaining lymphatic pathways, groin vascular anatomy, prior surgery or radiation, and the response to conservative care. ISL stage alone does not select a named operation. Extensive disease may require excision, functioning collectors may permit a lymphatic bypass, and healthy local skin may permit a simpler reconstruction.<sup>[[2]](#ref2)[[3]](#ref3)</sup>

The index series enrolled men with ISL stage III disease involving both scrotum and penis. Lymphedema was secondary in 75% and primary in 25%. Preoperative work-up included ICG lymphography and lymphoscintigraphy, and the authors recorded symptom duration and the pre- and postoperative duration of complex decongestive therapy.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup>

## Planning and operative outline

- Define the penile and scrotal defects separately. Excise the lymphedematous tissue to the required depth, and preserve the urethra, erectile structures, testes, spermatic cords and viable tissues.<sup>[[3]](#ref3)</sup>
- Map the proposed flaps' blood supply and relevant lymphatic pathways with ICG lymphography. Previous groin surgery, lymphadenectomy or radiation can affect feasibility.<sup>[[2]](#ref2)[[3]](#ref3)</sup>
- Raise a pedicled lymphatic SCIP flap from each groin. These are pedicled flaps, and no microvascular anastomosis is needed.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup>
- Inset the flaps to reconstruct the scrotum. The report describes skin-graft coverage for the penile shaft, with partial graft loss in one patient.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup>
- Distinguish a lymphatic-vessel-bearing SCIP flap from a flap that deliberately includes lymph nodes. These are different designs, and the LYST design transfers nodes together with their afferent vessels.<sup>[[2]](#ref2)[[5]](#ref5)</sup> Routine inclusion of superficial inguinal nodes is not required for every lymphatic SCIP reconstruction, and donor-leg drainage must be protected.<sup>[[2]](#ref2)[[3]](#ref3)</sup>
- The penis also has lymphatic drainage. Choose penile graft or flap coverage according to the defect, donor condition and reconstructive plan.<sup>[[3]](#ref3)</sup>

The abstract does not give the graft thickness, graft donor site, node-harvest template or pedicle dissection, and this page does not prescribe them. General excision and coverage principles are in [Modified Charles Procedure](/docs/surgical-techniques/04e-genital-reconstruction/modified-charles-procedure) and [Penile Skin Grafting](/docs/surgical-techniques/04e-genital-reconstruction/penile-skin-grafting).<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup>

## Index series

| Parameter | Reported result |
|---|---|
| Patients | 8 men, all ISL stage III |
| Mean age and BMI | 43 years; 28.4 kg/m² |
| Mean follow-up | 34 months |
| Mean weight of resected tissue | 1772.7 g |
| Mean estimated blood loss | 200.6 mL |
| Mean surgical time | 160 minutes |
| Complications | 2 of 8: one seroma with dehiscence, one partial skin-graft loss |
| Recurrence | None observed |
| Genital Lymphedema Score | Mean 6.6 before and 0.6 after (p &lt; 0.001) |
| Sexual dysfunction | 87.5% before and 0% after (p &lt; 0.001) |

These findings come from the index report.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup> The series is small and uncontrolled. The absence of recurrence is a follow-up observation, and the GLS change does not separate the contribution of the flaps from that of the excision. Sexual-function change was assessed without a stated validated instrument in the abstract.

For comparison, the 3R series of seven men (mean follow-up 22.7 months) reported a mean resected weight of 1511 g, no postoperative genital complication or recurrence, and a GLS change from 6.7 to 0.3.<sup>[[4]](#ref4)</sup> Operative time, blood loss and symptom scores cannot establish superiority of any of these operations across unmatched series. Two flaps also do not demonstrate twice the lymphatic capacity.

## Follow-up

Assess wound and graft healing, flap perfusion, donor-site symptoms, recurrent swelling or lymphorrhea, cellulitis, urinary and sexual function, and the ongoing need for compression or other conservative treatment. Record patient-reported benefit and recurrence over time. Compression independence and complete sexual-function recovery cannot be promised on the current evidence.<sup>[[2]](#ref2)[[3]](#ref3)</sup>
