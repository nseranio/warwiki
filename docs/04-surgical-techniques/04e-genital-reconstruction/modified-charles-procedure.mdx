# Modified Charles Procedure

**“Modified Charles” describes several excisional operations, not one uniform reconstruction.** In limb surgery it usually denotes extensive removal of diseased skin and subcutaneous tissue followed by grafting. In the genital series of Modolin and colleagues, it meant excision followed by local scrotal reconstruction and penile split-thickness skin grafting. The operative report should state the actual excision, tissues preserved and method of coverage.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[2]](#ref2)</sup>

The operation is named for Sir Havelock Charles, who described radical excision of elephantiasis tissue for the lower limb in 1912. This page concerns penoscrotal lymphedema. See [debulking scrotoplasty](/docs/surgical-techniques/04e-genital-reconstruction/debulking-scrotoplasty) for the broader operation and [genital lymphedema](/docs/clinical-conditions/03g-genital-scrotal/genital-lymphedema) for diagnosis and treatment selection.

## Etiology of Penoscrotal Lymphedema

Acquired causes include filariasis in endemic regions, inguinal or pelvic lymphadenectomy with or without radiotherapy for genitourinary or gynecologic malignancy, chronic infection, and morbid obesity (massive localized lymphedema).<sup>[[4]](#ref4)[[11]](#ref11)[[12]](#ref12)</sup> Primary lymphatic dysplasia is the other main group. In one German referral series of 51 men, 43 (84%) had primary lymphedema.<sup>[[13]](#ref13)</sup>

## Selection and Planning

Excision can address disabling fibrotic enlargement, diseased skin, lymphorrhea and recurrent infection when conservative measures do not adequately control the problem. It does not require every patient to fail LVA and VLNT first. Reconstructive choice depends on the residual healthy tissue, functional lymphatics, prior surgery or radiation, comorbidity and goals. ISL stages were formulated for the extremities; “stage II” alone is not a genital indication for radical resection.<sup>[[3]](#ref3)</sup>

Before surgery:

- Assess the cause, skin condition, extent of penile involvement, previous groin treatment and possible recurrent malignancy. Treat active infection and optimize modifiable perioperative risks.
- Use [decongestive therapy](/docs/surgical-techniques/04e-genital-reconstruction/complex-decongestive-therapy) when feasible to address the fluid component and skin care. A fixed inpatient course is not mandatory for every genital operation.
- Plan exposure of the penis, urethra, testes and cords before committing to the resection. Identify usable local skin and a backup coverage option; do not assume that all remaining skin is healthy.
- Explain the possibilities of wound breakdown, graft loss, altered sensation, recurrence and further surgery. The objective is functional improvement; lymphedema care continues afterward.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[3]](#ref3)[[4]](#ref4)</sup>

## Genital Operative Principles

1. **Define the diseased envelope and protect deeper structures.** Excise affected skin and fibrotic subcutaneous tissue while identifying and protecting viable testes, spermatic cords, penile structures and urethra. The limb instruction to excise to or through deep muscular fascia is not a genital dissection rule. Preserve useful vascularized tissue rather than applying circumferential fascial stripping by default.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup>
2. **Reconstruct the scrotum according to the defect.** Healthy adjacent scrotal or perineal skin may allow tension-free closure or local flaps. If it does not, choose a graft or another flap based on the recipient bed and coverage requirements. The term “modified Charles” does not require scrotal grafting.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[4]](#ref4)</sup>
3. **Resurface the penis when its skin must be removed.** A penile STSG is an established option. Provide a vascularized bed, adequate coverage for shaft expansion, hemostasis and secure fixation without constriction. Modolin's ventral zigzag suture is a described technique, and prevention of contracture has not been shown. In several series the healthy inner prepuce was used to cover the distal shaft when available.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[11]](#ref11)</sup> See [penile skin grafting](/docs/surgical-techniques/04e-genital-reconstruction/penile-skin-grafting).<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup>
4. **Treat associated pathology on its own merits.** Assess a hydrocele, hernia or suspicious lesion rather than automatically performing hydrocelectomy or orchiectomy. Testes and spermatic cords are spared in the series. No orchiectomy was needed in Wisenbaugh's series, including specimens up to 61 kg, and hydrocelectomy accompanied resection in 22 of 51 men in Torio-Padron's series.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[4]](#ref4)[[11]](#ref11)[[12]](#ref12)[[13]](#ref13)</sup>
5. **Select the dressing and timing.** Conventional bolsters and carefully applied negative-pressure dressings are options for graft fixation. Staged wound preparation and NPWT used over an already placed graft are different strategies; neither has a universal five-to-seven-day genital protocol.<sup>[[5]](#ref5)[[6]](#ref6)</sup>

:::warning[Do not transfer limb graft data to genital counseling]
The historical five-patient series reporting three amputations involved legs, not genital reconstruction. It cannot establish that full-thickness grafts are universally safer than STSGs for the penis or scrotum. Similarly, limb graft-take percentages and hospital stays should not be presented as expected genital outcomes.<sup>[[7]](#ref7)</sup>
:::

## What the Genital Evidence Shows

The literature largely consists of small, uncontrolled series with different causes, disease severity and reconstructive methods. It supports the feasibility of substantial functional improvement, but cannot reliably rank operations or establish a universal recurrence rate.

| Report | Findings and limits |
|---|---|
| **Modolin 2006** | Seventeen patients underwent excision, local scrotoplasty and penile STSG. Follow-up ranged from six months to six years. Function and hygiene improved; one patient previously treated with penile-cancer lymphadenectomy and radiotherapy developed recurrent scrotal lymphedema. Outcomes were not a controlled comparison or a validated sexual-function assessment.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup> |
| **Singh 2011** | Forty-eight men with penile or penoscrotal filarial lymphedema (14 isolated penile, 34 penoscrotal) had excision with STSG, preserving inner prepuce where possible, and scrotoplasty with lateral and posterior mobilized flaps. Median follow-up was 48 months. All reported satisfactory cosmesis, better standing voiding and ambulation, and resumed sexual activity. Twelve had local groin infection, all cured.<sup>[[11]](#ref11)</sup> |
| **Salako 2018** | Eleven of 19 presenting patients underwent surgery in a prospective study by a combined urology and plastic surgery team. Three developed hematoma, two superficial infection and one recurrence within 24 months. These are cohort-specific observations and not an expected complication percentage.<sup>[[8]](#ref8)</sup> |
| **Wisenbaugh 2018** | Eleven men with massive localized scrotal lymphedema (mean BMI 60, mean resected weight 21 kg, mean follow-up 26 months). One needed skin grafting and the rest were closed primarily. Wound complications were common. Overall quality of life rose from 1.3 to 7.7 of 10, although mean weight increased by 5.2 kg.<sup>[[12]](#ref12)</sup> |
| **Torio-Padron 2015** | Fifty-one men had perioperative complex decongestive physiotherapy with surgical reduction. Defects were closed primarily with adjacent skin, without flaps or grafts. Three (6%) needed revision, for hematoma in two and dehiscence in one. Glasgow Benefit Inventory scores improved for general functioning and physical health.<sup>[[13]](#ref13)</sup> |
| **Stokes 2006** | Eight men underwent penile degloving, reduction scrotoplasty and penile STSG. The six most recent cases received negative-pressure graft dressings and had complete reported graft take. This small series does not prove superiority over another dressing or guarantee 100% take.<sup>[[6]](#ref6)</sup> |

The van der Walt report often cited for delayed grafting after five to seven days of NPWT involved eight patients with severe primary limb lymphedema; three required additional grafting. It should not be conflated with the penile graft-bolster series above.<sup>[[5]](#ref5)</sup>

## Combining Excision With Lymphatic Reconstruction

LVA, VLNT or a lymphatic SCIP flap may be considered in selected patients by an experienced lymphatic reconstruction team. These procedures aim to improve drainage in addition to reducing tissue bulk. Current genital evidence does not establish that adding them routinely is superior to a well-selected excisional reconstruction or that it eliminates compression, recurrence or donor-site morbidity.<sup>[[3]](#ref3)[[4]](#ref4)</sup>

For example, the 2025 CHASCIP series combined excision with bilateral pedicled lymphatic SCIP flaps in eight men. At a mean 34-month follow-up no recurrence was observed, but two patients had postoperative complications: seroma or dehiscence in one and partial graft loss in another. There was no excision-only control group. Sexual dysfunction resolved in all affected patients (87.5% before, 0% after) and the Genital Lymphedema Score fell from 6.6 to 0.6. These are feasibility findings and do not establish a new standard.<sup>[[9]](#ref9)</sup>

Other small series report similar approaches without a control group:

| Series | Approach and findings |
|---|---|
| **Yamamoto 2022 (3R)** | Seven men had radical reduction and reconstruction with SCIP lymphatic flap transfer and no postoperative compression. There were no genital complications or recurrences at mean 22.7 months, and the Genital Lymphedema Score fell from 6.7 to 0.3.<sup>[[14]](#ref14)</sup> |
| **Ehrl 2023** | Nine men with penoscrotal lymphedema had resection and reconstruction, five with free vascularized lymph node transfer to the groin or scrotum. Median follow-up was 49 months with no recurrence. Scrotal transfer improved scrotal lymphatic transport. The authors propose a treatment-oriented classification to select resection alone or with transfer.<sup>[[15]](#ref15)</sup> |
| **Abdelfattah 2023** | Twenty-six men had excision with a pedicled SCIP lymphatic flap. Flap survival was 100% at mean follow-up of 44.9 months. Cellulitis rates fell (P &lt; .001) and the Genital Lymphedema Score fell from 6.2 to 0.05.<sup>[[16]](#ref16)</sup> |

Excision debulks tissue and does not bypass the lymphatic block, yet drainage can sometimes improve afterward. In a small genital MRL cohort, postoperative imaging showed reopening of functional pathways after excision. This observation does not establish a cure or validate imaging as a universal selection algorithm.<sup>[[10]](#ref10)</sup>

## Multidisciplinary Care and Access

Reconstruction is usually performed by a combined urology and plastic surgery team. The urologist protects the testes, cords and urethra, and the plastic surgeon plans soft-tissue coverage and any lymphatic reconstruction. Salako's authors recommend combined urologic and plastic surgical care.<sup>[[8]](#ref8)</sup> In a 2022 US cross-sectional analysis of 67 insurers, 13 (19.4%) covered surgical debulking and 5 (7.5%) covered physiologic procedures.<sup>[[17]](#ref17)</sup>

## Recovery and Follow-up

Monitor perfusion, hematoma, infection, urinary drainage and graft or flap adherence. Dressing changes, mobilization, drains and catheter removal follow the actual reconstruction and wound condition; limb-series hospital stays are not a genital discharge requirement. Persistent skin disease, graft contraction, lymphorrhea or recurrent enlargement warrants reassessment.

Continue skin care, and support or compression with the lymphedema team as needed. A successful operation does not guarantee that all long-term therapy can stop. Equally, postoperative compression cannot be prescribed as an identical lifelong regimen for every genital graft or flap.<sup>[[3]](#ref3)[[4]](#ref4)</sup>
