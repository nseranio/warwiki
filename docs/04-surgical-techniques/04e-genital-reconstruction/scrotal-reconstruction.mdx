# Scrotal Reconstruction

**Scrotal reconstruction** aims for durable, comfortable coverage that protects the testes and preserves function. The repair is chosen from the viable tissue, closure tension, defect depth and extent, contamination, prior treatment and patient goals. The percentage of skin lost describes the defect but does not by itself determine the operation. Schifano's systematic review found that primary tension-free closure and local pedicled flaps allow good outcomes for most scrotal defects, and that STSG or distant flaps are needed only for extensive skin loss.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup>

## Anatomy that changes the plan

The scrotal wall contains thin, elastic skin and dartos, a layer that carries smooth muscle and the arterial network. The deeper coverings are the external spermatic fascia, cremasteric muscle and fascia, internal spermatic fascia and tunica vaginalis, then the tunica albuginea of the testis itself. Viable coverings are preserved, and cord or testicular dissection is kept to what is needed.<sup>[[2]](#ref2)[[3]](#ref3)</sup>

Carrera's study of 15 male cadavers described two lateral cutaneous territories supplied by the inferior external pudendal arteries and a central territory supplied by continuations of the perineal arteries. The central arteries reach the scrotum posteriorly and run deeply on both sides of the septum. The three territories are widely inter-anastomosed, which supports reliable median island scrotal flaps. Infected or extensively undermined tissue can still become ischemic.<sup>[[4]](#ref4)</sup>

Scrotal skin has overlapping regional sensory supply. Karaçal used proximally based neurovascular pudendal-thigh flaps for 10 scrotal defects (up to 20 × 15 cm), and Mopuri's modified pudendal-thigh flap gave protective sensation. Sensation, mobility and thermal function still cannot be assumed from the flap name alone.<sup>[[5]](#ref5)[[6]](#ref6)</sup>

## Assess the cause before choosing coverage

| Context | Main planning issue |
|---|---|
| Fournier's gangrene | Resuscitation, urgent source control and complete debridement precede definitive coverage. |
| Trauma or burns | Assess viability of the testes, cords, urethra and remaining skin, and preserve salvageable tissue. |
| Cancer resection | Coordinate margins, pathological assessment and reconstructive timing with the oncological plan. |
| Genital lymphedema | Separate fluid swelling from fibrotic and fatty tissue, and assess the lymphatic problem as well as the skin defect. Resected massive localized lymphedema has weighed up to 61 kg. |
| Hidradenitis suppurativa | Combine medical and surgical disease control. Deroofing or excision depends on the disease, and total scrotectomy is not automatic. |
| Gender-affirming scrotoplasty | Plan labia majora tissue, urethral work, perineal closure and implant staging together. |
| Webbing or bothersome laxity | Establish functional symptoms and goals. Techniques and outcome evidence differ from postinfectious reconstruction. |

These populations have different complication profiles, so one pooled complication table is not used.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[7]](#ref7)[[8]](#ref8)[[9]](#ref9)[[21]](#ref21)</sup>

## Choosing the reconstruction

### Primary closure or local scrotal advancement

Remaining healthy scrotal tissue is used when it reaches without excessive tension or compromise of perfusion. Some large defects close because of skin redundancy, and smaller defects with scarring or poor tissue may not. The commonly cited 50% threshold comes from review recommendations and case series. The EAU Fournier section cites a systematic review of 16 case series that recommended closure for defects up to 50% and flaps or grafts beyond it (level of evidence 3).<sup>[[2]](#ref2)[[17]](#ref17)</sup>

Mobilization is limited to what is needed, in the plane that preserves the skin–dartos blood supply. Septal division, gubernacular division and upward relocation of the testes are options and not prerequisites. If closure would compress the contents or endanger the blood supply, another method is chosen. Small suitable wounds may heal by secondary intention, accepting prolonged healing, contraction and deformity.<sup>[[2]](#ref2)</sup> See [Scrotal Primary Closure](/docs/surgical-techniques/04e-genital-reconstruction/scrotal-primary-closure) for the maneuvers and outcome series.

### Split-thickness skin graft

A graft is an established option for an extensive superficial defect with a well-vascularized recipient surface. Exposed testes do not automatically require a flap. Viable spermatic coverings or an appropriately prepared granulating surface may support a graft. Absence of tunica vaginalis alone is not a universally accepted prohibition, and the actual bed is assessed while the underlying structures are protected.<sup>[[2]](#ref2)[[10]](#ref10)[[3]](#ref3)</sup>

Hayon described a technique in 10 patients after necrotizing fasciitis. Orchidopexy with interrupted Vicryl followed the initial debridement, with cord mobilization and coiling of redundant cord under the abdominal wall. A graft 18/1000 inch thick and meshed 2:1 was applied once no further debridement was planned, and a bolster was sutured on for 5 to 7 days. At median 8 months there was no chronic pain, discomfort or need for further intervention. These are one series' parameters and not a universal protocol.<sup>[[11]](#ref11)</sup>

Tan preserved viable spermatic fascia, sutured the testes together with broad contact so the fascial layers fused into a single sac, and applied a wrap-around STSG. In 27 patients, 66% had thin, pliable scrotums that closely mimicked the original and 18% had well-formed sacs with slight adhesions.<sup>[[10]](#ref10)</sup> These are selected operative approaches. Testicular fixation, cord mobilization and cord coiling are not required in every patient, and discomfort, adhesions and altered mobility are discussed when relevant.

See [Scrotal STSG](/docs/surgical-techniques/04e-genital-reconstruction/scrotal-stsg) and [STSG principles](/docs/foundations/surgical-principles/grafts/stsg).

### Regional flap

A flap suits a bed that cannot support a graft, substantial dead space, or a deeper or combined perineal defect that needs durable vascularized coverage. The pedicle, thickness and tissue components are chosen for the defect and the available donor tissue.<sup>[[12]](#ref12)[[2]](#ref2)</sup>

Alammar's 2026 systematic review pooled 107 reports covering 619 patients and 625 flaps after Fournier's gangrene. Medial thigh, pudendal-thigh, ALT and gracilis flaps were used most, and regional flaps were used far more often than free tissue transfer. The indications were coverage of exposed vital structures (52%), functional restoration (39%) and cosmesis (4%). Flap loss was reported in 1.6% of cases. That endpoint is not the rate of all complications or partial necrosis, and a flap-only review cannot compare flaps with grafts. Standardized functional and cosmetic measures were rarely used.<sup>[[12]](#ref12)</sup>

| Flap | Reported experience |
|---|---|
| Pudendal-thigh (Singapore) | Mopuri's modified flap in 5 patients gave a natural-looking neoscrotum with protective sensation. Karaçal's neurovascular flaps in 10 defects closed all donor areas primarily.<sup>[[5]](#ref5)[[6]](#ref6)</sup> |
| Medial thigh and gracilis | Local tissue for advancement or a planned vascular flap. Muscle fills dead space or covers exposed structures.<sup>[[12]](#ref12)</sup> |
| Island groin | Sahai used it in 25 Fournier cases and 4 avulsions over about 30 years. A single flap covered defects larger than 50%, and total scrotal loss needed the groin flap plus two superior medial pedicle thigh flaps.<sup>[[28]](#ref28)</sup> |
| ALT | A fasciocutaneous flap that can be thinned, and one of the most common in Alammar's review.<sup>[[12]](#ref12)</sup> |
| SCIP | Three post-Fournier patients (largest defect 22 × 10 cm) all had surviving flaps without significant complications.<sup>[[13]](#ref13)</sup> |

SCIP-lymphatic transfer for lymphedema serves a different purpose from skin coverage after infection. Abdelfattah's 26 patients had 100% flap survival and fewer cellulitis episodes. Small technical cohorts do not make one perforator flap the standard for every scrotal defect.<sup>[[14]](#ref14)[[13]](#ref13)</sup> See [Scrotal Flaps](/docs/surgical-techniques/04e-genital-reconstruction/scrotal-flaps).

### Testicular thigh pouches

Temporary or definitive relocation is considered when immediate scrotal coverage is unsuitable or when a patient chooses it after counseling. Cord tension, compression and twisting are avoided. Discomfort, altered appearance, access to the testes and uncertain long-term reproductive effects are discussed.<sup>[[2]](#ref2)[[15]](#ref15)[[16]](#ref16)</sup>

Okwudili's 12 patients had more than 50% scrotal skin loss. The testes went into anteromedial thigh pouches and were massaged back into the residual scrotum over 3 to 8 months. Testicular volume was maintained (mean 19.0 cm³). Staniorski's 20 patients had definitive pouches with fasciocutaneous perineal closure. All 20 closed, one reported pouch pain, and none requested elective scrotoplasty at median 9 months. Those results reflect selected cohorts and do not show lifelong satisfaction or normal fertility.<sup>[[15]](#ref15)[[16]](#ref16)</sup>

Karian's review cites a series in which all 26 thigh pouches and 4 orchiectomies were rated aesthetically unsatisfactory. Total scrotal skin loss does not automatically require thigh pouches before reconstruction. See [Testicular Thigh Pouches](/docs/surgical-techniques/04e-genital-reconstruction/scrotal-thigh-pouch).

## Fournier's gangrene: sequence

1. **Treat the emergency.** Give broad-spectrum antibiotics, resuscitate and obtain urgent surgical source control. The EAU guideline strongly recommends starting broad-spectrum antibiotics on presentation and repeated debridement within 24 hours of presentation. Debridement repeats as required, and an uncertain diagnosis does not delay exploration when suspicion is high.<sup>[[17]](#ref17)[[18]](#ref18)</sup>
2. **Reassess viability and contamination.** Preserve living structures while removing necrotic tissue. Urinary and fecal diversion are assessed individually with the relevant team.
3. **Choose definitive coverage when the patient and wound are ready.** Timing follows source control, tissue viability and reconstructive requirements, not one calendar interval or a fixed sequence of pouches, grafts and flaps.<sup>[[17]](#ref17)[[18]](#ref18)[[2]](#ref2)</sup>

NPWT can help manage a selected wound after adequate debridement and does not substitute for source control. In Iacovelli's retrospective 92-patient cohort, NPWT in patients with disseminated disease was associated with higher 10-week cumulative wound closure (OR 6.5, 95% CI 1.1–37.4) and better 90-day survival (hazard ratio for non-NPWT 3.4). Treatment selection and small subgroups limit causal inference. The EAU Fournier section found no evidence of benefit for NPWT (level of evidence 4) and gives a weak recommendation against adjunctive treatments outside clinical trials. Its literature search ended in July 2017.<sup>[[19]](#ref19)[[17]](#ref17)</sup>

See [Fournier's Gangrene](/docs/special-populations/05a-trauma-emergencies/fourniers-gangrene) for the full acute pathway.

## Genital lymphedema

The plan combines skin care, decongestive treatment where appropriate, management of the cause and selected surgery. Excision relieves disabling advanced fibrotic disease, and physiological procedures may suit selected lymphatic anatomy. Surgery does not guarantee cure or remove the need for continuing care.<sup>[[9]](#ref9)</sup>

Torio-Padron's retrospective 51-patient program combined perioperative decongestive therapy with tailored resection and primary closure. All defects closed primarily without flaps or grafts, and three complications (two hematomas, one dehiscence) required revision. In Wisenbaugh's 11-patient massive-localized-lymphedema series (mean BMI 60, mean resected weight 21 kg), 10 closed primarily. Wound complications were common, quality-of-life scores rose from 1.3 to 7.7, and most patients gained weight afterward (mean +5.2 kg).<sup>[[20]](#ref20)[[21]](#ref21)</sup>

Ehrl treated nine patients, eight with giant disease and one with pitting edema. Five received additional VLNT into the groin or scrotum. No recurrence was reported at median 49 months, including in patients without VLNT, and VLNT into the scrotum improved scrotal lymphatic transport. Donor-site lymphedema is discussed as a morbidity of lymphatic transfer.<sup>[[22]](#ref22)[[9]](#ref9)</sup>

See [Excision and Reconstruction for Lymphedema](/docs/surgical-techniques/04e-genital-reconstruction/excision-flap-reconstruction-lymphedema) and [Complex Decongestive Therapy](/docs/surgical-techniques/04e-genital-reconstruction/complex-decongestive-therapy).

## Gender-affirming and elective scrotoplasty

The labia majora are the embryological homologues of the scrotum and a common source for scrotoplasty. Their vascularity and position are planned with any urethral lengthening, phalloplasty and perineal reconstruction.<sup>[[8]](#ref8)</sup>

Pigot described a technique for scrotoplasty without urethral lengthening, reported in one 29-year-old patient. A pedicled horseshoe-shaped pubic flap, the clitoral hood and U-shaped labia majora flaps are used. The cranially pedicled labia majora flaps are rotated 90° medially to bring the neoscrotum in front of the legs, and pedicled labia majora fat pads are relocated into it for bulk. The meatus and vaginal orifice are diverted under the scrotum with a perineostomy. This modification applies to surgery without urethral lengthening.<sup>[[23]](#ref23)</sup>

Miller reported 147 scrotoplasties with bilateral labia majora rotational advancement flaps, 133 of them with single-stage phalloplasty (mean follow-up 12.5 months). Distal flap necrosis occurred in 6 (4.1%), 5 of them ipsilateral to the groin dissection for phalloplasty. Large (more than 1 cm) perineoscrotal dehiscence occurred in 7 (4.7%). Three patients developed urethrocutaneous fistulas requiring repair. Two scrotal (1.4%) and three perineal (2.0%) hematomas all required operative treatment. These are outcomes of a combined reconstruction and are not isolated scrotoplasty risks.<sup>[[8]](#ref8)</sup>

Selvaggi described more than 300 scrotal reconstructions with V-Y advancement of the labia majora and rotation of superiorly based labial flaps. A testicular implant and erection prosthesis were placed 12 months after the original operation. Implant timing depends on healing, available space, concurrent procedures and patient preference.<sup>[[24]](#ref24)</sup> See the [masculinizing surgery pathway](/docs/special-populations/05c-gender-affirming/masculinizing-procedures).

For bothersome laxity or webbing, excision, advancement or Z-plasty may be considered after individualized assessment. Thomas and Navia's systematic review found only 11 eligible articles, mostly case reports and technique descriptions. They propose vertical midline scrotal skin resection for scrotomegaly and a penoscrotal junction Z-plasty for webbing, alone or combined with penile prosthesis implantation. A pediatric series of 100 Z-plasty procedures (98% primary healing) included mostly hypospadias, chordee and webbing repairs and does not establish adult outcomes.<sup>[[25]](#ref25)[[26]](#ref26)</sup>

## Fertility, function and follow-up

In Demir's rat experiment at two months, grafting produced worse testicular histology (lower weight, shorter germinal epithelium, lower Johnsen score) than flaps or controls, and flap reconstruction matched controls. The authors suggested flaps as the first choice.<sup>[[27]](#ref27)</sup> No human comparative study shows that flaps preserve fertility better than grafts, and objective reproductive outcomes remain sparse.<sup>[[2]](#ref2)</sup> In young patients with fertility concerns, flap reconstruction is one option to discuss, based on this animal evidence.

Fertility goals are discussed before major reconstruction, with semen assessment or reproductive consultation when appropriate. Follow-up covers healing, pain, mobility, sensation, sexual function, appearance and disease recurrence. Infection, hematoma, threatened perfusion, graft or flap loss and urinary complications are evaluated by severity.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[8]](#ref8)[[9]](#ref9)</sup>

## Complications

| Complication | Reported frequency | Management |
|---|---|---|
| Wound complications | Common after lymphedema excision (Wisenbaugh) | Local wound care.<sup>[[21]](#ref21)</sup> |
| Hematoma | 1.4% scrotal and 2.0% perineal (Miller) | Operative evacuation in that series.<sup>[[8]](#ref8)</sup> |
| Distal flap necrosis | 4.1% (labia majora flaps); flap loss 1.6% (Fournier flap review) | Conservative management or debridement.<sup>[[8]](#ref8)[[12]](#ref12)</sup> |
| Perineoscrotal dehiscence | 4.7% (Miller) | Mostly conservative.<sup>[[8]](#ref8)</sup> |
| Urethrocutaneous fistula | 3 of 147 (Miller) | Surgical repair.<sup>[[8]](#ref8)</sup> |
| Impaired spermatogenesis | Animal data with grafts | Discuss reproductive goals.<sup>[[27]](#ref27)</sup> |
| Lymphedema recurrence | None at median 49 months (Ehrl, n = 9) | Small uncontrolled series.<sup>[[22]](#ref22)</sup> |

## Outline of the approach

1. **Acute phase.** Debridement, source control and NPWT if indicated. Let the wound declare its borders.
2. **Defect assessment.** Defects up to about 50% close primarily with mobilization. Larger defects with a clean bed can be grafted. Exposed vital structures, deep dead space or combined defects favor a flap. If immediate coverage is unsuitable, temporary thigh pouches are an option.<sup>[[2]](#ref2)[[17]](#ref17)</sup>
3. **Special settings.** Lymphedema needs decongestive therapy and excision, with or without VLNT. Gender-affirming scrotoplasty uses labia majora flaps.<sup>[[8]](#ref8)[[20]](#ref20)[[22]](#ref22)</sup>
