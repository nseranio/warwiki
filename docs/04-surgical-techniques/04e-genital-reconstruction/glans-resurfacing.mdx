# Glans Resurfacing

Glans resurfacing is an organ-preserving operation in which diseased glanular epithelium is excised and the surface is reconstructed with a graft. In contemporary penile-cancer care, it is principally considered for selected penile intraepithelial neoplasia (PeIN) and selected superficial invasive lesions; it should be planned within diagnostic staging, pathology review, and a cancer-surveillance strategy.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup>

This is not a universal treatment for every glans condition. The evidence and indications for trauma, inflammatory disease, distal urethral reconstruction, and phalloplasty are distinct and are not generalized here.

---

## Role in Penile Cancer Care

For extensive, residual, or recurrent PeIN after topical or ablative treatment, the EAU guideline describes glans resurfacing as excision of the glandular epithelium followed by graft reconstruction. Resection has the advantage of providing tissue for complete histopathological local staging; in one cited study of presumed PeIN, invasive disease was found in up to 20% of resection specimens.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup>

Partial or total resurfacing has also been reported for selected superficially invasive lesions. The guideline characterizes this literature as heterogeneous: in the five cited invasive-cancer studies (68 patients), most patients had pT1 disease, and reported recurrence-free results varied. These small series cannot establish equivalence to another oncologic operation or predict an individual result.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup>

| Question | Evidence-bounded answer |
|---|---|
| Who may be considered? | Selected people with glans-confined PeIN or superficial invasive disease after complete clinical and pathological assessment.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup> |
| What is reconstructed? | The denuded glans surface, commonly with a split-thickness skin graft in the published oncologic experience.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[2]](#ref2)</sup> |
| What remains uncertain? | Comparative cancer control, selection thresholds, technique details, graft choice, and durable functional outcomes. |

## Planning and Follow-up

The choice between topical/ablative treatment, resurfacing, local excision, glansectomy, and more radical surgery depends on lesion extent, pathological findings, feasibility of a safe resection, and the person’s priorities. The trade-off between organ preservation and the possibility of local recurrence should be explicit before treatment.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup>

Follow-up is essential after organ-sparing cancer treatment. It should include examination for local recurrence and assessment of graft healing, meatal or urethral symptoms, urinary and sexual function, appearance, and the need for further treatment. Specific schedules are determined by stage, pathological risk, and the responsible oncology team.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup>

## Outcomes and Limits

A contemporary systematic review of glansectomy and reconstruction found mostly small, single-centre series and wide ranges in recurrence, graft loss, meatal stenosis, and functional outcomes. Those data support careful counselling and outcome reporting, not fixed risk estimates or a preferred reconstructive method.<sup>[[2]](#ref2)</sup>
