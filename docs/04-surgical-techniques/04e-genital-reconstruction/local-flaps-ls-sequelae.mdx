# Local Skin Flaps for Lichen Sclerosus Sequelae

Local flaps can provide coverage after release of selected vulvar scars or contractures when direct closure would recreate narrowing. Surgery addresses an anatomical consequence of lichen sclerosus (LS); it does not reliably eliminate the underlying inflammatory disease. The 2026 German S3 guideline gives a weak recommendation ("suggest") for de-adhesion, perineoplasty or anatomical vulvoplasty in women with persistent introital stenosis that causes voiding or intercourse problems despite guideline-conform topical corticosteroid treatment.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup>

Local flaps are not a compulsory intermediate step between [Perineoplasty and De-Adhesion](/docs/surgical-techniques/04e-genital-reconstruction/perineoplasty-de-adhesion) and [aOAP Flap Reconstruction](/docs/surgical-techniques/04e-genital-reconstruction/aoap-flap). Choose the reconstruction according to the actual defect, adjacent tissue, previous surgery and the patient's goals. Comparative evidence establishing a preferred sequence is lacking.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[2]](#ref2)</sup>

## Assessment before reconstruction

- **Confirm the cause of symptoms.** Distinguish mechanical narrowing or recurrent fissuring from active inflammation, genitourinary syndrome of menopause, pelvic floor overactivity and other pain disorders. Surgery alone may not resolve coexisting pain.
- **Treat active LS and check treatment use.** Review application site, amount, adherence and response before labeling medical treatment unsuccessful. Suspicious persistent or new hyperkeratotic, eroded or ulcerated lesions require assessment and biopsy where indicated before reconstructive planning.
- **Define a meaningful functional goal.** This may be comfortable voiding, examination, hygiene or desired sexual activity; penetrative intercourse is not the only relevant outcome. Offer pelvic floor and sexual-health support, with partner involvement if the patient wants it.
- **Plan continuing care.** The S3 guideline states that women should be informed of, and agree to, the topical treatment needed before and after surgery (usually clobetasol propionate 0.05%), and should receive interdisciplinary counseling that includes pelvic floor physiotherapy and sex therapy.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[3]](#ref3)</sup>

## Choosing a local flap

| Tissue problem | Possible approach and limitation |
|---|---|
| A localized scar band with adequate healthy adjacent tissue | Scar release with an advancement or rearrangement design may avoid re-closing the same tight line. |
| A broader deficit after release | A rotational or transposition flap may bring additional tissue into the defect. |
| Extensive disease, complex previous surgery, or inadequate local tissue | Obtain specialist reconstructive input; regional or perforator reconstruction may be needed. |

Reid's mixed vulvar series (207 consecutive flaps in 191 women, 1992–1996) illustrates selection by anatomy. Simple sliding flaps (Y-V advancement or "maple leaf") repaired 95.8% of 146 contractures, and all 99 tissue deficits required a pivoting design (random transposition, Martius, pudendal thigh, gracilis or gluteus myocutaneous). Primary success fell with worsening deformity, underlying disease and flap complexity. The series was not restricted to LS and does not show that one flap class is superior in LS. Much of the wider vulvar-flap literature concerns oncologic resections and cannot supply LS-specific complication or recurrence rates.<sup>[[2]](#ref2)[[4]](#ref4)</sup>

### Sliding (advancement) flaps

- **Y-V advancement.** This is the usual design for a vulvar contracture band.<sup>[[2]](#ref2)</sup>
- **V-Y island advancement.** In a 20-reconstruction series after radical vulvectomy, Salgarello and colleagues preferred the island V-Y flap for small and medium defects, citing its versatility, reliability, technical simplicity and low complication rate.<sup>[[10]](#ref10)</sup>
- **Pubic V-Y amplified sliding flap.** It is used for symmetric anterior defects. It is supplied by the deep arterial network of the pubis and innervated by ilioinguinal nerve branches (14 of 22 reconstructions in Moschella and Cordova).<sup>[[11]](#ref11)</sup>

### Pivoting (rotation and transposition) flaps

- **Rhomboid (Limberg) flap.** Burke reported 15 flaps in 13 women after extensive vulvar resection, with two minor wound separations and no other complication.<sup>[[12]](#ref12)</sup>
- **Lotus petal (pudendal-thigh) flap.** In 80 flaps in 59 patients, medical or surgical complications occurred in 36% of patients and partial flap loss in 8.8% of flaps; no flap was lost completely. Most patients had vulvar cancer or dysplasia.<sup>[[13]](#ref13)</sup>
- **Gluteal-fold fasciocutaneous island flap.** It is supplied by musculocutaneous perforators of the pudendal artery and keeps pudendal sensory branches; it covers posterior defects.<sup>[[11]](#ref11)</sup>

In Reid's series, sliding designs suited contractures, the usual LS deformity, and pivoting designs were reserved for tissue deficit.<sup>[[2]](#ref2)</sup>

Preserve vascularity and avoid tension, distortion of the urethral meatus or introitus, and unnecessary injury to sensate tissue. Discuss wound separation, infection, altered sensation, persistent pain, recurrent stenosis and possible revision. The choice and extent of release should be tailored to the functional problem, rather than used to excise every visible area of LS.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[2]](#ref2)</sup>

## What the long-term evidence shows

### Rangatchew 2017: local-flap reconstruction

This retrospective series included 38 women treated between 1990 and 2013. Before surgery, 33 reported dyspareunia, including 24 unable to have intercourse. Mean follow-up differed by assessment: 10 months for the early review, 7.6 years for the survey and 8.4 years for the later clinical examination. Survey and clinical follow-up rates were 87% and 78%, respectively.<sup>[[5]](#ref5)</sup>

Reported early pain improved, but seven patients had an unknown early outcome. At the long-term survey, 20 of 24 women in the reported dyspareunia subgroup still had pain. The authors nevertheless reported benefit and satisfaction for many participants, with some able to have intercourse despite recurrent symptoms.<sup>[[5]](#ref5)</sup>

At the long-term clinical examination (mean 8.4 years), the authors attributed recurrent dyspareunia mainly to LS relapse: 50% had minor relapse and could still have intercourse with considerably less pain than before surgery, and 38% had more severe relapse with apareunia. The abstract does not give the denominators for these percentages, so they cannot be converted into a relapse rate or a rate of restored intercourse. Of the 33 women with preoperative dyspareunia, 75% reported a surgical benefit, 74% were satisfied or very satisfied, and 58% reported an improved sexual life at the survey. The authors conclude that the relapsing course of LS gives varying and often short-term coital improvement. This uncontrolled historical series supports counseling about recurrent disease and persistent pain, not a precise contemporary prognosis.<sup>[[5]](#ref5)</sup>

### Other surgical and patient-reported evidence

| Study | Useful finding | Limitation |
|---|---|---|
| Rouzier 2002, perineoplasty | Of 64 operated patients, 50 answered the outcome questionnaire; 45/50 reported improved dyspareunia and 43/50 improved quality of intercourse. | Respondent outcomes, not success in all 64 patients; no comparison with local flaps. |
| Lauber 2021, perineoplasty/de-adhesion | 41 of 70 invited patients responded at median 2.3 years; symptoms and satisfaction generally improved, but 10 patients still reported pain during intercourse (item-specific denominator 27). | Substantial nonresponse and an item-specific denominator; does not establish superior durability. |
| Brauer 2016, qualitative interviews | Among 19 interviewed women, 13 reported less sexual pain, including only four who were pain-free. | Selected interviews describe experience rather than provide a population success rate. |
| O'Dey 2024, skinning vulvectomy with aOAP flap | 61 patients treated 2014–2022, most with bilateral aOAP flaps; dyspareunia and inability to have intercourse were significantly reduced at 1 year (p &lt; 0.001). Minor reversible complications needing secondary intervention were reported; the authors suggest LS tissue quality and long-term corticosteroid use may predispose to minor complications.<sup>[[16]](#ref16)</sup> | Retrospective, 1-year follow-up, no comparator; see [aOAP Flap Reconstruction](/docs/surgical-techniques/04e-genital-reconstruction/aoap-flap). |
| Caretto 2024, capillary-perforator perineal flaps | In a retrospective 13-patient/29-flap series (10 LS, three post-cancer stenosis), the authors reported no postoperative complication or recurrent stenosis at one year among included patients.<sup>[[9]](#ref9)</sup> | Single-center, no comparator, small selected cohort and one-year follow-up; the paper's specific Doppler/tunnel protocol is not a universal requirement. |

These studies help explain the difference between anatomical improvement, reduced pain and satisfaction. They do not provide a valid cross-procedure ranking.<sup>[[3]](#ref3)[[6]](#ref6)[[7]](#ref7)[[9]](#ref9)[[16]](#ref16)</sup>

### Flap complication data

These series are mostly oncologic or mixed vulvar reconstructions and give only an approximate complication envelope for LS.

| Study | Flaps / patients | Indication | Reported healing and complications |
|---|---|---|---|
| Reid 1997<sup>[[2]](#ref2)</sup> | 207 / 191 | Mixed vulvar deformity | 89.9% healed by primary intention; 65 complications in 42 women; 21 needed Z-plasty or a second flap; disease cured and function restored in 93.2% |
| Burke 1994<sup>[[12]](#ref12)</sup> | 15 / 13 | Vulvar resection (rhomboid) | Two minor wound separations; no other complication |
| Kwong 2025<sup>[[14]](#ref14)</sup> | 136 / 69 | Benign, preneoplastic and malignant vulvar disease | None-to-mild complications in 92.6% at day 7 and 83.1% at days 8–30; five necrotic flaps in two patients debrided; all flaps healed or healing at 30 days |
| Commenge 2026<sup>[[15]](#ref15)</sup> | 46 / 27 | Vulvar cancer (genito-crural island perforator) | Complications in 33.3% of patients (dehiscence 17.4%, infection 21.7%); no total flap necrosis |

### Patient experience and psychosexual burden

In Brauer's interviews of 19 women, 13 (68%) reported less sexual pain (4 pain-free, 9 with discomfort in place of pain), and 18 (95%) reported fewer LS symptoms in daily life. Five (26%) stopped intercourse because of pain, including one woman with secondary vaginismus and one with premorbid generalized unprovoked vulvodynia, conditions surgery does not address. Four of the five could not talk with their partner about sex or adapt their sexual repertoire. The authors advise a preoperative couple-based sexological consultation to exclude vaginismus and generalized vulvodynia, to review the couple's sexual communication, and to assess willingness to include non-coital pain-free activity.<sup>[[3]](#ref3)</sup>

Women with genital LS report more depression, lower sexual quality of life and lower work productivity than healthy controls in a 51-patient case-control survey, and qualitative interviews describe suffering, isolation, interference with daily and sexual activity, and grieving.<sup>[[17]](#ref17)[[18]](#ref18)</sup> These burdens are why many women accept an operation with a recognized risk of recurrent symptoms.

## Maintenance and follow-up

Potent or ultrapotent topical corticosteroids remain standard LS treatment. Do not withhold appropriate treatment solely because of a general assertion that it impairs healing. Agent, potency and frequency should be individualized to disease control and tolerability. The guideline does not require every patient to use the same indefinite clobetasol schedule. Use the [topical corticosteroid hub](/docs/foundations/pharmacology/dermatologic-topical-urethral/high-potency-topical-corticosteroids) for medication detail.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup>

In Lee's prospective cohort of 507 women, individualized preventive treatment and adherence were associated with less scarring and fewer combined SCC or vulvar intraepithelial neoplasia events: 0/357 adherent versus 7/150 partially adherent patients over mean 4.7 years. Symptom suppression was reported in 93.3% versus 58.0% and adhesions or scarring in 3.4% versus 40.0%. This was an observational association, not proof that treatment eliminates cancer. Steroid-associated atrophy was uncommon (1.1% and 2.0%) and reversible.<sup>[[8]](#ref8)</sup>

Review postoperative healing, residual narrowing, pain, urinary symptoms and treatment response. Continue regular LS follow-up and prompt reassessment of suspicious lesions; the S3 guideline gives example intervals of every three to six months initially and annually once stable. A new lesion, worsening symptoms or treatment failure warrants earlier review.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup>
