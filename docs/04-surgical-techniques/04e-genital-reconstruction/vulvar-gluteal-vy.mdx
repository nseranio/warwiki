# Gluteal V-Y Advancement Flap for Vulvar Reconstruction

The **gluteal V-Y advancement flap** (gluteal fold V-Y flap) is a fasciocutaneous island flap raised from the gluteal fold / infragluteal region and advanced in V-Y fashion into a vulvoperineal defect. It is valued for its **thin profile, matched local skin quality, concealed donor-site scar, sensate reconstruction, and ability to cover medium-to-large vulvar defects**.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[2]](#ref2)[[3]](#ref3)</sup>

For the broader treatment menu see the [Vulvar Reconstruction Atlas](/docs/surgical-techniques/04e-genital-reconstruction/vulvar). For the underlying V-Y geometry see the foundations [V-Y Advancement Flap](/docs/foundations/surgical-principles/flaps/v-y-advancement) page. For adjacent vulvar atlas pages see [Medial-Thigh V-Y](/docs/surgical-techniques/04e-genital-reconstruction/vulvar-medial-thigh-vy), [Limberg / Rhomboid](/docs/surgical-techniques/04e-genital-reconstruction/vulvar-limberg-flap), [Lotus Petal](/docs/foundations/surgical-principles/flaps/lotus-petal), [Singapore (pudendal-thigh)](/docs/foundations/surgical-principles/flaps/singapore-pudendal-thigh), and [IGAP / Gluteal-Fold family](/docs/foundations/surgical-principles/flaps/igap-gluteal-fold).

## Terminology and Variants

Several related but distinct flaps arise from the gluteal region for vulvar reconstruction:

| Flap | Description |
|---|---|
| **Gluteal fold V-Y advancement** | Fasciocutaneous flap aligned along the gluteal crease, advanced medially. First vulvar description Lee 2006.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup> |
| **Gluteus maximus fasciocutaneous V-Y** | Larger flap on perforators from the inferior border of gluteus maximus + overlying fascia. Arkoulakis 2002 for extensive vulvectomy.<sup>[[3]](#ref3)</sup> |
| **Gluteal fold fasciocutaneous (transposition variant)** | Island flap along the gluteal fold *transposed* (not advanced). Franchelli 2009.<sup>[[4]](#ref4)</sup> |
| **Gluteal-thigh flap** | Larger posteriorly based flap extending down the posterior thigh on the IGA descending branch — used for extended posterior vulvar defects.<sup>[[5]](#ref5)</sup> |
| **IPAP flap** | Perforator-based evolution of the gluteal-fold flap as a free-style perforator flap. Hashimoto 2014.<sup>[[6]](#ref6)</sup> |

All share the gluteal-fold skin territory and its supply from the internal pudendal artery system.

---

## Vascular Anatomy and Blood Supply

| Source | Detail |
|---|---|
| **Internal pudendal artery perforators** | Dominant supply; emerge in the anogenital triangle near the ischial tuberosity. Cadaveric demonstration of a **direct cutaneous system** of IPA and vein.<sup>[[7]](#ref7)</sup> |
| **Perforator number and location** | 3–5 perforators consistently present in the anogenital triangle; mean **27.3 mm from the ischial tuberosity** to the IPA perforator.<sup>[[8]](#ref8)</sup> |
| **Gluteus maximus musculocutaneous perforators** | Supplementary supply through the fascial plexus.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup> |
| **Inferior gluteal artery descending branch** | Contributes to the posterior gluteal-thigh territory; absent / inadequate in ~20% of specimens — hence IPA perforators are the more reliable primary supply.<sup>[[9]](#ref9)</sup> |
| **Profunda femoris first-medial perforator** | Additional supply to the posteromedial territory.<sup>[[9]](#ref9)</sup> |

### Sensory innervation

- **Posterior cutaneous nerve of the thigh** — gluteal-fold skin sensation.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup>
- **Pudendal nerve** — sensation to the medial / perineal aspect of the flap.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup>
- Flap sensitivity is fully restored by **24 months** postoperatively in most patients.<sup>[[2]](#ref2)</sup>

## Indications

| Indication | Detail |
|---|---|
| Posterior and posterolateral vulvar defects | Primary niche |
| Medium-to-large vulvar defects | Mean monolateral dimensions 7.5 × 4.7 × 2.8 cm<sup>[[2]](#ref2)</sup> |
| Total vulvectomy | Bilateral flaps |
| Combined vulvovaginal defects | Flap can be folded / deepithelialized to line the vaginal canal<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[11]](#ref11)</sup> |
| Recurrent vulvar cancer | Secondary reconstruction<sup>[[12]](#ref12)</sup> |
| Extramammary Paget disease of the vulva | Reliable coverage<sup>[[11]](#ref11)[[13]](#ref13)</sup> |

Position in algorithms:

- **Höckel 2008** — V-Y advancement from a caudolateral (gluteal) position; the gluteal-thigh flap is reserved for extended posterior defects.<sup>[[5]](#ref5)</sup>
- **Toulouse 2025** — IPAP / DFAP perforator flaps first-line; V-Y is the reliable second-line.<sup>[[14]](#ref14)</sup>
- **Salgarello 2005** — V-Y is the workhorse for moderate defects.<sup>[[15]](#ref15)</sup>

## Surgical Technique — Standard Gluteal-Fold V-Y (Lee 2006)

1. **Position** — lithotomy.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup>
2. **Design** — V-shaped (triangular) skin island marked with its long axis along the gluteal fold; apex points laterally / posteriorly, base oriented toward the vulvar defect.
3. **Incision** through full-thickness skin and subcutaneous tissue.
4. **Fascial inclusion** — incorporate underlying fascia to capture the fascial plexus from IPA perforators and gluteus maximus musculocutaneous perforators.
5. **Elevation** subfascial, lateral to medial, preserving perforators entering from the deep surface.
6. **Advancement** medially on the subcutaneous pedicle.
7. **Inset** with interrupted sutures; for combined vulvovaginal reconstruction the distal portion can be folded inward to line the vaginal canal.
8. **Donor closure** primarily in V-to-Y, with the scar concealed within the gluteal fold.

### Why the gluteal-fold location

- Greater tissue laxity than the medial thigh — farther advancement than medial-thigh V-Y.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup>
- Concealed scar in the natural gluteal crease.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[2]](#ref2)</sup>
- Inherently thin (unlike medial-thigh flaps, which can be bulky in obese patients).<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[7]](#ref7)</sup>

## Modifications

| Modification | Author / year | Concept |
|---|---|---|
| **Modified gluteal-fold V-Y** | Benedetti Panici 2014<sup>[[10]](#ref10)</sup> | Bilateral or monolateral V-Y. vs direct closure for tumors &gt; 4 cm: dehiscence 11% vs 40% (p = 0.017); LOS 7 vs 10 d (p = 0.007); longer OR time (210 vs 120 min) |
| **Modified V-Y with added transposition flaps** | Lee 2013<sup>[[11]](#ref11)</sup> | 1–2 additional transposition flaps at the base of the advancement flap; covers defects 8 × 6 to 15 × 12 cm. 16 patients, all flaps survived; 11 bilateral, 5 unilateral |
| **Modified gluteal-fold flap** | Nakamura 2010<sup>[[13]](#ref13)</sup> | Minimal skeletonization of IPA perforators → non-bulky, matched-skin-quality flap. 7 flaps in 5 patients; 1 partial dehiscence |
| **Tension-line modification** | Fin 2019<sup>[[2]](#ref2)</sup> | Modified V-Y marking reduces apical-wound tension; 24/59 flaps with the modification had reduced complication rate vs the standard design |
| **Volume-adjusted (thinned)** | Hashimoto 2001<sup>[[7]](#ref7)</sup> | Direct-cutaneous-system anatomy allows thinning of adipose tissue while preserving the pedicle. 14 thinned flaps in 10 obese patients — all survived |
| **Perforator-based pentagonal V-Y** | Jaiswal 2025<sup>[[16]](#ref16)</sup> | Pentagonal modification based on perforator anatomy; 110 patients (33 with vaginal reconstruction): wound breakdown 10.9%, partial necrosis 2.7%; no hernia / nonhealing wound / pressure sore at 12 mo |

## Risk Factors for Complications (Lange 2017, n = 114)<sup>[[18]](#ref18)</sup>

- **Previous radiotherapy** significantly associated with more complications (p = 0.01).
- **Rotation design** vs V-Y advancement significantly associated with major complications (p = 0.02) — V-Y advancement is safer than rotation for the gluteal-fold flap.
- Recurrent disease, multifocal tumor, incomplete tumor removal, and bilateral flap procedure — clinically relevant but not statistically significant.

## Comparison with Other Flaps

### Gluteal-thigh transposition vs medial-thigh V-Y vs Limberg (Tock 2019)<sup>[[19]](#ref19)</sup>

| Parameter | Gluteal-Thigh | V-Y Advancement | Limberg |
|---|---|---|---|
| Median LOS | **24 d** | 14 d | 17 d |
| Reoperation rate | **69%** | 25% | 41% |
| Most common complication | Partial necrosis | Partial necrosis | Partial necrosis |
| OS / RFS | Comparable | Comparable | Comparable |

Note: the Tock "gluteal-thigh" cohort refers to the larger gluteal-thigh transposition flap (Hurwitz type), not the smaller gluteal-fold V-Y. The 69% reoperation rate reflects the complexity of the larger flap and the more extensive defects for which it is used.

### V-Y gluteal-fold vs Lotus Petal (Confalonieri 2017)<sup>[[20]](#ref20)</sup>

| | V-Y | Lotus Petal |
|---|---|---|
| Overall complications | 21% (27/128) | 13% (14/106) |
| Significance | NS (p = 0.588) | — |
| Tunneled LPF superiority | — | Superior functional / cosmetic for primary vulvar malignancies |

### V-Y gluteal-fold vs Direct closure (Benedetti Panici 2014; Giannini 2021)<sup>[[10]](#ref10)[[17]](#ref17)</sup>

- Tumors &gt; 4 cm: V-Y dehiscence 11% vs direct 40% (p = 0.017).
- Hospital stay 7 vs 10 d (p = 0.007).
- V-Y associated with higher rate of adequate margins (&gt; 8 mm) and reduced need for adjuvant RT.

### Gluteal-fold (thigh-based) vs VRAM (Nelson & Butler 2009)<sup>[[21]](#ref21)</sup>

- Thigh flaps (including gluteal variants) showed significantly greater major complications (42% vs 15%) compared with VRAM for perineal / pelvic defects.
- Higher rates of donor-site cellulitis (26% vs 6%), recipient-site cellulitis (21% vs 4%), pelvic abscess (32% vs 6%), and major wound dehiscence (21% vs 5%).
- VRAM requires laparotomy and rectus muscle sacrifice — major trade-off.

---

## Oncologic Impact (Giannini 2021)<sup>[[17]](#ref17)</sup>

- Adequate margins (&gt; 8 mm) achieved in 74.2% of all patients.
- V-Y flap group: 0% necrosis vs 5.1% in the direct closure group.
- Tension-free reconstruction allows wider resection, potentially reducing the need for adjuvant RT.

---

## Advantages

| Advantage | Detail |
|---|---|
| Thin flap | Inherently thinner than medial-thigh V-Y; further thinning possible by volume adjustment<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[7]](#ref7)</sup> |
| Matched local skin quality | Color, texture, thickness closely match vulvar skin<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[13]](#ref13)</sup> |
| Concealed donor scar | Hidden in the natural gluteal fold<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[2]](#ref2)</sup> |
| Sensate reconstruction | PFCN + pudendal innervation; full sensation at 24 mo<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[2]](#ref2)</sup> |
| Greater advancement | Greater laxity allows farther advancement than medial-thigh V-Y<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup> |
| No muscle sacrifice | Fasciocutaneous design preserves gluteus maximus<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[3]](#ref3)</sup> |
| Single-stage | Same OR session as the oncologic resection<sup>[[10]](#ref10)[[17]](#ref17)</sup> |
| Volume-adjustable | Can be thinned for superficial defects or kept full-thickness for deeper ones<sup>[[7]](#ref7)</sup> |
| Lithotomy-compatible | No repositioning required<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup> |
| Enables wider margins | Tension-free closure permits more radical oncologic resection<sup>[[17]](#ref17)</sup> |

---

## Limitations

| Limitation | Detail |
|---|---|
| Higher complication rate than medial-thigh V-Y | Particularly when using the larger gluteal-thigh transposition variant — Tock 2019<sup>[[19]](#ref19)</sup> |
| Prior radiotherapy | Significant risk factor (p = 0.01)<sup>[[18]](#ref18)</sup> |
| Limited for anterior vulvar defects | Caudolateral position favors posterior / posterolateral defects; anterior defects → pubolabial V-Y<sup>[[5]](#ref5)</sup> |
| Not for very large / extended defects | Inguinal extension → TFL; anterior extension → VRAM<sup>[[5]](#ref5)</sup> |
| Sitting discomfort | Temporary postoperative pain on sitting<sup>[[13]](#ref13)</sup> |
| Inferior to tunneled LPF | Tunneled lotus petal superior in functional / cosmetic outcomes for primary vulvar malignancies<sup>[[20]](#ref20)</sup> |

---

## Key Takeaways

1. The gluteal-fold V-Y advancement flap is a thin, sensate, reliable fasciocutaneous flap with a concealed donor scar, ideally suited for posterior and posterolateral vulvar defects.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[2]](#ref2)</sup>
2. Blood supply from the internal pudendal artery perforators is consistent — 3–5 perforators near the ischial tuberosity.<sup>[[7]](#ref7)[[8]](#ref8)</sup>
3. Flap survival approaches 100% across all published series; complications are predominantly minor and manageable.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[3]](#ref3)[[7]](#ref7)</sup>
4. The flap enables wider oncologic resection margins and may reduce the need for adjuvant RT.<sup>[[17]](#ref17)</sup>
5. Prior radiotherapy and rotation (vs V-Y) design are the most significant risk factors for complications.<sup>[[18]](#ref18)</sup>
6. Modifications (additional transposition flaps for wider / deeper defects, volume adjustment for obese patients, tension-line marking) have expanded versatility.<sup>[[2]](#ref2)[[7]](#ref7)[[11]](#ref11)</sup>
7. In modern algorithms, perforator-based variants (IPAP) are increasingly first-line; the traditional V-Y remains a reliable alternative.<sup>[[6]](#ref6)[[14]](#ref14)</sup>
