# Panniculectomy (Urologic Indications)

**Panniculectomy** removes an overhanging abdominal apron of skin and fat. In reconstructive urology, it is used in selected patients with buried penis, an abdominal contour that prevents a reliable urostomy appliance seal, or a pannus that complicates kidney-transplant access. It is distinct from localized suprapubic [Escutcheonectomy](/docs/surgical-techniques/04e-genital-reconstruction/escutcheonectomy). Unlike abdominoplasty, panniculectomy generally does not include abdominal muscle tightening and is not a substitute for weight management.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[2]](#ref2)[[3]](#ref3)[[4]](#ref4)</sup>

## Clinical role

### Adult-acquired buried penis

Add panniculectomy when the abdominal pannus contributes to concealment or impedes the planned reconstruction. Assess the penile skin, escutcheon and scrotal components separately. A PAS category describes the anatomy and does not require every component to be excised or grafted. In the 101-patient multi-institution PAS validation cohort, 7 (7%) had panniculectomy.<sup>[[5]](#ref5)[[6]](#ref6)</sup> Pestana's review lists panniculectomy among the options for complicated or severe buried penis, alongside penile split-thickness skin grafting, reduction scrotoplasty and lipectomy.<sup>[[2]](#ref2)</sup> See [Buried Penis Repair](/docs/surgical-techniques/04e-genital-reconstruction/buried-penis-repair).

Barrow's review identified four reports with 57 patients and a separate NSQIP analysis. Adding panniculectomy was not independently associated with more 30-day complications in that adjusted analysis. This does not establish absence of added risk: the groups differed in comorbidity and the evidence is observational.<sup>[[7]](#ref7)</sup>

In Staniorski's 103-patient high-complexity AABP cohort, 28% underwent panniculectomy. The overall complication rate was 50%, with 41% Clavien I–II, mostly wound dehiscence (31%) or infection (30%). The 3.9% revision rate at median 11 months and the frailty association (OR 6.41) describe the whole combined-repair cohort and not panniculectomy in isolation.<sup>[[8]](#ref8)</sup>

Other AABP series that included limited panniculectomy report the following.

| Series | Findings |
|---|---|
| Hampson 2017 | 42 men had repair with limited panniculectomy and penile STSG. The 90-day complication rate was 33%, and BMI was the only independent predictor (OR 1.1 per BMI unit). Among 27 reached at mean 39 months, 85% would have surgery again, 74% reported a positive life change and 85% called the result a long-term success.<sup>[[18]](#ref18)</sup> |
| Fuller 2017 | 12 patients had escutcheonectomy, scrotoplasty and STSG with durable unburying at mean 8 months. STSG take was 80–100% (mean 91.7%).<sup>[[40]](#ref40)</sup> |
| Erpelding 2019 | 16 patients had outpatient repair with penile liberation, escutcheonectomy and STSG. Ten were discharged the same day. STSG take was 100%, and 30- and 90-day complications were 19% and 25%, all Clavien II. Higher BMI was associated with complications.<sup>[[41]](#ref41)</sup> |

### Kidney-transplant candidates

A troublesome pannus may create a technical or wound-healing barrier in selected candidates. KDIGO recommends individual transplant-surgeon assessment, offers weight-loss interventions and advises against exclusion based on obesity alone; it does not impose a universal BMI cutoff. Its discussion describes possible benefit from pretransplant panniculectomy, without making the procedure mandatory.<sup>[[3]](#ref3)</sup>

| Approach | Relevant experience | Interpretation |
|---|---|---|
| Panniculectomy before transplantation | Troppmann: 36 patients were subsequently listed; 22 received a transplant. One of the 22 recipients had a minor posttransplant wound complication | The 5% wound rate applies to recipients after transplantation, not to all panniculectomy wounds |
| Concurrent living-donor transplant and panniculectomy | Ngaage: 58 selected patients; allograft survival was reported at one year, with 24% wound complications and 52% 90-day readmission (45% of readmissions medical) | An uncontrolled series, without a transplant-alone comparison |
| Concurrent operation with panniculectomy first | Ngaage 2020: 20 patients; panniculectomy was done first for exposure and closed by the plastic team. Mean operating time was 394 min, 17% of it panniculectomy. At 90 days all grafts survived with primary function; wound complications 25%, reoperation 15% | A small uncontrolled series from one center |

These approaches require coordinated transplant and plastic-surgical planning, considering operative access, wound healing under immunosuppression and the consequences of delaying transplantation. In the staged pilot, the reported five-year survival difference was between those who were transplanted and those who were not; it does not demonstrate a survival effect caused by panniculectomy.<sup>[[9]](#ref9)[[10]](#ref10)[[19]](#ref19)</sup>

### Difficult urostomy contour

First assess the stoma, abdominal wall and appliance with a stoma-care specialist and reconstructive team. Contouring can be considered when folds, scars or a pannus cause persistent mechanical leakage despite simpler measures. Mickute's report included four patients, with indications of a large abdominal apron (4), deep skin creases (2) and multiple scars (2). Three had failed earlier urostomy repositioning or peristomal liposuction. The joint plastic and urologic operations averaged 3 hours, with discharge at about 8 days. Three patients reported better fit and more than 50% less leakage. The fourth had intermittent leakage from a persistent upper abdominal fold and later had a reverse abdominoplasty. Two reported long-term lower abdominal numbness. This is limited salvage experience and not a standard first-line stoma revision.<sup>[[4]](#ref4)</sup>

### Urinary incontinence: evidence boundary

Panniculectomy is not an established operation for stress urinary incontinence. Karunaratne's 2025 review included 719 patients, predominantly women, and rectus plication was performed in about 91%. Incontinence was reported in 72.8% before and 38.9% after surgery; change was described as improved in 55.0%, unchanged in 35.2% and worse in 6.8%.<sup>[[11]](#ref11)</sup> Taylor's multicenter series of 214 abdominoplasties with rectus repair reported an ICIQ score falling from 6.5 to 1.6 at 6 weeks and 6 months.<sup>[[20]](#ref20)</sup> A single-arm meta-analysis of 5 studies (196 patients) found SUI improvement in 54.45%.<sup>[[21]](#ref21)</sup> In a survey of 102 massive-weight-loss patients, the 44 with preoperative incontinence had fewer and milder symptoms afterward, and over 20% reported resolution.<sup>[[22]](#ref22)</sup> These are uncontrolled data from mixed body-contouring procedures. They cannot isolate the effect of panniculectomy, and no mechanism has been shown. Incontinence requires its own diagnosis and established treatment pathway. See [Female Stress Urinary Incontinence](/docs/clinical-conditions/03a-storage-incontinence/sui-female).

## Preoperative planning

Assess the pannus, prior incisions, skin disease, hernia, stoma, nutritional status, diabetes, smoking and functional capacity. Arrange anesthetic assessment, appropriate equipment and perioperative thromboprophylaxis for the complete operation. Imaging and abdominal-wall specialist input are appropriate when a hernia is suspected or examination is inadequate.<sup>[[12]](#ref12)</sup> Pestana recommends preoperative CT for a "supersize" pannus (resected weight of at least 10 kg or extending to mid-thigh). CT may exclude an underlying hernia.<sup>[[25]](#ref25)</sup> In a comparison of two groups, perioperative complex decongestive physiotherapy in a lymphological clinic was associated with fewer major complications (P = .001), transfusions (P = .028) and wound-healing disorders (P = .021) after resection of panniculus morbidus. Twelve of 16 treated patients had an uneventful course, and all untreated patients had at least one complication.<sup>[[26]](#ref26)</sup>

Mark with the patient standing and confirm the proposed closure supine. Plan umbilical management, genital or stoma displacement and closure tension explicitly. Incorporate the patient's ongoing weight-loss plans, wound-care capacity and functional goals. Nutrition and glycemic decisions should follow the current [Perioperative Care](/docs/foundations/perioperative-care) pathways rather than a universal laboratory cutoff from an operative case series.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[12]](#ref12)</sup>

## Technique principles

| Pattern | Reconstructive role |
|---|---|
| Transverse panniculectomy | Removes the contributory lower abdominal apron |
| Fleur-de-lis pattern | Adds vertical excision for selected horizontal/upper abdominal excess; creates an additional junction requiring attention to perfusion and tension |
| Modified trapezoid mobilization for AABP | A described approach that can preserve superior attachments while suitable pannus skin is harvested for STSG |

No incision pattern has universal superiority or proven equivalent complications across different patients.<sup>[[12]](#ref12)[[13]](#ref13)</sup>

**Transverse panniculectomy.** Matory's series of 42 severely obese patients used a large transverse ellipse. A suprapubic wedge reduced the length mismatch between upper and lower incisions, and lateral V-flaps limited lateral dog-ears.<sup>[[27]](#ref27)</sup> Cooper compared fleur-de-lis (25), transverse with minimal undermining (30) and transverse with extensive undermining (37). Wound complications occurred in 43% overall, reoperation in 13%, and technique was not significantly related to the overall complication rate.<sup>[[34]](#ref34)</sup>

**Fleur-de-lis.** O'Brien compared 30 traditional and 100 fleur-de-lis panniculectomies after bariatric surgery, with complication rates of 17% in each group.<sup>[[30]](#ref30)</sup> Christopher found overall complications in 50% (fleur-de-lis 53.9%, traditional 46.7%; P = .592), with pannus weight associated with surgical-site occurrence (P = .04).<sup>[[31]](#ref31)</sup> In a 76-patient comparison, deepithelialized mediocaudal flap edges reduced full-thickness T-junction defects and seroma.<sup>[[32]](#ref32)</sup> In a 40-patient comparison, an absorbable dermal stapler shortened fleur-de-lis operating time (66.8 vs 125.3 min) without differences in surgical-site occurrences.<sup>[[33]](#ref33)</sup>

1. **Plan safe exposure.** Use a suitable operating table, positioning and retraction system. If a massive pannus requires suspension, use equipment approved for the load and confirm the absence/location of herniated contents before penetrating fixation. Matory suspended the pannus from overhead lighting on towel clips or K-wires or Steinmann pins, and reported that a two-team approach appeared to reduce blood loss and operating time.<sup>[[27]](#ref27)</sup> Reichenberger described a mechanical lift with tumescent solution in 20 patients (pannus 6.8–60 kg; uncomplicated healing in 55%).<sup>[[28]](#ref28)</sup>
2. **Resect to the planned defect.** Protect abdominal-wall structures and preserve flap perforators and lymphatic tissue where possible. Limit undermining to what the reconstruction requires.
3. **Secure hemostasis.** Account for large superficial vessels and the potential for substantial blood loss in extensive resections. Transfusion was needed in 39 of 100 patients in Leahy's series (more often with larger resections, P &lt; .01) and in 5 of 25 in Manahan's.<sup>[[23]](#ref23)[[24]](#ref24)</sup>
4. **Manage dead space and tension.** Select drainage, progressive-tension sutures and layered closure according to the actual wound. There is no universal requirement for two to four drains or one drain-removal day. Friedrich's evolved closure was three suture layers over four suction drains with a small wound-vacuum device at each end of the incision, and the authors describe a steep learning curve.<sup>[[29]](#ref29)</sup>

These are operative planning principles; extensive abdominal-wall, stoma or transplant reconstruction requires the relevant specialist team.<sup>[[12]](#ref12)</sup>

When panniculectomy accompanies AABP repair, preserve adequate healthy shaft skin and graft only the deficient area. Suitable pannus or escutcheon skin can avoid a separate donor wound, but skin quality and hair must be assessed. Figler's outpatient technique harvested an unmeshed STSG from the pannus and used a bolster for 5–7 days; that protocol does not establish a universal graft thickness, dressing or admission duration.<sup>[[13]](#ref13)[[14]](#ref14)</sup>

## Concurrent ventral hernia repair

**Adding panniculectomy to ventral hernia repair can increase wound morbidity.** A 2024 systematic review included 23,354 patients in 11 observational studies and two randomized trials; 2,972 underwent the combined operation. Compared with hernia repair alone, the combined approach was associated with more SSI (RR 1.31, 95% CI 1.13–1.51), skin necrosis and reoperation (RR 1.73, 95% CI 1.32–2.28). Lower recurrence was also reported, but selection, study design and follow-up limit causal interpretation.<sup>[[15]](#ref15)</sup>

This comparison differs from asking whether a patient already requiring panniculectomy should also have a hernia repaired. Discuss the sequence, mesh and wound considerations and alternatives with an abdominal-wall surgeon rather than assuming any combined operation carries no additional risk.<sup>[[15]](#ref15)[[12]](#ref12)</sup>

## Wound management and recovery

Choose standard dressings or closed-incision NPWT according to wound risk, device instructions and available support. Single-surgeon panniculectomy series with closed-incision NPWT reported major complications in 5 of 91 patients (5.5%), without a concurrent control.<sup>[[35]](#ref35)</sup> In open abdominal wall reconstruction with concomitant panniculectomy, a comparison of 67 patients per arm found wound complications of 15.6% with closed-incision NPWT versus 35.5% without (P = .01), and wound-related reoperation of 0% versus 13.3%.<sup>[[36]](#ref36)</sup> A 2025 review combined 11 comparative studies across panniculectomy, abdominoplasty and abdominal flap donor sites; it associated incisional NPWT with less dehiscence and unplanned reoperation, but not a significant SSI, seroma, hematoma or skin-necrosis difference. Those mixed procedures do not define a mandatory panniculectomy protocol.<sup>[[16]](#ref16)</sup>

The broader 2022 Cochrane review included 62 RCTs and 13,340 participants across several surgical populations. It found moderate-certainty evidence of fewer SSIs, but probably little or no difference in dehiscence; blistering may increase. Applicability, cost and outcomes vary by operation. Open-wound NPWT and deliberately partial closure are different strategies. In one retrospective comparison in super-obese patients, primary closure was followed by 44% readmission and 33% reoperation for wound complications, and the open-wound group had none. That group had a higher BMI, and the report does not establish superiority.<sup>[[37]](#ref37)[[17]](#ref17)</sup>

Counsel about infection, wound separation, seroma, hematoma, flap ischemia, transfusion, thromboembolism and revision according to the operation and patient. A low major-complication rate cannot be substituted for the total wound-complication rate, and comparison with historical cohorts does not establish a treatment effect. Ensure appropriate wound and drain review, mobilization and support after discharge.<sup>[[12]](#ref12)[[8]](#ref8)</sup>

Same-day or next-day discharge is possible in selected AABP repairs. Figler's 19-patient cohort had graft take of at least 95%, five cellulitis cases and three minor dehiscences. No observed DVT in such a small series does not establish negligible thromboembolic risk.<sup>[[13]](#ref13)</sup>

## Complication rates and risk factors

Reported wound-complication rates are high and vary with the population. Pestana's series of 26 "supersize" panniculectomies had wound complications in 42.3% and major complications requiring return to theatre in 11.5%.<sup>[[25]](#ref25)</sup> Cooper reported wound complications in 43%, and in Manahan's series 56% of patients had some complication (44% healed uncomplicated).<sup>[[34]](#ref34)[[24]](#ref24)</sup> In a comparison of 306 patients, 30-day complications were 21.2% after panniculectomy and 9.7% after abdominoplasty.<sup>[[39]](#ref39)</sup>

In a database analysis of panniculectomy patients, obesity, tobacco use and diabetes were associated with surgical-site infection, wound disruption and reoperation. Charlson index above 1, male sex, age over 60, COPD and hypertension were possible risk factors for other complications.<sup>[[38]](#ref38)</sup> Within buried-penis repair, BMI and frailty were the main predictors.<sup>[[8]](#ref8)[[18]](#ref18)</sup>

## Documentation

Document the functional impairment, the anatomy treated, conservative measures, combined procedures and intended goals. Panniculectomy is reported with CPT 15830. Coding and coverage depend on the actual procedure and current payer rules; a functional indication does not guarantee authorization.
