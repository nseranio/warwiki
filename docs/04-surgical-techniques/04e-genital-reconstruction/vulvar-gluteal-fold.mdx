# Gluteal-Fold (Sulcus / Infragluteal) Flap for Vulvar Reconstruction

The **gluteal-fold (sulcus / infragluteal) flap** is a fasciocutaneous flap harvested from the infragluteal crease, primarily vascularized by the **direct cutaneous system of the internal pudendal artery** and / or the **cutaneous branch of the descending branch of the inferior gluteal artery**. First applied to vulvar reconstruction by Yii and Niranjan (1996) as the original "lotus petal" flap and refined by **Hashimoto et al. (2001)** with cadaveric vascular studies enabling flap thinning, it is now one of the most widely used flaps in vulvoperineal reconstruction.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[2]](#ref2)[[3]](#ref3)[[4]](#ref4)</sup>

For the broader treatment menu see the [Vulvar Reconstruction Atlas](/docs/surgical-techniques/04e-genital-reconstruction/vulvar). For the broader gluteal-perforator family see the foundations [IGAP / SGAP / Gluteal-Fold](/docs/foundations/surgical-principles/flaps/igap-gluteal-fold) page. For related atlas pages see [Gluteal V-Y](/docs/surgical-techniques/04e-genital-reconstruction/vulvar-gluteal-vy) (V-Y advancement variant), [Lotus Petal](/docs/surgical-techniques/04e-genital-reconstruction/vulvar-lotus-petal) (parent concept), and [Pudendal-Thigh / Singapore](/docs/surgical-techniques/04e-genital-reconstruction/vulvar-singapore-flap) (shared IPA territory).

## Nomenclature

| Term | Scope |
|---|---|
| **Gluteal-fold flap (GFF)** | Most common in perineal reconstruction literature<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[15]](#ref15)</sup> |
| Gluteal sulcus flap | Used interchangeably with GFF |
| **Infragluteal / fasciocutaneous infragluteal (FCI) flap** | Emphasizes the donor site; Knol & Hage 1997, Windhofer 2010 / 2012<sup>[[4]](#ref4)[[8]](#ref8)[[9]](#ref9)</sup> |
| Lotus petal — lower petal | Corresponds to the gluteal-fold portion of the lotus design — "mainly applied because the donor-site scar can be hidden in the natural gluteal fold" (Höckel)<sup>[[5]](#ref5)</sup> |
| Modified gluteal-fold flap | V-Y advancement, thinned, propeller variants<sup>[[10]](#ref10)[[11]](#ref11)</sup> |

Key distinction from the broader lotus petal concept: the GFF specifically refers to flaps from the **infragluteal-crease territory**, whereas the lotus petal encompasses the entire perineal perforator territory from the genitocrural sulcus to the gluteal fold.<sup>[[5]](#ref5)[[6]](#ref6)</sup>

## Vascular Anatomy

### Primary supply — IPA (Hashimoto 2001)<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup>

Cadaveric pedicle study (n = 8) showed the flap is nourished by a **direct cutaneous system of the internal pudendal artery and vein**. The supply runs in a **defined subcutaneous plane**, enabling safe flap thinning — previously considered impossible. The pedicle vessels lie in the adipose tissue adjacent to the ischial tuberosity.

### Secondary supply — descending branch of IGA (Windhofer 2002)<sup>[[12]](#ref12)</sup>

The FCI variant is specifically based on the **cutaneous branch of the descending branch of the inferior gluteal artery**. Windhofer studied 118 cadaveric gluteal regions:

- Descending branch present in **91%**.
- When absent, the cutaneous branch arose from MCFA, LCFA, or a deep-thigh perforator.
- The **posterior femoral cutaneous nerve** was found in a common connective-tissue sheath with the descending branch in **72%**.
- **Nerve loops around the vessel** in **29%** — limits the arc of rotation for local flaps.

### Contributing supply

- **Gluteus maximus musculocutaneous perforators** contribute to the fascial plexus.<sup>[[13]](#ref13)</sup>
- **IPA perforators** — the same system supplying the lotus petal flap; 3–5 perforators in the perineal anogenital triangle, mean **27.3 mm** from the ischial tuberosity.<sup>[[6]](#ref6)</sup>

### Fascial-plexus model (Lee 2006)<sup>[[13]](#ref13)</sup>

The gluteal-fold V-Y advancement flap is supplied by an underlying fascial plexus from IPA perforators + gluteus-maximus musculocutaneous perforators — a dual-source plexus delivering exceptional reliability.

### Angiosome data (Zaussinger 2019)<sup>[[14]](#ref14)</sup>

Fasciocutaneous infragluteal angiosome: mean area 77.2 ± 9.0 cm²; mean external pedicle diameter 2.9 ± 0.7 mm; **mean pedicle length 12.5 ± 1.5 cm** (longest among compared upper-thigh flaps).

### Sensory innervation

- **Posterior femoral cutaneous nerve** — primary sensory nerve; common sheath with the IGA descending branch in 72%.<sup>[[9]](#ref9)[[12]](#ref12)[[13]](#ref13)</sup>
- **Perineal branches of the pudendal nerve** — contribute medially.<sup>[[3]](#ref3)[[13]](#ref13)</sup>
- Can be raised as a **sensate flap** without ambulatory restrictions.<sup>[[8]](#ref8)</sup>

| Other | Labia majora reconstruction, RVF closure, perineal body reconstruction, buttock defects (pressure sores / post-oncologic), anal / pelvic-floor reconstruction<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[4]](#ref4)[[8]](#ref8)</sup> |

## Surgical Technique — Standard Island Transposition (Franchelli 2009)<sup>[[2]](#ref2)</sup>

1. **Position** — lithotomy (no need to reposition the patient — a key advantage over VRAM).
2. **Design** — oval or triangular skin island marked along the gluteal fold; long axis aligned with the infragluteal crease.
3. **Incision and elevation** — through skin, subcutaneous tissue, and **always including the fascial layer** (subfascial dissection).
4. **Perforator identification** — flap raised until an IPA perforator branch is identified.
5. **Island mobilization** — harvested as an island flap for better mobility.
6. **Transposition** — rotated medially into the vulvar defect on its vascular pedicle.
7. **Donor closure** — primary, with the scar concealed in the gluteal fold.

Franchelli outcomes (n = 33, 1998–2007): no major complications; 2 marginal necrosis, 8 significant seromas. Advantages over gracilis / rectus flaps — reduced scar dimensions, no flap liponecrosis, no position change, very limited blood loss.<sup>[[2]](#ref2)</sup>

## Variants and Modifications

| Variant | Author / year | Concept |
|---|---|---|
| **Gluteal-fold V-Y advancement** | Lee 2006<sup>[[13]](#ref13)</sup> | V-shaped triangular flap advanced V-Y; fascial-plexus supply; n = 9 / 17 flaps, all survived (3 small wound disruptions); advanced farther than other flaps due to gluteal-fold laxity |
| **Modified gluteal-fold V-Y** | Benedetti Panici 2014<sup>[[11]](#ref11)</sup> | n = 29 V-Y vs n = 78 direct closure — LOS 7 vs 10 d (p = 0.0067); tumors &gt; 4 cm dehiscence 11% vs 40% (p = 0.0172) |
| **Tension-line modification** | Fin 2019<sup>[[19]](#ref19)</sup> | Reduces apical-wound tension; n = 30 / 59 flaps; minor complications 23% pts (14% flaps); sensitivity fully restored at 24 mo |
| **Thinned gluteal-fold** | Hashimoto 2001<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup> | Direct-cutaneous-system anatomy enables thinning while preserving the pedicle; n = 10 / 14 flaps, all survived; particularly useful in obese patients |
| **Modified V-Y fashion** | Nakamura 2010<sup>[[10]](#ref10)</sup> | n = 5 / 7 flaps for EMPD / vulvar carcinoma; all survived; 1 partial dehiscence; not bulky; temporary sitting discomfort |
| **FCI flap** | Windhofer 2010 (buttock) / 2012 (vulvovaginal)<sup>[[8]](#ref8)[[9]](#ref9)</sup> | Based on the IGA descending branch; can be designed outside an irradiated field; vulvovaginal n = 15 / 17 — 16/17 survived; buttock n = 14 / 15 — 14/15 healed uneventfully |
| **Infragluteal skin flap** | Knol & Hage 1997<sup>[[4]](#ref4)</sup> | Earliest dedicated infragluteal series; n = 16 (vaginal-introitus widening 6, labia-majora 5, RVF 4, perineal body 1); 100% flap survival |
| **V-Y outcomes after radical surgery** | Giannini 2021<sup>[[20]](#ref20)</sup> | n = 23 V-Y vs n = 39 direct closure — dehiscence 8.7% vs 10.3%; necrosis 0% vs 5.1%; V-Y increased adequate-margin rate (&gt; 8 mm) and reduced adjuvant-RT need |
| **Perforator-based pentagonal V-Y** | Jaiswal 2025<sup>[[21]](#ref21)</sup> | n = 110 (33 with vaginal reconstruction); wound breakdown 10.9%; partial necrosis 2.72%; no perineal herniation at 12 mo |

## Risk Factors for Complications

Lange 2017 (largest prospective GFF risk-factor study, n = 75 / 114):<sup>[[22]](#ref22)</sup>

- **Previous radiotherapy** (p = 0.01) — significantly more complications.
- **Rotation flap design** vs V-Y advancement (p = 0.02) — significantly more **major** complications.
- Recurrent disease, multifocal tumor, incomplete tumor removal, bilateral flap procedure — clinically relevant but not statistically significant.

Winterton 2013 additional risk factors:<sup>[[15]](#ref15)</sup>

- Number of comorbidities — prolongs both time to discharge and time to healing.
- Anorectal resection — prolonged healing when anus resected.
- Preoperative radiotherapy — prolonged discharge time.

## Comparison with Other Flaps

### GFF vs gracilis (Thiele 2020, n = 29)<sup>[[7]](#ref7)</sup>

| Parameter | GFF (n = 12) | MGF gracilis (n = 17) |
|---|---|---|
| Flap loss | 0% | 0% |
| Complication rate | Comparable | Comparable |
| Unilateral OR time | **70 ± 13 min** | 105 ± 9 min |
| Bilateral OR time | **107 ± 14 min** | 163 ± 11 min |
| Pain-free sitting | Slower | Faster |

GFF significantly faster to elevate; pain-free sitting slower due to donor-site location.

### GFF vs rhomboid vs V-Y (Tock 2019, n = 61)<sup>[[23]](#ref23)</sup>

| Parameter | Gluteal-thigh | Rhomboid | V-Y |
|---|---|---|---|
| Median LOS | **24 d** | 17 d | **14 d** |
| Reoperation | **69%** | 41% | **25%** |

V-Y had shortest LOS and lowest reoperation; gluteal-thigh (larger / more complex variant) had highest reoperation.

### IGAM vs VRAM for pelvic exenteration (Read 2025, n = 130)<sup>[[24]](#ref24)</sup>

IGAM flaps had significantly lower rates of flap failure (p = 0.01), early return to theatre, dehiscence, and infection compared with VRAM.

## Position in Reconstructive Algorithms

| Algorithm | Position |
|---|---|
| **Höckel 2008**<sup>[[5]](#ref5)</sup> | GFF / sulcus flap is one of 11 recommended vulvovaginal procedures; lower-petal (gluteal-sulcus) preferred because the donor scar is concealed in the natural gluteal fold; V-Y advancement can be slid from a caudolateral (gluteal) position |
| **Salgarello 2005**<sup>[[25]](#ref25)</sup> | Small-to-medium defects → island V-Y, island gluteal-fold, or pedicled pudendal-thigh; large posteriorly extended defects → V-Y preferred; very large anterior + posterior defects → VRAM |
| **Toulouse 2025**<sup>[[26]](#ref26)</sup> | Perforator flaps first-line over traditional musculocutaneous; IPAP (perforator-based evolution of the gluteal-fold / lotus-petal flap) preferred for posterior and lateral defects |
| **Winterton 2013**<sup>[[15]](#ref15)</sup> | Small perineal → primary closure; medium → unilateral GFF; large → bilateral GFF; very large with pelvic dead space → VRAM |

## Advantages

| Advantage | Detail |
|---|---|
| Thin and pliable | Inherently thin; can be further thinned by removing excess adipose<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[13]](#ref13)</sup> |
| Sensate | PFCN + pudendal innervation; sensitivity restored at 24 mo<sup>[[8]](#ref8)[[13]](#ref13)[[19]](#ref19)</sup> |
| Concealed donor scar | Hidden in the natural infragluteal crease<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[4]](#ref4)[[13]](#ref13)</sup> |
| Exceptionally reliable | Flap survival 94–100% across all major series<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[2]](#ref2)[[4]](#ref4)[[13]](#ref13)</sup> |
| Fast and technically simple | Unilateral ~70 min; bilateral ~107 min — faster than gracilis<sup>[[7]](#ref7)</sup> |
| No position change | Performed in lithotomy without repositioning (unlike VRAM)<sup>[[2]](#ref2)</sup> |
| No muscle sacrifice | Fasciocutaneous design<sup>[[2]](#ref2)[[8]](#ref8)</sup> |
| Compatible with inguinofemoral LND | Donor pedicle preserved<sup>[[3]](#ref3)</sup> |
| Can be designed outside RT field | FCI variant<sup>[[9]](#ref9)</sup> |
| Adjustable volume | Thinning for vulvar; full-thickness for dead-space obliteration<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup> |
| Primary donor closure | All donor sites closed primarily<sup>[[2]](#ref2)[[4]](#ref4)</sup> |
| Reduced LOS | 7 vs 10 d (Benedetti Panici)<sup>[[11]](#ref11)</sup> |
| Improved margin adequacy | V-Y correlates with adequate margins and reduced adjuvant-RT need<sup>[[20]](#ref20)</sup> |
| Versatile | Vulvar / vaginal / perineal / buttock / pelvic-floor — unilateral or bilateral<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[4]](#ref4)[[8]](#ref8)[[15]](#ref15)</sup> |

## Limitations

| Limitation | Detail |
|---|---|
| Rotation design associated with more major complications | Lange — rotation vs V-Y (p = 0.02)<sup>[[22]](#ref22)</sup> |
| Previous radiotherapy | p = 0.01 risk factor for complications<sup>[[22]](#ref22)</sup> |
| Pain-free sitting may be delayed | Donor-site location<sup>[[7]](#ref7)[[10]](#ref10)</sup> |
| Bulky in obese patients | Standard (non-thinned) flap may be excessively thick<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup> |
| Limited arc of rotation | IGA-descending-branch + PFCN common-sheath (72%) / nerve-loops (29%) limits rotation<sup>[[12]](#ref12)</sup> |
| Higher reoperation than V-Y | Gluteal-thigh variant 69% vs V-Y 25% (Tock)<sup>[[23]](#ref23)</sup> |
| Longer LOS than V-Y | GTF 24 d vs VYF 14 d (Tock)<sup>[[23]](#ref23)</sup> |
| Not suitable for very large pelvic dead-space defects | Use musculocutaneous (VRAM / gracilis / gluteal-thigh)<sup>[[5]](#ref5)[[25]](#ref25)</sup> |
| Wound breakdown commonest complication | 30% in Winterton n = 77 — usually self-healing<sup>[[15]](#ref15)</sup> |
| IGA descending branch absent in ~9% | Alternative sources less reliable<sup>[[12]](#ref12)</sup> |

## Key Takeaways

1. Thin, sensate, reliable fasciocutaneous flap from the infragluteal crease — dual blood supply from the IPA and the IGA descending branch.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[12]](#ref12)</sup>
2. Hashimoto's 2001 cadaveric study was pivotal — demonstrated the direct-cutaneous-system IPA supply and enabled safe flap thinning.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup>
3. V-Y advancement design preferred over rotation — rotation is associated with significantly more major complications (p = 0.02).<sup>[[22]](#ref22)</sup>
4. Flap survival 94–100% across all major series; wound breakdown is the most common (usually minor / self-healing) complication.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[2]](#ref2)[[4]](#ref4)[[13]](#ref13)[[15]](#ref15)</sup>
5. Significantly faster to elevate than gracilis (70 vs 105 min unilateral) and does not require patient repositioning.<sup>[[7]](#ref7)</sup>
6. For pelvic exenteration, the IGAM variant is superior to VRAM in a 130-patient comparative study.<sup>[[24]](#ref24)</sup>
7. Previous radiotherapy is the most important risk factor for complications; the FCI variant can be designed outside the irradiated field.<sup>[[9]](#ref9)[[22]](#ref22)</sup>
8. Central position in all major vulvoperineal reconstructive algorithms — primary option for posterior / lateral vulvar defects and medium-sized perineal defects.<sup>[[5]](#ref5)[[25]](#ref25)[[26]](#ref26)</sup>
