# Testicular Thigh Pouch

A **testicular thigh pouch** relocates a viable testis into a subcutaneous pocket in the anteromedial thigh. It is used as a temporary bridge, as a selected definitive position, or as the first stage of a neoscrotum. McAninch's algorithm places the testes temporarily in thigh pouches after total scrotal loss and later builds a new scrotum with meshed split-thickness grafts or thigh-based flaps.<sup>[[11]](#ref11)</sup> Exposed testes can also be grafted directly, so a pouch is one option after total scrotal skin loss and not a required first step: Tan grafted exposed, apposed testes directly in 27 consecutive patients.<sup>[[12]](#ref12)</sup>

See the [scrotal reconstruction overview](/docs/surgical-techniques/04e-genital-reconstruction/scrotal-reconstruction) for closure, graft and flap alternatives.

## Choosing a pouch

| Setting | Pouch strategy |
|---|---|
| Fournier's gangrene with more than 50% scrotal skin loss, residual scrotal skin and a wound not yet ready for closure | Temporary pouches with delayed return of the testes (Okwudili, n = 12).<sup>[[2]](#ref2)</sup> |
| Older, comorbid patient with a large perineal wound | Definitive pouches with fasciocutaneous perineal closure (Staniorski, n = 20: median age 64, BMI 34, Charlson index 5).<sup>[[3]](#ref3)</sup> |
| Total scrotal loss when a formal neoscrotum is planned | Two-stage "Hiawatha" reconstruction (Mandel, one patient).<sup>[[6]](#ref6)</sup> |
| Exposed testes and a viable wound bed | Direct grafting (Tan, n = 27) or a flap are alternatives to a pouch.<sup>[[12]](#ref12)</sup> |

The decision also depends on testicular viability, cord reach and perfusion, expected future reconstruction, and the patient's preferences about appearance, pain with movement or sitting, access to the testes and fertility.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[3]](#ref3)</sup>

In Fournier's gangrene, resuscitation, antibiotics and prompt debridement come first. A pouch is not used to cover nonviable tissue or to move uncontrolled infection into a new compartment.<sup>[[4]](#ref4)[[1]](#ref1)</sup>

## Technical principles

- Preserve testicular and cord vascularity. Previous inguinal or testicular operations and debridement change the available blood supply and the safe route.
- Cord length limits how far the testis can be placed. Create enough room for a comfortable position without cord tension, kinking, twisting or compression, and plan the site around adjacent wounds and hip movement.
- Gubernacular and cremasteric collateral vessels can contribute to testicular supply, especially after previous vessel-dividing orchiopexy. Ellis mapped these vessels in children undergoing second-stage Fowler–Stephens surgery, so gubernacular division is not a routine step.<sup>[[5]](#ref5)</sup>
- Size each pouch to hold the testis without constriction. A snug pocket limits mobility, and an oversized or tight one risks torsion or pressure.
- Published series use anteromedial or medial thigh pouches. Position affects sitting comfort, and one of Staniorski's 20 patients reported pouch pain.<sup>[[2]](#ref2)[[3]](#ref3)</sup>

The reports below describe distinct strategies. Their technical details are not interchangeable.

## Temporary pouch with later return

**Okwudili 2016** described 12 patients with Fournier's gangrene and more than 50% scrotal skin loss, in whom residual scrotal skin could not be closed over the testes. Patients had serial debridement (mean 2.3 ± 0.5) until the wound granulated, then the testes were placed in anteromedial thigh pouches to allow scrotal wound closure. The testes were massaged gradually back into the residual scrotal pouch, which acted as a tissue expander.<sup>[[2]](#ref2)</sup>

- The residual scrotal pouches expanded enough to hold the testes over 3 to 8 months.
- There was no mortality. Testicular volume was maintained (mean 19.0 ± 3.2 cm³) at mean follow-up of 14.8 ± 9.7 months.
- The author concluded that the approach avoids specialized reconstructive surgery.

The approach depends on residual scrotal skin, so it does not apply to total scrotal loss. Volume is not a semen or fertility endpoint, and semen analysis was not reported.

## Definitive pouch with perineal flap closure

**Staniorski 2023** combined permanent thigh pouches with fasciocutaneous perineal closure in 20 patients after scrotectomy. The reported sequence is completion of debridement or scrotectomy, creation of thigh pouches, and perineal closure with fasciocutaneous flaps raised from the thigh (18 patients) or the abdominal wall (8 patients). Three patients needed abdominal STSG to complete closure. Only the abstract of this report was checked, so operative details beyond this sequence are not described here.<sup>[[3]](#ref3)</sup>

| Outcome | Result |
|---|---|
| Median wound area | 443 cm² (IQR 225–600) |
| Perineal closure | 20/20 |
| Complications | 3/20 (15%): one wound infection and two bleeding episodes |
| Discharged directly home | 40% |
| Pain related to the thigh pouches | 1/20 |
| Elective scrotoplasty requested | 0/20 at median follow-up of 9 months |

This was a reconstructive operation in a selected, frail cohort. It combines pouches with flap surgery and is not simple relocation of the testes. Later revision remains possible, so counseling covers relocation despite an initial decision for a definitive pouch.

## Staged "Hiawatha" neoscrotum

**Mandel 1980** reported a two-stage reconstruction in one young adult after total scrotal loss. The testes were first implanted in the thighs, where they developed firm pseudocapsules. In the second stage the pseudocapsules were turned on themselves and sutured together to provide a smooth vascular bed for a split-thickness skin graft. The report described a normal-appearing scrotum, functional testes and a normal sperm count at three years.<sup>[[6]](#ref6)</sup>

The interval for pseudocapsule maturation is not given in the abstract. Contemporary selection compares this option with direct graft or flap reconstruction when those are feasible.

## Late relocation and complications

Berli described a man treated for Fournier's gangrene in whom bilateral hydroceles formed in the thigh pouches eight years after testicular transposition. Conservative measures failed, and the hydroceles were treated during a desired testicular relocation and neoscrotum creation. Lymphatic injury during transposition was proposed as the mechanism.<sup>[[7]](#ref7)</sup>

| Complication | Detail |
|---|---|
| Pain | 1/20 in Staniorski's series.<sup>[[3]](#ref3)</sup> |
| Wound infection or bleeding | 1/20 and 2/20 in Staniorski's series.<sup>[[3]](#ref3)</sup> |
| Ectopic hydrocele | Single case report, eight years after transposition.<sup>[[7]](#ref7)</sup> |
| Testicular atrophy | Volume was preserved in Okwudili's series; testicular or cord necrosis has been reported in the broader thigh-pouch literature.<sup>[[2]](#ref2)[[1]](#ref1)</sup> |
| Appearance | Karian's review cites a series in which all 26 thigh pouches and 4 orchiectomies were rated aesthetically unsatisfactory on patient satisfaction.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup> |
| Impaired spermatogenesis | Expected from thermal physiology. Objective histology and semen data in thigh-pouch patients are limited.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup> |

Relocation changes examination access. A new mass, swelling or persistent pain is assessed clinically and imaged when indicated.

## Comparison of the three strategies

| Feature | Temporary (Okwudili) | Definitive with flap closure (Staniorski) | Hiawatha neoscrotum (Mandel) |
|---|---|---|---|
| Residual scrotal skin required | Yes | No | No |
| Position of testes at the end | Returned to the scrotum | Thigh | Perineum, in a pseudocapsule neoscrotum |
| Neoscrotum mechanism | Expansion of residual scrotal skin | None | Fused pseudocapsules covered with STSG |
| Time to testicular return | 3 to 8 months, by manual return | Not applicable | After the second stage |
| Evidence | 12 patients | 20 patients | 1 patient |

## Fertility and thermal function

Spermatogenesis is temperature-dependent and occurs optimally slightly below core temperature. The testis is kept 2 to 7 °C below core.<sup>[[8]](#ref8)[[9]](#ref9)</sup> In experimental heat-stress protocols, loss of sperm production appears 1 to 2 weeks after heat exposure and peaks 4 to 5 weeks thereafter. Hotter or longer exposure causes more germ cell loss, and surviving sperm are more often abnormal and poorly motile.<sup>[[10]](#ref10)</sup> Karian's review states that thigh placement may compromise fertility because of high temperatures, and that objective histology and spermatogenesis data are limited.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup>

Animal heating schedules do not translate into a clinical deadline. Preserved volume in Okwudili's series and a normal sperm count in Mandel's single patient are not evidence of preserved reproductive function. Fertility goals are discussed before relocation when circumstances allow, with semen assessment or fertility-preservation consultation when appropriate. Source control and salvage of viable tissue are not delayed for an elective reproductive workup.<sup>[[2]](#ref2)[[6]](#ref6)[[1]](#ref1)</sup>
