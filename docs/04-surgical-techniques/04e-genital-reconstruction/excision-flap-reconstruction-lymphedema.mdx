# Excision With Flap Reconstruction for Genital Lymphedema

**Excision with flap reconstruction** removes diseased genital tissue and replaces it with vascularized coverage. A flap is used when the remaining healthy skin cannot close without tension, the recipient surface is unsuitable for grafting, dead space needs filling, or the defect needs a particular shape or tissue quality. Lymphatic reconstruction is an additional, selected objective. Transferring skin or muscle does not by itself restore normal drainage.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[2]](#ref2)</sup>

See [debulking scrotoplasty](/docs/surgical-techniques/04e-genital-reconstruction/debulking-scrotoplasty) for the excisional operation, [modified Charles](/docs/surgical-techniques/04e-genital-reconstruction/modified-charles-procedure) for the variable use of that name, and [scrotal flaps](/docs/surgical-techniques/04e-genital-reconstruction/scrotal-flaps) for the broader coverage inventory.

## Decide From the Defect and Donor Tissue

Assess penile versus scrotal involvement, the actual viable skin remaining, perfusion, prior groin operations or radiation, and the location of the testes, cords and urethra. A 50% scrotal skin-loss threshold for flap coverage has been cited, but it is not a validated genital-lymphedema rule. Exposure of a viable testis does not automatically preclude grafting. What matters is an adequately vascularized recipient surface and the full coverage requirement.<sup>[[2]](#ref2)[[3]](#ref3)</sup>

| Finding | Reconstructive consideration |
|---|---|
| Sufficient healthy mobile skin | Tension-free closure or local advancement may provide coverage with limited donor morbidity. Preserve useful local vascularity. |
| Broad superficial defect with a suitable bed | Consider a graft; a regional flap is not obligatory simply because the defect is large. |
| Poor graft bed, deeper exposed structure, dead space or complex contour | A regional flap can supply vascularized tissue and appropriate bulk. Plan reach, tunneling, pedicle protection and donor closure. |
| Deficient lymphatic drainage with suitable donor/recipient anatomy | Discuss a lymphatic procedure in addition to the coverage plan. Imaging can assist selection, but no universal genital algorithm has been validated. |
| Prior failed closure, graft or flap | Reassess residual disease, perfusion, infection, mechanical tension and proximal obstruction before choosing the next operation. |

These are planning principles. They do not form a mandatory progression through procedures. Massive perineal involvement may need combined coverage, for example bilateral gracilis flaps with a skin graft overlay<sup>[[20]](#ref20)</sup> or bilateral medial thigh flaps with a composite ALT flap.<sup>[[22]](#ref22)</sup><sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[2]](#ref2)</sup>

## Coverage Options

| Option | Useful features and limitations |
|---|---|
| **Local scrotal/perineal flaps** | Use healthy adjacent tissue where available. Halperin's two cases used anterior and posterior perineal flaps. The authors proposed that posteriorly based flaps may preserve perirectal collateral lymphatics and noted that thigh flaps alter testicular thermoregulation and may cause infertility. Neither point was compared directly with another reconstruction.<sup>[[4]](#ref4)</sup> Yormuk described one patient with a suprapubic U-shaped flap anteriorly and posterior scrotal skin posteriorly. The denuded penis was passed through a buttonhole in the anterior flap, and the testes were fixed in pouches between the two flaps.<sup>[[15]](#ref15)</sup> |
| **Pudendal-thigh flap** | A regional skin option based on the internal pudendal vessels, with protective sensation when elevated as a neurovascular flap. Mopuri's five-case report describes a modified elevation and inset and a natural-appearing neoscrotum.<sup>[[5]](#ref5)</sup> Karaçal used proximally based neurovascular flaps up to 20 × 15 cm in 10 scrotal defects and closed all donor sites primarily.<sup>[[16]](#ref16)</sup> Both series were mixed-etiology and neither was comparative. |
| **Anterolateral thigh (ALT)** | Can cover a larger or more complex defect. Thickness, arc of rotation, tunneling and donor morbidity matter. Yu's seven-patient series included perineoscrotal wounds and ischial ulcers. All flaps survived, and three patients had minor posterior wound dehiscence from fecal contamination.<sup>[[6]](#ref6)</sup> Spyropoulou reported 11 pedicled thigh flaps (10 ALT, 1 anteromedial) for perineoscrotal and vaginal defects, with pedicle lengths of 10–12 cm, stable closure and no donor-site complications.<sup>[[17]](#ref17)</sup> Lin's series of 42 pedicled ALT flaps included 10 penoscrotal reconstructions.<sup>[[18]](#ref18)</sup> None was specific to lymphedema. |
| **Gracilis or gracilis-perforator coverage** | Muscle may help fill dead space; perforator designs can preserve muscle. Kayikçioğlu's short gracilis flap is based on the muscle origin at the pubis, which gives greater mobility, avoids pedicle-related dog ears and atrophies with time (two Fournier cases).<sup>[[19]](#ref19)</sup> Hsu's longitudinal myofasciocutaneous advancement flap incorporates the perigracilis fascia to widen the reliable skin territory. In eight Fournier patients one had a hematoma and one a donor-thigh abscess at three months.<sup>[[21]](#ref21)</sup> Coskunfirat's perforator flap preserves the muscle; seven Fournier cases had direct donor closure and two wound dehiscences. Despite the paper's title, it made no comparative demonstration of superiority.<sup>[[7]](#ref7)</sup> |
| **Internal pudendal artery perforator (IPAP) propeller flap** | Can provide local contour and a concealed donor scar when the perforator and rotation are suitable. Han reported ten penoscrotal reconstructions. The perforator was located by handheld Doppler and the flap was rotated more than 90° with its long axis in the gluteal fold. Mean flap size was 6.7 × 11.7 cm and one flap had partial distal necrosis that healed spontaneously.<sup>[[8]](#ref8)</sup> This is limited experience and does not show the lowest complication rate. |

Flap anatomy and technique are covered in the [scrotal flap overview](/docs/surgical-techniques/04e-genital-reconstruction/scrotal-flaps) and its foundations companions.

### Penile Coverage From Healthy Scrotal Tissue

A scrotal flap can cover a penile skin defect only when the proposed donor is suitable. Lymphedematous scrotal skin is not automatically an acceptable donor. Hair, bulk, contraction and loss of scrotal volume are discussed with the patient.

Mendel's 22-patient retrospective penile-shaft series included diverse indications, particularly buried penis and foreign-material injection. Early dehiscence occurred in 31.8% and late skin retraction in 27.3%. Testicular ascension occurred in 22.7%. Early and late revision rates were 9.1% and 27.3%. Only 12 patients returned the functional questionnaire. Median global satisfaction was 8/10 and the median erection hardness score was 3.5/4. Neither establishes preservation of baseline erectile function, and the series was not specific to genital lymphedema.<sup>[[9]](#ref9)</sup>

## Lymphatic Flaps: Promising, With Limited Comparative Evidence

SCIP lymphatic flaps combine soft-tissue coverage with transfer of lymphatic structures. Flap survival and drainage benefit are separate outcomes, and a pedicled lymphatic flap differs from a free vascularized lymph-node transfer. Selection requires specialist assessment of donor lymphatics, perfusion and potential donor-site harm.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[10]](#ref10)</sup>

| Report | Actual population and interpretation |
|---|---|
| **Abdelfattah 2023** | Twenty-six men: 15 isolated scrotal and 11 penoscrotal cases. All flaps survived, and symptom/cellulitis scores improved at a mean 44.9 months. Mean genital lymphedema score fell from 6.2 to 0.05. The cohort was uncontrolled, so comparative recurrence prevention is not established.<sup>[[10]](#ref10)</sup> |
| **Yamamoto 2022, 3R** | Seven men had excision with SCIP lymphatic flap reconstruction. No genital complication or recurrence was observed over mean 22.7-month follow-up. Postoperative compression was not used in that study; this is a protocol observation and does not show that all patients can stop compression permanently.<sup>[[11]](#ref11)</sup> |
| **Ciudad 2025, CHASCIP** | Eight men had excision and bilateral pedicled lymphatic SCIP flaps. No recurrence was observed at mean 34 months, but two patients had wound complications, including partial graft loss. There was no excision-only comparison.<sup>[[12]](#ref12)</sup> |

These small uncontrolled series support individualized use. They do not establish lymphatic SCIP transfer as the standard or demonstrate cure, and their populations should not be pooled into a single complication rate.

## Interpret the Comparative Literature Carefully

Guiotto's review combined 20 studies (1980–2016, 151 patients). Excision with flap reconstruction accounted for 39.1% of procedures and had a 54.2% overall complication rate, against 10% for excision with primary closure or graft and 9% for lymphovenous anastomosis. Patients receiving excisional procedures tended to have later-stage disease, and the review found no consensus on assessment or management.<sup>[[13]](#ref13)</sup> These pooled figures cannot rank a flap against primary closure, grafting or LVA for an otherwise identical patient.

Complications cluster around perineal contamination (fecal and urinary soiling drives dehiscence and infection)<sup>[[6]](#ref6)</sup> and around thigh-flap bulk, muscle atrophy and donor-site morbidity. Results after Fournier gangrene, cancer, trauma and genital lymphedema are not interchangeable. Flap loss is one endpoint; a surviving flap may still be associated with wound breakdown, bulk, altered sensation, recurrent edema or revision. Regional-flap experience informs coverage, while lymphedema-specific drainage and recurrence need separate assessment.

**Fertility and thermoregulation remain incompletely studied.** Thin, pliable coverage is desirable. Thigh flaps have been proposed to alter testicular thermoregulation and impair fertility,<sup>[[4]](#ref4)</sup> but no study has compared spermatogenesis among local flaps, thigh flaps and grafts. Fertility goals are addressed separately when relevant.<sup>[[3]](#ref3)</sup>

Chitale proposed that a tensor fascia lata musculocutaneous flap absorbs lymph, after observing no lymphorrhea following groin block dissection. In 12 patients with tubercular or filarial lymphedema, groin block dissection with the flap reduced edema; in one patient with congenital leg lymphedema the flap was used as a bridge to the normally draining thigh. The mechanism was inferred and the series mixed lower-limb and genital disease. It does not support routine groin block dissection or a claim that any muscle flap restores lymphatic function.<sup>[[14]](#ref14)</sup>

## Recovery and Assessment

Monitoring covers flap perfusion, compression or kinking of the pedicle, hematoma, infection, wound separation and urinary or fecal contamination. Mobilization and thromboprophylaxis are planned to protect the inset and fit the operation and patient. Local support or compression is begun or resumed when appropriate for the reconstruction, in coordination with the lymphedema team.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup>

Follow-up covers wound healing, recurrence, lymphorrhea, cellulitis, voiding, sexual symptoms, donor morbidity and patient priorities. Flap viability alone does not define success.
