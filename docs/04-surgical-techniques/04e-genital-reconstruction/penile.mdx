# Penile Reconstruction

This atlas organizes reconstruction of penile skin, glans and more extensive defects after trauma, infection, inflammatory disease, congenital differences or tumor resection. Start with the [General Principles](/docs/surgical-techniques/04e-genital-reconstruction/genital-reconstruction-principles), then use the technique links according to the tissue missing and the patient's functional goals. [Peyronie's surgery](/docs/surgical-techniques/04j-sexual-dysfunction/peyronies-disease) and [Gender-Affirming Surgery](/docs/surgical-techniques/04k-gender-affirming-surgery) have separate pathways.

## Assess the problem

Determine what viable skin and dartos remain, whether the urethra, glans or corpora are involved, and whether the wound is ready for reconstruction. Preserve useful native tissue and avoid a closure that restricts erection. Review sensation, voiding, sexual activity, appearance, donor morbidity and the likelihood of staged treatment.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[2]](#ref2)</sup>

| Presentation | Assessment that changes the plan |
|---|---|
| Adult-acquired buried penis | Separate shaft-skin deficiency from contributory suprapubic tissue, abdominal pannus and scrotal disease; evaluate associated urinary symptoms |
| Infection or Fournier's gangrene | Control infection and complete debridement before definitive coverage; determine bed viability and any need to fill dead space |
| LS or another inflammatory skin disorder | Assess skin, meatus and urethra separately; coordinate disease treatment and reconstruction |
| Foreign-body granuloma | Establish the extent of material and diseased tissue, healthy donor options and whether immediate coverage is appropriate |
| Lymphedema or hidradenitis | Assess combined penile, scrotal and perineal involvement; tailor tissue removal and coverage |
| Tumor excision | Establish histology, oncologic extent and appropriate margins before selecting reconstruction |
| Circumcision injury with shaft skin loss | Measure residual skin. In a 12-patient series with 65% to 95% skin loss, a Mogen clamp caused 9 injuries; the authors reconstructed 10 with FTSG and 2 with tissue expanders and prefer expansion when enough residual skin can be expanded<sup>[[13]](#ref13)</sup> |
| Trauma or congenital/redo defect | Assess all involved structures and previous repairs; use specialist reconstruction where necessary. Tissue expansion is used after failed hypospadias or exstrophy-epispadias repair when residual skin is scarce |

These are planning categories, not automatic instructions to use one flap or graft for each diagnosis.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[2]](#ref2)</sup>

## Choose coverage by tissue requirements

| Findings | Options to consider | Important tradeoff |
|---|---|---|
| Enough viable skin for closure without distortion | Primary closure, local advancement or selected Z-plasty | Avoid tension and a constricting ring |
| Superficial loss with a vascularized bed | STSG or FTSG | Donor quality, graft take, contracture, sensation and hair characteristics |
| Need for vascularized coverage or additional tissue | Suitable local, regional or free flap | Pedicle availability, donor morbidity, bulk, hair and possible staging |
| Matrix-assisted grafting being considered | Product-specific dermal matrix plus STSG | Uncertain comparative benefit, cost and product-dependent timing |
| Residual skin suitable for planned expansion | Selected staged tissue expansion | Device complications, repeated visits and reoperation burden |

No validated circumference threshold universally separates primary closure, grafting and flap reconstruction. A graft bed and the covering over exposed corpora or testes must be assessed directly. Scrotal donor skin may be hair-bearing; neither a flap nor a graft guarantees normal sensation or elasticity.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[2]](#ref2)[[3]](#ref3)</sup>

Do not place Integra on a clinically infected wound. NPWT or a silicone cover does not remove this contraindication. MatriDerm and Integra are not interchangeable staged protocols: selected MatriDerm formulations have been used with simultaneous STSG, while bilayer Integra is often followed by delayed grafting.<sup>[[4]](#ref4)[[5]](#ref5)[[6]](#ref6)</sup> Details are in [Penile Skin Grafting](/docs/surgical-techniques/04e-genital-reconstruction/penile-skin-grafting) and [Penile Tissue Substitutes](/docs/surgical-techniques/04e-genital-reconstruction/penile-tissue-substitutes).

Retrospective series report the following outcomes for common shaft-skin options. In 54 adults, STSG gave more than 90% graft take in 52, with maintained or improved erection and normal voiding.<sup>[[2]](#ref2)</sup> In 22 patients repaired with bilateral pedicled scrotal flaps, early complications were suture dehiscence (31.8%), infection (13.6%) and hematoma (4.6%), with 9.1% early revisions. Late complications were skin retraction (27.3%), testicular ascension (22.7%) and penile shortening (13.6%), with 27.3% late revisions.<sup>[[3]](#ref3)</sup> These are different populations and are not a comparison.

## Buried-penis classification

PAS describes the penile skin and escutcheon complex (P), abdominal pannus (A) and scrotal skin (S). In its 101-patient multicenter validation cohort from 10 institutions, agreement between two reviewers was high (κ = 0.95) and categories correlated with the operations performed. The most common subtypes were P2c (contributory escutcheon with insufficient penile skin; 27%), A0 (no pannus; 41%) and S0 (normal scrotal skin; 71%). Repairs included escutcheonectomy (55%), scrotoplasty (48%), STSG (47%), penile skin excision (44%) and panniculectomy (7%). This supports structured description. It does not show that a PAS category dictates the best operation or predicts every outcome.<sup>[[7]](#ref7)</sup>

Mirastschijski proposed a three-type system. Type 1 (pseudo-buried penis) has the penis under lax skin without retraction and is treated by apronectomy and prepubic lift with tissue fixation. Type 2 (intermediate) has partial invagination and is treated by prepubic apronectomy with anchoring sutures. Type 3 (classical) has complete retraction into prepubic fat, often with stenotic scar after circumcision, and is treated by scar excision, fat reduction with extraction of the shaft, anchoring sutures and reconstruction of the penile envelope. It is another descriptive planning framework and not an interchangeable validated treatment rule. Use classifications to communicate anatomy while matching the operation to actual findings and patient goals.<sup>[[8]](#ref8)</sup> See [Buried Penis Repair](/docs/surgical-techniques/04e-genital-reconstruction/buried-penis-repair).

## Congenital penile torsion and penoscrotal web

Torsion and curvature are reassessed after degloving with an artificial erection. Degloving and release of dartos at the penoscrotal junction may itself reduce torsion; for persistent torsion, rotation of a dorsal dartos flap has been described.<sup>[[14]](#ref14)[[15]](#ref15)</sup> For severe torsion, one described technique divides the dysgenetic dartos bands at the penile base and, through a ventral proximal midline incision when needed, fixes the base of the right corporal body to the pubic symphysis to add clockwise rotation, placing the suture lateral to the 10-o'clock dorsal-nerve branch; radical urethral mobilization has not been needed for isolated torsion in this author's experience. During skin closure in severe torsion, the median raphe is not used as the midline reference, because this can recreate counterclockwise rotation. One chapter recommends deferring newborn circumcision when counterclockwise torsion exceeds 60 degrees so repair and circumcision can occur under one anesthetic, and reports that dorsal dartos rotation, often combined with ventral dartos resection, has corrected torsion of 90 degrees or more, without a denominator or cohort.<sup>[[15]](#ref15)</sup>

For penoscrotal webbing, one described approach uses inverted-V incisions on either side of the web and establishes the penoscrotal junction by securing ventral shaft skin to the corpora on either side of the urethra, avoiding skin dimpling.<sup>[[14]](#ref14)</sup> These are chapter-attributed operative descriptions without denominators or comparative outcomes; the existing curvature guidance on the [Peyronie's Disease](/docs/surgical-techniques/04j-sexual-dysfunction/peyronies-disease) pages remains the reference for acquired curvature.

## Glans and cancer-related reconstruction

Select organ-preserving treatment when oncologically appropriate, with histologically negative margins and a feasible surveillance plan. The EAU guideline states that an excision margin of 5 to 10 mm gives acceptably low recurrence rates and that recurrence rose considerably only when the margin was under 1 mm. It allows minimal macroscopic margins for smaller, less aggressive lesions and suggests a wider margin or partial penectomy for higher-risk lesions. Margin width therefore depends on size, grade and invasion, and a universal 5–10 mm prescription is inappropriate for bulky or higher-risk tumors. **Glans resurfacing** preserves the underlying glans; **glansectomy with neoglans reconstruction** addresses a different defect. Local recurrence after organ-sparing treatment requires restaging and individualized salvage.<sup>[[9]](#ref9)</sup>

Elst's 550-patient retrospective study involved a lower-risk glans-sparing cohort (79% pT1) and excluded lymphatic or systemic disease preceding or accompanying local recurrence. At a median follow-up of 41 months it observed 29% local recurrence and 99% five-year cancer-specific survival. Penile intraepithelial neoplasia in the surgical margin independently predicted local recurrence (HR 2.28). That finding cannot establish harmlessness of local recurrence in every penile-cancer population. Current EAU guidance specifically distinguishes higher-risk cohorts in which local recurrence is associated with poorer survival.<sup>[[10]](#ref10)[[9]](#ref9)</sup>

A review of 14 glansectomy studies encompassing 327 procedures found generally favorable functional outcomes but heterogeneous definitions and reporting. At a mean follow-up of 40.7 months the mean recurrence rate was 9.1%. Mean percentages across studies were 91.1% with preserved erectile function, 62.5% sexually active, 75.6% voiding standing, 83.7% with maintained glans sensation and 86.3% satisfied with appearance; partial graft loss (6.1%) and meatal stenosis (8.1%) were the main complications. These study-level means are not an individual guarantee or proof of equivalence between reconstructive options.<sup>[[11]](#ref11)</sup> See [Glans Reconstruction](/docs/surgical-techniques/04e-genital-reconstruction/glans-reconstruction).

NCCN Version 2.2026 considers partial penectomy the standard surgical approach for high-grade primary tumors when a functional stump and negative margins can be retained; total penectomy is recommended when this is not feasible (category 2A). Cavernosal invasion requires partial or total penectomy, with intraoperative frozen sections to assess margins. Reconstruction is planned around the tissue remaining after an adequate resection.<sup>[[17]](#ref17)</sup> NCCN restricts pT1a glansectomy to cases requiring it for complete clearance and lists penectomy for T2 or greater disease; EAU supports suitable organ-sparing surgery for glans-confined cT1–T2 disease. These selection criteria differ from the EAU organ-sparing approach.<sup>[[17]](#ref17)[[9]](#ref9)</sup>

For partial penectomy specifically, one described meatal-maturation sequence mobilizes the urethra distally from the corpus spongiosum (about 1–1.5 cm) before transection, spatulates it dorsally, and approximates it to the penile skin to form an oblique meatus whose open side faces 12 o'clock; adequate spatulation, mucosal eversion at skin approximation and a long elliptical suture line are proposed to reduce meatal stenosis, though the chapter gives no comparative stenosis rate for these steps.<sup>[[16]](#ref16)</sup>

## Interpret the technique database

The database includes established procedures and less common described techniques. Listing a technique is not a claim that it is preferred for a diagnosis or that its outcomes are equivalent to other options. For example, the EPAP hemi-scrotal flap report describes one patient; it cannot establish elimination of pain or superiority over other scrotal flaps.<sup>[[12]](#ref12)</sup>

Plan the sequence from the wound bed and defect extent: wound bed and infection status first, then circumference and structures exposed, then graft, flap, matrix or expansion, and finally concurrent procedures such as escutcheonectomy, scrotoplasty, panniculectomy or urethroplasty. Plan wound and donor-site care, dressing and catheter needs, and follow-up according to the actual reconstruction. Assess healing, sensation, erection-related tethering, urinary function, recurrent disease and patient-reported outcomes over time. Graft take, flap survival and success of the complete reconstruction are different endpoints.<sup>[[2]](#ref2)[[3]](#ref3)</sup>
