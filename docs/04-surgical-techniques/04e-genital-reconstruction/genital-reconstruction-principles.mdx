# Principles of Genital Reconstruction

**Genital reconstruction** repairs penile, scrotal, vulvar and perineal defects after disease, injury or tissue loss. The plan follows the tissue actually missing and the patient's priorities for urinary function, sexual activity, sensation, appearance and protection of exposed structures. Coverage alone is not a complete functional outcome.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[2]](#ref2)</sup>

The dedicated [Gender-Affirming Surgery](/docs/surgical-techniques/04k-gender-affirming-surgery) section covers those pathways. [Peyronie's surgery](/docs/surgical-techniques/04j-sexual-dysfunction/peyronies-disease) addresses tunical deformity. These skin and soft-tissue principles do not replace that assessment.

## Define the defect before choosing a technique

| Domain | Questions that change the operation |
|---|---|
| Skin and soft tissue | What viable skin remains? Is the problem limited to surface coverage, or is there fibrosis, lymphedema or substantial dead space? |
| Urethra and voiding | Is there a meatal/urethral lesion, fistula or obstruction requiring separate evaluation or reconstruction? |
| Glans, corpora and neurovascular structures | Which structures are intact, exposed or injured? What can be preserved safely? |
| Scrotum and testes | Are the testes and their coverings viable? Can residual scrotal tissue provide tension-free coverage? |
| Vulva, vagina and perineum | Will closure narrow or displace the introitus, urethral opening or anus? Is deeper vaginal or pelvic reconstruction required? |
| Recipient and donor tissues | Consider previous surgery, radiation, scars, vascular supply, tissue quality and donor morbidity |

Deep pelvic defects need a plan for filling dead space and protecting organs as well as replacing skin. Extensive resections are planned jointly with the urologic, gynecologic, colorectal and plastic-surgery teams.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[3]](#ref3)[[2]](#ref2)</sup>

## Choose the reconstruction that meets the need

The reconstructive ladder organizes the available options. A suitable flap can be chosen without first failing every simpler operation.

| Option | Typical role | Main limitation |
|---|---|---|
| Primary closure or local advancement | Adequate viable tissue that closes without tension or distortion | Excessive tension can compromise healing or narrow adjacent openings |
| STSG or FTSG | Surface coverage on a vascularized bed | Requires revascularization; cannot fill substantial dead space; contracture and sensory changes remain possible |
| Local or regional flap | Provides vascularized coverage, sometimes with useful bulk | Pedicle reach, prior treatment and donor morbidity constrain choice |
| Free tissue transfer | Selected extensive, composite or otherwise unsuitable defects | Requires recipient vessels, microsurgical expertise and a postoperative monitoring plan |

Vascularity, depth and functional requirements matter alongside defect size. A large superficial genital wound may accept a graft, while a smaller but deep defect may need vascularized tissue. Exposed testes alone do not automatically require a flap if a suitable vascularized covering remains.<sup>[[4]](#ref4)[[1]](#ref1)[[3]](#ref3)</sup>

Examples by tier: local or regional fasciocutaneous flaps depend on the internal pudendal territory, the upper medial thigh plexus or the descending branch of the inferior gluteal artery, and pudendal-thigh and lotus petal flaps are examples. When the defect is pelviperineal, myocutaneous flaps supply resurfacing and the bulk to fill the pelvic cavity. Rectus and gluteus flaps have largely superseded gracilis in most situations because of the gracilis flap's intrinsic limitations. Perforator-based muscle-sparing flaps are increasingly reported, and free tissue transfer is limited to defects that standard techniques cannot cover.<sup>[[8]](#ref8)</sup> In a two-center series of 46 patients, smaller perineal defects were treated with local flaps, and the rectus abdominis flap remained the standard for larger defects that also needed dead-space closure.<sup>[[9]](#ref9)</sup>

## Establish readiness and preserve viable tissue

Control infection and remove nonviable tissue before definitive reconstruction. After destructive infection, reconstruction proceeds when the wound is stable and further debridement is not needed. No fixed waiting interval suits every patient. Tailor excision to the disease while preserving unaffected skin, dartos and important neurovascular structures when appropriate.<sup>[[4]](#ref4)</sup>

For grafts, establish hemostasis and close contact with a viable recipient bed, then protect against shear, fluid collection and constriction. Dermal matrices and NPWT are selective adjuncts and do not replace wound preparation. The Integra labeling contraindicates placement on a clinically infected wound; follow the instructions for the exact product.<sup>[[4]](#ref4)[[5]](#ref5)</sup>

Radiated, scarred or repeatedly operated tissues may require recruitment of healthier vascularized tissue. Donor and pedicle choices account for abdominal incisions, previous flap harvest and stoma requirements. Immediate versus staged reconstruction depends on source control, margin assessment, the exposed structures and the consequences of leaving the wound open.<sup>[[3]](#ref3)</sup>

## Match the plan to the anatomy

**Penile skin:** preserve adequate mobility for erection and avoid a constricting closure. Assess shaft skin, glans, urethra and corpora separately. Graft thickness and donor selection involve tradeoffs, and small retrospective comparisons do not show STSG and FTSG to be equivalent. See [Penile Skin Grafting](/docs/surgical-techniques/04e-genital-reconstruction/penile-skin-grafting).<sup>[[4]](#ref4)</sup>

**Scrotum:** preserve viable residual tissue when possible. Grafts and regional flaps are both established options. A 2026 review of 107 reports encompassing 619 patients and 625 flaps after Fournier gangrene reported flap loss in 1.6%. Heterogeneous populations, limited comparative data and inconsistent functional reporting prevent a universal graft-versus-flap rule.<sup>[[6]](#ref6)</sup> See [Scrotal Reconstruction](/docs/surgical-techniques/04e-genital-reconstruction/scrotal).

**Vulva and perineum:** preserve or restore the position and caliber of urinary, vaginal and anal openings; minimize painful scar tension and consider the patient's sexual goals. Choose among local, thigh-based, abdominal or other flaps according to the three-dimensional defect and donor tissues. No single standardized flap sequence fits every vulvovaginal resection.<sup>[[2]](#ref2)[[1]](#ref1)</sup> See [Vulvar Reconstruction](/docs/surgical-techniques/04e-genital-reconstruction/vulvar).

## Reconstruction after penile tumor excision

The oncologic plan comes first: establish histology, achieve negative margins and choose organ preservation when suitable. The EAU guideline strongly recommends organ-sparing surgery and reconstructive techniques for lesions confined to the glans and prepuce (PeIN, Ta, T1–T2) in patients willing to comply with strict follow-up.<sup>[[7]](#ref7)</sup> The reconstructive options run from tissue-sparing to radical.<sup>[[18]](#ref18)</sup>

| Option | Reconstruction | EAU-reported recurrence-free rate |
|---|---|---|
| Mohs micrographic surgery | Margin-controlled excision of superficial disease | Mean 5-year rate 88.2% in 51 men, mostly T1. Data are very limited and the panel does not routinely recommend it<sup>[[7]](#ref7)</sup> |
| Glans resurfacing | Excision of glans epithelium with split-thickness skin graft | 5-year rate 75–96.6% (5 studies)<sup>[[7]](#ref7)</sup> |
| Partial or total glansectomy | Neoglans covered with split-thickness skin graft over the corpora | 5-year rate 78.0–95.8% (6 studies)<sup>[[7]](#ref7)</sup> |
| Partial penectomy | Preserves a stump for standing micturition; no universal stump length guarantees urinary or sexual function | Not tabulated here |
| Total penectomy | Perineal urethrostomy, or phalloplasty with a later erectile device in selected patients; phalloplasty is not obligatory | Not tabulated here |

Reconstruction is planned within the oncologic team so that surveillance and further treatment options are preserved. This section addresses defect reconstruction, not the complete cancer-management pathway.

## Scrotal reconstruction after Fournier gangrene

Reconstruction follows debridement and source control. The choice scales with the defect.<sup>[[6]](#ref6)[[12]](#ref12)[[17]](#ref17)</sup>

| Defect | Reconstruction |
|---|---|
| Small | Primary tension-free closure or local pedicled flaps from residual scrotal skin. Split-thickness grafts and distant flaps are reserved for extensive loss<sup>[[17]](#ref17)</sup> |
| Moderate, with exposed testes | Orchidopexy followed by a meshed split-thickness skin graft once the patient is stable. In 10 patients this gave acceptable cosmetic results, with no chronic pain or need for further intervention at a median follow-up of 8 months<sup>[[12]](#ref12)</sup> |
| Large | Regional flaps. Medial thigh, pudendal thigh, ALT and gracilis flaps were used most often in the 2026 review, and flap loss was reported in 1.6% of cases<sup>[[6]](#ref6)</sup> |

Testicular thigh pouches are another described option.<sup>[[12]](#ref12)</sup> The pedicled SCIP flap has been described in small reports (three patients with all flaps surviving, and one further case) as a thin, pliable flap with consistent anatomy for perineoscrotal defects.<sup>[[13]](#ref13)[[14]](#ref14)</sup>

## Vulvovaginal and perineal reconstruction after cancer resection

Treatment of vulvar and vaginal neoplasia aims at local control and often does not restore form and function. Höckel and Dornhöfer argue that reconstruction integrated into primary treatment improves aesthetic and functional results and quality of life. They note that functional restoration in irradiated tissue is limited and that a few standardized procedures cannot serve every patient.<sup>[[2]](#ref2)</sup>

Options are matched to defect size and site.<sup>[[10]](#ref10)</sup>

| Defect | Options |
|---|---|
| Small | Local advancement (for example V-Y or rhomboid flaps) |
| Moderate | Fasciocutaneous flaps from the internal pudendal or medial thigh territories, such as pudendal-thigh (Singapore) and lotus petal flaps, or gracilis flaps<sup>[[8]](#ref8)</sup> |
| Large or pelviperineal, including post-exenteration | Myocutaneous flaps such as the VRAM, which combine pelvic dead-space obliteration with perineal resurfacing<sup>[[8]](#ref8)[[9]](#ref9)</sup> |
| Vaginal reconstruction | Myocutaneous flaps (rectus, gluteus or gracilis) are of proven advantage<sup>[[8]](#ref8)</sup> |

In a 34-patient series after bowel, gynecologic or sacral tumor resection, surgical site infection and dehiscence were more frequent with thigh flaps than with abdominal flaps. Only 15 of 34 patients reached 5-year disease-free survival, and the authors describe most operations as salvage procedures.<sup>[[11]](#ref11)</sup>

## Gender-affirming genital surgery

Phalloplasty, metoidioplasty and vaginoplasty are covered in [Masculinizing Surgery](/docs/surgical-techniques/04k-gender-affirming-surgery/masculinizing-surgery) and [Feminizing Surgery](/docs/surgical-techniques/04k-gender-affirming-surgery/feminizing-surgery), including eligibility, staging and complications. The neourethra is the main source of morbidity after phalloplasty. A meta-analysis of 21 studies (1,566 patients) found a pooled urethral fistula or stenosis rate of 48.9%, with 91.5% able to void standing and 90.5% reporting a satisfactory outcome. Prelaminated-flap urethras had a higher stricture rate than tube-in-tube urethras.<sup>[[15]](#ref15)</sup> In a patient-reported survey of 129 transmasculine patients, 40% reported urethrocutaneous fistula, 32% urethral stricture and 19% worsened mental health. This suggests that surgeon-reported series underestimate the burden.<sup>[[16]](#ref16)</sup>

## Counseling and follow-up

Baseline function and the outcomes the patient values are documented. Counseling covers donor wounds, staged surgery, loss of sensation, painful erection or intercourse, contracture, wound problems, recurrent disease and possible revision. A favorable early appearance or graft-take percentage does not guarantee normal long-term sensation or function.<sup>[[4]](#ref4)[[2]](#ref2)</sup>

Follow-up assesses wound and donor-site healing, urinary function, sensation, sexual comfort, contracture and recurrence, with disease-specific surveillance when indicated. Patient-reported outcomes are recorded, and results distinguish people, operations, graft area and time to recurrence.
