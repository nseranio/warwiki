# Principles of Genital Reconstruction

Genital reconstruction sits at the intersection of plastic surgery, urology, and urogynecology. The reconstructive question is rarely "which operation" in isolation; it is "which defect, which tissue bed, which functional goal, and which stage." A short framework covers the decisions that drive most cases.

## Defect inventory

Every genital-reconstruction plan starts with an explicit inventory:

- **Skin / fasciocutaneous** — what coverage is missing and how much?
- **Corporal / erectile** — are the corpora healthy, scarred, or absent?
- **Urethral** — is the urethra continuous, strictured, fistulized, or absent over a segment?
- **Composite** — is there a combined skin + corporal + urethral defect requiring layered reconstruction?

The answer dictates whether the operation is a graft, a flap, a combined flap plus graft, or a staged reconstruction.

## Tissue-bed quality

The graft bed is as important as the graft itself. Hostile beds (radiation, infection, lichen sclerosus, dense scar) need vascularized coverage — omentum, gracilis, Martius, or a rotational flap — before any graft is placed. The same logic applies to prosthesis surgery: a hostile bed is a contraindication to an implant until the bed is fixed.

## Functional goals

Reconstruction is goal-directed. For buried penis, the goal is the exit of the penis from the pannus and a lined, healthy shaft. For Peyronie's, the goal is a straight, pain-free, erection-capable penis. For gender-affirming surgery, the goal is defined jointly with the patient and includes urinary function, sensation, and sexual function. For post-Fournier's reconstruction, the goal is durable coverage of the testes, penile shaft, and perineum. Clarifying the goal up front constrains the technique selection.

## Staging

Single-stage operations are elegant when the defect and the tissue bed support them. Most complex genital reconstructions are staged: resurface first, reconstruct second, implant third. The primary reason to stage is uncertainty — about graft take, about infection, about urethral healing, about patient readiness. Forcing a single stage in hostile conditions produces the complications the stages were designed to avoid.

## Erectile-tissue preservation

Corporal tissue is not regenerated. Every operation that involves the shaft should minimize corporal injury, preserve the dorsal neurovascular bundle, and avoid devascularizing the glans. Peyronie's correction, buried-penis repair, phalloplasty urethral tubularization, and oncologic resection all hinge on this principle.

## Primary perineal urethrostomy as a legitimate endpoint

In panurethral stricture, recurrent failed urethroplasty, lichen sclerosus with poor graft bed, or catastrophic penile injury, a primary perineal urethrostomy is a reconstructive *answer*, not a failure. Framing it up front as an option changes the conversation from "how many operations will it take to rebuild the urethra" to "what does this patient want to accomplish."
