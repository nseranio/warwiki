# Suction-Assisted Protein Lipectomy (SAPL / Liposuction)

Lymphedema liposuction removes excess subcutaneous adipose and fibrotic tissue in selected patients whose swelling remains predominantly non-pitting despite appropriate conservative treatment. It is a reductive operation: it does not reliably restore normal lymphatic drainage, and established standalone protocols require ongoing, generally lifelong, compression to maintain the result. The supporting evidence comes mainly from arm and leg lymphedema and cannot be transferred directly to penile or scrotal surgery.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[2]](#ref2)[[3]](#ref3)</sup>

For conservative care see [CDT](/docs/surgical-techniques/04e-genital-reconstruction/complex-decongestive-therapy). For genital disease see [Genital Lymphedema](/docs/clinical-conditions/03g-genital-scrotal/genital-lymphedema), [Debulking Scrotoplasty](/docs/surgical-techniques/04e-genital-reconstruction/debulking-scrotoplasty) and [Modified Charles Procedure](/docs/surgical-techniques/04e-genital-reconstruction/modified-charles-procedure). Physiologic options are discussed in [LVA](/docs/surgical-techniques/04e-genital-reconstruction/lymphaticovenous-anastomosis), [VLNT](/docs/surgical-techniques/04e-genital-reconstruction/vascularized-lymph-node-transfer) and [LYST](/docs/surgical-techniques/04e-genital-reconstruction/lyst).

## What the Operation Treats

Chronic lymphatic failure can produce both fluid accumulation and enlargement of adipose and fibrotic tissue. The fluid component is often pitting and responds to compression. Physiologic procedures such as LVA and VLNT may improve drainage in selected patients, with the choice guided by functional collectors, prior treatment and anatomy.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[2]](#ref2)</sup> The solid component is non-pitting adipose hypertrophy and fibrosis that typically presents later. Compression and physiologic surgery do not remove it; liposuction or excision does.<sup>[[9]](#ref9)</sup> The components overlap. Pitting, tissue consistency, skin changes and imaging when useful guide treatment; the ISL limb stage alone does not identify the amount of removable fat or dictate a genital operation.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[3]](#ref3)</sup>

Adipose tissue from lymphedematous arms differs from obesity-related adipose tissue. In 20 women with breast-cancer-related lymphedema, transcriptomic analysis showed upregulated inflammatory response, chemotaxis and angiogenesis pathways, with few genes shared with obesity-induced adipose tissue.<sup>[[14]](#ref14)</sup> A hypothesis-generating study of 11 affected women and 11 healthy controls found higher basal lipolysis, inflammatory cytokine secretion and (lymph)angiogenic capacity in lymphedema adipose tissue, and exposure of preadipocytes to undrained lymphedema fluid improved their adipogenic conversion in vitro.<sup>[[15]](#ref15)</sup> These findings suggest a self-reinforcing tissue process.

| Dominant finding | Practical implication |
|---|---|
| Substantial mobile fluid/pitting | Optimize conservative edema control and assess lymphatic function before deciding on tissue reduction |
| Persistent excess adipose tissue with little pitting | Specialized liposuction may reduce bulk when conservative care has reached a plateau |
| Diseased skin, ulceration, large redundant folds or dense genital elephantiasis | Liposuction alone cannot replace removal and reconstruction of a diseased skin envelope |
| Mixed disease or previous lymphatic reconstruction | Consider an individualized combined or staged plan that protects useful collectors, bypasses and transferred tissue |

These are selection principles. No validated sequence requires LVA or VLNT before every liposuction procedure.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[2]](#ref2)[[3]](#ref3)[[8]](#ref8)</sup>

**SAPL**, suction-assisted lipectomy, circumferential suction-assisted lipectomy (CSAL), debulking liposuction and lymphedema liposuction describe the therapeutic aim. Power assistance describes the cannula device; dry, wet and tumescent techniques describe infiltration. The operative method varies by cannula device and infiltration approach.<sup>[[4]](#ref4)[[7]](#ref7)[[9]](#ref9)</sup>

## Selection and Preparation

Assessment should include the cause of swelling, cancer status where relevant, previous lymphatic operations, skin health, recurrent infection, functional burden and the patient's goals. Treat active infection before elective tissue reduction. Establish access to an experienced lymphedema therapist and a feasible compression plan before surgery.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[3]](#ref3)[[7]](#ref7)</sup>

- Document a plateau after appropriate conservative treatment, rather than requiring an arbitrary duration of failed therapy.
- Measure the affected region consistently. A normal opposite limb is useful in unilateral disease; bilateral disease needs a different reference and follow-up strategy, not automatic exclusion.
- Use MRI or other appropriate imaging when needed to distinguish fluid from adipose tissue or plan around prior reconstruction. The ALERT program used MRI to document increased adipose tissue with minimal fluid in the affected limb. Bioimpedance spectroscopy (L-Dex) measures extracellular fluid; it does not itself measure the amount of fat to remove.<sup>[[3]](#ref3)</sup>
- ICG lymphography, where available, maps superficial lymphatic function and can help protect functioning channels during liposuction. It does not by itself determine the sequence of LVA, VLNT and liposuction.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup>
- Order appropriate postoperative compression in advance and assess whether the patient can apply, tolerate and replace it. Consider assistance, travel, cost and long-term follow-up.

Published centers use different entry criteria: for example, the Australian ALERT cohort required a greater than 20% limb or segment volume difference plus minimal fluid and substantial morbidity.<sup>[[3]](#ref3)</sup> Selection criteria vary across centers, without a common 25% cutoff.<sup>[[3]](#ref3)</sup> The AVF/AVLS/SVM expert panel itself did not reach consensus on offering reductive surgery after failed conservative care.<sup>[[3]](#ref3)[[12]](#ref12)</sup>

## Operative Principles — Extremity Protocols

These principles describe specialized limb liposuction. They are not instructions for passing a cannula around the penis, scrotum or spermatic cords.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[3]](#ref3)[[11]](#ref11)</sup>

1. **Map and protect.** Mark the planned treatment region and any previous LVA or VLNT sites. Define the tissue plane and skin viability to preserve. Choose anesthesia, infiltration and tourniquet use for the actual operation.<sup>[[3]](#ref3)[[6]](#ref6)</sup>
2. **Use controlled longitudinal passes.** The ALERT Brorson-based protocol is performed under general anesthesia with a tourniquet applied as proximally as possible after exsanguination with Esmarch bandages. It uses multiple small stab incisions and a power-assisted system with spiral cannulas, and cannula movement is longitudinal to the limb to reduce lymphatic injury. Circumferential treatment of the limb does not mean circumferentially sweeping the cannula through its lymphatic pathways. A 2026 dry-liposuction video article from another group describes 3 mm and 4 mm cannulas.<sup>[[3]](#ref3)[[7]](#ref7)</sup>
3. **Distinguish dry from tumescent portions.** Tourniquet-assisted dry treatment can be followed by tumescent treatment of the proximal region previously under the tourniquet. Both ALERT and the 2026 Sollie technical report describe this combination. Power assistance can be combined with tumescent infiltration; comparative evidence on lymphatic injury from infiltration versus dry treatment is lacking.<sup>[[3]](#ref3)[[7]](#ref7)</sup>
4. **Reassess contour, skin and hemostasis.** In the ALERT protocol, limb measurements are checked against the unaffected limb during the operation, and aspiration continues until the aspirated volume matches the calculated volume difference. Use repeat measurements and the patient's anatomy to judge the endpoint. A reported reduction beyond 100% is an excess-volume calculation and does not call for over-resection.<sup>[[3]](#ref3)</sup>
5. **Provide planned compression and recovery support.** In the ALERT tourniquet protocol, compression is applied to the treated segment before tourniquet release, followed by treatment and compression of the proximal segment. Compression before release limits hematoma and early refilling. This protocol requires trained postoperative care.<sup>[[3]](#ref3)[[7]](#ref7)</sup>

Blood loss is a material risk in historical limb series. Hoffner reported transfusion in 15/27 dry cases without a tourniquet, 5/35 dry cases with a tourniquet and 0/43 later cases with tourniquet plus tumescence.<sup>[[17]](#ref17)</sup> Karlsson reported five transfusions early in the leg series and none after October 2006.<sup>[[20]](#ref20)</sup> The techniques described here use different devices, cannula sizes and infiltration methods without establishing one preferred approach for all patients; the consensus review leaves the timing of liposuction with physiologic procedures unresolved.<sup>[[2]](#ref2)[[7]](#ref7)</sup> The 2026 dry-technique article is a descriptive technical report without a comparative cohort or standardized outcome analysis.<sup>[[2]](#ref2)[[7]](#ref7)</sup>

| Technique | Description | Reported points |
|---|---|---|
| Dry liposuction | No tumescent infiltration; the Brorson original | Proponents argue it is gentler on lymph vessels; this is an expert-opinion claim without comparative data<sup>[[7]](#ref7)</sup> |
| Power-assisted (PAL) | Vibrating cannula tip | Used in the ALERT and Granoff protocols<sup>[[3]](#ref3)[[4]](#ref4)</sup> |
| Wet, superwet, tumescent | Tumescent fluid infiltrated before aspiration | Used for the proximal segment after tourniquet release in ALERT<sup>[[3]](#ref3)</sup> |
| Laser-assisted, water-assisted | Energy or a water jet to loosen fat before aspiration | Listed among current practices; no consensus on effectiveness<sup>[[7]](#ref7)</sup> |

## Compression and Follow-Up

Ongoing compression is part of the treatment. It does not indicate that the operation has cured lymphatic dysfunction. Compression garments are applied in the operating room. In the ALERT protocol, arms receive a class 2 flat-knit sleeve and glove, and legs receive an adjustable Velcro wrap with foam padding and then a class 3 garment. In the ALERT protocol, arm garments were changed after 2 days and every other day until patients resumed daily alternate wash and wear; leg wraps were exchanged for garments within a week. Garments were typically replaced every 3 to 6 months during the first year and every 6 months afterward once limb volume reduction was stable.<sup>[[3]](#ref3)</sup> One group recommends lifelong class 4 garments (36 mmHg or higher) as its own practice.<sup>[[7]](#ref7)</sup> In a 1998 prospective study, garments were removed for one week in six patients with complete reduction 1 year after surgery; arm volume increased markedly and returned to baseline on reapplication.<sup>[[16]](#ref16)</sup> Follow swelling, skin integrity, infection episodes, function and garment fit. Adjust pressure and wearing time with the treating team; new pain, skin breakdown or impaired perfusion requires reassessment.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[3]](#ref3)[[6]](#ref6)</sup>

The frequently quoted lower-leg pressure around 40 mmHg and thigh pressure around 20 mmHg came from a retrospective study of 19 patients (21 legs), all previously treated with LVA and vascularized lymphatic transfer. Compression groups were small and not randomized. One patient developed a skin ulcer, and the authors explicitly could not establish whether pressure could be reduced after six months following liposuction alone. The 19-patient limb study cannot define safe pressure, genital compression or a six-month taper.<sup>[[6]](#ref6)</sup>

Combined surgery can sometimes reduce compression burden, but cessation should follow documented individual response. A reconstruction does not guarantee permanent freedom from garments.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[2]](#ref2)[[5]](#ref5)[[8]](#ref8)</sup>

## Interpreting Outcomes

**Excess volume** is the difference between affected and comparison limbs. If that excess is fully eliminated, its reduction is 100%; a value above 100% means the treated limb measured smaller than its comparator.<sup>[[3]](#ref3)</sup> Report the definition, comparator, follow-up denominator and compression protocol alongside percentages.<sup>[[3]](#ref3)[[4]](#ref4)</sup>

| Evidence | Finding | Limitation that changes interpretation |
|---|---|---|
| Brorson and Svensson 1998 | Matched pairs of liposuction plus controlled compression versus compression alone (n = 14 each) favored liposuction (p < 0.0001). Edema reduction at 1 year was 104% in the complete liposuction group (n = 30) versus 47% with compression alone | Small early prospective study of arm lymphedema<sup>[[16]](#ref16)</sup> |
| Hoffner 5-year registry | 105 women with non-pitting arm lymphedema after breast cancer treatment; mean reduction of 117% at 5 years | Arm only; 105/127 operated women had five-year data under continued controlled compression; single-center registry without a comparison group<sup>[[17]](#ref17)</sup> |
| Boyages ALAC cohort | 21 patients (15 arm, 6 leg) had at least 3 months' follow-up; six-month volume data were available for 12 arm and 5 leg patients. Mean volume difference was 44.2% before surgery and 3.6% at 6 months for arms, and 47.3% and 4.3% for legs. Eligibility required more than 25% volume difference | Short follow-up; small leg group<sup>[[18]](#ref18)</sup> |
| Lamprou CSAL series | 47 patients with primary and 41 with secondary leg lymphedema; volume difference reduced 79% and 101% at 2 years | Descriptive study; outcome depended on preoperative volume difference and sex<sup>[[19]](#ref19)</sup> |
| Granoff US implementation | 39 patients, 41 procedures; reported one-year excess-volume reductions of 116% in arms and 115% in legs | Retrospective, selected extremity cohort; no genital efficacy estimate or randomized comparison<sup>[[4]](#ref4)</sup> |
| Karlsson/ALERT five-year report | 59 included patients: 29 arms and 30 legs; five-year median residual excess volumes 22 mL and 669 mL | Only 12 arm and 6 leg patients had five-year measurements. Changing follow-up populations prevent a simple conclusion that all legs recur or all arms continue improving; five compression-related superficial wounds occurred<sup>[[3]](#ref3)</sup> |
| Stewart lower-limb series | 69 patients/72 legs; reported 90% mean excess-volume reduction at five years | Five-year result was available for 15 legs, compared with 72 at three months; nine years describes the operative experience<sup>[[13]](#ref13)</sup> |
| Karlsson Swedish lower-limb cohort | 67 unilateral legs analyzed; median excess-volume reduction 115% (IQR 98–124) at five years with controlled compression | Sixty-seven of 96 treated patients were analyzed; excludes bilateral disease and cannot predict genital results<sup>[[20]](#ref20)</sup> |
| Chen 2025 systematic review | 52 mostly observational studies of liposuction-based care, predominantly extremities | The detailed results report pooled reductions of 92.83% (upper limb), 91.08% (lower limb), 99.74% (standalone liposuction) and 87.31% (combined approaches); the text alternates between excess-volume and excess-circumference terminology, while the Figure 2–3 captions label the endpoint excess circumference. The abstract gives inconsistent limb figures and says 23 studies were analyzed quantitatively; the study-selection section reports 21, with 17 in the excess-volume analysis. Marked heterogeneity, inconsistent reported analytic totals and mixed volume/circumference terminology.<sup>[[5]](#ref5)</sup> |

The Chen review narratively summarized compression burden and quality of life.<sup>[[5]](#ref5)</sup>

Cellulitis may decrease after successful tissue reduction and continued care, but denominators matter. The often repeated 58% to 15% figure in Granzow's report describes all 26 patients receiving different operations; only ten underwent SAPL. It is not the standalone-SAPL treatment effect.<sup>[[9]](#ref9)</sup>

## Combining Liposuction With LVA or VLNT

The AAPS consensus supports a role for debulking of the non-fluid component and for selected combination treatment, while stating that timing remains unresolved. A sequence may prioritize the dominant component, available collectors, existing reconstruction and the feasibility of postoperative compression.<sup>[[2]](#ref2)</sup>

Brazio's 21-patient retrospective series proposed sequencing by presentation:<sup>[[8]](#ref8)</sup>

| Presentation | Proposed sequence |
|---|---|
| Predominantly non-pitting | Liposuction first, then LVA or VLNT to improve drainage |
| Predominantly pitting | Physiologic procedure first, with selective liposuction as a second stage |
| Mixed | Single-stage combination |

Across all four groups, including patients treated with LVA or VLNT without liposuction, mean excess-volume reduction ranged from 82% to 106% and was maintained for up to 2.4 years. Groups differed in stage and baseline excess volume. Across the cohort, self-reported daily garment time decreased from 12.5 to 7.5 hours (p = 0.003), but garment class was not reduced. Ten of 11 patients with prior cellulitis had no recurrence.<sup>[[8]](#ref8)</sup>

## Comparison With Open Excision

| Feature | Liposuction | Tailored open excision |
|---|---|---|
| Access | Small stab incisions | Excision extent and plane depend on the site and diseased tissue; limb deep-fascia excision is not a genital dissection rule<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[10]](#ref10)</sup> |
| Skin | Preserved | Diseased skin is excised; closure depends on the defect and available healthy skin, and was primary in Torio-Padron's 51-patient genital series<sup>[[10]](#ref10)</sup> |
| Lymphatics | Not improved | Superficial lymphatics are excised with the tissue |
| Compression | Ongoing compression needed | Varies |
| Typical indication | Non-pitting, adipose-predominant limb disease | Massive skin excess or advanced elephantiasis |

Liposuction cannot remove diseased skin.

## Role in Genital Reconstruction

Direct evidence for liposuction of genital lymphedema is limited. Advanced penile and scrotal disease often includes abnormal skin, lymphorrhea, dense fibrosis and redundant folds that require tailored excision and coverage. Adjacent suprapubic or thigh bulk and coexisting limb disease should be assessed separately from the genital skin envelope.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[11]](#ref11)</sup>

Several features limit liposuction in genital disease. Scrotal and penile skin is thin and mobile, with little subcutaneous fat for cannula work. Advanced disease often involves lymphorrhea, verrucous change and fibrosis beyond the fat layer. Excisional operations such as debulking scrotoplasty remove the entire diseased envelope. A supporting role may exist for adjacent mons pubis or proximal thigh bulk and for combination with physiologic surgery, but this is extrapolation from limb series. Genital compression can be difficult to fit and maintain. Determine whether a realistic maintenance plan exists before any operation whose result depends on compression.<sup>[[11]](#ref11)</sup>

Torio-Padron's 51-patient genital series used perioperative CDT and open excision with primary closure, not SAPL. In this excision cohort, three patients required revision (two hematomas and one wound dehiscence), five of 51 underwent surgery for scrotal recurrence, and Glasgow Benefit Inventory scores showed a median benefit among 24 of 51 questionnaire respondents (46% response); these are not SAPL outcomes.<sup>[[10]](#ref10)</sup> Limb liposuction series provide no comparison with selected genital excision for complications, recovery or cosmesis.<sup>[[10]](#ref10)</sup>
