# Defibulation (Deinfibulation) for Type III FGM/C

**Deinfibulation** is surgery to reopen the vaginal introitus in women and girls living with Type III female genital mutilation (FGM). It involves division of the midline scar tissue and suturing the cut edges to keep the introitus open. It may improve access for bodily functions, intercourse, examination, or childbirth, but it does not restore tissue removed by FGM.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup>

For clinical context, see [Female Genital Mutilation / Cutting (FGM/C)](/docs/clinical-conditions/03g-genital-scrotal/female-genital-mutilation-cutting). This is an evidence overview, not an operative protocol.

## Current guideline position

WHO recommends deinfibulation for women and girls with Type III FGM as a **conditional recommendation based on low-certainty evidence**.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup> The guideline notes that the direct outcome evidence was largely from pregnant women and that there is no paediatric evidence on benefits for non-pregnant girls. It emphasizes access to trained care, informed decision-making, and the individual context rather than a universal timing or anaesthesia rule.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup>

Reinfibulation should not be performed; WHO considers it a form of FGM medicalization.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup>

## Pregnancy and timing

For a pregnant woman with Type III FGM in whom deinfibulation is needed to facilitate vaginal birth, WHO states that **either antepartum or intrapartum deinfibulation can be considered**. This is a conditional recommendation based on very-low-certainty evidence; the evidence was conflicting and should not be presented as a fixed “best” trimester or labor-stage rule.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup>

The 2026 systematic review/meta-analysis is a current synthesis of the limited observational evidence, but its results should be interpreted with its study-level and certainty limitations rather than used as a patient-specific prediction.<sup>[[2]](#ref2)</sup>

## Counselling and care boundary

WHO advises offering counselling before deinfibulation. Partners or family members may be included **only at the woman’s request**, particularly where reinfibulation is commonly requested.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup> Counselling should give balanced information about expected anatomical change, possible benefits and risks, and available support. Qualitative research also documents that preferences, concerns about body image, and service access can shape the decision.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[3]](#ref3)</sup>

Defibulation should be planned by trained clinicians with trauma-informed, culturally safe care, appropriate interpretation when needed, and a referral pathway that reflects local legal and safeguarding requirements. This page does not prescribe examination methods, incision limits, suture material, anaesthesia, catheter management, pathology decisions, or postpartum care.

## Evidence limits

- The WHO recommendation is conditional and the underlying evidence is low or very low certainty.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup>
- Direct evidence for non-pregnant girls and women is limited; decisions should not be reduced to symptom checklists or a mandatory timetable.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup>
- The available timing studies are observational and do not establish that one approach is superior in every setting.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[2]](#ref2)</sup>
- Reported satisfaction and psychosocial findings are context-dependent and should not be represented as guaranteed outcomes.<sup>[[3]](#ref3)</sup>

## Key takeaways

1. Deinfibulation is a surgical option for Type III FGM; WHO recommends it conditionally on low-certainty evidence.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup>
2. For facilitating vaginal birth, antepartum and intrapartum timing can both be considered; individual preference, access, and clinical context matter.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup>
3. Counselling is central, and partner/family participation is only at the woman’s request.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup>
