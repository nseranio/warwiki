# Defibulation (Deinfibulation) for Type III FGM/C

**Defibulation** (deinfibulation) opens the infibulated scar in Type III FGM/C, exposing the urethral meatus and vaginal introitus. It can relieve obstruction, facilitate examination or intercourse and enable vaginal birth. It does not replace tissue removed by FGM/C. WHO's 2025 recommendation is conditional, based on low-certainty evidence, with informed decision-making and access to trained care.<sup>[[8]](#ref8)</sup>

For the disease context, see [Female Genital Mutilation / Cutting (FGM/C)](/docs/clinical-conditions/03g-genital-scrotal/female-genital-mutilation-cutting); for other reconstructive approaches, see the [Vulvar Reconstruction database](/docs/surgical-techniques/04e-genital-reconstruction/vulvar).

---

## Indications

WHO recommends offering deinfibulation to women and girls with Type III FGM, while noting limited direct evidence outside pregnant populations and no pediatric benefit studies. Symptoms and goals to explore during assessment include:<sup>[[8]](#ref8)</sup>

- **Menstrual obstruction.** Prolonged, painful, foul-smelling menstruation behind the neo-introitus; rarely hematocolpos or hematometra
- **Dysmenorrhea** from menstrual retention
- **Dyspareunia / apareunia** in sexually active patients
- **Recurrent UTI** and urinary obstruction
- **Scar pain or a suspected neuroma.** Assess separately; opening the infibulation alone may not address the cause
- **Pregnancy.** Defibulation is performed to facilitate vaginal delivery; observational data associate it with fewer emergency cesarean deliveries and genital-tract lacerations<sup>[[2]](#ref2)</sup>
- Gynecologic-exam access and pre-conception preparation

---

## Timing

| Context | Planning considerations |
| --- | --- |
| **Before pregnancy** | An elective discussion may address urinary, menstrual, sexual or examination concerns and the person's preference. |
| **Antepartum** | Allows counseling, healing and adjustment before birth. Access to trained clinicians and the woman's wishes are considered. |
| **Intrapartum** | An accepted alternative when opening is needed to facilitate vaginal birth; plan assessment, analgesia and availability of a trained clinician. |
| **After delivery or outside pregnancy** | Remaining needs and the person's wishes are reassessed; a missed earlier opportunity is not a contraindication. |

WHO states that either antepartum or intrapartum deinfibulation can be considered to facilitate vaginal birth; the timing recommendation is conditional on very-low-certainty evidence. No preferred trimester, 34-week cutoff or labor-stage rule has been established. In the UK qualitative FGM Sister study, survivors preferred deinfibulation before pregnancy and health-care professionals preferred antenatal deinfibulation, provided it was the survivor's choice; men had no consensus. Timing is agreed through informed discussion.<sup>[[4]](#ref4)[[8]](#ref8)</sup>

## Anesthesia and preparation

Local, regional and general anesthesia have been described. Choice depends on the extent of scarring, age, obstetric setting, anticipated discomfort, prior trauma, patient preference and available anesthesia expertise. The examination and procedure are discussed beforehand. Local anesthesia is an option for adults and is not required for every patient.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[3]](#ref3)</sup>

Offer an interpreter, chaperone and support person according to the patient's wishes. Ask about concerns around pain, memories of the original cutting, body image and the appearance after opening. For children or adolescents, involve pediatric, safeguarding and consent expertise.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[4]](#ref4)</sup>

---

## Operative description

The core maneuver is division of the midline scar followed by suturing the divided edges to keep the introitus open. The following sequence summarizes published operative descriptions; the extent of opening depends on the actual anatomy.<sup>[[3]](#ref3)[[8]](#ref8)</sup>

1. With suitable positioning, exposure and anesthesia, identify the inferior opening and assess the location of the urethral meatus and underlying structures.
2. **Midline scar division.** Open the fused scar progressively while protecting the urethra and any residual clitoral tissue; define these structures before extending the incision.
3. The cut edges are everted and oversewn with fine absorbable suture, creating two labial-like edges and a patent introitus.
4. Confirm the intended urethral and vaginal access, hemostasis and separation of the released edges.
5. Send any excised tissue for histopathology if clinically indicated.

Day-case care is often feasible outside pregnancy; discharge and follow-up depend on the procedure, anesthesia and clinical circumstances.

## Obstetric and Functional Outcomes

### Okusanya 2026 meta-analysis (8 studies, 3,166 women)

The review included eight observational studies with serious risk of bias and 3,166 participants overall. The endpoints below draw on much smaller subsets, with very-low-certainty evidence. The cesarean estimate is from one study rather than a pooled result. These are associations, not guaranteed individual benefits:<sup>[[2]](#ref2)</sup>

| Outcome | Effect (vs non-deinfibulated Type III) |
|---|---|
| Emergency cesarean delivery | **OR 0.16 (95% CI 0.06–0.42)**; one study, **n = 250** (45/230 vs 12/20), with an especially small non-deinfibulated comparison group |
| Genital-tract lacerations | **OR 0.48 (95% CI 0.29–0.79)**; three studies, **n = 1,067** |
| Antepartum vs intrapartum defibulation | Low-certainty evidence: antepartum defibulation may shorten labor, and was associated with higher postpartum hemorrhage (OR 1.51, 95% CI 1.03–2.22; two studies, n = 597) and cesarean birth (OR 1.66, 95% CI 1.22–2.25; three studies, n = 912). No single preferred time is established; WHO considers either acceptable |

Deinfibulation was not associated with significantly lower rates of episiotomy, prolonged second stage or neonatal resuscitation, and the review found no eligible studies of gynecologic, urologic or sexual-health outcomes. The nonobstetric indications above rest on anatomy, WHO guidance and separate clinical reports.<sup>[[2]](#ref2)[[8]](#ref8)</sup>

### Patient satisfaction

- Berg 2017 reviewed experiences with different FGM/C surgical interventions; overall satisfaction with defibulation ranged from 50% to 100% across studies. These mixed-procedure findings are not a deinfibulation-specific success rate.<sup>[[5]](#ref5)</sup>
- Nour's single-center Type III series included 40 operated women, 32 reached by telephone 6 months to 2 years later. Among respondents, 94% would highly recommend defibulation, and all patients and husbands were satisfied with the results. The series reported no intraoperative or postoperative complications. This respondent sample is not a generalizable success rate.<sup>[[9]](#ref9)</sup>
- A minority experience distress related to the new genital appearance, and medicalized defibulation has limited social acceptance in some communities.<sup>[[5]](#ref5)[[6]](#ref6)</sup>

## Complications

Discuss bleeding or hematoma, infection, pain, urinary difficulty, wound separation or re-adhesion, and the possible psychological response to anatomic change. Frequency varies by setting, and the available series are small:

- Hematoma, transient urinary retention, wound dehiscence.
- Psychological response to anatomic change, with multidisciplinary support recommended.

### Re-infibulation prohibition

A patient or family may request re-closure of the vulva after defibulation or after delivery. WHO considers reinfibulation a form of FGM medicalization and states that it should not be performed. Discuss this before the procedure and offer appropriate counseling and support.<sup>[[8]](#ref8)</sup>

## Counseling and Psychosocial Considerations

Defibulation decisions are rarely single-visit:<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[4]](#ref4)[[6]](#ref6)</sup>

- **Multiple visits.** Several visits are commonly required to address fears, particularly loss-of-virginity concerns in unmarried patients.
- **Cultural barriers.** Qualitative work in Somali and Sudanese migrant populations in Norway identifies perceived loss of male virility and sexual pleasure as a deterrent to medicalized defibulation. Such concerns can be discussed, but partners or family participate only at the woman's request, and their agreement is not a prerequisite.<sup>[[6]](#ref6)[[8]](#ref8)</sup>
- **Mental-health integration.** PTSD, depression and somatization are common in this population, and mental-health service provision remains deficient.<sup>[[4]](#ref4)</sup>
- **Adolescent autonomy.** Legal and ethical complexity arises when an adolescent seeks defibulation but fears parental refusal or stigma; local statutes and best-interest standards apply.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup>
- **Trauma-informed care.** Care includes a survivor-chosen support person, respect for provider-gender preference and a language-concordant interpreter where needed.

## Positioning vs Reconstructive Procedures

Defibulation opens the infibulation but does not restore tissue lost in Types I / II / III. Additional reconstruction is a separate, preference-sensitive discussion. The approaches below have differing and often limited evidence and are not routine additions to deinfibulation.<sup>[[7]](#ref7)</sup>

| Goal | Procedure | Cross-link |
|---|---|---|
| Open Type III introital fusion | **Defibulation** (this page) | — |
| Restore the glans clitoridis from scar | Foldès reconstruction | [Foldès](/docs/surgical-techniques/04e-genital-reconstruction/foldes-clitoral-reconstruction) |
| Restore labia minora / vestibule | aOAP flap ± OD preputial flap | [aOAP](/docs/surgical-techniques/04e-genital-reconstruction/aoap-flap) |
| Alternative non-Foldès clitoral coverage | Mañero vaginal-mucosal graft | [Mañero](/docs/surgical-techniques/04e-genital-reconstruction/manero-vaginal-graft) |
