# Skin Grafts in Vulvar Reconstruction (STSG / FTSG)

Skin grafts serve a specific but limited niche in the vulvar reconstructive ladder — primarily for **superficial defects after skinning vulvectomy for VIN or vulvar Paget disease**, and for vaginal reconstruction after exenteration. Although among the earliest reconstructive techniques used in vulvar surgery, grafts have been largely supplanted by flap-based reconstruction for most deep or complex vulvar defects.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup>

For the broader treatment menu see the [Vulvar Reconstruction Atlas](/docs/surgical-techniques/04e-genital-reconstruction/vulvar). For tension-free direct repair see [Primary Closure of the Vulva](/docs/surgical-techniques/04e-genital-reconstruction/vulvar-primary-closure). For graft fundamentals see [STSG](/docs/foundations/surgical-principles/grafts/stsg) and [FTSG](/docs/foundations/surgical-principles/grafts/ftsg).

---

## Historical Context

Skin grafts were among the first reconstructive techniques applied to vulvar defects in the 1950s and 1960s, used for vulvar resurfacing and neovaginal creation after vaginectomy or pelvic exenteration. The development of musculocutaneous flaps in the late 1970s and fasciocutaneous / perforator flaps in subsequent decades progressively displaced grafts for most deep vulvar defects — flaps provide their own blood supply, better tissue bulk, and superior functional outcomes.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup>

## Graft Types

| Feature | **STSG** | **FTSG** |
|---|---|---|
| Composition | Epidermis + partial dermis (0.008–0.012 in) | Epidermis + full dermis |
| Described donor options | Scalp, thigh or buttock; choose for available skin, wound size and donor-site priorities<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup> | Groin crease can offer a concealed scar and color match when enough skin is available<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup> |
| Surface area | Large | Limited |
| Donor healing | Re-epithelialization | Primary closure |
| Contracture | Higher | Lower |
| Durability | Depends on thickness, bed and aftercare | May resist secondary contraction better, but requires a suitable recipient bed |
| Cosmesis | Depends on donor match, contour and contracture | May improve color/texture match in selected small defects |
| Typical role in vulva | Most commonly used graft type<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[2]](#ref2)</sup> | Small defects when elasticity / color match is preferred |

## Primary Indications

### 1. Skinning vulvectomy for VIN or vulvar Paget disease

The classic and most well-established indication for grafting in vulvar reconstruction. Skinning vulvectomy removes only the vulvar skin while preserving the underlying subcutaneous tissue, creating a superficial wound bed ideal for graft take.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[3]](#ref3)</sup>

Two patient populations are selected:<sup>[[3]](#ref3)</sup>

- Multicentric VIN with colposcopic evidence of normal intervening skin.
- Localized but wide-surface-area VIN where primary closure would yield poor cosmetic results.

In a series of 24 skinning vulvectomies with skin graft, all patients achieved very good cosmetic and functional results over 6 months – 11 years.<sup>[[3]](#ref3)</sup> A study of 48 patients with multifocal VIN treated with skinning vulvectomy and STSG found recurrence in 13 (27%); recurrence risk was not related to margin status.<sup>[[4]](#ref4)</sup>

ACOG notes that skinning vulvectomy is rarely needed but may be useful for confluent multifocal lesions, particularly in immunocompromised patients.<sup>[[5]](#ref5)</sup>

### 2. QOL and sexual-function outcomes

Lavoué et al. (n = 13, median follow-up 77 mo, skinning vulvectomy + STSG for VIN or Paget):<sup>[[6]](#ref6)</sup>

- No significant difference in SF-36 QOL scores vs the general population.
- Among patients assessed with the FSFI, the authors reported normal-range sexual function; this small retrospective series does not establish recovery for every operated patient.
- **Occult invasive cancer diagnosed in 31%** — a key advantage of excisional over ablative approaches.
- Clear excision margins in only 46%; mean disease-free survival 58 mo.

### 3. Vaginal reconstruction

Skin grafts (or buccal grafts) covering a tubularized omentum-majus flap can be used for **neovaginal creation** after exenteration. STSGs can also line a vaginal mold for vaginal reconstruction.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup>

## Challenges in the Vulvar / Perineal Region

The perineum poses unique challenges for graft survival:<sup>[[7]](#ref7)[[8]](#ref8)[[9]](#ref9)</sup>

| Challenge | Detail |
|---|---|
| Moist, contaminated environment | Proximity to urine and feces raises infection risk |
| Irregular contour | Vulvar anatomy makes uniform graft-to-bed contact difficult |
| High mobility | Thigh / perineal motion creates shear forces that displace grafts |
| Infection / sloughing | Dainty et al. cite up to **22%** with conventional methods, particularly at irradiated sites; this is background context, not a measured failure rate in their seven-case adjunct series<sup>[[7]](#ref7)</sup> |
| Poor graft bed | Fatty subcutaneous tissue is a suboptimal vascular bed — why deep defects need flaps<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[9]](#ref9)</sup> |

## Techniques to Improve Graft Take

### Negative-pressure wound therapy (NPWT)

NPWT has emerged as a significant adjunct:

| Study | Cohort | Finding |
|---|---|---|
| Lee et al. (mixed-sex perineal defects)<sup>[[8]](#ref8)</sup> | 26 patients; retrospective, surgeon-selected dressing | Higher graft take (p = 0.036) and shorter healing time (p = 0.01) than tie-over dressings; not a vulvar-only randomized comparison |
| Cao et al. RCT (mixed anatomic sites)<sup>[[10]](#ref10)</sup> | 86 patients | Overall graft take 97.2% vs 90.2% (p = 0.005); irregular/high-mobility subgroup 97.6% vs 81.7% (p &lt; 0.001); not vulvar-specific |
| Lee et al. meta-analysis (mixed sites)<sup>[[11]](#ref11)</sup> | 16 RCTs | Reported **+8.3% overall graft take**, OR 1.86 for success, OR 0.44 for graft loss and OR 0.31 for reoperation; source-level methods/subgroups still require review |

Mechanisms: prevention of subgraft hematoma / seroma, reduction of shear forces, stimulation of angiogenesis, and improved microcirculatory flow.<sup>[[12]](#ref12)</sup>

### Fibrin tissue adhesives

Fibrin glue can be used alone or in combination with NPWT to augment graft adherence and viability during vulvovaginal reconstruction, particularly at irradiated sites.<sup>[[7]](#ref7)</sup>

## Skin Grafts vs Flaps — Decision-Making

| Feature | Skin grafts | Flaps |
|---|---|---|
| Best indication | Superficial defects (skinning vulvectomy) | Deep / complex defects (radical vulvectomy) |
| Blood supply | Dependent on recipient bed | Intrinsic vascular pedicle |
| Tissue bulk | None (skin only) | Volume to fill dead space |
| Contracture risk | Higher (especially STSG) | Lower |
| Donor-site morbidity | Varies by harvest size and site | Varies substantially by flap and donor site |
| Viability in perineum | Depends strongly on vascular bed and fixation | Depends on pedicle integrity, design and recipient context |
| Irradiated beds | Take can be impaired; an adequate vascular bed is essential | Vascularized tissue may be useful, but no design is universally superior |
| Operative complexity | Lower | Higher |

<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[2]](#ref2)[[3]](#ref3)</sup>

For a deep defect or exposed poorly vascularized tissue, a flap may supply the needed blood flow and bulk; skin grafts remain useful for selected superficial resurfacing and as adjuncts. The Toulouse paper proposes a location-based perforator-flap approach, but it is not a universal guideline that rules out grafting.<sup>[[13]](#ref13)[[14]](#ref14)</sup> Reported advancement- and transposition-flap complication rates (26.7% and 22.3%) and satisfaction ranges come from heterogeneous series, not a direct graft-versus-flap trial.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[13]](#ref13)</sup>

## Postoperative Complications of Genital Skin Grafts

Long-term complications can include pain, contractures, hypertrophic scarring, hypersensitivity and recurrent wounds, with effects on walking, sitting, elimination and intimacy. Individualized perineal or pelvic-floor rehabilitation may address these problems, but the cited report describes **two male patients after necrotizing infection**, not measured efficacy in women after vulvar grafting.<sup>[[15]](#ref15)</sup>

---

## Key Takeaways

1. Skin grafts are best suited for superficial defects after skinning vulvectomy for VIN or Paget disease, and for vaginal reconstruction.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[3]](#ref3)</sup>
2. Scalp, thigh and buttock STSGs and selected groin-crease FTSGs are described; donor selection depends on the defect and patient.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup>
3. Consider **NPWT fixation** for difficult perineal contours or mobile beds; supportive evidence includes a small nonrandomized perineal series and mixed-site trials, not a universal vulvar standard.<sup>[[8]](#ref8)[[10]](#ref10)[[11]](#ref11)</sup>
4. For deep, radical, or irradiated vulvar defects, flap reconstruction is preferred.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[13]](#ref13)[[14]](#ref14)</sup>
5. Skinning vulvectomy with STSG permits histologic assessment; in one **13-person selected series**, occult invasion was found in 31%, with favorable reported QOL and FSFI results among respondents.<sup>[[6]](#ref6)</sup>
