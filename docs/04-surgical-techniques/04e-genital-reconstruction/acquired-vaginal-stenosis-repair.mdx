# Acquired Vaginal Stenosis and Constriction Repair

**Acquired vaginal stenosis** is narrowing or shortening of the vaginal canal that develops after pelvic radiotherapy, prolapse surgery, vaginectomy, fistula repair, obstetric laceration repair or other vaginal injury, as distinct from a congenital anomaly or a neovaginal stricture after gender-affirming vaginoplasty.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[2]](#ref2)</sup> Management ranges from dilation and scar release to flap or graft reconstruction, selected by the location, extent and cause of the narrowing. For lichen sclerosus-related introital scarring see [Perineoplasty / De-Adhesion](/docs/surgical-techniques/04e-genital-reconstruction/perineoplasty-de-adhesion); for stenosis after gender-affirming vaginoplasty see [Neovaginal Stenosis Management](/docs/surgical-techniques/04k-gender-affirming-surgery/neovaginal-stenosis-management).

---

## Etiology

| Cause | Mechanism |
|---|---|
| **Pelvic radiotherapy** | Fibrosis and reduced vascularity of the vaginal wall after radiotherapy for cervical, vaginal, vulvar, rectal or anal cancer<sup>[[3]](#ref3)[[4]](#ref4)</sup> |
| **Prolapse or incontinence surgery** | Vaginal mesh contracture, mesh exposure with scarring, or an overcorrected colporrhaphy that leaves inadequate vaginal caliber<sup>[[5]](#ref5)</sup> |
| **Vaginectomy or extensive vaginal excision** | Loss of vaginal wall for oncologic or benign disease, leaving a short or narrow canal |
| **Fistula repair** | Scarring at a prior vesicovaginal, urethrovaginal or rectovaginal fistula closure site |
| **Obstetric laceration or episiotomy repair** | An overaggressive or malpositioned perineal repair that constricts the introitus<sup>[[2]](#ref2)</sup> |
| **Caustic or traditional-medicine injury** | Reported outside North America and Europe after intravaginal use of caustic agents; a different population from the causes above<sup>[[6]](#ref6)</sup> |

Lichen sclerosus and gender-affirming neovaginal stenosis are managed on their own dedicated pages and are not covered further here.

---

## Evaluation and Classification

Examination documents the level, length and circumference of the narrowed segment, whether the stenosis is a discrete ring, a longitudinal band or circumferential, and whether it is reachable in the office or requires examination under anesthesia. Relevant history includes radiation dose and field, prior vaginal mesh or graft, the indication and technique of any prior vaginal surgery, and the patient's goals for penetrative intercourse or future reconstruction.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[5]](#ref5)</sup> For a cervical-cancer survivor with symptoms or findings suggestive of persistence, recurrence or a second primary, arrange oncologic assessment before elective scar reconstruction; the 2023 ESGO/ESTRO/ESP guideline strongly recommends histologic verification of suspected disease [V, A].<sup>[[10]](#ref10)</sup>

Gebhart and Karram describe a focal ring or band amenable to release and transverse closure; broader or circumferential scarring can be treated with multiple relaxing incisions and transverse closures, while extensive or near-obliterative defects may require flap or graft reconstruction of the canal wall.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup>

## Management

### Dilation

After the acute inflammatory phase of radiotherapy, dilation is commonly offered to maintain vaginal patency and may stretch an already narrowed canal. In a prospective series of women with postoperative vaginal constriction, all participants were offered manual dilation before surgery; the 20 women who underwent repair had failed that trial.<sup>[[9]](#ref9)</sup> Evidence that dilator use itself prevents stenosis or reverses established fibrosis remains insufficient.<sup>[[3]](#ref3)[[4]](#ref4)[[7]](#ref7)[[8]](#ref8)</sup> In an observational EMBRACE-I analysis of 882 women treated with chemoradiation and brachytherapy for locally advanced cervical cancer (median follow-up 60 months), regular dilator use and/or sexual activity (565 women) was associated with lower five-year actuarial grade ≥2 vaginal stenosis than no or infrequent use (317 women; 23% versus 37%), but higher grade ≥1 bleeding (61% versus 34%); the combined exposure does not isolate dilator use.<sup>[[11]](#ref11)</sup> A Cochrane review found no randomized trials of dilation and could draw no conclusion about whether routine dilation during or immediately after radiotherapy prevents stenosis; observational series are mixed, with some suggesting lower self-reported stenosis in women who dilate and others showing no difference.<sup>[[4]](#ref4)</sup> An earlier systematic review warned that dilation during the acute post-radiotherapy inflammatory phase may add tissue injury, while later dilation may stretch an already-stenosed vagina; a small case series described length restoration years after radiotherapy.<sup>[[3]](#ref3)</sup> The 2014 Cochrane review also cites a report of two rectovaginal fistulas associated with rigid dilation of irradiated vaginal cuffs.<sup>[[4]](#ref4)</sup> The 2014 Cochrane review found no eligible trial for its analysis, but later randomized trials tested post-radiotherapy dilation. Among 88 women after gynaecological brachytherapy, there was no intention-to-treat difference in vaginal dimensions at three months, with substantial follow-up loss.<sup>[[7]](#ref7)</sup> Among 142 women who completed 12 months of a four-arm cervical-cancer trial (195 began an intervention), vaginal-volume loss did not differ significantly among arms; CTCAE stenosis change within 27 dilator completers was not significant (p = 0.372) and does not establish a between-arm benefit.<sup>[[8]](#ref8)</sup> Neither trial tested reversal of established fibrosis.

For mesh-related constriction, assess the exposed or palpable mesh segment and associated pelvic-floor pain. Pelvic floor physical therapy is a nonoperative option; a constricting band or symptomatic exposure may require release or segmental mesh excision.<sup>[[5]](#ref5)</sup> Karram and Zoorob describe that a focal constricting band from mesh or scar can be released in the office or operating room in a manner analogous to the surgical releases below, and that an extensively contracted vagina from mesh may require formal reconstruction with local flaps or grafts once the mesh issue itself is addressed.<sup>[[5]](#ref5)</sup>

### Surgical release for a focal ring or band

For a discrete constricting ring or band, a described release completely divides the constriction, mobilizes adjacent vaginal mucosa and excises excess scar as needed. The longitudinal incision may be closed transversely without tension, applying the [Heineke-Mikulicz](/docs/foundations/surgical-principles/flaps/heineke-mikulicz) principle to widen the lumen, or left to heal by secondary intention; multiple relaxing incisions and a Z-plasty for a localized band have also been described.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup> Gebhart and Karram's chapter describes Z-plasty limbs of approximately 2 cm at a 60-degree angle for this purpose; this is the chapter's own operative dimension and has not been validated as a threshold.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup>

A post-episiotomy or post-laceration introital scar causing dyspareunia has been managed, as described by Karram, by dividing the midline constriction, sharply mobilizing the posterior vaginal wall off the anterior rectal wall, advancing the vaginal edges, and closing transversely with delayed-absorbable suture; in the author's illustrated cases a porcine small-intestinal-submucosa (Surgisis) graft covered the residual perineal-skin defect. These are individual operative examples without a series of measured outcomes.<sup>[[2]](#ref2)</sup>

### Flap reconstruction for lateral or circumferential scar

For stenosis extending laterally or circumferentially, hinged local perineal flaps have been described: a flap is raised with a broad vascular base and some underlying fat, its distal tip rounded, and it is inset into the released defect without tension; bilateral flaps are used for more extensive or near-obliterative stenosis.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup> This is the same reconstructive principle used for larger vulvovaginal defects with the [Singapore / pudendal thigh flap](/docs/foundations/surgical-principles/flaps/singapore-pudendal-thigh) and [lotus petal flap](/docs/foundations/surgical-principles/flaps/lotus-petal), which can be considered when local tissue at the stenosis itself is insufficient.

### Graft reconstruction

When closure would otherwise narrow or shorten the vagina, relaxing incisions through the stenosis followed by inset of a [full-thickness skin graft](/docs/foundations/surgical-principles/grafts/ftsg) over the raw surface, with fat removed from the graft, fine absorbable fixation sutures and vaginal packing for at least 24 hours, has been described; because a free vaginal graft remains susceptible to contraction, a vaginal mold or a dilation regimen is typically continued afterward to maintain depth and caliber.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup> See the [FTSG page](/docs/foundations/surgical-principles/grafts/ftsg) for graft-biology detail and inset technique. A four-layer porcine small-intestinal-submucosa matrix placed under undermined vaginal epithelium has also been described as an alternative to primary closure when closure would narrow the introitus, without a reported denominator or measured outcome for that specific use.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup>

### Salvage reconstruction for extensive or multiply-operated stenosis

Severely scarred, irradiated or multiply-operated stenosis that has exhausted local tissue options may require regional or free flap neovaginal reconstruction. One described example is a multiply operated, irradiated patient with a stenosed sigmoid neovagina managed by excision of the stenosed segment and construction of a helical [VRAM](/docs/foundations/surgical-principles/flaps/vram) neovagina, preserving the inferior epigastric blood supply.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup> A single salvage case establishes no general indication, outcome estimate or preferred technique for this setting; active underlying disease, such as uncontrolled Behçet disease in another case, should be controlled before repeat reconstruction.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup>

### Summary by extent of scar

| Pattern | Reported options |
|---|---|
| **Focal ring or band** | Direct release with transverse (Heineke-Mikulicz) closure; multiple relaxing incisions; Z-plasty |
| **Post-episiotomy / post-laceration introital scar** | Midline release, posterior vaginal wall mobilization, transverse advancement closure |
| **Lateral or circumferential scar with local tissue available** | Hinged local perineal flap(s), uni- or bilateral |
| **Closure would narrow or shorten the canal** | Relaxing incisions with full-thickness skin graft inset and postoperative dilation/mold |
| **Extensive, multiply-operated or irradiated stenosis** | Individualized regional or free flap neovaginal reconstruction |

No comparative trial establishes superiority among these options; selection follows the extent and location of scar, the tissue available and the cause of the original injury.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[2]](#ref2)</sup>

## Postoperative Care

Postoperative management follows the technique used: Gebhart and Karram describe caliber maintenance after focal ring release and Z-plasty as well as graft or flap reconstruction to resist contraction, using dilation or molding according to the repair. After graft inset, vaginal packing in the first 24 to 48 hours is followed by continued molding or dilation; the shared positioning, drain and flap-monitoring principles for pelvic and perineal flaps are described in [Pelvic and Perineal Flap Postoperative Care](/docs/foundations/surgical-principles/flaps/pelvic-perineal-flap-postoperative-care).<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup>

## Complications and Outcomes

Reported complications include recurrent stenosis, graft or flap loss and dyspareunia. A prospective single-center series of 20 women with postoperative vaginal constriction, all after failed manual dilation, reported pain-free intercourse in 15/20 and objective cure on examination in 17/20 at mean 17 months after Z-plasty, ring incision, vaginal advancement or a free skin graft.<sup>[[9]](#ref9)</sup> The study did not compare techniques and does not supply an outcome estimate for radiation-, vaginectomy- or fistula-related stenosis; those settings remain represented here by technique descriptions and individual cases.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[2]](#ref2)</sup> A retrospective series of severe, longstanding vaginal stenosis after caustic injury from traditional intravaginal agents reported surgery followed by dilation in 20 of 21 women; successful outcome allowing intercourse was reported in 47.6% of the 21 cases and failure in 52.4%. The caustic-injury population does not estimate outcomes for radiation-, surgery- or fistula-related stenosis.<sup>[[6]](#ref6)</sup>
