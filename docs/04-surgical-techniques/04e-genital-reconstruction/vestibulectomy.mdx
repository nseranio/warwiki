# Vestibulectomy for Localized Provoked Vestibulodynia

**Vestibulectomy** excises painful vestibular mucosa in selected patients with localized provoked vestibulodynia (PVD) refractory to conservative and medical management. Excision may be posterior or total, with or without vaginal-mucosal advancement.<sup>[[7]](#ref7)</sup> It is reserved for carefully selected patients because it is an irreversible procedure directed at a chronic pain condition rather than a structural lesion.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[2]](#ref2)</sup> For diagnosis, including the cotton-swab clock-face test that localizes vestibular pain, and for first-line and medical management of vestibulodynia, see [Chronic Pelvic Pain](/docs/clinical-conditions/03h-pelvic-pain/chronic-pelvic-pain#vestibulodynia--cotton-swab-test-and-clock-face-mapping).

---

## Terminology and Patient Selection

The 2015 ISSVD, ISSWSH and IPPS consensus classifies vulvodynia by location (localized, generalized or mixed), by whether pain is provoked, spontaneous or mixed, and by onset and temporal pattern. **Localized provoked vestibulodynia** (previously called vulvar vestibulitis syndrome) denotes provoked pain confined to the vestibule, and is the phenotype vestibulectomy addresses; it is not the appropriate terminology or target for generalized or spontaneous (unprovoked) vulvar pain.<sup>[[2]](#ref2)</sup>

For localized provoked vestibulodynia, evaluate whether a specific disorder explains the vestibular pain before considering vestibulectomy when symptoms remain refractory to appropriate nonsurgical care.<sup>[[2]](#ref2)</sup> Depending on examination findings, care may include patient education, pelvic-floor physical therapy, topical or oral medication, and psychosexual support.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[2]](#ref2)[[3]](#ref3)</sup> The 2015 consensus classifies vulvodynia by location and provocation; cotton-swab mapping localizes tenderness but does not by itself establish a neuroproliferative, hormonal or muscular pain mechanism.<sup>[[2]](#ref2)[[4]](#ref4)</sup> In a 113-woman insertional-dyspareunia cohort, all had posterior tenderness and 41 also had anterior tenderness; the posterior-only group included both constricting hymenal rings and pelvic-floor hypertonicity. Examine the hymenal ring and pelvic floor separately before selecting treatment.<sup>[[4]](#ref4)</sup>

## Surgical Technique

The sequence below is a chapter-described technique attributed to contributing author Irwin Goldstein and used by his group; it is one described approach, and other vestibulectomy and perineoplasty techniques are reported.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup> A narrative review describes marking the planned excision and catheterizing the urethra as technical options; in its total vestibulectomy with advancement description, it specifies sufficient vaginal-mucosal mobilization for tension-free closure and omits anterior deep mattress sutures to avoid urethral injury.<sup>[[6]](#ref6)</sup>

1. **Excision.** The vestibular mucosa is completely excised from the periurethral/Skene-gland region anteriorly, laterally, and posteriorly to the hymen.
2. **Posterior vaginal advancement.** The posterior vaginal wall is mobilized and advanced to resurface the resulting defect.
3. **Fixation of the advancement flap.** Interrupted 3-0 absorbable braided (Vicryl) horizontal mattress sutures secure the advancement flap in an anterior-to-posterior direction.
4. **Perineal approximation.** The remaining mucosal flap is approximated to the perineum with interrupted 4-0 absorbable braided (Vicryl) suture.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup>

A related operation, **perineoplasty**, can excise scarred tissue and perineal skin beyond the vestibule and has also been used for provoked vestibular pain.<sup>[[3]](#ref3)[[8]](#ref8)</sup> Published reports sometimes use 'modified perineoplasty' and 'vestibulectomy' with overlapping meanings.<sup>[[8]](#ref8)</sup>

## Complications

Reported complications include bleeding, infection, pain, hematoma, wound-healing problems, vaginal or introital stenosis, scarring and Bartholin-duct cyst or obstruction.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup> At six weeks, Goldstein's group repeats cotton-swab testing and assesses clinically suspected Bartholin cysts with vulvoscopy and vaginal ultrasonography.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup> A 2024 systematic review of surgery for provoked vulvodynia (29 studies) reported Bartholin cysts in up to 9% of cases as its most commonly reported complication.<sup>[[3]](#ref3)</sup> In a separate 2024 single-surgeon retrospective vestibulectomy series, limited wound dehiscence occurred in 18/80 patients with complete data; median follow-up was 21 months.<sup>[[5]](#ref5)</sup>

---

## Outcomes

A randomized comparison assigned 87 women with vulvar vestibulitis to vestibulectomy, group cognitive-behavioral therapy, or surface-EMG biofeedback; 78 received treatment. At six months, analyses of the treated group (with missing follow-up values imputed) found lower cotton-swab vestibular pain scores after vestibulectomy than after either behavioral treatment, but seven women assigned to surgery had declined it.<sup>[[9]](#ref9)</sup> At 2.5 years, 51 of those 78 women were reassessed; sexual-function measures did not differ among groups.<sup>[[10]](#ref10)</sup> That same 2024 systematic review found that surgery, most often vestibulectomy or perineoplasty, is typically offered only after conservative treatment has failed. Across the 15 studies that defined success as a significant reduction in dyspareunia, reported success rates ranged from 52% to 93%; six studies using validated pain scales found a significant reduction in vulvar pain after surgery (p &lt; .001); two studies reported improved sexual function in 57% to 87% of patients; and three studies found that 89% to 97% of women regained the ability to have intercourse.<sup>[[3]](#ref3)</sup> Patient satisfaction ranged from 79% to 93%.<sup>[[3]](#ref3)</sup> The review's authors concluded that surgery appears to be an effective and reasonably safe option for refractory provoked vulvodynia, while noting that randomized trials with standardized outcome measures are still needed to compare approaches and minimize operative risk.<sup>[[3]](#ref3)</sup>
