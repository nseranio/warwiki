# Microsurgical Penile / Glans Replantation

Penile amputation is an uncommon surgical emergency. The current American College of Surgeons (ACS) guideline recommends timely reconstruction with, at a minimum, corporal and urethral anastomosis; for optimal results it recommends urgent microsurgical replantation by urologic and microvascular specialists, with urgent transfer when those resources are not available.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup>

This page is an emergency-management overview, not an operative manual. Exact repair, monitoring, adjunctive treatment, and rehabilitation must be directed by the receiving trauma, urology, and microvascular teams.

---

## Immediate Priorities

- Stabilize the patient and address associated injuries.
- Preserve the amputated segment using the ACS two-bag method: wrap it in saline-soaked gauze in one bag, then place that bag in a second bag on ice.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup>
- Arrange urgent transfer to a center with urologic and microvascular expertise when necessary.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup>
- If microsurgery or transfer is unavailable, the ACS guideline describes macroscopic repair of the corpora, urethra, and skin by an experienced surgeon as a fallback; the urethra is repaired over a catheter.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup>
- Obtain mental-health evaluation when self-inflicted injury is suspected or confirmed, given its association with psychiatric comorbidity and psychosis.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup>

## Reconstruction Boundary

Microsurgical neurovascular repair can help preserve penile skin and improve functional results, but published outcome evidence is largely case reports and small series. No recovered source establishes universal ischemia thresholds, a minimum set of vessel or nerve repairs, a standard suture plan, a routine use of leeches or hyperbaric oxygen, or a predictable recovery of sensation, erections, voiding, or appearance.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[2]](#ref2)</sup>

Isolated glans injury, complete penile amputation, pediatric injury, self-inflicted injury, and injuries with contamination or delayed presentation each require separate specialist assessment. Case-series findings should not be generalized across those settings.

## Follow-up

Follow-up should assess tissue viability, wound healing, urethral complications, urinary and sexual function, sensation, appearance, psychological recovery, and need for secondary reconstruction. The schedule and services required are determined by injury mechanism, repair, complications, and the responsible clinical team.
