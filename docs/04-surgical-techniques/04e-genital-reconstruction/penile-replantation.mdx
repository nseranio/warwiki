# Microsurgical Penile / Glans Replantation

**Urgent microsurgical replantation** is the preferred reconstructive approach for traumatic penile amputation when the amputated segment and specialist resources are available. It offers the possibility of restoring appearance, voiding, erectile function, and sensation, but recovery and complications vary. A 2022 case report estimated that approximately 200 cases had been reported in the Chinese- and English-language literature, mostly as case reports or small series.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[2]](#ref2)[[3]](#ref3)[[4]](#ref4)</sup>

For acute trauma workup, transfer logistics, and the wider injury context see [Genitoscrotal Trauma](/docs/special-populations/05a-trauma-emergencies/genital-scrotal-trauma), [GU Injury Overview](/docs/special-populations/05a-trauma-emergencies/gu-injury-overview), and [Trauma Assessment](/docs/special-populations/05a-trauma-emergencies/trauma-assessment). For broader penile-reconstruction options, see [Penile Reconstruction](/docs/surgical-techniques/04e-genital-reconstruction/penile). For salvage approaches when the amputated segment is non-viable, see [Total Anterior Scrotal Flap (Zhao)](/docs/surgical-techniques/04e-genital-reconstruction/total-anterior-scrotal-flap) and phalloplasty options under [GAS — Masculinizing Surgery](/docs/surgical-techniques/04k-gender-affirming-surgery/masculinizing-surgery).

## Etiology

| Mechanism | Notes |
|---|---|
| **Self-inflicted injury** | Prominent in the published adult case literature and often associated with acute psychiatric illness; 2 of the 4 patients in the small Sanger series had a history of recurrent self-mutilation, which is not an estimated population risk<sup>[[5]](#ref5)[[3]](#ref3)</sup> |
| **Felonious assault** | Partner-inflicted ("Bobbitt" scenario) |
| **Accidental / iatrogenic** | Circumcision complications (Mogen, Gomco, disposable staplers), industrial accidents, mechanical injury<sup>[[6]](#ref6)[[7]](#ref7)[[8]](#ref8)</sup> |
| **Neonatal / pediatric** | Circumcision-related glans amputation is the dominant mechanism<sup>[[6]](#ref6)[[7]](#ref7)[[8]](#ref8)[[9]](#ref9)</sup> |

## Guideline Recommendations (ACS 2025 and EAU 2026)

The American College of Surgeons Best Practices Guidelines for Management of GU Injuries (2025):<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup>

- Perform timely penile reconstruction including, at a minimum, anastomosis of the corporal bodies and the urethra
- For optimal results, penile amputation requires urgent microsurgical replantation by a urologist and a microvascular surgeon
- Transfer the patient urgently to a specialty facility if those specialists are not available
- Transport the amputated penis in a two-bag system, wrapped in saline-soaked gauze in one bag and placed in a second bag on ice
- If microsurgery is unavailable and transfer is not possible, perform macroscopic anastomosis of corpora, urethra, and skin with small absorbable sutures
- Obtain a mental-health evaluation because of the strong association with psychiatric comorbidity, psychosis, and self-amputation

AUA advises prompt replantation and two-bag transport with saline-soaked gauze on ice (Statement 32; Clinical Principle). It advises psychological, interpersonal or reproductive counseling when loss of sexual, urinary or reproductive function is anticipated (Statement 33; Expert Opinion).<sup>[[29]](#ref29)</sup>

The EAU Urological Trauma guideline (2026) agrees on the main points and adds detail:<sup>[[28]](#ref28)</sup>

- Consider replantation in every patient and perform it within 24 hours of amputation. Case reports do not define a fixed viability cutoff, so treat 24 hours as a target rather than a reason to discard a salvageable part.
- Wash the part with sterile saline, wrap it in saline-soaked gauze, place it in a sterile bag and immerse the bag in iced water, without direct contact with ice.
- Control stump bleeding with a pressure dressing or a tourniquet around the stump.
- A microsurgical repair is preferred: corpora cavernosa, urethra, dorsal arteries, dorsal vein and dorsal nerves. The cavernosal arteries are generally too small to anastomose. Close fascia and skin in layers, and place both a urethral and a suprapubic catheter. A non-microsurgical repair is possible.
- If the part is lost or unsuitable, close the stump as in partial penectomy; later options include suspensory-ligament division with V-Y plasty, pseudo-glans formation with split-thickness grafting, and phalloplasty for a very short or non-functioning stump.

## Penile Vascular Anatomy

| Artery | Mean diameter at base | Perfusion territory | Relevance |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Dorsal artery** | 1.43 mm | Glans, corpus spongiosum, distal skin (perforators) | Primary microvascular target |
| **Cavernosal artery** | 0.80 mm | Corpora cavernosa (helicine → lacunar) | Important for erection; technically challenging |
| **Inferior external pudendal artery (IEPA)** | 0.94 mm | Penile shaft skin (main supply) | Additional option to support shaft-skin perfusion; does not guarantee skin survival |
| **Urethral / bulbar artery** | Variable | Corpus spongiosum, urethra | Usually not individually anastomosed |

Anatomical findings:<sup>[[10]](#ref10)[[11]](#ref11)[[12]](#ref12)[[13]](#ref13)</sup>

- **Dorsal arteries** principally perfuse the glans and spongiosum; perforating branches also supply the distal third of the corpora cavernosa
- **IEPA** is the main supply to penile shaft skin with midline anastomoses; diameter sufficient for microanastomosis
- **Cavernosal artery** strongly perfuses the corpora with minimal communication to skin
- Extensive anastomotic connections between all four arterial axes around multiple neurovascular shunts

Venous: deep dorsal vein (primary), superficial dorsal vein. Nerves: dorsal nerves (somatic; penile skin sensation, in Buck's fascia, absent at 12 o'clock); cavernosal nerves (autonomic; erection).

## Historical Evolution

- **Macrosurgical era (pre-1977).** Ehrich 1929 first replantation; macroscopic-only reattachment had high rates of distal skin necrosis, stricture, fistula, and incomplete or absent sensation<sup>[[5]](#ref5)</sup>
- **Microsurgical era (Cohen 1977 onward).** Cohen reported the first clinical replantation by microneurovascular repair.<sup>[[27]](#ref27)</sup> Vascular and neural anastomosis became standard; potential recovery of urinary, sexual, and sensory function, with important complications still possible<sup>[[3]](#ref3)[[4]](#ref4)[[14]](#ref14)</sup>

## Surgical Technique — Complete Penile Replantation

Multidisciplinary team (urology and microvascular / plastic surgery) under general anesthesia.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[2]](#ref2)</sup>

### Preoperative

1. **Two-bag preservation.** Saline-moistened gauze in a sealed inner bag, placed in a second bag on ice; avoid direct tissue–ice contact. Arrange urgent specialist assessment and transfer. Published successful ischemia intervals are case reports, not fixed viability cutoffs<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[8]](#ref8)[[15]](#ref15)</sup>
2. **Patient stabilization.** Stump pressure dressing (do not clamp; the EAU also allows a tourniquet around the stump), IV fluids, type and screen, broad-spectrum antibiotics
3. Obtain early psychiatric assessment.<sup>[[5]](#ref5)</sup>

### Wang / Luo video-demonstrated case protocol<sup>[[2]](#ref2)</sup>

This Wang/Luo sequence describes one operative protocol. Target selection, order, and suture choice depend on the injury level, vessel quality, ischemic exposure, and available expertise.

1. **Amputated-segment preparation.** Under the operating microscope, identify, dissect and tag the dorsal arteries, deep and superficial dorsal veins and dorsal nerves
2. **Proximal-stump preparation.** Identify the corresponding structures and mobilize
3. **Urethral anastomosis.** End-to-end mucosal repair over a Foley with 5-0 / 6-0 PDS; spatulate to reduce stricture risk
4. **Corporal body repair.** 3-0 / 4-0 PDS interrupted to the tunica albuginea
5. **Deep dorsal vein anastomosis.** 9-0 / 10-0 nylon under the microscope (prevents venous congestion)
6. **Dorsal artery anastomosis.** 9-0 / 10-0 nylon; repair suitable arterial targets as the injury anatomy permits
7. **Superficial dorsal vein anastomosis.** Augments venous drainage
8. **Dorsal nerve repair.** 10-0 / 11-0 nylon (multiple nerves) to support sensory recovery
9. **Closure.** Close Buck's fascia and skin

### Optional additional anastomoses

- **Inferior external pudendal artery** (Lohasammakul, mean 0.94 mm) provides an additional source of shaft-skin perfusion. Cadaveric perfusion findings cannot establish a clinical zero-necrosis rate<sup>[[11]](#ref11)</sup>
- **Cavernosal artery** augments corporal inflow for erection (Tuffaha), but successful erections have been reported without it because dorsal-artery perforators supply the corpora<sup>[[10]](#ref10)[[12]](#ref12)[[18]](#ref18)</sup>

## Operative Parameters

| Parameter | Evidence-bounded point |
|---|---|
| Operative time | Wang reported 7 h in one case; duration is case- and team-dependent |
| Ischemia assessment | Minimize delay; viability depends on warm/cold exposure, injury mechanism, and tissue findings rather than a universal cutoff |
| Common microsurgical targets | Urethra, corpora, suitable dorsal arteries and veins, and nerves as anatomy permits |
| Additional targets | Additional suitable arterial, venous, and neural repairs may be considered when anatomy permits; no fixed count guarantees survival |

## Functional Outcomes — Microsurgical Penile Replantation

| Study | n | Ischemia time | Erectile function | Sensation | Voiding | Follow-up |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Wang 2022<sup>[[2]](#ref2)</sup> | 1 | 10 h | EHS 4 at 6 mo | Near-normal at 6 mo | Qmax 25 mL/s after catheter removal | 6 mo (erection/sensation) |
| Salem 2009<sup>[[16]](#ref16)</sup> | 1 | Presented 2 h after injury | Morning erections reported; two nocturnal emissions within first month | Preserved distally | Urinated smoothly after catheter removal on postoperative day 11 | First postoperative month reported |
| Sanger 1992<sup>[[5]](#ref5)</sup> | 4 | Variable | Reported normal erections in all 4 | Reported excellent return | Reported normal | Historical series |
| Lowe 1991<sup>[[19]](#ref19)</sup> | 1 | — | Documented by NPT | — | — | — |
| Szasz 1990<sup>[[20]](#ref20)</sup> | 1 | 14 h | Full erection by 32 wk (with testosterone) | Not reported | Normal by 3 wk | 32 wk |
| Fan 1996 (skin and glans survived)<sup>[[15]](#ref15)</sup> | 1 | 15 h | — | — | Not reported | — |
| Jiménez-Cruz 1995<sup>[[21]](#ref21)</sup> | 1 | — | Spontaneous erections | Sensate glans | Normal | — |

The Sanger historical series reported return of sensation and normal erections in all four patients. Its small, selected denominator does not establish an expected success rate. A later multicentre retrospective series reported 19/20 successful reattachments, but included 17 shaft and three isolated-glans amputations with only 6–8 months of follow-up; anatomical survival was not equivalent to normal erectile or urinary function.<sup>[[32]](#ref32)</sup>

## Microsurgical vs Macroscopic Replantation

The following patterns come mainly from case reports and small, heterogeneous series; they do not provide reliable head-to-head complication rates or guarantee an individual result.

| Parameter | **Microsurgical** | Macroscopic |
|---|---|---|
| **Skin necrosis** | Remains possible despite vascular repair | Frequently reported in historical series |
| **Urethral stricture** | Reported after microsurgical repair (1/4 in Sanger, a mild stricture that responded to dilation)<sup>[[5]](#ref5)</sup> | Reported in historical series |
| **Urethral fistula** | Reported | Reported in historical series |
| **Sensation recovery** | Meaningful recovery reported after nerve repair; extent and timing vary | Recovery also reported, but less predictable |
| **Erectile function** | Recovery has been reported; reliable rates are unavailable | Recovery has also been reported; reliable comparative rates are unavailable |
| **Cosmetic result** | Acceptable or near-normal results are reported in selected cases | Variable results are reported |
| **Availability** | Microvascular surgeon + operating microscope | Most centers |

Macroscopic replantation remains a fallback when microsurgery and transfer are unavailable, as specified by the ACS guidance. Putra reported one loupe-assisted (2.5x), non-microscopic repair of an incomplete amputation with preserved appearance, sensitivity and adequate Doppler arterial flow; that case does not establish equivalence or a comparative effect size.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[22]](#ref22)</sup>

## Microscopic Glans Replantation (Isolated Glans Amputation)

Glans-only amputation is a distinct entity, most commonly iatrogenic from circumcision (Mogen / Gomco / disposable stapler).<sup>[[6]](#ref6)[[7]](#ref7)[[8]](#ref8)</sup>

### Jin JoVE 2022 video protocol<sup>[[6]](#ref6)</sup>

1. **Amputated-glans preparation.** Under the microscope, identify the terminal branches of the dorsal arteries, the spongiosal vessels and the urethral mucosa
2. **Proximal-stump preparation.** Corporal tips and urethral stump; identify bleeding vessels as anastomosis targets
3. **Urethral anastomosis.** Repair over a catheter with fine absorbable suture
4. **Spongiosal and vascular anastomosis.** Perform under the microscope
5. **Closure.** Close the coronal skin

**Reported outcome in the Jin protocol or case:**

- The authors described restoration of the original glans shape with satisfactory appearance
- The authors reported normal micturition without an obvious complication
- The authors reported no significant reduction in sensation in the amputated-glans area

### Neonatal glans replantation (Sherman n = 7, including 6 neonates + 1 infant)<sup>[[8]](#ref8)</sup>

- Glanular tissue was successfully reconstructed after delays up to 8 hours in this historical report; this does not define a universal viability limit
- All patients with acceptable cosmetic results
- No long-term complications in neonates; distal urethral fistula formed in the 5-month-old

## Complications

| Complication | Incidence | Management |
|---|---|---|
| **Penile skin necrosis** | Reported in 26/46 selected published cases in a 2015–2023 review (56.5% of that case set, not a population risk); additional IEPA anastomosis has been described to support shaft-skin perfusion | Debridement, skin graft or scrotal flap as indicated<sup>[[11]](#ref11)[[23]](#ref23)[[33]](#ref33)</sup> |
| Venous congestion / edema | Reported early complication | Case-specific decompression or revision; leech therapy has been reported |
| Urethral stricture | Reported after both microsurgical and historical macroscopic repair | Dilation or urethroplasty |
| Urethral fistula | Reported | Surgical repair |
| Wound infection (incl. *Pseudomonas*) | Variable | Antibiotics ± HBO |
| Partial replant loss | Reported; a reliable incidence is unavailable | Debridement, secondary reconstruction |
| Recurrent self-mutilation | History of recurrent self-mutilation in 2/4 patients in one small Sanger series; not a population estimate<sup>[[5]](#ref5)</sup> | Psychiatric management |

### Skin necrosis salvage

Ching reported salvage of a complicated penile replantation with skin necrosis using a bipedicled scrotal flap, with satisfactory cosmetic and functional outcomes at 1 year.<sup>[[23]](#ref23)</sup>

## Adjunctive Postoperative Therapies

### Leech therapy (*Hirudo medicinalis*)

Adult and neonatal case reports describe leech therapy for venous congestion; comparative data and a standard regimen are lacking:<sup>[[7]](#ref7)[[9]](#ref9)[[24]](#ref24)</sup>

- Provides passive blood drainage and secretes hirudin (anticoagulant), hyaluronidase (tissue penetration), and vasodilators that improve microcirculation
- **Banihani.** First report in neonatal replantation (7-day-old, Mogen-clamp amputation at penopubic junction); successful replantation
- **Mousa.** Leeches, topical heparin and caudal analgesia in 28-day-old neonate with midshaft amputation; successful
- **Mineo.** After non-microsurgical replantation, edema resolved quickly; overlying skin loss required debridement; glans re-epithelialized with normal voiding, sensation, and erection
- When leech therapy is used, plan *Aeromonas*-active prophylaxis with local infectious-disease or antimicrobial-stewardship guidance and monitor for infection; resistance to commonly used agents has been reported.<sup>[[30]](#ref30)[[31]](#ref31)</sup>

### Hyperbaric oxygen (HBO)

- **Zhong.** The authors used adjuvant HBO and reported successful healing<sup>[[25]](#ref25)</sup>
- **Landström.** HBO for postoperative *Pseudomonas* wound infection threatening the replant; at 1 y normal flow, spontaneous erection with intromission, sensate glans<sup>[[17]](#ref17)</sup>
- Mechanisms: anti-inflammation, angiogenesis, fibroblast activity, bactericidal effects<sup>[[26]](#ref26)</sup>

### Reported postoperative measures — individualized by the treating team<sup>[[2]](#ref2)</sup>

- Broad-spectrum antibiotics
- Analgesia
- **Antithrombotic therapy** has been used in reports; agent, indication and bleeding risk need a case-specific plan, and it is not routine or automatic
- Anxiolytics (especially after self-mutilation)
- **Urethral catheter 7–14 days**
- Bed rest with penile monitoring (color / temperature / capillary refill) q 1–2 h initially
- Psychiatric evaluation and ongoing mental-health support

## When Replantation Is Not Possible — Penile Allotransplantation

When the amputated segment is lost or non-viable, penile allotransplantation is an investigational reconstructive option at highly specialized programs, with lifelong immunosuppression and major eligibility requirements; it is not an immediate substitute for emergency replantation or established autologous reconstruction. Van der Merwe (first functionally successful penile allotransplant, 24-mo follow-up post-ritual-circumcision aphallia, South Africa):<sup>[[18]](#ref18)</sup>

- Normal urination, erections suitable for vaginal penetration, and normal orgasm and ejaculation were reported. Measured at 24 months: maximum flow 16.3 mL/s and IIEF overall satisfaction 8 of 10, with regular intercourse from 3 months.<sup>[[18]](#ref18)</sup> Complications in the first weeks included an arterial thrombus needing urgent revision 8 h after surgery, and an infected hematoma with proximal skin necrosis treated surgically on day 6
- **Inferior epigastric artery** mobilized for anastomosis to the right dorsal artery when native dorsal arteries were obliterated by fibrosis
- Dorsal arteries clearly supplied corpora and spongiosum (cavernosal backflow after clamp release)
- Small contracted cavernosal arteries were not anastomosed; erectile recovery in this one allotransplant supports technical feasibility but does not define a universal replantation target set

## Pediatric Replantation Considerations

| Consideration | Detail |
|---|---|
| Most common mechanism | Circumcision complication (Mogen, stapler) |
| Tissue viability | Successful delayed repairs are reported; urgent assessment remains necessary, without an automatic time-based exclusion |
| Microsurgical feasibility | Technically challenging because of vessel size; successful in neonates as young as 7 days<sup>[[9]](#ref9)</sup> |
| Leech therapy | Successful in neonates; monitor for *Aeromonas* |
| Caudal analgesia | Used with leeches and topical heparin in a 28-day-old; sympathetic blockade with vasodilation is the proposed mechanism for improved replant perfusion<sup>[[7]](#ref7)</sup> |
| Long-term outcomes | Acceptable cosmesis was reported in the Sherman series; the single older infant developed a distal urethral fistula |

## Key Principles

1. **Time.** Use appropriate cold preservation and arrange urgent specialist transfer; isolated successful time intervals do not define a safe waiting period or futility cutoff
2. Microsurgical repair is preferred when available. Discuss variable functional recovery and the possibility of necrosis, urethral complications, and revision
3. **Repair planning.** Restore urethral and corporal continuity and repair suitable vessels and nerves as injury anatomy permits; a fixed vessel count does not guarantee perfusion
4. **Additional repairs.** Repair suitable additional arteries, veins, and nerves when anatomy permits; consider shaft-skin perfusion explicitly, without treating any fixed vessel count as a guarantee
5. Nerve repair is a microsurgical aim that supports sensory recovery; the extent and timing of recovery vary
6. **Adjuncts.** Leech therapy, HBO, and antithrombotics are described in individual reports; use is case-specific and comparative benefit is not established
7. Mental-health assessment and ongoing support are needed after self-inflicted injury; the history of recurrent self-mutilation in 2/4 patients in one small series is not a general 50% risk<sup>[[5]](#ref5)</sup>
8. Transfer urgently to a specialty center if microsurgery is not available (ACS recommendation)
