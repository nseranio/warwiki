# Testicular Prosthesis

Testicular prosthesis placement can restore scrotal contour after orchiectomy or congenital / acquired anorchia. The AUA testicular-cancer guideline says the option should be discussed before orchiectomy; observational series generally report high satisfaction among recipients, but acceptance is preference-sensitive and many patients are never offered the option.<sup>[[6]](#ref6)[[19]](#ref19)[[26]](#ref26)</sup>

For the broader scrotal-reconstruction ladder see [Scrotal Reconstruction Techniques](/docs/surgical-techniques/04e-genital-reconstruction/scrotal-reconstruction).

---

## History and Materials

| Era | Material |
|---|---|
| **1941** | First implant — Vitallium ball.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup> |
| **1950s–60s** | Glass, polyethylene, acrylic. |
| **1970s–90s** | Silicone gel-filled (Dow Corning) — worldwide standard.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup> |
| **1995** | Silicone gel manufacture **discontinued in the US** (breast-implant fallout) despite no evidence of systemic disease attributable to testicular silicone.<sup>[[2]](#ref2)[[3]](#ref3)</sup> |
| **2002** | FDA approves the **Torosa** saline-filled prosthesis (Coloplast).<sup>[[2]](#ref2)</sup> |
| **2020s** | **Rigicon Testi10™** saline — a manufacturer-linked retrospective chart review (n = 427) estimated **99.8% freedom from removal or revision at 54 months**; this is short-term device-survival evidence, not a comparative effectiveness result.<sup>[[4]](#ref4)</sup> |

Silicone gel devices have been widely used and remain available in some non-US markets; current product availability and labeling must be checked by jurisdiction.<sup>[[5]](#ref5)</sup>

### Available devices

- **Saline.** Torosa (Coloplast) is a silicone-elastomer shell filled intraoperatively with sterile saline and currently supplied in four sizes (XS / S / M / L). Fill only through the reinforced port and within the manufacturer range.<sup>[[2]](#ref2)[[6]](#ref6)[[27]](#ref27)</sup> Rigicon Testi10™ is available in firm, intraoperatively filled saline, and prefabricated-saline models in five sizes; availability depends on jurisdiction.<sup>[[28]](#ref28)</sup>
- **Silicone gel (international).** Cohesive gel devices are available outside the US. Claims of a more natural feel are not supported by head-to-head randomized evidence; firmness remains a common complaint across heterogeneous cohorts and devices.<sup>[[5]](#ref5)[[7]](#ref7)[[8]](#ref8)</sup>
- **Other / experimental.** Elliptical silastic shapes that better approximate the native ovoid testis (Ning 2011, n = 20; 100% satisfied with body image and position).<sup>[[9]](#ref9)</sup>

## Indications

| Indication | Detail |
|---|---|
| Post-orchiectomy for **testicular cancer** | Most common indication.<sup>[[6]](#ref6)</sup> |
| **Testicular torsion** | Non-salvageable testis — intravaginal exchange at orchiectomy is feasible (Bush 2012 n = 12 adolescents: no infection / extrusion, 91.7% symmetric).<sup>[[13]](#ref13)</sup> |
| **Congenital anorchia / vanishing testis** | Pediatric indication.<sup>[[14]](#ref14)</sup> |
| **Cryptorchidism** with no testis found at inguinal / abdominal exploration | Pediatric indication.<sup>[[14]](#ref14)</sup> |
| **Trauma** | Post-orchiectomy for unreconstructable injury.<sup>[[6]](#ref6)</sup> |
| **Atrophy** (post-orchitis, post-torsion) | Selected.<sup>[[6]](#ref6)</sup> |
| **Gender-affirming masculinizing surgery** | Neoscrotal augmentation as part of phalloplasty / scrotoplasty.<sup>[[5]](#ref5)</sup> |

**Contraindications and caution zones are device-specific.** Current Rigicon labeling lists active urogenital or skin infection, insufficient or poorly vascularized tissue, existing local or metastatic carcinoma, silicone sensitivity, and physiologic or psychological unsuitability; selected irradiated tissue requires particular caution. Use the current IFU for the chosen device rather than carrying forward the original pivotal-trial exclusions as universal contraindications.<sup>[[28]](#ref28)</sup>

## Preoperative Counseling

| Topic | Counseling point |
|---|---|
| **Offer rate** | 91% of orchiectomy patients consider being offered the prosthesis "extremely important" regardless of whether they accept. 42% of men without a prosthesis report never having been offered one.<sup>[[8]](#ref8)[[11]](#ref11)[[19]](#ref19)</sup> |
| **Firmness** | Will feel firmer than a native testis — the **most common complaint** (25–70% across studies); 59.3% satisfied with "feel" vs 89.8% satisfied with "look".<sup>[[4]](#ref4)[[7]](#ref7)[[8]](#ref8)[[10]](#ref10)</sup> |
| **Position** | High-riding (20–39%) is the second most common complaint.<sup>[[8]](#ref8)[[10]](#ref10)</sup> |
| **Weight** | Generally lighter than a native testis.<sup>[[6]](#ref6)[[9]](#ref9)</sup> |
| **Size** | Measure the contralateral testis (ultrasound or calipers); have sample sizes in clinic; intraoperative comparison.<sup>[[11]](#ref11)</sup> |
| **Timing** | Simultaneous and delayed placement are both options. In one retrospective cancer cohort, complication rates did not differ significantly among patients who later received chemotherapy, radiotherapy, or neither; that result does not remove the need to assess infection, tissue quality, oncologic plan, and preference individually.<sup>[[12]](#ref12)</sup> |
| **Academic-center effect** | Patients at academic institutions are more likely to be offered and to receive a prosthesis (OR 2.87).<sup>[[19]](#ref19)</sup> |

## Timing

### Simultaneous (at orchiectomy)

- **Testicular cancer.** Musi 2020 (n = 393). No significant difference in complication rate between adjuvant ChT (4.34%), RT (6.20%), and no further treatment (6.03%).<sup>[[12]](#ref12)</sup>
- **Torsion (adolescent).** Bush 2012 (n = 12). Intravaginal prosthesis at orchiectomy: no infection, no extrusion, 91.7% symmetric scrotum.<sup>[[13]](#ref13)</sup>

### Delayed

- Active infection at orchiectomy.
- Patient undecided.
- Concerns about wound healing.
- Pediatric: implantation within 1 year of orchiectomy is associated with fewer complications than later placement (p &lt; 0.05).<sup>[[14]](#ref14)</sup>

## Surgical Approaches

| Approach | Detail |
|---|---|
| **Inguinal** | Standard when simultaneous with radical inguinal orchiectomy. After cord ligation and orchiectomy, prosthesis inserted through the inguinal incision into a dartos pouch, secured to dartos or distal gubernacular remnant.<sup>[[6]](#ref6)[[15]](#ref15)</sup> |
| **Scrotal** | Direct scrotal incision into a dartos pouch. Higher theoretical infection / extrusion risk because of direct prosthesis–suture-line contact and thin scrotal skin.<sup>[[16]](#ref16)</sup> |
| **Suprascrotal ("Wink", Libman)** | 2-cm semilunar incision 2–3 cm lateral to the penis, just above the scrotum; blunt dissection into the scrotum. In Osemlak's single-center pediatric cohort (n = 290), suprascrotal access had fewer complications than transverse scrotal access (p = 0.01); this does not establish a universally superior approach.<sup>[[14]](#ref14)[[16]](#ref16)</sup> |
| **Intravaginal** | Prosthesis placed under the tunica vaginalis with preplaced sutures; native tunica becomes an extra barrier layer. Animal model favors this over the tunica-albuginea position (better mobility, less chronic inflammation).<sup>[[13]](#ref13)[[17]](#ref17)</sup> |

### Technical pearls<sup>[[6]](#ref6)[[11]](#ref11)</sup>

- Dartos pouch sized adequately; too small produces high-riding prosthesis.
- Decide whether to fix the implant using the device IFU, pocket geometry, tissue quality, and patient priorities; fixation is not automatically beneficial in every cohort.
- For saline devices: fill intraoperatively to the matched volume; protect against inadvertent puncture.
- Antibiotic prophylaxis; some surgeons irrigate the pocket with antibiotic solution.
- Outpatient procedure.

### The fixation question

Besombes 2024 (169 implants; 146 respondents) found no positioning benefit from fixation and more pain or discomfort among fixed implants (30.8% versus 9.78%, p &lt; 0.001). This retrospective, non-randomized association challenges routine fixation, but it does not override a particular device's IFU or prove that fixation caused the symptoms.<sup>[[21]](#ref21)</sup>

## Outcomes — Satisfaction

| Series | n | Overall satisfaction | "Too firm" | High-riding | Would repeat |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Clifford 2018 | 40 | **82.5%** good / excellent | 44% | 20% inappropriate | 87.5%<sup>[[8]](#ref8)</sup> |
| Araújo 2024 | 51 with prosthesis | **96.1%** | 25.5% | — | —<sup>[[7]](#ref7)</sup> |
| Yossepowitch 2011 | 86 | **77%** good / excellent | 70% | 39% | —<sup>[[10]](#ref10)</sup> |
| Adshead 2001 | 71 | **73%** | — | — | —<sup>[[11]](#ref11)</sup> |
| Incrocci 1999 | 22 | 68% improved body image | — | — | —<sup>[[18]](#ref18)</sup> |
| Nichols 2019 | 59 with prosthesis | 89.8% satisfied with **look**; 59.3% with **feel** | — | — | —<sup>[[19]](#ref19)</sup> |

In one observational survey, receiving a prosthesis was associated with greater overall genital satisfaction (adjusted OR 3.29).<sup>[[19]](#ref19)</sup> In Araújo's retrospective survey, sexual-function measures did not differ significantly between recipients and nonrecipients (all p &gt; 0.05); the observed 74% versus 50% normal-activity and 0% versus 16.7% severe-ED differences therefore should not be presented as proof that implantation preserves or improves sexual function.<sup>[[7]](#ref7)</sup>

## Complications

In the saline pivotal study (n = 149), 19% of participants had a reported adverse event during the study. The individual events below are study-specific and not all were necessarily device-caused.<sup>[[2]](#ref2)</sup>

| Complication | Rate |
|---|---|
| Device-related discomfort / pain | 3% |
| Scrotal edema | 1.3% |
| Infection | 1.3% (may require explantation) |
| Extrusion | 2.6% (requires explantation) |
| Deflation / leakage (saline only) | 0.7% |
| Pulmonary embolism | 0.7% (rare) |
| Migration / high-riding | 20–39% patient-reported |
| "Too firm" | 25–70% patient-reported |

**Population-specific complication signals:**

- **Transmasculine neoscrotal augmentation.** In Pigot's retrospective series, 20.8% of implants were explanted overall and 11.5% after the authors' later technique change; infection, extrusion, discomfort, leakage, and urethral problems contributed. Smoking was associated with infection and explantation. These historical, technique-specific findings are not general prosthesis rates.<sup>[[5]](#ref5)</sup>
- **Pediatric** (Osemlak n = 290). The cohort reported 92% good long-term results; complications were less frequent in boys implanted at age 1–3 years (p = 0.002), within 1 year of the primary operation (p &lt; 0.05), and through suprascrotal rather than transverse-scrotal access (p = 0.01). These are single-center observational associations, not universal timing or approach standards.<sup>[[14]](#ref14)</sup>
- **Post-chemotherapy / radiotherapy.** One retrospective cohort found no significant difference in complications according to subsequent adjuvant treatment.<sup>[[12]](#ref12)</sup>
- **Rigicon Testi10™.** One manufacturer-linked retrospective chart review of 427 implants reported one revision and estimated 99.8% freedom from removal or revision at 54 months; the adolescent subgroup contained 45 patients.<sup>[[4]](#ref4)</sup>
- **Capsular contracture.** Unlike breast implants, clinically significant capsular contracture is rarely reported; a fibrous capsule does form.<sup>[[17]](#ref17)</sup>

## Saline vs Silicone Gel: Design Considerations

| Parameter | Saline-filled | Silicone gel-filled |
|---|---|---|
| Availability | Torosa is the established US saline-filled option; other devices vary by jurisdiction | Primarily available outside the US |
| Sizing | Some models permit intraoperative fill adjustment within a labeled range | Preselected size |
| Material-specific issue | Deflation/leakage or filling-port injury is possible | Gel bleed/rupture and shell integrity are device considerations |
| Tactile result | Firmness and position dissatisfaction occur in observational cohorts | Cohesive fill may feel different, but comparative superiority is unproven |
| Evidence boundary | Outcomes depend on device, approach, cohort, follow-up, and survey method | Outcomes depend on device, approach, cohort, follow-up, and survey method |

No head-to-head randomized trial establishes superior satisfaction or complication rates for one fill material. Do not compare percentages drawn from different cohorts as if they were material-specific treatment effects.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[2]](#ref2)[[5]](#ref5)[[7]](#ref7)[[8]](#ref8)[[10]](#ref10)</sup>

## Pediatric Considerations<sup>[[14]](#ref14)[[20]](#ref20)</sup>

- **Timing evidence.** Osemlak's single-center cohort associated implantation at age 1–3 years and within 1 year of the primary operation with fewer complications; timing remains individualized because no standard is established.<sup>[[14]](#ref14)</sup>
- **Approach evidence.** The same cohort favored suprascrotal over transverse-scrotal access; it did not randomize approach.<sup>[[14]](#ref14)</sup>
- **Sizing.** Infant-sized prostheses available; Elder 1989 reported good outcomes in 41 boys.
- **Adolescent torsion.** Bush 2012 reported no infection or extrusion and a symmetric scrotum in 11 of 12 adolescents after intravaginal exchange at orchiectomy; this was a small series.<sup>[[13]](#ref13)</sup>
- **Adolescent Rigicon Testi10™.** The Atwater chart review reported no removal or revision among its 45 adolescent patients during available follow-up.<sup>[[4]](#ref4)</sup>

## Gender-Affirming Use<sup>[[5]](#ref5)</sup>

For the dedicated transmasculine-specific page (delayed-implantation timing, Hoebeke versus bilateral-rotational scrotoplasty selection, ZSI 475 FtM testicle-shaped-pump overlap, staging within the masculinizing reconstruction sequence), see [Testicular Implants (Gender-Affirming)](/docs/surgical-techniques/04k-gender-affirming-surgery/testicular-implants) in the 04k gender-affirming-surgery section.

- Largest series (n = 206; mean follow-up 11.5 yr): overall explantation 20.8%; modern technique 11.5%.
- Multiple brands used (Nagor, Promedon, Sebbin).
- The authors described a later practice pattern using smaller or lighter prostheses and delayed implantation rather than placement at scrotoplasty; comparative benefit was not randomized.
- Smoking is a significant risk for infection / explantation.

## Emerging — Hormone-Eluting and Tissue-Engineered Devices

Future-direction concepts shift from passive cosmetic replacement toward endocrinologically active implants that deliver testosterone, eliminating separate hormone replacement.<sup>[[11]](#ref11)</sup>

| Concept | Stage | Key feature |
|---|---|---|
| **Radiation-polymerized testosterone prosthesis (Yoshida)** | Preclinical (castrated rabbits) | Subcutaneous slow-release concept; not a clinical testicular-prosthesis outcome study.<sup>[[22]](#ref22)</sup> |
| **Radiation-polymerized testosterone prosthesis (Imai)** | Small clinical series (n = 12 hypogonadal men) | Historical testosterone-replacement experience; it does not establish current availability, regulatory approval, fertility restoration, or equivalence to cosmetic scrotal implants.<sup>[[23]](#ref23)</sup> |
| **Double-layer silastic STPT (Chen / Ning)** | Preclinical (castrated rats) | Controlled testosterone-undecanoate release via semi-permeable membranes; normalized T / LH, preserved ICPmax.<sup>[[24]](#ref24)</sup> |
| **Tissue-engineered cartilage prosthesis (Raya-Rivera / Atala)** | Preclinical (castrated athymic mice) | Bioengineered cartilage shell with hollow refillable center; physiologic T sustained through 14 wk in vivo; ex vivo release &gt; 500 ng/dL for 16 wk.<sup>[[25]](#ref25)</sup> |
| **Improved biomimetic designs** | Conceptual / patent | Softer, more natural tactile properties.<sup>[[11]](#ref11)</sup> |

## Practical Takeaways<sup>[[6]](#ref6)[[7]](#ref7)[[8]](#ref8)[[11]](#ref11)[[14]](#ref14)[[16]](#ref16)[[26]](#ref26)</sup>

1. **Discuss the option before orchiectomy** and document an informed, preference-sensitive decision; a patient may choose simultaneous, delayed, or no implantation.
2. **Assess timing individually.** Retrospective data support feasibility with subsequent chemotherapy or radiotherapy, but active infection, tissue quality, oncologic plan, and patient readiness still matter.
3. **Size selection** guided by contralateral measurement; sample implants in clinic.
4. **Counsel** about firmness (the most common complaint) and high-riding risk.
5. **Choose approach and fixation deliberately.** A pediatric cohort favored suprascrotal access, while a separate adult cohort associated fixation with more discomfort; neither observation is a universal rule.
6. **Pediatric timing is unsettled.** Discuss growth, replacement risk, scrotal development, psychosocial goals, and the limitations of the single-center timing data.
7. **Set realistic expectations.** Satisfaction is often high, but firmness, position, pain, infection, extrusion, migration, and revision remain possible; evidence does not prove a sexual-function benefit.
