# Escutcheonectomy

**Escutcheonectomy** removes contributory suprapubic skin and fat overlying the pubic region. It may form part of [Buried Penis Repair](/docs/surgical-techniques/04e-genital-reconstruction/buried-penis-repair), but the need for grafting, scrotal surgery or [Panniculectomy](/docs/surgical-techniques/04e-genital-reconstruction/panniculectomy) depends on the remaining anatomy. In the 101-patient TURNS cohort used to validate the PAS classification, escutcheonectomy was part of 55% of adult acquired buried penis (AABP) repairs and panniculectomy of 7%.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[2]](#ref2)</sup>

## Anatomy

The escutcheon is the suprapubic complex of skin, subcutaneous fat and underlying fascia over the pubic bone. In obesity it can enlarge and descend over the penile base, and it is distinct from the overhanging abdominal pannus.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup>

| Feature | Escutcheon | Pannus |
|---|---|---|
| Location | Over the pubic symphysis | Overhanging abdominal apron above the escutcheon |
| Procedure | Escutcheonectomy | [Panniculectomy](/docs/surgical-techniques/04e-genital-reconstruction/panniculectomy) |
| Share of AABP repairs in the TURNS cohort | 55% | 7%<sup>[[2]](#ref2)</sup> |

## Select the operation

Assess the patient standing and supine, including pannus descent, suprapubic tissue, penile skin quality, glans and meatal exposure and scrotal disease. Determine how much concealment is caused by suprapubic descent versus constriction or skin deficiency. The PAS classification (penis and escutcheon, abdomen, scrotum) documents these features. Its validation showed interrater reproducibility (kappa 0.95) and correlation with the operations performed, and it does not prescribe a fixed combination. The most common penile subtypes were P2c (contributory escutcheon with insufficient penile skin, 27%) and P2a (contributory escutcheon with sufficient penile skin, 21%).<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[2]](#ref2)</sup>

| Finding | Implication |
|---|---|
| Contributory escutcheon with adequate healthy shaft skin | Escutcheon correction and fixation may be sufficient; do not create a graft requirement by routine skin removal |
| Contributory escutcheon with diseased or deficient shaft skin | Combine with appropriate skin treatment and coverage |
| Abdominal pannus also contributes | Consider a coordinated panniculectomy |
| Diseased scrotum or urethral stricture | Plan the associated reconstruction and its timing separately |

Limited fat-pad surgery can leave unresolved concealment when other disease is present. Fuller and colleagues note that isolated resection of the escutcheon often leads to reburying. Grafting does not always follow escutcheonectomy.<sup>[[3]](#ref3)[[1]](#ref1)</sup>

## Operative approach

### Mark and expose

Individualize the incision to the tissue requiring removal, closure tension and surrounding perfusion. A transverse curvilinear design and modified patterns for associated panniculectomy have been described. In Baumgarten's IPP-setting technique, the transverse incision begins slightly medial to the anterior superior iliac spine and crosses to the contralateral spine, and the inferior border curves down to a medial apex about 1 cm above the penile base. These landmarks come from one operation and are not universal incision limits.<sup>[[4]](#ref4)[[1]](#ref1)</sup>

Excess suprapubic tissue is mobilized and removed in the planned plane. In Baumgarten's technique, dissection is carried to the lower abdominal anterior fascia; this exposure can also support fixation. Protect the penile neurovascular structures, urethra and spermatic cords, and identify the actual structures before further dissection.<sup>[[4]](#ref4)[[1]](#ref1)</sup>

### Preserve usable penile skin

Release constricting scars and remove diseased tissue when indicated. Preserve adequate viable shaft skin rather than routinely degloving and circumferentially excising it. Assess suspected LS, meatal disease and suspicious lesions, with histopathology of abnormal excised tissue. The 5% penile-cancer figure reported in Daly's selected repair-or-penectomy cohort is not a cancer rate in escutcheon specimens.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[5]](#ref5)</sup>

### Fixation and closure

Reconstruct the penopubic angle and secure mobile subdermal tissues to suitable deep fascia when needed. Fixation supports the repair; it does not guarantee against recurrent concealment. Plan a layered, low-tension closure, hemostasis and dead-space management. Drains are used according to the operation; Baumgarten placed two, one subcutaneous over the fat-pad bed and one in the scrotum around the pump.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[4]](#ref4)</sup>

### Grafting when required

Suitable suprapubic tissue can provide STSG or FTSG and avoid a separate donor wound. Donor skin quality and hair are assessed, because excised tissue is not suitable merely because it is available. For FTSG, preserve the dermis and carefully remove adherent fat. Graft sizing, fixation and dressing must preserve contact and permit functional shaft extension.<sup>[[6]](#ref6)[[7]](#ref7)</sup>

In Monn's series of 13 FTSG repairs (median follow-up 8 months), all grafts were successful, two patients had wound infections and none had reburying of the penis. Superior cosmesis or durability of FTSG over STSG has not been shown. Jeng's 32-patient LS series (median follow-up 8 months) had one complete and two partial graft losses, and four patients (13%) needed reoperation for graft loss, AABP recurrence or lymphedema; 28/32 without reoperation is not a graft-take rate. All three patients with graft loss had diabetes (69% of the cohort), but the association with graft loss was not statistically significant (P = .08).<sup>[[7]](#ref7)[[8]](#ref8)</sup> See [Penile Skin Grafting](/docs/surgical-techniques/04e-genital-reconstruction/penile-skin-grafting).

## Concurrent penile prosthesis placement

Simultaneous suprapubic fat-pad excision and IPP placement has been described in selected patients. Baumgarten's report included eight patients (mean BMI 36.6), one of whom developed prosthetic infection after inadvertently removing his drains. The series is uncontrolled and cannot show that combined surgery carries the same infection risk as IPP alone. The authors advise counseling about a theoretical increase in complications such as infection.<sup>[[4]](#ref4)</sup> Cosmetic procedures such as suspensory-ligament division are not routine components of functional escutcheonectomy.

## Outcomes and recovery

| Source | What was actually studied |
|---|---|
| Tang 2008 | Five men underwent combined reconstruction; four had escutcheonectomy. Penile graft take was 80–100% at two months; two abdominal wound complications occurred |
| Fuller 2017 | Twelve combined escutcheonectomy/scrotoplasty/STSG repairs; all had durable unburying at mean follow-up of eight months; STSG take 80–100% (mean 91.7%); operative time 312 ± 59 minutes, blood loss 304 ± 133 mL, stay 5.3 ± 1.1 days |
| Staniorski 2023 | 103 high-complexity repairs including escutcheonectomy and penile grafting; 50% complications (mostly Clavien I–II, 41%), wound dehiscence 31%, infection 30%, and 3.9% revision for poor outcome at median 11-month follow-up |
| Erpelding 2019 | Sixteen selected combined repairs; ten discharged the same day and six the next day, with all recorded 30-/90-day complications Clavien II |

These are outcomes of different combined repairs, not head-to-head comparisons or an escutcheonectomy-specific pooled success rate.<sup>[[9]](#ref9)[[3]](#ref3)[[10]](#ref10)[[11]](#ref11)</sup>

Counsel about wound breakdown, infection, fluid collections, graft problems when grafting is used, recurrent concealment and possible revision. Optimize relevant medical and functional risks, including weight-related disease, diabetes, smoking and frailty. In Staniorski's series, frailty (two or more Modified Frailty Index factors) was associated with complications (71% vs 41%; OR 6.41, 95% CI 1.77–23.22), an observational odds ratio and not an individualized probability. In Erpelding's series, higher BMI was significantly associated with complications; diabetes and tobacco use were not. Tailor discharge, mobilization, thromboprophylaxis, catheter and wound follow-up to the complete operation and available support.<sup>[[10]](#ref10)[[11]](#ref11)</sup>
