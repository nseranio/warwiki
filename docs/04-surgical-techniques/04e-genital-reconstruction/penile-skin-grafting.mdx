# Penile Skin Grafting

Penile skin grafting restores coverage after skin loss from buried penis, infection, lymphedema, trauma, inflammatory disease or tumor excision. Split-thickness (STSG) and full-thickness (FTSG) grafts are both used. The choice depends on the defect, viable recipient tissue, donor skin and the patient's goals for voiding, sensation, appearance and erection. Early graft take is only one outcome: contracture, recurrent disease and sensory change require longer follow-up.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup>

For the complete operation, see [Buried Penis Repair](/docs/surgical-techniques/04e-genital-reconstruction/buried-penis-repair), [Penile Reconstruction](/docs/surgical-techniques/04e-genital-reconstruction/penile), [Scrotal Reconstruction](/docs/surgical-techniques/04e-genital-reconstruction/scrotal) and [Glans Reconstruction](/docs/surgical-techniques/04e-genital-reconstruction/glans-reconstruction).

## Planning and indications

| Setting | Planning priority |
|---|---|
| Adult-acquired buried penis | Determine whether shaft skin is salvageable and whether suprapubic/scrotal tissue or urethral disease also needs treatment |
| Fournier's gangrene or other destructive infection | Complete source control and debridement; reconstruct once the wound is viable and further debridement is not required |
| Lymphedema, hidradenitis or foreign-body granuloma | Define disease extent and preserve viable, unaffected tissue; recipient-bed readiness determines immediate versus staged coverage |
| Trauma or circumcision injury | Assess skin, glans, urethra and corporal injury separately |
| Penile neoplasia | Establish histology and oncologically appropriate margins before reconstruction; glans resurfacing and reconstruction after glansectomy create different recipient beds |

The principles of wound preparation are supported by genital STSG experience; tumor-related selection follows the EAU penile-cancer guideline.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[2]](#ref2)</sup> Buried penis is not synonymous with lichen sclerosus (LS): Daly's selected 180-patient repair or penectomy cohort reported LS in 23%; a smaller study found LS in 55% of its 22 graft recipients. These are not population prevalence estimates.<sup>[[3]](#ref3)[[4]](#ref4)</sup>

Santucci's staging describes escalating adult-acquired buried penis repair: stage 1 involves only a phimotic band, stage 2 needs skin excision with STSG, stage 3 adds scrotal excision, stage 4 escutcheonectomy and stage 5 panniculectomy.<sup>[[20]](#ref20)</sup>

Examine suspicious lesions and obtain appropriate biopsy or specimen histology. The 5% penile-cancer finding in Daly's selected cohort should not be presented as an incidental-cancer probability for every buried-penis skin specimen.<sup>[[3]](#ref3)</sup>

## STSG and FTSG

| Feature | STSG | FTSG |
|---|---|---|
| Tissue transferred | Epidermis plus part of the dermis | Epidermis and the full dermis |
| Donor wound | Usually heals by re-epithelialization | Usually requires primary closure; donor availability limits size |
| Practical advantages | Covers larger areas; established genital reconstruction experience | Greater dermal content may help limit secondary contraction in suitable wounds |
| Tradeoffs | Secondary contracture, texture and pigment changes | More demanding graft-bed requirements; donor scar and complete defatting are considerations |
| Hair | Select donor site and thickness deliberately; absence of hair is not guaranteed | Donor-dependent; avoid an unsuitable hair-bearing surface |

A retrospective comparison included 39 buried-penis repairs, of which 22 received grafts: nine FTSG and 13 STSG. No statistically significant differences were detected in the reported graft groups, but this small study does not establish equivalence or one universally preferred graft.<sup>[[4]](#ref4)</sup>

Reported experience by indication:

- **Lymphedema.** Nine patients treated by wide excision and single-stage STSG had no recurrence of lymphedema, no compromise of sexual function and satisfactory cosmesis.<sup>[[21]](#ref21)</sup>
- **Fournier's gangrene.** In a 10-year single-institution review, most defects were repaired with split skin grafts. The authors advise full-thickness grafts for penile defects where possible, to limit contracture and revision surgery.<sup>[[22]](#ref22)</sup>
- **Circumcision-related buried penis.** In 13 men (mean age 22 years) treated with STSG, 4 (30%) had long-term complications at a median of 44 months: reduced graft sensation (2), graft contracture (1) and retarded ejaculation (1). IIEF rose from 14.1 to 22.8 (p = 0.03).<sup>[[23]](#ref23)</sup>
- **Organ-sparing penile cancer surgery.** A review reported local recurrence of 0% to 10% after glans resurfacing for cancer.<sup>[[24]](#ref24)</sup>

## Donor selection

Thigh STSG is a conventional option. An excised escutcheon or pannus can provide graft skin in selected buried-penis repairs, reducing the need for a separate donor wound. Assess that skin's quality and suitability; not every excised specimen is an acceptable graft. FTSG requires removal of adherent fat while preserving the dermis; retained fat can impede contact and revascularization, but it is not established as the dominant cause of every penile FTSG failure.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[5]](#ref5)[[6]](#ref6)[[7]](#ref7)</sup>

In Jeng's 32-patient escutcheon-FTSG series (69% with diabetes, median follow-up 8 months), four patients (13%) underwent reoperation for surgical failure: complete graft loss (1), recurrence of buried penis (1) and lymphedema (2). Two further patients had partial graft loss managed conservatively. All three patients with graft loss had diabetes, but the reported diabetes or cardiovascular association was not statistically significant (P = .08); this does not establish a dominant independent predictor.<sup>[[8]](#ref8)</sup>

Donor options and reported experience:

- **Anterior thigh.** In Alwaal's series, nearly all patients had donor-site pain for the first few weeks, and no donor-site infection occurred.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup>
- **Excised escutcheon.** The specimen is a same-field donor that avoids a second wound. Strother harvests the graft from the escutcheonectomy specimen and reports lower morbidity.<sup>[[6]](#ref6)</sup> Fuller's 12 patients (escutcheonectomy, scrotoplasty, penile STSG) had a take of 80% to 100% (mean 91.7%) at a mean follow-up of 8 months.<sup>[[25]](#ref25)</sup> In Monn's 13 patients with escutcheon FTSG, all grafts were successful and two developed wound infections, at a median follow-up of 8 months.<sup>[[5]](#ref5)</sup>
- **Pannus.** Figler mobilizes the pannus through a modified trapezoid incision with its superior attachments intact and harvests an unmeshed STSG at a depth of 0.018 inch in 2-inch sections. Take was at least 95% in 19 patients, with cellulitis in 5 (26%) and minor dehiscence in 3 (16%) at a median of 11.5 months.<sup>[[7]](#ref7)</sup>
- **Oral mucosa** is used for urethral lining, not shaft coverage (see lichen sclerosus below).

## Operative principles

### Excision and recipient-bed preparation

Tailor excision to the pathology and viable margins. Preserve healthy skin and dartos when appropriate; routine circumferential removal down to Buck's fascia is not necessary for every defect. Avoid damaging the urethra, neurovascular structures or corporal tissue. Obtain hemostasis and a stable, vascularized surface for graft contact.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup>

For a previously infected wound, readiness is determined by infection control and tissue viability, not a universal one- or two-week delay. A graft or matrix does not replace debridement. Integra is contraindicated on clinically infected wounds.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[9]](#ref9)</sup>

If a dermal matrix is selected, distinguish the product and protocol: Liguori used staged Integra followed by STSG at three weeks; Kang used single-stage MatriDerm Flex plus STSG. These small series do not justify an automatic three-week staging rule for all substitutes.<sup>[[10]](#ref10)[[11]](#ref11)</sup> See [Penile Tissue Substitutes](/docs/surgical-techniques/04e-genital-reconstruction/penile-tissue-substitutes).

### Harvest, sizing and fixation

Select STSG thickness for the donor, bed and reconstructive goal. Published genital techniques include a 0.015-inch thigh graft and 0.018-inch pannus harvest; these are technique descriptions, not a single required depth for every patient.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[7]](#ref7)</sup>

Keep the graft moist and minimize trauma. Size and secure it with the penis gently stretched so the reconstruction accommodates erection without tension. Ensure close contact, fluid egress and appropriate fixation while avoiding a constricting ring. In Alwaal's technique, viable dartos was spared when possible and the penis remained stretched during dressing placement.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup>

An unmeshed sheet can be useful when appearance and expansion during erection are priorities. Meshed unexpanded 1:1 STSG is also an established technique. Black's nine consecutive patients received 0.012 or 0.016-inch grafts meshed 1:1 with transverse slits and a zigzag ventral graft juncture. All nine had 100% take, and cosmesis was satisfactory at a mean of 6 months in all but one (chronic penile manipulation). The series does not support a blanket prohibition of all meshing or prove that one seam configuration prevents every contracture.<sup>[[12]](#ref12)</sup>

Pharmacologically induced erection during sizing is a described adjunct, not a requirement. A four-patient protocol combining prostaglandin injection, NPWT and postoperative tadalafil cannot establish the independent benefit or routine safety of any component.<sup>[[13]](#ref13)</sup>

### Bolster or NPWT

Immobilize the graft, reduce shear and fluid accumulation, and protect against urine contamination. Conventional bolsters and NPWT are both used. Avoid excessive circumferential pressure and monitor distal perfusion. Dressing interface, suction settings, catheter need and removal timing follow the operation and local protocol; no fixed rule applies to every graft.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup>

| Technique | Description | Reported experience |
|---|---|---|
| Conventional bolster | Alwaal: Xeroform, mineral-oil-soaked cotton, fluffs and bolsters, and a penile splint, with immobility for 5 days emphasized.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup> Figler: bolster secured to the penis for 5 to 7 days<sup>[[7]](#ref7)</sup> | Used in the series above |
| Rook eggcrate-foam bolster | Nonadherent dressing, saline-moistened silver dressing and gauze under eggcrate foam with the spikes toward the wound, stapled cylindrically with the penis extended | 21 men: median removal at 4 days, median graft take 100% through postoperative day 14 and 95% at days 15 to 30, 1/21 below 80% take<sup>[[26]](#ref26)</sup> |
| NPWT | Applied circumferentially; Kang used −125 mmHg over dermal substitute and STSG | See the trial data below and Kang's 11 patients<sup>[[11]](#ref11)</sup> |
| TODGA tie-over (glans) | Proflavine-soaked gauze with tie-over sutures, left undisturbed for 10 days | 41 operations in 40 patients; after the protocol was standardized, 1 of 29 operations needed re-grafting<sup>[[27]](#ref27)</sup> |

A 2025 meta-analysis of 16 randomized skin-graft trials found an 8.3-percentage-point average take improvement with NPWT, with substantial heterogeneity. These were not penile-only trials. Likewise, the 97.6% versus 81.7% take comparison in Cao's study was an irregular or high-mobility subgroup; it is not proof that NPWT outperforms a bolster on every penis or that one pressure is optimal.<sup>[[14]](#ref14)[[15]](#ref15)</sup>

## Postoperative care

Document graft inspection and a plan for dressing and catheter removal, hygiene, mobility and return to sexual activity. Review earlier for increasing pain, fever, discharge, bleeding, urinary obstruction or concern about perfusion. Protection from shear must be balanced with mobilization and the patient's thromboembolic risk. Resume sexual activity when the reconstruction has healed adequately and the operating team has reviewed it.

Assess sensation, erection-related pain, tethering, recurrent skin disease, urinary symptoms and graft durability after initial healing. Early take does not guarantee normal sensation. Alwaal reported that deep touch was preserved enough to maintain erectile function, whereas light touch was nearly always affected to varying degrees and delayed ejaculation occurred in many patients.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup>

Routine daily tadalafil to prevent graft contracture is not supported by comparative evidence. A recent rehabilitation report described only two men after complex genital infections; it does not establish a standard pelvic-floor therapy regimen that prevents penile graft contracture. Persistent functional problems merit individual assessment and targeted treatment.<sup>[[13]](#ref13)[[16]](#ref16)</sup>

## Complications

| Complication | Reported experience |
|---|---|
| Wound infection | Cellulitis in 5/19 (26%) after pannus-donor STSG<sup>[[7]](#ref7)</sup>; wound infection in 41% of 32 escutcheon FTSG patients<sup>[[8]](#ref8)</sup> |
| Wound dehiscence | Minor dehiscence in 3/19 (16%)<sup>[[7]](#ref7)</sup> |
| Partial or complete graft loss | Jeng: 2 partial losses (6%) managed conservatively and 1 complete loss, all in patients with diabetes (P = .08 for the association)<sup>[[8]](#ref8)</sup>. Alwaal: 1 poor take among 54 patients; 16 complications in total, including graft failure, infection, testicular necrosis, fistulae and bleeding<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup> |
| Sensory change and contracture | Light touch nearly always affected and delayed ejaculation in many (Alwaal)<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup>; reduced graft sensation in 2/13, contracture in 1/13 and retarded ejaculation in 1/13 (Kara)<sup>[[23]](#ref23)</sup> |
| Recurrence or reburying | Recurrence in 26/204 (12.7%); hematoma was associated with recurrence<sup>[[17]](#ref17)</sup> |

## Interpreting outcomes

| Study | What was measured | Limits |
|---|---|---|
| Alwaal 2015 | 52/54 adults with mixed genital diagnoses had greater than 90% graft-area take | Retrospective; variable follow-up; functional assessments were not standardized validated instruments |
| Gül, online 2024 / issue 2026 | Nine FTSG versus 13 STSG recipients within 39 buried-penis repairs | Small nonrandomized comparison; no detected difference does not establish equivalence |
| Plamadeala 2026 | 204 first-time buried-penis reconstructions; **91 received grafts**. Stretched-length gain **3.0 cm**; 26/204 recurrence, 55/204 complications | Only 68 completed functional questionnaires; outcomes describe the complete operation, not isolated graft performance |

The populations, denominators and outcome definitions differ, limiting direct comparison across studies.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[4]](#ref4)[[17]](#ref17)</sup>

In the 204-patient cohort, more complex repair was associated with less recurrence and more complications. Baseline differences and selection limit causal interpretation; the finding does not justify more aggressive surgery for every patient. The source also contains inconsistent subgroup percentages, so those should not be used for individualized risk prediction.<sup>[[17]](#ref17)</sup>

## Special situations

**LS and urethral disease:** genital skin should not be used for LS-related urethral reconstruction; oral mucosa is the usual graft option. That recommendation concerns the urethral lining. External shaft coverage is a separate decision, using suitable unaffected donor skin and continuing surveillance for LS or suspicious lesions.<sup>[[18]](#ref18)[[19]](#ref19)[[4]](#ref4)</sup>

**Flap versus graft.** Choose vascularized coverage when the bed cannot support a graft, when dead space needs filling or when critical structures need additional protection. Exposed testes or corpora alone do not automatically mandate a flap: the retained covering tissues and vascularity matter.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup> A review supports full-thickness grafts and pedicled scrotal flaps for penile shaft skin defects.<sup>[[24]](#ref24)</sup> Yao described a modified bilateral (butterfly) scrotal flap pedicled on the anterior scrotal arteries in 7 patients; two had necrosis of small flap areas that healed with wound care.<sup>[[28]](#ref28)</sup>

**Glans reconstruction.** Glans resurfacing removes the glans epithelium and subepithelial tissue and resurfaces the remaining glans, often with STSG. It is distinct from forming a neoglans after glansectomy, when the graft may cover distal corporal tissue. Match grafting to the oncologic resection; do not conflate the two recipient beds.<sup>[[2]](#ref2)</sup> Malone's TODGA tie-over dressing fixes a split-skin graft to the raw glans or penile stump firmly enough to allow immediate mobilization, and it suits the spherical glans surface.<sup>[[27]](#ref27)</sup>
