# Primary Closure of the Scrotum

**Primary closure** or local advancement preserves healthy native scrotal tissue and avoids a graft donor site. It is used when the tissue is viable, infection is controlled and the edges meet comfortably without tension. Karian's review recommends grafting or a flap for defects larger than 50% of the scrotum, and advancement or secondary intention for smaller defects that cannot be closed without tension. The 50% figure is a guide, and the amount and quality of remaining tissue matter more.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup>

See the [reconstruction overview](/docs/surgical-techniques/04e-genital-reconstruction/scrotal-reconstruction) for alternatives when a closure would compromise coverage or perfusion.

## Anatomy relevant to mobilization

The scrotal wall consists, from superficial to deep, of skin, dartos fascia (which contains smooth muscle), external spermatic fascia, cremasteric muscle and fascia, internal spermatic fascia and tunica vaginalis. The skin is thin, rugated and elastic. Mobilization preserves the skin–dartos envelope and its vessels and keeps it distinct from the deeper spermatic coverings.

Carrera's study of 15 cadavers found two lateral cutaneous territories supplied by the inferior external pudendal arteries and a central territory supplied by continuations of the perineal arteries. These reach the scrotum posteriorly and run deeply on both sides of the septum. The territories are widely inter-anastomosed. Dissection near the septum keeps clear of these vessels.<sup>[[2]](#ref2)</sup>

Gubernacular and cremasteric collateral vessels can contribute to testicular supply, especially after prior vessel-dividing orchiopexy. Ellis showed robust cremasteric, deferential and spermatic-stump collaterals in children at second-stage Fowler–Stephens surgery. This is a pediatric observation, not an adult scrotoplasty outcome. Previous testicular and inguinal operations are reviewed before the gubernaculum or cord is mobilized.<sup>[[3]](#ref3)</sup>

## Selection and timing

| Setting | Decision |
|---|---|
| Elective scrotal incision or limited defect | Layered closure after hemostasis when viable tissue approximates easily.<sup>[[8]](#ref8)</sup> |
| Trauma | Evaluate the testis, cord and associated injuries. Debride nonviable tissue, then choose immediate or delayed skin closure by contamination and viability.<sup>[[4]](#ref4)</sup> |
| Fournier's gangrene | Urgent source control first. Same-admission closure is considered once the patient and wound are suitable.<sup>[[9]](#ref9)[[10]](#ref10)</sup> |
| Hidradenitis | Treat the underlying disease and close only if adequate healthy tissue remains after the indicated excision. |
| Genital lymphedema | Tailored excision may allow native-skin closure. Torio-Padron closed 51 of 51 primarily and Wisenbaugh 10 of 11.<sup>[[5]](#ref5)[[6]](#ref6)</sup> |

A graft or flap is kept as a backup plan. Mobilization is not escalated solely to avoid one.

## Operative principles

1. **Assess and preserve viable structures.** In trauma, confirm the testicular repair before addressing the skin envelope. Suspected rupture or an inconclusive ultrasound calls for exploration. Tunica albuginea closure, with tunica vaginalis coverage when needed, is a testicular repair and is separate from scrotal skin closure.<sup>[[4]](#ref4)</sup>
2. **Control bleeding and contamination.** Do not bury inadequately debrided infection, uncontrolled hematoma or necrotic tissue beneath a skin closure.
3. **Mobilize only what is necessary.** Preserve a tissue layer around the vascular supply and tailor any advancement to the defect. Avoid cord twisting, compression or forced superior relocation of the testes.<sup>[[2]](#ref2)[[3]](#ref3)</sup>
4. **Test the closure before committing.** Assess perfusion, tension, room for the contents and the effect of expected swelling. If closure is unsatisfactory, use delayed closure, grafting or a planned flap.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup>
5. **Close the layers with minimal tissue trauma.** Approximate viable dartos and skin with absorbable suture, without constricting the blood supply. Suture caliber, pattern, drainage and support depend on tissue quality and contamination. A drain does not replace hemostasis.<sup>[[7]](#ref7)[[8]](#ref8)</sup>

After hydrocelectomy, orchiectomy, spermatocelectomy or epididymectomy, hematoma and edema are the most frequent complications of scrotal surgery. Oesterling described a simple method to prevent them.<sup>[[8]](#ref8)</sup>

## Maneuvers for larger defects

These maneuvers are often combined to convert a defect that looks too large into a tension-free closure. Each is described in the technical literature, and none is a required step.

- **Division of the scrotal septum.** Dividing the midline partition joins the two compartments and recruits contralateral skin across the midline. It is most useful for predominantly unilateral defects. Carrera's vascular map places the central arteries on both sides of the septum, so the plane is chosen with that in mind.<sup>[[2]](#ref2)</sup>
- **Dartos mobilization off the tunica vaginalis.** Dissection in the plane between dartos and the testicular coverings releases the skin–dartos envelope and gives laxity while preserving the envelope's blood supply.
- **Orchidopexy and cord mobilization.** Hayon mobilized the testes and cords and brought the testes and inferior penis together with interrupted Vicryl. Redundant cord was coiled under the abdominal wall.<sup>[[19]](#ref19)</sup> This reduces the volume that needs skin cover. Gubernacular division is not a standard prerequisite, given the collateral vessels described above.<sup>[[3]](#ref3)</sup>
- **Component separation.** Extensive undermining and mobilization of genital, perineal and adjacent skin allows primary closure. This is the principle in Sandberg's series (below).
- **Delayed primary closure.** Closure follows serial debridement once the wound is clean (Kumar's series, below).

## Delayed closure and local advancement after Fournier's gangrene

**Sandberg 2022** retrospectively evaluated 84 patients. Forty-eight (57%) were closed primarily and 36 healed by secondary intention. Closure was by a reconstructive urologist using wide genital tissue mobilization, or by the general surgery or burn service. Anatomical extent differed among groups, being greatest in the general surgery and burn group and least in the secondary-intention group. Wound convalescence was 64% shorter with closure after adjustment for anatomical extent. Secondary procedures were needed in 6.3% of closed and 11% of open wounds (p = 0.67). Grafting was predicted by wound size, specifically suprapubic or penile wounds with more than 50% involvement. The authors concluded that early, same-admission primary closure of stable wounds is safe.<sup>[[9]](#ref9)</sup>

**Lauerman 2018** reported complete primary closure in 39.9% of 168 patients in a mixed-sex trauma-center cohort. Closed patients had lower SOFA scores, more often had perineum-limited disease and needed fewer debridements. Wound healing without further intervention reached 82.1% beyond six months, and even large wounds often closed with long-term wound care without flaps or early grafting.<sup>[[10]](#ref10)</sup>

**Kumar 2023** described 16 selected patients who had delayed primary closure. Median time to closure was 6.5 days (IQR 3–11) and mean defect area was 119 cm² (range 44–346). Eight patients (50%) were closed with scrotal flaps alone. The others had advancement flaps from the inner thigh, lower abdomen or perineum. Four patients had Clavien–Dindo III complications: two partial flap necroses, one dehiscence and one bleeding episode. Sixty-three percent were discharged directly home. The 6.5-day median describes this cohort and is not a timing requirement.<sup>[[11]](#ref11)</sup>

## Selected expansion and staging techniques

| Technique | Reported experience |
|---|---|
| Rapid intraoperative expansion | Kwon treated three patients with about 75% scrotal skin loss. Bilateral pockets in residual lateral scrotal and medial thigh skin received round 300-mL expanders, inflated over 20 minutes for tension-free closure. All three had complete testicular coverage at 12 months without complications. This is a preliminary report.<sup>[[12]](#ref12)</sup> |
| Tissue-expanded V-Y scrotoplasty | Rapp described staged expansion followed by V-Y advancement scrotoplasty after extensive skin loss. It needs a separate expansion stage and a second operation.<sup>[[13]](#ref13)</sup> |
| Expanded groin flap | Atik described three total-loss reconstructions. An expander was placed through an inferolateral groin incision, and after 3 weeks a superthin subfascial flap was raised and the donor site closed primarily. This is regional flap transfer, not primary closure.<sup>[[14]](#ref14)</sup> |
| Temporary thigh pouches | Okwudili's 12-patient series relocated the testes to anteromedial thigh pouches and returned them over 3 to 8 months. Testicular volume was maintained. The abstract reports no semen data.<sup>[[15]](#ref15)</sup> |

See [Testicular Thigh Pouches](/docs/surgical-techniques/04e-genital-reconstruction/scrotal-thigh-pouch) for that operation. These maneuvers are not an obligatory sequence before a graft is offered.

## Technique selection by defect

| Defect | Approach | Point |
|---|---|---|
| Elective or small | Layered closure of dartos and skin, with or without a drain | Hemostasis to prevent hematoma.<sup>[[8]](#ref8)</sup> |
| Less than 50% loss | Primary closure with the maneuvers above | Closure without tension is the aim.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup> |
| Larger loss, clean field | Component separation or wide mobilization, with or without delayed primary closure | 57% primary closure in Sandberg.<sup>[[9]](#ref9)</sup> |
| Larger loss, contaminated | Serial debridement, then delayed primary closure | Kumar: 16 patients, mean 119 cm².<sup>[[11]](#ref11)</sup> |
| About 75% loss, selected | Rapid intraoperative tissue expansion | Preliminary, n = 3.<sup>[[12]](#ref12)</sup> |
| Extensive loss, stable patient | Tissue-expanded V-Y scrotoplasty or expanded groin flap | Staged.<sup>[[13]](#ref13)[[14]](#ref14)</sup> |
| Closure not feasible immediately | Temporary thigh pouches with later return | Preliminary experience.<sup>[[15]](#ref15)</sup> |

## Function and follow-up

**Appearance.** Native tissue keeps the rugated, elastic envelope. Grafts lack rugation and elasticity. Alwaal's 54-patient mixed genital cohort had more than 90% graft take in 52 patients with good cosmetic outcomes. Konofaos reported that a two-stage wrap-around graft with reapproximation of the grafted testes improved cosmesis in six patients. Pedicled vertical DIEP flaps were used for large penoscrotal defects in Yalçın's 17 patients, with partial necrosis in 3.<sup>[[20]](#ref20)[[21]](#ref21)[[22]](#ref22)</sup>

**Thermoregulation and fertility.** In Demir's rat experiment, grafts produced lower testicular weight, shorter germinal epithelium and lower Johnsen scores than controls, and flaps matched controls. In humans, grafted skin has impaired cutaneous vasodilation and sweating that persists 4 to 8 years after grafting, with preserved vasoconstriction.<sup>[[16]](#ref16)[[17]](#ref17)[[18]](#ref18)</sup> These burn-graft studies did not measure fertility after scrotal reconstruction. Wang followed two men with free skin grafts for penoscrotal avulsion and found normal sperm quality and no contracture.<sup>[[23]](#ref23)</sup>

**Sexual function and quality of life.** After Fournier's gangrene, 65% of patients in Czymek's multicenter series reported deterioration in sexual function, and SF-36 physical scores were below population norms. Genital graft complications such as contracture, hypertrophic scar and hypersensitivity can affect sitting, voiding and intimacy, and pelvic-floor rehabilitation has been described for them in two patients. Yalçın's mean CSFQ-14 score after DIEP reconstruction was 41.47 ± 19.96. Long-term sexual-function data after primary closure specifically are lacking.<sup>[[24]](#ref24)[[25]](#ref25)[[22]](#ref22)</sup>

**Follow-up.** Reproductive goals are discussed, and pain, mobility, sexual function and healing are evaluated. Marked bleeding, threatened perfusion, infection, testicular compromise or major dehiscence may need intervention.

**Lymphedema.** Massive localized lymphedema and genital lymphedema need continuing disease-directed care. Torio-Padron's 51 patients had an integrated decongestive and surgical program, and three complications required revision (two hematomas and one dehiscence). In Wisenbaugh's 11 patients, wound complications were common but generally managed with local care. Quality-of-life scores improved from a mean of 1.3 to 7.7, and most patients gained weight after surgery (mean +5.2 kg).<sup>[[5]](#ref5)[[6]](#ref6)</sup>

## Complications

| Complication | Notes |
|---|---|
| Hematoma and edema | The most frequent complications of scrotal surgery. Meticulous hemostasis and layered closure reduce them.<sup>[[8]](#ref8)</sup> |
| Wound dehiscence and wound complications | Common in massive lymphedema excision (Wisenbaugh) and usually managed with local wound care.<sup>[[6]](#ref6)</sup> |
| Tension necrosis and testicular displacement | Avoided by adequate mobilization rather than closure under tension. Cord position is checked at closure. |
| Partial flap necrosis after delayed primary closure | Two of 16 in Kumar's series, within four Clavien–Dindo III complications.<sup>[[11]](#ref11)</sup> |
| Sitting pain after thigh-pouch bridging | One of 20 in Staniorski's definitive-pouch series.<sup>[[26]](#ref26)</sup> |
