# 3R / SCIP Lymphatic Flap Transfer for Male Genital Elephantiasis

**Radical reduction and reconstruction (3R)** combines excision of advanced genital lymphedema, soft-tissue coverage, and an attempt to improve lymphatic drainage with a pedicled superficial circumflex iliac artery perforator (SCIP) flap. The initial seven-patient report described no postoperative genital complication or recurrence at a mean of 22.7 months. It does not establish cure, superiority over other reconstructions, or freedom from long-term compression and recurrence.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup>

For diagnosis and broader treatment selection, see [Genital Lymphedema](/docs/clinical-conditions/03g-genital-scrotal/genital-lymphedema) and [Giant Penoscrotal Lymphedema](/docs/clinical-conditions/03g-genital-scrotal/giant-penoscrotal-lymphedema). Related procedures include [Complex Decongestive Therapy](/docs/surgical-techniques/04e-genital-reconstruction/complex-decongestive-therapy), [Lymphaticovenous Anastomosis](/docs/surgical-techniques/04e-genital-reconstruction/lymphaticovenous-anastomosis), [Vascularized Lymph Node Transfer](/docs/surgical-techniques/04e-genital-reconstruction/vascularized-lymph-node-transfer), [Modified Charles Procedure](/docs/surgical-techniques/04e-genital-reconstruction/modified-charles-procedure), and [Debulking Scrotoplasty](/docs/surgical-techniques/04e-genital-reconstruction/debulking-scrotoplasty).

## What the Operation Transfers

The author's subsequent technical review describes 3R using the **lymph-interpositional-flap transfer (LIFT)** concept: mapped lymphatic collectors are retained in the flap's deep fat and directed toward preserved inguinal nodes around its pedicle. The operation does not routinely transfer the inguinal nodes within the skin paddle.<sup>[[2]](#ref2)</sup>

| Component | Intended contribution | Important distinction |
|---|---|---|
| Excision | Remove diseased skin and fibrotic subcutaneous tissue that conservative treatment will not reverse | Preserve the penis, urethra, neurovascular structures, testes, and cords; complete removal is not always anatomically possible |
| SCIP skin coverage | Reconstruct the scrotum and, when necessary, provide a separate thin penile skin paddle | A skin flap's survival does not demonstrate restored lymphatic drainage |
| SCIP-LIFT | Retain patent collectors in deep fat and align their cut ends with the affected recipient tissue | Reconnection depends on healing and lymphangiogenesis; this is not an immediately sutured lymphatic bypass |

These distinctions follow the published 3R technical description.<sup>[[2]](#ref2)</sup> **LYST**, which deliberately incorporates nodes and afferent vessels, and other complete-system transfer variants should be described according to their own operative design. Neither the SCIP name nor a shared vascular pedicle makes these operations interchangeable. See [LYST](/docs/surgical-techniques/04e-genital-reconstruction/lyst) and [Complete Functional Lymphatic System Pedicled Transfer](/docs/surgical-techniques/04e-genital-reconstruction/complete-functional-lymphatic-pedicled-transfer).<sup>[[3]](#ref3)[[4]](#ref4)</sup>

## Selection and Planning

Consider this combined approach for substantial scrotal or penoscrotal disease with fibrotic tissue and damaged skin requiring excision, when an appropriate groin donor site and specialist reconstructive expertise are available. Stage labels alone do not select a flap. Establish the cause of swelling, assess associated leg disease and the condition of both groins, treat active infection, and agree on functional goals and realistic follow-up. Conservative care remains part of the overall treatment plan.<sup>[[2]](#ref2)[[5]](#ref5)</sup>

- **Define the defect.** Assess penile, scrotal and suprapubic involvement separately; identify usable residual skin and the coverage needed after excision.
- **Evaluate the donor site.** Previous groin dissection, radiation, scars, lymphatic impairment, and vascular anatomy may limit a pedicled SCIP option. Protect drainage of the donor leg as well as the genital region.
- **Map vessels and lymphatics separately.** Duplex ultrasound helps locate arterial branches and perforators; ICG lymphography delineates superficial collectors and their direction. A vascular map alone cannot establish that the lymphatic pathway is usable.
- **Plan the reach and closure.** Confirm the skin and deep-fat components, pedicle route, recipient contact, and donor closure without excessive tension. A thin penile skin paddle and a lymphatic-bearing deep-fat component serve different purposes.<sup>[[2]](#ref2)</sup>

## SCIP Flap Anatomy

The superficial circumflex iliac artery (SCIA) arises from the femoral artery and divides into a superficial and a deep branch. Both branches were found in every specimen in a 20-groin cadaveric study.<sup>[[6]](#ref6)</sup>

| Branch | Course and supply | Surgical relevance |
|---|---|---|
| Superficial (SCIAs) | Runs toward the anterior superior iliac spine (ASIS) in the subcutaneous fat, above the deep fascia | Usual pedicle for skin and lymphatic-bearing SCIP flaps; perforators from the inferolateral branches enter the dermal plexus and allow superthin flaps without defatting<sup>[[13]](#ref13)[[17]](#ref17)</sup> |
| Deep (SCIAd) | Runs beneath the deep fascia and gives branches to the sartorius and the iliac bone | Allows chimeric designs; the deep branch perfused the cephalad sartorius (up to 8 cm from the ASIS) and the superficial iliac bone (up to 1.5 cm from the crest) in cadavers<sup>[[6]](#ref6)</sup> |
| Transverse (SCIAt) | Lies deep to the fascia caudal to the ASIS, a mean of 25.5 mm from it (range 5–50 mm) | Landmark for finding the deep branch<sup>[[15]](#ref15)</sup> |

Landmarks come from anatomical and imaging series and vary between patients:

- **Bifurcation.** The superficial and deep branches separated within 2 cm of a fixed point (6 cm from the pubic tubercle toward the ASIS, then 3 cm caudal) in 85% of 20 cadaveric groin regions.<sup>[[6]](#ref6)</sup>
- **Exit point.** In 91 CT angiograms, the superficial branch passed through Hesselbach's fascia within a 21 mm radius circle centered 18 mm medial and 17 mm distal to the ASIS in 90% of patients.<sup>[[14]](#ref14)</sup>
- **Axiality.** In a prospective ultrasound series, 91.9% of pedicles followed an axial pattern extending cephalad beyond the ASIS.<sup>[[13]](#ref13)</sup>
- **Perforators.** In 14 cadaveric flaps, major perforators supplying large perforasomes (mean 178.6 cm² superficial, 156.2 cm² deep) were always present, with numerous variations in size and location.<sup>[[7]](#ref7)</sup>
- **Veins.** The superficial circumflex iliac vein with accompanying venae comitantes was seen in 22 of 30 cadaveric specimens. In a 64-patient clinical series there were two total flap losses and one minor hematoma, with no venous congestion.<sup>[[16]](#ref16)</sup>

Cadaver landmarks are orientation aids, not fixed safe-dissection coordinates for an individual patient.<sup>[[6]](#ref6)[[7]](#ref7)</sup> In a 12-patient study, color-coded duplex mapping matched the number and emergence points of the branches found at surgery in every patient. It did not validate every anatomical measurement or guarantee flap survival.<sup>[[8]](#ref8)</sup>

## Operative Principles

The following summarizes the authors' SCIP-LIFT version; it is not a universal protocol for all SCIP or lymph-node-transfer operations.<sup>[[2]](#ref2)</sup>

### 1. Excise disease while protecting genital structures

Resect the involved skin and subcutaneous tissue according to the mapped extent and reconstructive plan. During penile skin resection, preserve Buck's fascia and the underlying dorsal neurovascular structures. Identify and protect the urethra, testes, and spermatic cords, and avoid cord twisting during reconstruction. The authors describe orchidopexy; its use depends on the anatomy and reconstruction. Preserve uninvolved tissue where useful rather than treating “radical” as a requirement to sacrifice healthy structures.<sup>[[2]](#ref2)</sup>

### 2. Raise the planned lymphatic-bearing flap

Maintain perfusion to the skin and the deep-fat component containing mapped lymphatic collectors. Preserve the inguinal nodes around the pedicle and avoid injuring donor-leg drainage. Additional deep fat can extend the collector-bearing component's reach. Aggressive thinning of this component would conflict with the intended LIFT design; do not substitute a superthin coverage flap and assume the same lymphatic content.<sup>[[2]](#ref2)</sup>

The proximal-to-distal technique of Yoshimatsu and Yamamoto begins by identifying the SCIA pedicle and its branches, then designs the flap from the anatomy found rather than starting from a skin perforator. In 27 patients, mostly with extremity defects, this allowed chimeric components (iliac bone in 4, sartorius in 3, vascularized lymph node in 2, deep fascia in 1, intercostal nerve in 1); all but three flaps survived completely.<sup>[[11]](#ref11)</sup> A quadruple-component case report combined sartorius, deep fascia, an inguinal lymph node and skin/fat.<sup>[[12]](#ref12)</sup>

| Component | Vascular source | Use in 3R or related work |
|---|---|---|
| Skin and fat paddle | Superficial or deep-branch perforators | Scrotal reconstruction<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup> |
| Lymphatic collectors and perinodal fat | Along the SCIA system | Lymphatic component of SCIP-LIFT<sup>[[2]](#ref2)</sup> |
| Second thin skin paddle (pure skin perforator) | Separate perforator from the same SCIA system | Penile skin in 3 of 7 patients<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup> |
| Sartorius muscle | Deep branch | Dead-space filling in chimeric designs elsewhere<sup>[[6]](#ref6)[[11]](#ref11)</sup> |
| Deep fascia | Deep branch | Structural support in chimeric designs elsewhere<sup>[[11]](#ref11)</sup> |
| Iliac bone | Deep branch | Described for extremity and head and neck defects, not for genital reconstruction<sup>[[6]](#ref6)[[11]](#ref11)</sup> |

### 3. Transfer and orient the flap

Bring the pedicled flap through an adequately sized suprapubic tunnel or the planned route without constricting, twisting, or stretching its pedicle. Orient the collector-bearing fat so that distal or lateral lymphatic ends contact the affected deep subcutaneous tissue and drainage is directed toward the preserved groin pathway. Recheck perfusion and freedom from compression after positioning and closure. The published mechanism relies on subsequent lymphatic reconnection, rather than direct lymphatic suturing.<sup>[[2]](#ref2)</sup>

### 4. Reconstruct scrotal and penile coverage

The original series used a full-thickness SCIP lymphatic flap for the scrotum in all seven patients and an additional pure-skin-perforator flap for penile coverage in three. The later technical description also allows preservation of uninvolved prepuce or selected graft coverage for a residual defect. Choose the reconstruction for pliability, adequate coverage and perfusion; penile disease still requires assessment even when its resurfacing component does not carry the main lymphatic reconstruction.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[2]](#ref2)</sup>

### 5. Close and monitor

Close the donor site without compromising its tissues or the transferred pedicle. Monitor flap perfusion, wound healing, hematoma or seroma, infection, genital swelling and donor-leg symptoms. A pedicled design avoids a free vascular anastomosis, but still requires expertise in perforator and lymphatic surgery; it is not a low-risk substitute for specialist care.<sup>[[2]](#ref2)[[16]](#ref16)</sup>

## Outcomes and Their Limits

| Report | Population and findings | What it does not establish |
|---|---|---|
| Yamamoto et al., 2022 | Retrospective seven-patient series: four scrotal and three penoscrotal cases. No postoperative genital complication or genital recurrence was reported at mean 22.7 months. GLS improved from 6.7 to 0.3; no postoperative compression was applied | A zero rate for every possible donor/systemic event, lifelong freedom from recurrence, or superiority of the lymphatic component over excision and coverage alone |
| Abdelfattah et al., 2023 | Separate 26-man series: 15 scrotal and 11 penoscrotal cases; mean follow-up 44.9 months. All flaps survived; GLS changed from 6.2 to 0.05, cellulitis decreased, and all 26 reported some quality-of-life improvement (median GBI +41) | An identical operative design to the original 3R, zero complications or recurrence, or a comparative proof of lymphatic restoration |

These are uncontrolled series in which improvement followed combined excision and reconstruction.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[4]](#ref4)</sup> Their results are not pooled into a single success percentage, a mean follow-up is not a minimum for every patient, and the authors' description of the operation as curative is not an established treatment effect.

Historical genital-surgery reviews contain heterogeneous case series with different disease severity and techniques. Their pooled complication proportions cannot rank 3R against conventional flaps, grafting, excision, LVA, or VLNT. In the 20 studies (151 patients, 1980–2016) of one such review, complication rates were 10% after resection with primary closure or grafting (46.4% of procedures), 54.2% after resection with flap reconstruction (39.1%) and 9% after lymphovenous anastomosis (14.5%). Excisional groups tended to have later-stage disease, so the 54.2% figure is not a matched comparator for SCIP-LIFT.<sup>[[9]](#ref9)</sup>

## Compression, Complications and Follow-up

The absence of compression in the initial protocol is a study observation. It does not show that compression is uniquely unnecessary after 3R or that it must be lifelong after every alternative. Genital garments can be difficult to fit; individualize postoperative support and longer-term lymphedema treatment to wound or flap status, symptoms, concomitant leg disease and specialist assessment.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[5]](#ref5)</sup>

Discuss wound breakdown, infection, fluid collection, partial or total flap loss, persistent or recurrent edema, donor scarring and possible donor lymphatic injury. Preserving nodes and collectors reduces avoidable injury but cannot justify a promise of zero donor-leg risk. Follow both recipient and donor regions and document urinary and sexual function, cellulitis, symptoms, further procedures and any ongoing compression needs.<sup>[[2]](#ref2)[[5]](#ref5)[[16]](#ref16)</sup>

## Flap Reliability and Donor-Site Data From Other Indications

These series come from non-genital or mixed indications and inform flap reliability and donor-site behavior, not lymphatic outcomes.

- **Perineoscrotal defects.** Three pedicled SCIP flaps for defects after Fournier gangrene debridement all survived without significant complications. The authors describe the flap as thin, pliable and of consistent anatomy, and the largest defect covered measured 22 × 10 cm.<sup>[[18]](#ref18)</sup>
- **Transverse-branch (SCIP-t) free flaps.** In 57 patients, skin area ranged from 35 to 355 cm² (mean 129 cm²) and pedicle length from 8 to 17 cm (mean 11.9 cm). Twelve flaps carried two skin paddles, all donor sites closed primarily, and two flaps (3.5%) had partial necrosis managed conservatively.<sup>[[20]](#ref20)</sup>
- **Propeller flaps.** In 72 propeller SCIP flaps for abdominopelvic and thigh-root defects, the mean rotation was 163° (range 130–180°). Two flaps necrosed after venous congestion, and the authors report no donor-site complications while taking care to protect the lateral femoral cutaneous nerve.<sup>[[19]](#ref19)</sup>
- **Head and neck.** Twenty-one of 22 flaps (95.5%) succeeded at mean 5.3 months of follow-up, and two patients (9%) had minor donor-site complications managed conservatively.<sup>[[21]](#ref21)</sup>

## LYST in Extremity Lymphedema

Lymphatic system transfer adds a portion of the afferent lymphatic vessels to the transferred nodes, with the aim of stimulating lymphangiogenesis.<sup>[[3]](#ref3)</sup> Reported experience is in limb lymphedema, not genital disease.

- **SCIP-LYST.** In 8 patients with unilateral leg lymphedema followed a mean of 39 months (24–60), the excess volume percentage improved by a mean of 11.2% and cellulitis decreased.<sup>[[3]](#ref3)</sup>
- **Pedicled SCIP-LYST.** Six patients with lymphedema and chronic venous disease, five with additional distal lymphovenous bypass, had a mean 15-point fall in the Lymphedema Life Impact Scale and a mean 30-unit fall in L-Dex at a mean of 31 weeks, with no immediate complications. The authors suggest that including long afferent vessels may reduce the lymphangiogenesis required. Follow-up is short.<sup>[[22]](#ref22)</sup>

## Distinguish Prophylactic Groin Reconstruction

Caretto et al. studied 31 women undergoing bilateral inguinofemoral dissection for vulvar cancer with unilateral lymphatic SCIP reconstruction chosen for the side requiring flap coverage. Mean leg-volume increase was smaller on the reconstructed side (479 versus 683 mL) at mean 30-month follow-up. This was a nonrandomized, side-comparison study with unequal reconstructive indications. It supports further study of lymphatic-preserving groin reconstruction; it does not establish routine prophylactic SCIP transfer during prostate or bladder pelvic lymphadenectomy or prove treatment of male genital elephantiasis.<sup>[[10]](#ref10)</sup>
