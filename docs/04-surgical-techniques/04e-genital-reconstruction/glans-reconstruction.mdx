# Glans Reconstruction / Glansplasty

Glans reconstruction is an umbrella term for restoring contour and coverage after glans-preserving oncologic surgery, distal injury, or creation of a neoglans during phalloplasty. The indication determines the priority: cancer surgery requires oncologic staging and local control first; trauma and gender-affirming reconstruction require a separate, patient-specific reconstructive assessment.

For penile squamous cell carcinoma, the current EAU guideline includes wide local excision, partial glansectomy, and total glansectomy with reconstruction among options for appropriately selected glanular or coronal disease. Organ preservation and a wider resection should be discussed in the context of tumour extent, local-recurrence risk, function, and patient priorities.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup>

---

## Oncologic Reconstruction

| Clinical context | Evidence-bounded role | Important limit |
|---|---|---|
| Extensive or recurrent PeIN | Glans resurfacing removes glandular epithelium and reconstructs the surface with a graft; it also provides a specimen for histopathology.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup> | Suitability depends on clinical and pathological assessment, including cancer staging and margin planning. |
| Invasive disease confined to the glans | When local excision or resurfacing is unsuitable, glansectomy with or without distal corporal resection and neoglans reconstruction may be considered.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup> | Published glansectomy evidence is principally non-comparative and heterogeneous. |
| More extensive disease | More radical surgery may be necessary when a safe organ-preserving resection is not feasible.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup> | Reconstruction should not compromise definitive oncologic treatment. |

The guideline notes that a split-thickness skin graft is commonly used for neoglans reconstruction after glansectomy. A systematic review of 20 studies reported wide ranges for local recurrence (2.6–16.7%), graft loss (1.5–23.5%) and meatal stenosis (2.8–14.3%), with normal erections in 50–100% and good cosmesis in 95–100%; the studies were mostly small single-centre series, so the ranges do not predict an individual result.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[2]](#ref2)</sup>

## Reconstructive Planning

Planning should establish the underlying diagnosis, defect depth and urethral involvement, oncologic requirements where applicable, tissue quality, vascular risk factors, and the person’s urinary, sexual, and appearance goals. In cancer care, multidisciplinary assessment and scheduled surveillance are part of treatment; local recurrence risk should be discussed before choosing organ-sparing surgery.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup>

The literature does not establish a single graft, flap, or contouring method as best across these distinct indications. Detailed operative choices belong in the indication-specific procedure page and should be made by the treating reconstructive and, when relevant, oncologic team.

## Outcomes and Follow-up

Outcome reporting should remain indication-specific and include cancer control where relevant, graft healing, meatal/urethral complications, urinary function, erectile and sensory outcomes, appearance, patient-reported quality of life, and revision surgery. Existing studies use inconsistent definitions and follow-up, so pooled figures should not be treated as a guarantee.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[2]](#ref2)</sup>
