# Anterior Obturator Artery Perforator (aOAP) Flap — Vulvovestibular Reconstruction

The **aOAP flap** is a fasciocutaneous perforator flap developed by Dan Mon O'Dey (Aachen, Luisenhospital, Germany) for vulvovestibular reconstruction. First described in 2010 from cadaveric vascular anatomy for vulvar reconstruction after oncologic resection, the aOAP flap has subsequently been reported for reconstruction after female genital mutilation/cutting (FGM/C) and for selected women with refractory lichen sclerosus et atrophicus (LSA) and sexual dysfunction.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[2]](#ref2)[[3]](#ref3)</sup>

For the broader treatment menu see the [Vulvar Reconstruction Atlas](/docs/surgical-techniques/04e-genital-reconstruction/vulvar). The aOAP shares its vascular territory with the genitocrural island perforator flap (GCIPF) used for oncologic reconstruction; see [GCIPF (Vulvar)](/docs/surgical-techniques/04e-genital-reconstruction/vulvar-gcipf). For the FGM/C complement technique see [Foldès Clitoral Reconstruction](/docs/surgical-techniques/04e-genital-reconstruction/foldes-clitoral-reconstruction); for the alternative coverage technique see [Mañero Vaginal Mucosal Graft](/docs/surgical-techniques/04e-genital-reconstruction/manero-vaginal-graft).

## Vascular Anatomy

The original anatomical study (O'Dey, Bozkurt and Pallua, 2010) examined 20 specimens from 10 female cadavers.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup>

| Parameter | Detail |
|---|---|
| **Source vessel** | Anterior branch of the **obturator artery** (and accompanying vein), exits the pelvis through the obturator foramen |
| **Perforator type** | 80% musculocutaneous (pierces the gracilis muscle); 20% septocutaneous (passes along the posterior border of the gracilis)<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup> |
| **Perforator location** | 1.3 ± 0.3 cm lateral to the inferior pubic ramus |
| **Skin territory** | About 7 × 15 cm, centered on the sulcus genitofemoralis (genitocrural fold) |
| **Tissue characteristics** | Thin, pliable genitocrural skin may match adjacent vulvar tissue, but assess hair distribution and subcutaneous thickness before transfer; hair-bearing tissue at the vestibule or introitus may need epilation.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[17]](#ref17)</sup> |

Jin et al. identified four constant perineal perforator arteries with rich vascular anastomoses in the deep fascia above the adductor compartment, which is consistent with this anatomy.<sup>[[4]](#ref4)</sup>

## Key Advantages

| Advantage | Detail |
|---|---|
| **Thin and pliable tissue** | Thin, pliable genitocrural tissue can cover selected vulvar or vestibular defects; evidence is insufficient to determine whether aOAP improves sexual function or cosmesis versus gracilis or VRAM flaps.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[2]](#ref2)</sup> |
| **Proximity to defect** | The genitocrural donor site is adjacent to the vulva and permits tunneled-island transfer, but reach and rotation depend on the pedicle and defect; one oncologic case required 90° rotation plus advancement.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[19]](#ref19)</sup> |
| **Concealed donor site** | Scar hidden within the natural genitocrural fold; preserves self-image<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup> |
| **Primary donor closure** | Donor site closed primarily in most cases<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup> |
| **Anatomy in the reported sample** | aOAP perforator was identified in all 20 cadaveric specimens; clinical presence and course still require patient-specific assessment<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup> |

## Surgical Technique

1. **Preoperative planning.** Use the reported cadaveric landmark (about 1.3 cm lateral to the inferior pubic ramus) as a starting point, then map the individual perforator with handheld Doppler along the genitocrural sulcus. The flap is designed as an elliptical island centered on the confirmed perforator.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup>
2. **Flap dimensions.** The 2010 cadaveric study mapped a skin territory of about 7 × 15 cm; tailor each paddle to the defect and Doppler-mapped perforator. Bilateral flaps were used in 53/61 patients in the LS series.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[3]](#ref3)</sup>
3. **Flap elevation.** The flap is raised as a fasciocutaneous island. Dissection from periphery toward the perforator. If musculocutaneous (80%), a small cuff of gracilis muscle is included around the pedicle to protect it; if septocutaneous (20%), perforator dissected along the intermuscular septum.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup>
4. **Tunneled transfer.** The flap is transferred to the vulvar or vestibular defect through a subcutaneous tunnel, preserving the pedicle without skeletonization. This tunneled-island design distinguishes the aOAP from simple transposition flaps.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup>
5. **Inset and closure.** The flap is inset with fine absorbable sutures; donor site closed primarily.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup>

## Clinical Application 1 — FGM/C Reconstruction (O'Dey 2024, n = 119)<sup>[[2]](#ref2)</sup>

In O'Dey's 2014–2021 FGM/C cohort of 119 women with all types of FGM/C, the aOAP flap was used in 36% of patients (approximately 43 women) for vulvovestibular reconstruction, including the labia minora, vestibule, and/or vaginal introitus.<sup>[[2]](#ref2)</sup>

### Three-technique reconstructive system

| Technique | Purpose | Frequency |
|---|---|---|
| **Omega Domed (OD) Flap** | Preputial (clitoral hood) reconstruction | 85%<sup>[[2]](#ref2)</sup> |
| **NMCS Procedure** (Neurotizing and Molding of Clitoral Stump) | Clitoral reconstruction | 82%<sup>[[2]](#ref2)</sup> |
| **aOAP Flap** | Vulvovestibular reconstruction (labia minora, vestibule) | 36%<sup>[[2]](#ref2)</sup> |

### Outcomes (combined techniques; 119 enrolled, 94.1% attended one-year follow-up)<sup>[[2]](#ref2)</sup>

These one-year, uncontrolled results belong to the entire reconstructive program, and some patients did not attend follow-up. Because only 36% received aOAP, improvements cannot be attributed to this flap alone.<sup>[[2]](#ref2)</sup>

- Significant postoperative improvement in clitoral sensation and ability to achieve orgasm (reported p &lt; 0.001).
- Significant reduction in dysmenorrhea, dysuria, and dyspareunia (reported p &lt; 0.001).

## Clinical Application 2 — Skinning Vulvectomy + aOAP Flap for Refractory LSA

O'Dey 2024 (n = 61, 2014–2022) describes the most extensive surgical option in the LS ladder, explicitly framed as last-resort.<sup>[[3]](#ref3)</sup> See the [perineoplasty + de-adhesion page](/docs/surgical-techniques/04e-genital-reconstruction/perineoplasty-de-adhesion) for the rest of the LS surgical ladder.

### Indications and patient selection

- Severe diffuse vulvovestibular LS with extensive architectural distortion, beyond what perineoplasty or simple adhesiolysis can address.<sup>[[3]](#ref3)</sup>
- Disabling dyspareunia or complete apareunia refractory to maximal medical therapy (ultrapotent TCS, topical calcineurin inhibitors, intralesional steroids).<sup>[[3]](#ref3)[[6]](#ref6)</sup>
- Consider aOAP reconstruction for extensive LS-related scarring with functional impairment when a larger vulvovestibular repair is needed; prior failure of each simpler operation is not required. The 2026 German S3 guideline weakly suggests de-adhesion, synechiolysis, perineoplasty or anatomic vulvoplasty after guideline-concordant topical corticosteroids, without prescribing an operative sequence.<sup>[[3]](#ref3)[[5]](#ref5)[[18]](#ref18)</sup>
- Stated patient desire to regain sexual function.
- Confirm the LS diagnosis and evaluate for VIN / SCC before reconstruction. Biopsy is indicated for an uncertain diagnosis, treatment failure, or a suspicious lesion, rather than mandatory for every clinically typical LS case; excised tissue should undergo histopathology.<sup>[[14]](#ref14)[[8]](#ref8)</sup>

### Operative steps

The procedure is performed under general anesthesia in three components:<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[3]](#ref3)[[2]](#ref2)</sup>

1. **Skinning vulvectomy.** Excise all affected vulvovestibular skin en bloc in a superficial plane while preserving subcutaneous fat and deeper structures.<sup>[[9]](#ref9)</sup> Describe the extent of excision separately from its depth: deep vulvectomy extends to the perineal membrane, whereas skinning does not.<sup>[[15]](#ref15)</sup> Send all excised tissue for histopathology.<sup>[[8]](#ref8)</sup>
2. **Bilateral aOAP flap harvest and inset.** Perforator localized by handheld Doppler at the genitocrural sulcus; fasciocutaneous island flap raised on each side; tunneled island transfer into the vulvar defect for tension-free inset. Bilateral flaps used in 53/61 (87%) of the O'Dey LS series.<sup>[[3]](#ref3)</sup>
3. **Adjunctive procedures as indicated.**
    - **Omega Domed (OD) flap.** Add preputial or clitoral-hood reconstruction when indicated in the LS operation; the 85% use rate and combined-program sexual outcomes come from the separate FGM/C cohort.<sup>[[3]](#ref3)[[2]](#ref2)</sup>
    - **Clitoral re-exposure.** Sharp unroofing of the buried glans from scarred preputium.
    - **Scar surgery** for additional fibrotic tissue not addressed by the vulvectomy.

### Outcomes (O'Dey 2024)

| Parameter | Result |
|---|---|
| n | 61 |
| Bilateral aOAP flaps | 53/61 (87%)<sup>[[3]](#ref3)</sup> |
| Period | 2014–2022 |
| Follow-up | 1 yr |
| Dyspareunia reduction | Significant (p &lt; 0.001) |
| Inability to have intercourse | Significant reduction (p &lt; 0.001) |
| Complications | Several minor, reversible; subset required secondary intervention |

### LSA-specific complication framing

The LS authors proposed the following contributors to minor wound complications; their uncontrolled series does not quantify an excess risk versus non-LS reconstruction:<sup>[[3]](#ref3)[[10]](#ref10)</sup>

- **Chronic topical corticosteroid exposure** was discussed as a possible contributor to tissue fragility; this is not a reason to abandon appropriate anti-inflammatory LS treatment.
- **Altered tissue quality** intrinsic to LS biology, complicating flap inset.

The FGM/C report recorded one major flap loss and secondary interventions within its combined reconstructive program; these events are not an aOAP-specific complication rate. The separate 61-patient LS series reported minor, reversible complications and some secondary interventions. These distinct populations are not pooled into a single flap-safety denominator.<sup>[[2]](#ref2)[[3]](#ref3)</sup>

### LSA recurrence — the durability caveat

The chronic relapsing biology of LS is unmodified by surgery. Historical vulvectomy series for LS reported recurrence rates as high as 50%. In Rangatchew's 38-patient local-flap series, clinical follow-up reached 78% at a mean 8.4 years, and the authors reported 38% severe LS relapse with apareunia; the 1-year O'Dey aOAP follow-up cannot assess relapse over that interval.<sup>[[5]](#ref5)[[8]](#ref8)</sup> Indefinite topical maintenance and SCC surveillance are part of long-term care.

## Perioperative Management (LS-Specific)

- **Preoperative:** Control LS with topical corticosteroids; assess clinically, biopsy uncertain or suspicious areas and treatment failures, evaluate for VIN or SCC, and discuss chronic relapsing disease and realistic expectations.<sup>[[14]](#ref14)[[8]](#ref8)</sup> The 2024 EuroGuiDerm guideline describes surgery and perioperative care for selected symptomatic women.<sup>[[16]](#ref16)</sup> For persistent functional introital stenosis despite topical corticosteroids, the 2026 German S3 guideline weakly suggests de-adhesion, synechiolysis, perineoplasty or anatomic vulvoplasty (100% consensus; consensus-based), without specifically endorsing an aOAP flap.<sup>[[18]](#ref18)</sup> It strongly recommends agreement on topical corticosteroid care before and after surgery and interdisciplinary counseling, including specialized pelvic floor physiotherapy and sex therapy.<sup>[[18]](#ref18)</sup>
- **Intraoperative:** localize the perforator with handheld Doppler, obtain meticulous hemostasis, assess tissue quality during dissection and inset, and send all excised tissue for histopathology.<sup>[[3]](#ref3)</sup>
- **Postoperative**:
  - **Resume topical clobetasol.** In one patient of a 23-woman case series, postoperative discontinuation of dermatosteroid was followed by LS reactivation with bullae.<sup>[[10]](#ref10)</sup>
  - Perineal-zone wound care with attention to urinary / fecal contamination.
  - Vaginal dilators considered to maintain introital patency.
  - **Long-term follow-up:** examine initially about every 3–6 months and, once LS is stable, about every 12 months; assess treatment response, voiding and sexual function, adherence, and precancerous or cancerous change.<sup>[[6]](#ref6)[[18]](#ref18)</sup>

## Skinning Vulvectomy — Historical Context

Skinning vulvectomy was originally developed for vulvar intraepithelial neoplasia (VIN), then adapted to LS.<sup>[[8]](#ref8)[[9]](#ref9)[[13]](#ref13)</sup>

- Removes involved vulvar skin in a superficial plane while preserving subcutaneous and deeper tissue; the extent may be partial or total. This is less extensive in depth than deep vulvectomy, but the cited uncontrolled series does not establish comparative morbidity.<sup>[[15]](#ref15)[[9]](#ref9)</sup>
- Historically combined with split-thickness skin grafting (STSG), with better sexual function and cosmetic results than simple vulvectomy without coverage.<sup>[[8]](#ref8)</sup>
- Abramov's 1996 review reported LS recurrence as high as 50% after skinning or simple vulvectomy; it limited vulvectomy to malignant transformation or histologic features indicating higher malignant risk and, outside that setting, reserved surgery for failure of multiple medical treatments.<sup>[[8]](#ref8)</sup>
- The O'Dey innovation is replacing the STSG with a vascularized fasciocutaneous flap, which provides vascularized coverage; comparative superiority in durability or sensation has not been established.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup>

## Comparison with Other Vulvar Reconstruction Flaps

| Flap | Type | Tissue quality | Bulk | Donor scar | Muscle sacrifice | Key advantage | Key limitation |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| **aOAP** | Fasciocutaneous perforator | Pliable genitocrural tissue; hair and thickness vary | Variable | Concealed (genitocrural fold) | None (or minimal gracilis cuff) | Adjacent local tissue | Limited clinical cohorts; an independent oncologic case exists<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[17]](#ref17)[[19]](#ref19)</sup> |
| **Lotus petal** | Perineal perforator-based fasciocutaneous | Local perineal tissue | Variable | Perineal fold | None | Tailored local perforator coverage | Reach depends on perforator position and defect<sup>[[20]](#ref20)</sup> |
| **Pudendal-thigh (Singapore)** | Neurovascular fasciocutaneous | Groin-crease skin | Variable | Groin crease | None | Sensate reconstruction in the original small series | Large flaps risk distal or apical ischemia<sup>[[21]](#ref21)[[22]](#ref22)</sup> |
| **PAP / DFAP** | Fasciocutaneous perforator | Moderate thickness | Low-moderate | Posterior medial thigh | None | Large skin paddle | Donor and recipient morbidity depend on defect and technique; cross-series percentages are not head-to-head evidence |
| **Gracilis** | Musculocutaneous | Moderate-thick | Moderate-high | Medial thigh | Gracilis muscle | Reliable; fills dead space | Excessive bulk; functional muscle loss |
| **VRAM** | Musculocutaneous | Thick | High | Abdominal wall | Rectus abdominis | Large volume; fills pelvic dead space | Abdominal-wall donor morbidity; not a direct comparator for small benign vestibular defects |

The Wendelspiess 2024 SR and meta-analysis of perforator versus non-perforator flaps for oncologic vulvoperineal reconstruction (49 studies, n = 1,840) found a tendency toward fewer complications with perforator flaps, though the difference did not reach statistical significance; long-term outcomes and QOL assessment were sparse. Its cancer-reconstruction population provides indirect context, not direct evidence for benign FGM/C or LS reconstruction.<sup>[[7]](#ref7)</sup>

## Limitations and Considerations

| Limitation | Detail |
|---|---|
| **Limited independent outcomes** | The reported FGM/C and LS cohorts come from O'Dey's center; an independent team published one oncologic aOAP case, while independent FGM/C or LS cohorts and multicenter outcomes have not been established.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[2]](#ref2)[[3]](#ref3)[[19]](#ref19)</sup> |
| **No comparative trials** | One case report compared aOAP with IPAP in the same patient; no controlled comparative trial establishes relative outcomes.<sup>[[19]](#ref19)</sup> |
| **Perforator variability** | aOAP present in 100% of cadaveric specimens, but 20% are septocutaneous rather than musculocutaneous, which affects elevation technique<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup> |
| **Learning curve** | Perforator-flap dissection requires microsurgical expertise and familiarity with obturator vascular anatomy |
| **LSA recurrence** | Chronic relapsing nature of LSA means long-term durability of reconstruction is uncertain<sup>[[5]](#ref5)</sup> |
| **Combined technique** | In FGM/C, aOAP was performed within a program that also used OD and NMCS, so the contribution of the aOAP to overall outcomes cannot be isolated<sup>[[2]](#ref2)</sup> |

---

## Key Takeaways

1. The aOAP flap is a fasciocutaneous perforator flap (anterior branch of the obturator artery) developed by O'Dey for vulvovestibular reconstruction.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup>
2. Two major clinical indications: FGM/C (vulvovestibular reconstruction in 36% of O'Dey's n = 119 cohort) and refractory LSA (87% of n = 61 received bilateral aOAP after skinning vulvectomy).<sup>[[2]](#ref2)[[3]](#ref3)</sup>
3. Vascular anatomy: perforator present in 100% of cadaveric specimens, located 1.3 ± 0.3 cm lateral to the inferior pubic ramus, supplying an approximately 7 × 15 cm skin territory centered on the sulcus genitofemoralis.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup>
4. Reported design advantages include pliable adjacent tissue, a concealed donor scar, tunneled-island transfer, and possible primary donor closure; assess individual hair distribution and thickness before flap selection.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[17]](#ref17)</sup>
5. FGM/C and LS outcome cohorts remain from O'Dey's center; an independent oncologic case and same-patient technical IPAP comparison exist, while multicenter and controlled comparative validation are lacking.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[2]](#ref2)[[3]](#ref3)[[19]](#ref19)</sup>
6. The aOAP vascular territory is adjacent to or overlaps the [GCIPF](/docs/surgical-techniques/04e-genital-reconstruction/vulvar-gcipf) territory used for oncologic vulvar reconstruction; each named design and pedicle requires separate review of its original description.
