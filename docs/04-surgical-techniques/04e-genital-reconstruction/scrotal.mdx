# Scrotal Reconstruction

Choose a technique from the cause, viable tissue, defect depth, closure tension and patient goals. This atlas links the individual procedures; the [scrotal reconstruction overview](/docs/surgical-techniques/04e-genital-reconstruction/scrotal-reconstruction) explains the evidence and operative choices in more detail.

## Decision Framework

### Assess the wound and the patient

- Establish whether infection, trauma, lymphedema, cancer treatment, congenital anatomy or elective symptoms are driving the reconstruction.
- Assess testicular and cord viability, available skin, the actual vascularity of the recipient bed, dead space, and any penile, urethral or perineal injury.
- Include wound-healing risks, mobility, fertility goals, comfort, appearance and the patient's preference about staged procedures or implants.

Scrotal redundancy often permits primary closure. A systematic review of Fournier reconstruction (16 case series, 425 men) proposed primary closure, secondary healing or a scrotal advancement flap for defects of less than 50% of the scrotum, and a graft or flap for larger defects or defects that extend beyond the scrotum. The EAU Urological Infections guideline (2026) summarizes this as level 3 evidence.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[11]](#ref11)</sup> The 50% figure is a guide, not a validated cutoff. A small scarred defect may need a graft or flap, and a larger defect may have enough healthy tissue for closure. The 2022 systematic review of scrotal reconstruction concluded that tension-free primary closure and local pedicled flaps usually suffice, and that split-thickness skin grafts (STSGs) or distant flaps are needed mainly for extensive skin loss.<sup>[[13]](#ref13)</sup> Healthy native dartos is vascularized tissue and is preserved where possible.

### Match the operation to the defect

| Finding | Options and limits |
|---|---|
| Healthy skin reaches without excessive tension | Primary closure or local scrotal advancement, preserving perfusion and comfortable testicular position |
| Superficial skin loss with a vascularized recipient bed | STSG may cover an extensive or total defect; exposed testes alone do not rule out grafting |
| Bed unsuitable for graft, deep dead space or combined perineal loss | Consider a regional flap with the necessary tissue components and a reliable pedicle |
| Immediate scrotal coverage unsuitable | Temporary or definitive thigh pouches may be considered after counseling; they are not required for all total defects |
| Fibrotic or fatty genital lymphedema | Tailored excision and coverage, with continuing conservative care; physiological lymphatic procedures require separate selection |
| Absent testis with an acceptable healed or reconstructable envelope | Offer prosthesis counseling; immediate or delayed placement depends on context and patient preference |

The choice should protect viability and comfort without unnecessary septal division, cord dissection or testicular repositioning. Apposition, orchidopexy and expansion techniques are selected operations, not required steps of every closure.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[3]](#ref3)[[4]](#ref4)[[5]](#ref5)</sup>

### Evidence that affects counseling

- **Graft versus flap.** No reliable human comparative evidence establishes a fertility advantage. A systematic review found no conclusive evidence that flap coverage of exposed testes is better than a skin graft.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup> In a rat experiment, grafts gave a lower germinal epithelium height and Johnsen score than flaps. That experiment cannot rank human reconstruction, and donor and recipient morbidity matter.<sup>[[6]](#ref6)</sup>
- **Flap outcomes.** The 2026 Alammar systematic review of Fournier reconstruction included 619 patients and 625 flaps, with flap loss reported in 1.6%. This does not mean a 1.6% overall complication rate and does not compare flaps with grafts.<sup>[[7]](#ref7)</sup>
- **Lymphedema.** Advanced excision and selected physiological procedures can improve symptoms, but uncontrolled series do not prove cure or that a lymphatic flap prevents recurrence. Continue individualized skin care and decongestive management, and discuss donor-site risks.<sup>[[8]](#ref8)[[9]](#ref9)</sup>
- **Implants.** Discuss size, position, firmness, complications and the option to decline. A manufacturer's retrospective device-survival estimate is not a guarantee of satisfaction or lifelong durability.<sup>[[10]](#ref10)[[5]](#ref5)</sup>

### Fournier's gangrene

Debridement, resuscitation and broad-spectrum antibiotics come first. The EAU guideline gives strong recommendations to start broad-spectrum antibiotics on presentation and to begin repeated debridement within 24 hours. A 2024 JAMA Surgery review advises debridement as soon as possible, ideally within 6 hours, and repeat debridement every 12 to 24 hours until no necrotic tissue remains.<sup>[[11]](#ref11)[[12]](#ref12)</sup> Urinary and fecal diversion are decided case by case. Reconstruction follows once no further debridement is planned and the patient is medically stable.<sup>[[3]](#ref3)</sup>

In the Hayon technique, the testes are brought together with the inferior penis after initial debridement using interrupted absorbable sutures, redundant cord is coiled under the abdominal wall, and a meshed STSG is placed once debridement is complete.<sup>[[3]](#ref3)</sup> NPWT can be considered for selected wound management or graft fixation after adequate debridement. The EAU guideline found no evidence of benefit for NPWT in Fournier's gangrene (level 4) and gives a weak recommendation to use adjuncts only in clinical trials. NPWT does not replace source control.<sup>[[11]](#ref11)</sup>

### Reported series

These are mostly small uncontrolled series. Figures are as reported in the abstracts, and none establishes superiority of one technique.

| Series | Design | Reported result |
|---|---|---|
| Hayon 2021<sup>[[3]](#ref3)</sup> | 10 patients after Fournier debridement; orchidopexy, then STSG (0.018 inch, meshed 2:1) under a bolster sutured for 5 to 7 days | Median follow-up 8 months. No chronic pain, discomfort or further intervention. Median stay after graft 9 days |
| Alwaal 2015<sup>[[2]](#ref2)</sup> | 54 adults with genital STSG (13 tissue loss, 13 lymphedema, 12 buried penis, others) | Graft take above 90% in 52 of 54 |
| Sahai 2021<sup>[[14]](#ref14)</sup> | Single-surgeon island groin flap series: 25 Fournier, 4 avulsion | Defects over 50% covered by one groin flap. Total loss covered by a groin flap plus two superior medial pedicle thigh flaps. Results reported as satisfactory, without complication denominators in the abstract |
| Mopuri 2016<sup>[[15]](#ref15)</sup> | 5 patients, modified pudendal thigh flap | Technique and vascular basis described |
| Lee 2012<sup>[[16]](#ref16)</sup> | Retrospective series of 7 (6 Fournier, 1 extramammary Paget disease); gracilis plus internal pudendal artery perforator flap | All flaps survived. Mean defect 10.1 by 9.3 cm |
| Okwudili 2016<sup>[[4]](#ref4)</sup> | 12 men with more than 50% scrotal skin loss; temporary anteromedial thigh pouches | No mortality. Residual scrotum expanded over 3 to 8 months to accept the testes, with normal testicular volume. Mean follow-up 14.8 months |
| Staniorski 2023<sup>[[20]](#ref20)</sup> | 20 patients; thigh pouches with fasciocutaneous perineal closure | Perineal closure in all, with STSG needed to complete closure in 3. Complications in 3 (15%). Median follow-up 9 months. One patient had thigh-pouch pain and none requested scrotoplasty |
| Wisenbaugh 2018<sup>[[17]](#ref17)</sup> | 11 patients with massive localized scrotal lymphedema (mean resected tissue 21 kg) | One graft, the rest closed primarily. Wound complications were common. Mean weight change +5.2 kg. Quality of life 1.3 to 7.7 of 10 at mean 26 months |
| Abdelfattah 2023<sup>[[19]](#ref19)</sup> | 26 men with advanced genital lymphedema; excision with SCIP lymphatic flap | Flap survival 100%. Cellulitis rates fell. Mean follow-up 44.9 months. Uncontrolled |
| Singh 2016<sup>[[18]](#ref18)</sup> | 40 gracilis perineal reconstructions after abdominoperineal resection or exenteration (not scrotal) | Donor-site complications 12.5%, recipient-site 40%. Obesity (OR 7.5) and active smoking (OR 9.3) were associated with minor complications |

Most patients in the Wisenbaugh series gained weight after surgery, so a weight-management plan is paired with excision of massive localized lymphedema.<sup>[[17]](#ref17)</sup>

### Concurrent procedures

Scrotal reconstruction is often combined with other operations. Orchidopexy secures exposed testes before graft or flap cover. Penile shaft reconstruction is common when Fournier's gangrene, lymphedema or buried penis involves the penoscrotal unit (see [penile reconstruction](/docs/surgical-techniques/04e-genital-reconstruction/penile)). Perineal defects can be closed with fasciocutaneous flaps. A testicular prosthesis can be placed at orchiectomy or in a staged procedure.

### Follow-up

Assess wound healing, collections, threatened perfusion, pain, testicular position and accessibility, sexual function and recurrence of the underlying disease. Treat significant necrosis, bleeding or dehiscence according to severity. Graft fixation and mobilization schedules depend on the reconstruction. The Hayon protocol used a bolster for 5 to 7 days; neither prolonged bed rest nor a particular dressing duration is universal.<sup>[[3]](#ref3)</sup>
