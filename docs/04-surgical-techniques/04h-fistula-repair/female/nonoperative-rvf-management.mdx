# Nonoperative Management of Rectovaginal Fistula

**Nonoperative management of rectovaginal fistula (RVF)** consists of observation with local care, symptom control, drainage, treatment of the underlying disease and, in selected patients, fecal diversion. It can precede or accompany repair. Seton placement and fecal diversion are procedural or surgical adjuncts and not medical treatments. Their purpose may be drainage or symptom relief rather than definitive fistula closure.

See [Rectovaginal Fistula](/docs/clinical-conditions/03f-fistulas/in-females/rectovaginal), [ERAF ± Sphincteroplasty](/docs/surgical-techniques/04h-fistula-repair/female/eraf) and [Fecal Diversion](/docs/surgical-techniques/04h-fistula-repair/fecal-diversion) for evaluation and operative options.

## Conservative care and reassessment

ASCRS 2022 supports initial nonoperative care for most obstetric RVFs and selected benign, minimally symptomatic fistulas (2C). A period of 3–6 months is typical in suitable patients. The reported 52–66% healing comes from selected observational evidence in obstetric fistulas and is not a universal prognosis. Symptoms and tissue recovery determine when to reassess, and no waiting period is mandatory.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup>

| Measure | Purpose |
|---|---|
| Sitz baths | Perineal hygiene and comfort |
| Local wound care | Gentle cleansing; keep the perineum clean and dry |
| Debridement | Removal of necrotic tissue at the fistula site when needed |
| Antibiotics | Treatment of clinical infection (cellulitis, abscess, purulent drainage); not routine |
| Stool-bulking fiber and softeners | Formed soft stools reduce mechanical trauma to the tract and avoid straining |

The best candidates are those with a small, low obstetric RVF, an acute or early presentation, minimal symptoms, no sphincter defect and no active Crohn's disease or radiation injury. This candidate profile is conventional teaching that extends the guideline's indication, and no series tests it directly.

A small opening can coexist with a deeper infected cavity. Worsening pain, fever or suspected abscess requires prompt assessment and source control; observation is not a reason to postpone drainage. Suspected malignancy requires diagnosis and an oncologic plan before choosing a benign fistula repair.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[2]](#ref2)</sup>

## Drainage and setons

A loose draining seton may reduce or prevent infection in a narrow tract, a small vaginal opening or multiple tracts, and can provide longer-term symptom relief when repair is unsuitable. It is not required for every RVF and may leave ongoing drainage.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup>

Multidisciplinary examination under anesthesia can clarify the tract, associated defects and rectal disease. A published technical demonstration describes curettage of the tract followed by seton placement. In the usual technique the tract is probed, and a vessel loop or silastic seton is passed from the rectal to the vaginal opening and tied loosely. The demonstration reports no comparative closure rate.<sup>[[3]](#ref3)</sup> A prior seton was associated with healing after later endorectal advancement flap in Sonoda's retrospective cohort (P = 0.025), and setons rarely close the fistula themselves.<sup>[[13]](#ref13)</sup>

A seton serves as a bridge to repair when it relieves edema, inflammation and infection, and as long-term symptom control when repair is unsuitable. It suits a narrow tract with a small vaginal opening, multiple tracts, or an inflammatory or neoplastic process that needs treatment before repair.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup>

## Crohn's disease: control sepsis and inflammation

Coordinate colorectal surgery, gastroenterology and urogynecology. Drain infection when present, assess rectal inflammation and strictures, and optimize medical treatment. Control of proctitis is important before elective closure; it is not a prerequisite for urgent abscess drainage. ECCO 2024 suggests medical treatment and consideration of surgical closure in selected anogenital or rectogenital fistulas; the specific supporting evidence is low level.<sup>[[4]](#ref4)</sup>

ACG 2025 notes that internal fistulas, including RVF, have limited trial evidence. Infliximab with or without an immunomodulator is commonly used before selected repair; the guideline does not require an immunomodulator for every patient. Drug selection, safety screening, dosing and monitoring belong in a gastroenterology-directed plan.<sup>[[2]](#ref2)</sup>

### Distinguish RVF evidence from perianal-fistula evidence

| Evidence | What it supports—and its limits |
|---|---|
| **ACCENT II main trial** | In 306 adults with draining abdominal or perianal fistulas, infliximab 5 mg/kg was given at weeks 0, 2 and 6, and responders were randomized to placebo or 5 mg/kg every 8 weeks. Median time to loss of response was more than 40 weeks with infliximab versus 14 weeks with placebo (P &lt; 0.001). At week 54, 36% versus 19% had no draining fistulas (P = 0.009). Most fistulas in the trial were perianal, so the result is not RVF-specific.<sup>[[12]](#ref12)</sup> |
| **ACCENT II RVF post hoc analysis** | Twenty-five women had 27 draining RVFs at baseline. After infliximab induction, 13/29 evaluated fistulas were closed at week 14; the evolving denominators count fistulas, not women. Median closure duration was 46 weeks with maintenance infliximab versus 33 with placebo. This small subgroup supports a possible medical response, not guaranteed durable closure.<sup>[[5]](#ref5)</sup> |
| **AGA 2021, active perianal fistula** | Recommends infliximab; suggests adalimumab, ustekinumab or vedolizumab. In patients without a perianal abscess, it recommends a biologic plus an antibiotic over biologic alone and suggests against antibiotics alone. These recommendations are explicitly for perianal fistulas, and their strength does not transfer to RVF.<sup>[[6]](#ref6)</sup> |
| **Seton plus medical therapy** | Drainage and biologic treatment are complementary when sepsis is present. The often-quoted 46% versus 13% comparison in the ASCRS discussion is anti-TNF versus placebo from a perianal-fistula trial subgroup and is not a randomized estimate of the added benefit of a seton in RVF.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup> |

Antibiotics can treat infection and may be adjunctive to biologics. The ACG discussion of multiweek metronidazole or fluoroquinolone regimens for simple perianal fistulas should not become a default prescription for every RVF. An abscess generally needs drainage; repeated antibiotics alone do not correct the underlying anatomy.<sup>[[2]](#ref2)</sup>

## Fecal diversion: a separate decision

Diversion may help manage an anastomotic complication, substantial contamination, severe refractory Crohn's disease or distressing symptoms. It reduces fecal contamination of the tract as a bridge to repair, offers palliation for patients who are poor surgical candidates or decline repair, and can accompany a planned repair. In refractory Crohn's disease, proximal diversion may allow rectal or perianal healing. ASCRS lists diversion as the initial step for RVFs after colorectal anastomotic complications, but selected patients may instead require reoperation and reconstruction, so no single pathway applies.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[2]](#ref2)</sup>

In Obi's 2026 retrospective cohort, 158 women underwent 424 procedures: 100 were diverted and 58 were not. Five-year estimated cure was 72.7% versus 64.3% (P = .38). Patients with repeated repairs were more likely to be diverted, so these results do not prove that diversion improves cure or equalizes risk.<sup>[[7]](#ref7)</sup>

For radiation-associated RVF, diversion can relieve symptoms without closing the fistula. Selected restorative resections or permanent diversion require separate assessment of morbidity and function; see [Transabdominal RVF Repair](/docs/surgical-techniques/04h-fistula-repair/female/transabdominal-rvf-repair).

## Adjuncts with limited evidence

- **Fibrin glue and plugs:** ASCRS excludes them from its RVF repair recommendations because reported results are poor. A historical glue series healed 4/5 RVFs, but only 6/10 fistulas overall; this tiny mixed series does not establish reliable efficacy.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[8]](#ref8)</sup>
- **Vaginal estrogen:** one pessary-associated RVF closed after pessary removal plus estradiol, so the estrogen effect cannot be isolated. Treating symptomatic atrophy is a different goal from claiming that estrogen closes a fistula.<sup>[[9]](#ref9)</sup>
- **Experimental healing strategies:** a menopausal-rat vaginal-injury study found different epithelial and stromal responses to postoperative estrogen. It was not a human RVF trial and does not establish either clinical benefit or harm in this setting. Microbiome-directed fistula treatment remains investigational.<sup>[[10]](#ref10)[[11]](#ref11)</sup>

## Approach by etiology

| Etiology | First nonoperative approach | Consideration |
|---|---|---|
| Obstetric | Local care, fiber, antibiotics for infection; seton if abscess risk | Most likely to heal spontaneously (52–66% in the ASCRS-cited series) |
| Crohn's disease | Seton with medical therapy directed by gastroenterology | Rectal inflammation and stricture are assessed before elective closure |
| Anastomotic | Fecal diversion as the initial step | Persistent fistulas need reconstruction |
| Radiation | Fecal diversion with medical optimization | Poor tissue quality; resection or permanent diversion is assessed separately |
| Minimally symptomatic benign | Conservative wound care | Limited data; repair if the fistula persists |

## When to move to repair

Elective repair is considered when persistent drainage or functional burden outweighs continued conservative care, after the cause, infection, tissue quality and patient preferences are addressed. Common triggers are a fistula that persists after a 3–6-month trial, disabling stool or flatus per vagina, recurrent infection, an abscess that seton drainage does not control, and concern for malignancy. In Crohn's disease, repair follows control of rectal inflammation without anorectal stricture. Closure, continence, sexual function and the possibility of retaining a stoma are discussed separately.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[2]](#ref2)</sup>
