# Anal Sphincteroplasty

**Anal sphincteroplasty** is a surgical repair of a disrupted anal sphincter, most commonly performed for **fecal incontinence (FI) due to obstetric anal sphincter injury (OASIS)**. Within reconstructive practice, it is encountered in the fistula world in three settings: as a **simultaneous component of [ERAF + sphincteroplasty](/docs/surgical-techniques/04h-fistula-repair/female/eraf)** for low RVF with anterior sphincter defect, as the **sphincter-reconstruction step of [episioproctotomy](/docs/surgical-techniques/04h-fistula-repair/female/episioproctotomy)**, and as a **staged procedure for residual fecal incontinence** after fistula closure. Short-term improvement is reported in up to **85%** of patients, but the dominant long-term issue is **progressive deterioration**, with only **10–14% maintaining sustained improvement at 5 years**.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup>

For fecal-incontinence-pathway context (sacral neuromodulation, biofeedback, PTNS, bulking agents, slings), the broader incontinence procedures live under [Incontinence Procedures](/docs/surgical-techniques/incontinence).

---

## Indications

| Setting | Notes |
|---|---|
| **OASIS-related FI** | Primary indication — 3rd or 4th degree perineal tear, either acute primary repair at delivery or delayed (secondary) repair<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[2]](#ref2)</sup> |
| **Other traumatic sphincter injury** | Iatrogenic post-anorectal surgery, perineal trauma<sup>[[3]](#ref3)</sup> |
| **Component of RVF + sphincter-defect repair** | ERAF + sphincteroplasty (raises healing 41% → &gt; 80%); built-in to episioproctotomy<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup> |
| **Residual FI after fistula closure** | Staged sphincteroplasty 3–6 mo after fistula healing confirmed |

**Position in algorithm:** ACG recommends sphincteroplasty when FI persists despite adequate management of coexisting bowel disturbance, or when medical / non-invasive therapies and SNS have failed or are not recommended.<sup>[[3]](#ref3)</sup>

---

## Preoperative Evaluation

| Modality | Role |
|---|---|
| **Anorectal manometry** | Resting and squeeze pressures; interpret for age and sex<sup>[[3]](#ref3)</sup> |
| **Endoanal ultrasound** | Criterion standard for sphincter anatomy and defect identification (ASCRS conditional, very low certainty) — defect size does not necessarily correlate with FI severity<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup> |
| **MRI** | Better than EAUS for distinguishing EAS tear from scar and identifying external sphincter atrophy; consider when EAUS unavailable or normal in a symptomatic patient<sup>[[3]](#ref3)</sup> |
| **PNTML** | **Not** routinely recommended (ASCRS)<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup> |
| **Continence scoring** | Wexner / Cleveland Clinic Incontinence Score or St. Mark's score documented preoperatively for objective comparison<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup> |

## Surgical Technique

### Overlapping anterior sphincteroplasty (delayed / secondary repair)

The standard for established sphincter injuries.

![Overlapping anal sphincteroplasty: the retracted external-sphincter ends are mobilized and overlapped vest-over-pants with mattress sutures](/img/diagrams/sphincteroplasty.svg)
*Perineal view (anterior up). The disrupted **external anal sphincter** has an anterior defect with retracted, scarred ends around an intact internal sphincter. The ends are mobilized, the intervening **scar transected (not excised — it gives suture purchase)**, and the two ends **overlapped "vest-over-pants"** with two rows of horizontal mattress sutures placed through the fascial sheath, restoring a continuous ring with extra anterior bulk. Overlapping and end-to-end repairs give similar long-term continence, which tends to deteriorate over years. (Original WARWIKI schematic)*

1. **Position:** prone jackknife or lithotomy.
2. **Incision:** curvilinear perineal incision over the perineal body (posterior fourchette).<sup>[[4]](#ref4)[[5]](#ref5)</sup>
3. **Dissection:** scarred sphincter remnant dissected back to healthy muscle on either side through the ischiorectal fat. **Transect — do not excise — the scar**; use it as part of the overlapping repair to restore an intact muscle ring.<sup>[[5]](#ref5)</sup>
4. **Overlap:** one EAS end brought over the other and sutured with mattress sutures **through the fascial sheath** (not just muscle).<sup>[[5]](#ref5)[[6]](#ref6)</sup>
5. **IAS repair:** if identified, repair separately with 3-0 monofilament polydioxanone — not universally practiced.<sup>[[6]](#ref6)[[7]](#ref7)</sup>
6. **Levatorplasty:** anterior plication of levator ani lengthens the restored anal canal — done in 75% of patients in one large series; some surgeons avoid it for dyspareunia concern; objective benefit unproven.<sup>[[5]](#ref5)[[8]](#ref8)</sup>
7. **Closure:** perineal-body reconstruction and skin closure.
8. **No diverting stoma:** RCT data show no benefit and increased morbidity from diversion.<sup>[[5]](#ref5)</sup>

**Suture material:** 3-0 polyglactin, 3-0 polydioxanone, or 2-0 polyglactin (expert opinion).<sup>[[6]](#ref6)</sup>

### End-to-end repair (acute / primary repair)

Direct approximation and suturing of torn EAS ends — typically used at the time of delivery for acute OASIS. **Overlap repair should not be used for grade 3a and partial-thickness 3b injuries**, as it requires full-thickness disruption with 1–1.5 cm of torn muscle on either end.<sup>[[6]](#ref6)</sup>

### End-to-end vs overlap

Meta-analysis of 6 RCTs (n = 588 women, grade 3c or greater laceration): **no differences** at 12 months in perineal pain, dyspareunia, or flatal incontinence.<sup>[[6]](#ref6)</sup> ACOG: similar outcomes up to 36 mo postoperatively.<sup>[[2]](#ref2)</sup>

## Outcomes

| Timeframe | Continence | Key findings |
|---|---|---|
| **Short-term (≤ 12 mo)** | Up to **85%** improvement | 60–88% achieve excellent / good outcome<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[2]](#ref2)[[3]](#ref3)</sup> |
| **Medium-term (3–5 yr)** | ~50% | Continence averages 50% at 40–60 mo; 28% continent at 40 mo in one study<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[4]](#ref4)</sup> |
| **Long-term (5–10 yr)** | **10–14%** sustained improvement | Half of women report FI symptoms at 5–10 yr<sup>[[2]](#ref2)[[5]](#ref5)</sup> |
| **&gt; 10 yr** | Further deterioration | At median 18 yr, only 45% reported better outcome; scores return near baseline<sup>[[2]](#ref2)</sup> |

Despite objective deterioration, **74%** of patients remain satisfied at long-term follow-up.<sup>[[2]](#ref2)</sup> Cerdán Santacruz 2022 — single-center multimodal approach (sphincteroplasty + selective levatorplasty / IAS repair / biofeedback / PTNS / SNS): **93.3% good or excellent satisfaction at median 10 yr**.<sup>[[8]](#ref8)</sup>

## Complications

Overall surgical complication rate **5–27%**:<sup>[[2]](#ref2)</sup>

- **Wound infection** — most common, **6–35%**<sup>[[2]](#ref2)[[9]](#ref9)</sup>
- Fecal impaction
- Wound hematoma
- UTI
- Dyspareunia
- Defecatory dysfunction (incomplete evacuation, straining, manual disimpaction)<sup>[[2]](#ref2)</sup>

Jain 2023 audit (n = 239 OASIS): structural failure on EAUS in **41.8%**; only 20% of structural failures were symptomatic at mean 23 mo. No modifiable variable (location, operator hierarchy, repair type, suture material) significantly reduced secondary-failure risk.<sup>[[11]](#ref11)</sup>

---

## Prognostic Factors

Technical and patient factors are **not clearly established**:<sup>[[9]](#ref9)</sup>

- Age, sex, extent of sphincter injury
- Etiology and duration of FI
- Pudendal neuropathy
- Surgical technique (end-to-end vs overlap)

Some signals:

- **Pudendal neuropathy** — patients may not improve<sup>[[10]](#ref10)</sup>
- **&gt; 10 years of symptoms before repair** — worse outcomes<sup>[[10]](#ref10)</sup>
- The multifactorial nature of post-OASIS FI (mechanical disruption + neuropathy + pelvic-floor dysfunction + altered rectal sensation) likely explains the high long-term failure rate<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup>

---

## Sphincteroplasty vs Sacral Neuromodulation (SNM)

The role of sphincteroplasty has been increasingly challenged:

- **ASCRS 2023** recommends **SNM as a first-line surgical option** for incontinent patients with or without sphincter defects (conditional, low certainty).<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup>
- Emile 2025 meta-analysis (n = 779): SNM yields **better continence improvement than sphincteroplasty** (OR 1.68, P = 0.006) with similar complication rates.<sup>[[12]](#ref12)</sup>
- Rodrigues 2017 (matched 13 vs 13): SNM significantly reduced Wexner (15.9 → 8.4, P = 0.003) while sphincteroplasty did not (16.9 → 12.9, P = 0.078).<sup>[[13]](#ref13)</sup>
- SNM is effective even with sphincter defects up to **120°**.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup>
- US sphincteroplasty volume **decreased 7-fold** between 2009–2015, reflecting the shift to SNM.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup>
- Less SNM benefit when there is prior sphincteroplasty, EAS defect &gt; 120°, or low resting anal pressure.<sup>[[12]](#ref12)</sup>

## Repeat Sphincteroplasty

ASCRS 2023 — **repeat sphincteroplasty after a failed overlapping sphincteroplasty should generally be avoided** (conditional, very low certainty):<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup>

- Retrospective n = 56 repeat sphincteroplasty: mean Wexner 16.5 → 11.9 (P &lt; 0.001 short-term). At 74 mo: only **28.6%** reported a good result; 21.4% required further procedures; 5.4% required colostomy.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup>
- Consider only when a specific failure cause is identified (e.g., recurrent sphincter injury from repeat vaginal delivery).<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup>
- Confirm muscle wrap on follow-up US; persisting defects can undergo repeat repair after 6–12 mo. Biofeedback is effective salvage for suboptimal result.<sup>[[5]](#ref5)</sup>

## Management Algorithm (FI with Sphincter Defect)

1. **Conservative** — diet, fiber, antidiarrheals (first-line)<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup>
2. **Biofeedback**<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup>
3. **Sphincteroplasty** — best for **recent / acute** sphincter injury or postpartum FI recognized shortly after delivery<sup>[[3]](#ref3)[[9]](#ref9)</sup>
4. **Sacral neuromodulation** — first-line surgical option for FI with or without sphincter defects, particularly delayed presentation or failed sphincteroplasty<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup>
5. **Other** — PTNS, injectable bulking agents (not routinely recommended), or **colostomy** as last resort<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup>

---

## Place in the RVF Repair Atlas

- **Concurrent with [ERAF + sphincteroplasty](/docs/surgical-techniques/04h-fistula-repair/female/eraf)** for low / mid RVF with anterior sphincter defect — raises healing from 41% to &gt; 80%.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup>
- **Built into [Episioproctotomy](/docs/surgical-techniques/04h-fistula-repair/female/episioproctotomy)** as Step 7 — overlapping reconstruction of the anterior sphincter after intentional division and layered RVF repair.
- **Staged after fistula closure** — defer 3–6 months for residual FI; counsel re: long-term deterioration and the SNM alternative.

---

## Key Takeaways

- Good short-term results, **progressive deterioration** over time
- Best for **recent sphincter injury** with early symptom onset (especially postpartum)
- For patients **decades after obstetric trauma**, **SNM may be more appropriate first-line** surgical option
- A **multimodal approach** (sphincteroplasty + biofeedback ± neuromodulation) optimizes long-term outcomes
- **Repeat sphincteroplasty should generally be avoided** unless a specific correctable failure cause is identified
