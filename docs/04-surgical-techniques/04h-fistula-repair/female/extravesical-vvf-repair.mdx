# Extravesical Transabdominal VVF Repair

**Extravesical repair** approaches a vesicovaginal fistula (VVF) through the vesicovaginal space, avoiding a separate access cystotomy or bladder bivalving. Separating the fistula still creates bladder and vaginal defects that require repair. It is an alternative to [O'Conor transvesical exposure](/docs/surgical-techniques/04h-fistula-repair/female/oconor-vvf-repair), not a proven superior operation.<sup>[[3]](#ref3)[[5]](#ref5)</sup>

Miklos and colleagues described their laparoscopic variant in 1999; later reports detail the technique and a 44-patient series. Other extravesical descriptions preceded that report, so 1999 should not be treated as the first use of the approach.<sup>[[2]](#ref2)[[3]](#ref3)</sup>

## Choosing the Approach

Extravesical abdominal exposure can be useful for a high cuff fistula, difficult vaginal access, or selected recurrent fistulas. Assess the ureters, bladder capacity, tissue quality, malignancy or radiation history and previous surgery. A high location alone does not exclude vaginal repair by an experienced fistula surgeon.<sup>[[3]](#ref3)[[5]](#ref5)[[6]](#ref6)[[9]](#ref9)</sup>

| Consideration | Implication for planning |
|---|---|
| Accessible high fistula with separable bladder/vaginal planes | Extravesical repair can avoid an additional access cystotomy. |
| Dense scarring or uncertain ureteral relationships | Transvesical exposure may make identification and dissection safer. |
| Trigonal location or associated ureteral injury | Define anatomy carefully; this does not create an absolute extravesical prohibition. Cystotomy or ureteral reconstruction may be required. |
| Need for reimplantation, augmentation or another abdominal procedure | Plan the combined reconstruction and exposure. These procedures remain possible during an abdominal operation, although a strictly no-access-cystotomy plan may need modification. |

These anatomical considerations do not guarantee comparative outcomes. The O'Conor series and ERUS consensus support choosing exposure that permits safe mobilization and closure; neither establishes that extravesical repair always loses less tissue, takes less time or shortens hospitalization.<sup>[[5]](#ref5)[[8]](#ref8)</sup>

## Operative Framework

The following describes the published extravesical principles and selected technique variants. Port geometry, dissection extent, sutures and drainage are adapted to the anatomy.

### Positioning and ports

Dorsal lithotomy allows simultaneous abdominal and vaginal access. Laparoscopic series used four or five ports; Miklos placed a 10-mm umbilical port by open technique and three further ports under direct vision. Robotic repair uses standard robotic port placement with an assistant port. Adhesiolysis is often needed after prior hysterectomy.<sup>[[3]](#ref3)[[5]](#ref5)[[10]](#ref10)[[11]](#ref11)</sup>

### Identify the fistula and protect the ureters

Perform cystoscopy and establish the number and location of openings. A guidewire, small catheter or ureteral stent passed through the fistula can mark it; Miklos left a stent in the fistula to identify it during dissection. A vaginal sizer, sponge stick or end-to-end anastomosis (EEA) sizer supports the vaginal cuff and gives a firm backstop for the dissection between bladder and vagina. It should not be forced through the fistula. The ERUS consensus panel recommends preoperative marking of the fistula with a guidewire or ureteral catheter and a protective ureteral JJ stent.<sup>[[3]](#ref3)[[5]](#ref5)</sup>

### Separate the bladder and vagina

Develop the vesicovaginal space with atraumatic exposure, using sharp dissection with blunt dissection as needed. Identify the marked tract, work around the fistula until the bladder and vagina are separated, and preserve enough vascularized tissue for independent, tension-free closure. Retrograde bladder filling with dye can help confirm the tract. The Miklos technique excised the tract and extended dissection approximately 1–2 cm distally, and other series excised the tract with sharp dissection and trimmed the bladder edge. Lecoanet's robotic series excised the tract in every case. A fixed margin is not a rule, and viable bladder or vaginal tissue is not sacrificed to achieve one.<sup>[[3]](#ref3)[[12]](#ref12)[[14]](#ref14)</sup>

### Close the defects and test the repair

Miklos described one vaginal layer using 2-0 polyglactin and two bladder layers using 3-0 polyglactin, with interrupted figure-of-eight sutures and testing after each bladder layer. The bladder was filled retrograde with 300–400 mL of dye-tinted fluid; the volume in practice accounts for bladder capacity and avoids overdistension. The goal is a watertight, tension-free repair.<sup>[[3]](#ref3)</sup>

Abdel-Karim's series closed the bladder longitudinally and the vagina transversely, with omentum interposed between them. Orienting the closures perpendicular to each other keeps the suture lines from overlapping where the defects allow. The 2015 review associated documented leak testing with success; this is an association, and testing also permits immediate recognition of a leak.<sup>[[4]](#ref4)[[11]](#ref11)</sup>

### Decide on interposition

Vascularized omentum or peritoneum can separate the closures, particularly when tissue quality is compromised. The choice depends on the available tissue, previous operations and repair complexity. Miklos reported 42/43 closures without omentum in a mostly primary series, and the EAU robotic consensus regards interposition as seemingly beneficial. Omental harvest adds operative time. Neither the small series without a flap nor those with one establish a universal rule.<sup>[[3]](#ref3)[[5]](#ref5)</sup>

Evans' retrospective abdominal series reported 10/10 benign fistulas healed with interposition versus 12/19 without. The often repeated 12/12 figure combines benign and malignant flap cases. Selection and the small groups limit causal interpretation.<sup>[[16]](#ref16)</sup>

Giusti used a collagen and fibrin sealant patch (TachoSil) in 16 early post-hysterectomy repairs; Yang reported a peritoneal flap in 15 selected patients. These reports do not demonstrate equivalence of a sealant patch to living vascularized tissue or prove that either adjunct reduces morbidity.<sup>[[10]](#ref10)[[13]](#ref13)</sup>

## Outcomes and Their Limits

| Study | Actual population | Findings and interpretation |
|---|---|---|
| **Miklos 2015** | 44 laparoscopic VVFs, including 11 recurrent | Overall closure 43/44 at mean 17.3 months. The abstract reports 42/43 closures without omentum. The manuscript has inconsistent secondary denominators and perioperative values, so precise blood-loss/stay comparisons are not reproduced here. “15-year experience” describes the accrual period, not 15-year patient follow-up.<sup>[[3]](#ref3)</sup> |
| **Abdel-Karim 2011** | 15 supratrigonal VVFs; malignant, radiation-related and recurrent fistulas excluded | All healed at mean 18.9 months after repair with omentum; no control group.<sup>[[11]](#ref11)</sup> |
| **Giusti 2018** | 16 early post-hysterectomy repairs with TachoSil | All reported continence at one month and good quality of life at three months; no high-grade complications reported. This short retrospective series does not justify early repair irrespective of inflammation or oncologic status.<sup>[[10]](#ref10)</sup> |
| **Lecoanet 2023** | 22 robotic repairs: 9 extravesical, 13 transvesical | No clinical recurrence at median 15 months. Four postoperative complications occurred, including one major; 20/22 had interposition. Zero recurrence in both small groups does not prove equivalence.<sup>[[12]](#ref12)</sup> |
| **Miklos systematic review 2015** | 44 reports, predominantly case reports/series; laparoscopic and robotic repairs | Reported closure 98.04% extravesical versus 95.89% transvesical; no significant difference. Heterogeneity prevented a formal statistical meta-analysis. No randomized equivalence inference is warranted.<sup>[[4]](#ref4)</sup> |
| **Tavares synthesis 2026** | 14 studies, 206 robotic repairs: 169 transvesical and 37 extravesical | Descriptive overall complication and recurrence rates were 4.37% and 2.91%, with major complications 0.97%. Exploratory comparisons found no significant difference in operative time, complications or recurrence. Blood loss, stay and catheter duration were not statistically compared. Small retrospective groups and variable follow-up limit conclusions.<sup>[[7]](#ref7)</sup> |

Tavares performed a descriptive synthesis rather than a formal meta-analysis. Its two comparative studies had moderate and serious risk of bias. The reported absence of recurrence over varying follow-up periods is not a uniform primary-closure endpoint, and its rates should not be used to rank robotic repair against unrelated transvaginal series.<sup>[[7]](#ref7)</sup>

Wang's 2026 retrospective cohort compared 78 open (31), laparoscopic (31) and robotic (16) transabdominal repairs and did not compare extravesical with transvesical exposure. Closure was assessed at 3 months. Post-hysterectomy fistulas had the highest success (95%), while radiation-induced fistulas (P = 0.031) and trigonal fistulas (32% failure versus 6% non-trigonal, P = 0.004) were associated with poorer outcomes. Approach was not an independent predictor of closure, and minimally invasive repair was associated with shorter operative time (P = 0.014) and less blood loss (P &lt; 0.001) than open repair. These associations inform counseling and are not an absolute contraindication to extravesical repair.<sup>[[15]](#ref15)</sup>

The 2026 Cochrane publication is a review protocol; it contains no completed comparative treatment results.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup>

Series differ in patient selection, interposition and definitions, so the figures are descriptive and not comparative.

## Selected Variants

- **Single-site laparoscopy:** Abdel-Karim reported five patients with an additional suturing port, omental interposition and urethral drainage for three weeks; all were continent without recurrence at mean eight months. This is feasibility evidence for skilled laparoscopic surgeons.<sup>[[14]](#ref14)</sup>
- **Omental interposition demonstration:** Watts reports one uncomplicated robotic case with a negative two-week cystogram and no reported incontinence at three months; one case cannot estimate a success rate.<sup>[[9]](#ref9)</sup>
- **Peritoneal flap:** Yang's 15-patient series included seven prior repair failures and reported closure without complications at mean 7.8 months. The cohort included both supratrigonal and trigonal anatomy.<sup>[[13]](#ref13)</sup>
- **Oncologic treatment interval:** Dayan-Schwartz's video case was repaired during a treatment-free interval after conservative treatment failed. It does not demonstrate that operating during active chemotherapy or immunotherapy is routinely safe.<sup>[[17]](#ref17)</sup>

## Drainage, Follow-up and Complications

Maintain unobstructed bladder drainage and specify who will assess healing before removal. Miklos used suprapubic drainage for 2–3 weeks with cystoscopic, filling and vaginal assessment; ERUS describes urethral drainage for 10–14 days and cystography before removal. These are published protocols and are not interchangeable mandatory schedules. Stent removal depends on its indication, especially if ureteral reconstruction was performed.<sup>[[3]](#ref3)[[5]](#ref5)</sup>

Miklos reported no major complications and no conversion to laparotomy in 44 laparoscopic repairs, and Tavares reported a 4.37% overall complication rate across robotic repairs.<sup>[[3]](#ref3)[[7]](#ref7)</sup> Monitor for recurrent leakage, catheter obstruction, infection, bleeding, ureteral injury or obstruction, bowel or rectal injury during deep dissection, inadvertent cystotomy (repaired primarily), ileus after omental harvest and new urinary symptoms. A stent assists identification but does not eliminate ureteral injury. Counsel separately about anatomical closure, continence and sexual recovery. Activity and intercourse restrictions follow healing, and no universal cystoscopy or imaging schedule is supported.<sup>[[5]](#ref5)[[7]](#ref7)[[12]](#ref12)</sup>
