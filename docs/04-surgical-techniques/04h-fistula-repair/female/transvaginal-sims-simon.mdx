# Transvaginal Sims-Simon Multilayered Closure

The Sims-Simon multilayered closure separates bladder and vaginal tissues for independent closure. It aims to preserve vaginal anatomy without partial colpocleisis, but final length and sexual function depend on tissue loss, scarring and the reconstruction. A frequently cited 47/47 success report involved different nonradiogenic GU fistula types and not 47 isolated VVFs.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup>

For the broader VVF clinical algorithm, see the [vesicovaginal fistula clinical page](/docs/clinical-conditions/03f-fistulas/in-females/vesicovaginal). For other transvaginal options, see [Conservative VVF Management](/docs/surgical-techniques/04h-fistula-repair/female/conservative-vvf-management), [Endoscopic VVF Management](/docs/surgical-techniques/04h-fistula-repair/female/endoscopic-vvf-repair), and [Latzko Repair](/docs/surgical-techniques/04h-fistula-repair/female/transvaginal-latzko).

---

## Historical Origins

The name reflects historical contributions by Sims and Simon to vaginal fistula repair, tissue preparation and closure. Modern modifications are not a single standardized operation. Sims developed techniques through repeated operations on enslaved Black women; that history belongs alongside technical attribution rather than a simplified account of surgical progress (see the [History of Prolapse Surgery & Urogynecology](/docs/roots/history/prolapse-urogynecology#james-marion-sims-and-vesicovaginal-fistula-18131883)).<sup>[[2]](#ref2)[[3]](#ref3)[[4]](#ref4)[[5]](#ref5)</sup>

---

## Fundamental Principle

Unlike the Latzko (imbrication of denuded tissue over an intact tract), the Sims-Simon is built on anatomic tissue-plane separation and independent layered closure:<sup>[[6]](#ref6)[[1]](#ref1)[[7]](#ref7)</sup>

- **Wide mobilization** of the bladder off the vaginal wall creates distinct planes.
- **Freshening or excision** of the fistula edges is traditional and debated.
- **Separate closure** of the bladder wall (one or two layers) and the vaginal wall produces independent layers.
- **Preservation of vaginal anatomy** is a goal. No colpocleisis is planned, but scarring or shortening can still occur.

## Indications

Consider separate-layer repair when vaginal access and available tissue permit:<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[8]](#ref8)[[9]](#ref9)[[10]](#ref10)</sup>

- **Any transvaginally accessible VVF.** Mid-vaginal, trigonal, or apical (not limited to cuff fistulae).
- **Fistulae where vaginal length matters.** Especially in sexually active patients.
- **Non-irradiated, benign fistulae.** Highest success.
- **Selected obstetric fistulae**, with reconstruction tailored to urethral involvement, tissue loss and scarring.
- **Fistulae with surrounding scar.** Mobilize enough viable tissue for closure while avoiding unnecessary excision.

---

### Poor fit for a vaginal approach

A fixed high vault fistula that cannot be exposed adequately from below needs another route. Suspected ureteric involvement may require upper-tract evaluation and a repair plan that includes ureteral reconstruction.<sup>[[18]](#ref18)</sup>

---

## Surgical Technique

The classic and modified technique:<sup>[[6]](#ref6)[[1]](#ref1)[[7]](#ref7)[[9]](#ref9)</sup>

### 1. Positioning and exposure

- **Dorsal lithotomy** (or exaggerated lithotomy). Original Sims left-lateral-decubitus with right knee drawn up is now rare but available for difficult posterior-wall exposure.
- **Sims speculum** or weighted posterior retractor + lateral retractors.
- **Cystoscopy** to assess fistula size and location relative to the ureteral orifices; consider protective ureteral stents when an orifice is close to the planned closure. If a concomitant ureterovaginal fistula is suspected, obtain contrast-enhanced CT with a late excretory phase and/or MRI to define ureteric integrity before choosing the repair route (EAU Weak recommendation).<sup>[[18]](#ref18)</sup>
- Probe / small catheter through the fistula to confirm location.
- When adequate exposure cannot be obtained and an abdominal approach is not available, a relaxing incision at the 5 or 7 o'clock position of the vaginal introitus (or both) has been described to improve visualization; this can increase postoperative pain, which should be discussed with the patient beforehand.<sup>[[23]](#ref23)</sup>
- A pediatric Foley catheter (6 or 8 Fr) placed through the fistula tract, directly or over a guidewire, with its balloon inflated for gentle traction has been described to bring a deep or apical fistula into view; gentle dilation, a ureteral stent, or a Fogarty catheter are described alternatives when the tract is too small for the Foley.<sup>[[24]](#ref24)</sup>

### 2. Hydrodissection / vasoconstriction

- Hydrodissection may help identify the subepithelial plane. Any vasoconstrictor requires an agreed drug, concentration, total dose and anesthesia protocol; an unspecified dilution is not a dosing instruction.
- Hydrodissection elevates vaginal epithelium from the underlying pubocervical fascia; provides hemostasis.

### 3. Circumscribing incision of the vaginal epithelium

- **Circumferential incision** in vaginal epithelium 1–2 cm from the fistula margin.
- Some surgeons use a U-shaped or inverted-U flap depending on fistula location and preference.

### 4. Wide mobilization (the defining step)

Distinguishes Sims-Simon from Latzko:<sup>[[6]](#ref6)[[1]](#ref1)</sup>

- Sharply dissect vaginal epithelium off the underlying pubovesical (endopelvic) fascia and bladder wall, creating wide flaps.
- Extend dissection only as needed for viable, mobile tissue and a tension-free repair; a fixed circumferential distance is not required for every defect.
- Enter the vesicovaginal space and mobilize the bladder wall off the vaginal wall circumferentially.
- Identify and protect nearby ureters during dissection and closure, particularly for trigonal defects.
- When mobilization is difficult, additional circumferential vaginal incisions at a distance from the fistula have been described as a bailout to gain tissue excursion for a low-tension closure; after hemostasis these incisions are left open to heal by secondary intention.<sup>[[20]](#ref20)</sup>

### 5. Fistula-edge management — the "to trim or not to trim" debate

- Edge preparation should remove nonviable tissue without sacrificing tissue needed for reconstruction.
- **Pro-trim arguments:** removes epithelialized non-healing tissue; creates fresh wound edges; removes fibrotic / scarred tissue.
- **Anti-trim arguments:** enlarges the defect; risks injury to adjacent structures; may not be necessary if mobilization achieves tension-free closure. Shaker randomized 64 women with obstetric VVF, using a Martius flap in both groups. Closure was not significantly different with versus without trimming, while recurrent defects tended to be larger after trimming. The obstetric VVF trial does not establish equivalence for other fistula types.<sup>[[11]](#ref11)</sup>
- **Current practice:** debated; varies by surgeon and institution.<sup>[[6]](#ref6)</sup> A 2020 EAU Robotic Urology Section consensus describes careful sharp edge dissection for robotic repair; it does not define a transvaginal excision requirement. The EAU female-LUTS guideline reports that one randomized obstetric-VVF trial found no difference in closure rates with versus without trimming.<sup>[[16]](#ref16)[[18]](#ref18)</sup> The reconstructive endpoint is mobile, vascularized, tension-free tissue rather than a predetermined amount of excision.

### 6. Layered bladder closure

After adequate mobilization:<sup>[[6]](#ref6)</sup>

- Close the bladder with appropriate absorbable suture using secure, tension-free bites. Knots may be placed extravesically.
- A second muscular or fascial layer may be used when tissue permits; avoid added tension or ureteral distortion.
- **Single full-thickness layer** is also acceptable.
- Confirm watertight closure by retrograde-filling the bladder with methylene blue or indigo carmine and inspecting the suture line.
- One described sequence places interrupted extramucosal bladder-edge sutures, then an imbricating muscular layer, tests the closure at that point with methylene blue or sterile milk while avoiding overdistention, and then attempts an additional interrupted pubocervical-fascial layer when usable tissue is available. This is a described three-layer sequence, not a required rule; the source reports no comparison of closure patterns.<sup>[[20]](#ref20)</sup>
- 3-0 polyglactin or 3-0 chromic are described examples of suitable absorbable suture. Permanent suture should not be used because of infection and stone risk; delayed absorption of an intravesical knot has been associated with calcification or infection while exposed to urine. Absorbable knot position varies by closure technique.<sup>[[21]](#ref21)</sup>

### 7. Interposition layer (optional)

- Terminology overlaps: separate fascial closure is also described in a report labeled modified Latzko. Do not use that report alone to define every Sims-Simon modification.<sup>[[12]](#ref12)</sup>
- A [Martius flap](/docs/foundations/surgical-principles/flaps/martius) can be added for recurrent or irradiated fistulae.
- Mörgeli & Tunn used no flaps in their selected series. This supports feasibility, not a rule that interposition is unnecessary in every non-irradiated repair.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup>

### 8. Vaginal-epithelial closure

- Preserve viable vaginal tissue; tailor edges only as needed for closure.
- Close with interrupted or running 2-0 delayed absorbable.
- Offset adjacent closure lines where feasible; this is a reconstructive principle rather than a quantified guarantee against recurrence.
- Vaginal pack as needed.

### 9. Catheter

- Provide reliable postoperative bladder drainage; catheter route, size and duration are individualized.<sup>[[6]](#ref6)[[9]](#ref9)</sup>

### Vaginal flap / flap-splitting variant

One described alternative combines several of the above steps into a single technique. The fistula tract is left in place rather than excised, which its authors say limits defect enlargement, dissection toward the ureter and bleeding edges. The defect is closed horizontally as a first layer starting about 0.5 cm from the defect margin, the perivesical fascia and detrusor are imbricated as a perpendicular second layer, tested by filling the catheter with 200 mL saline (methylene blue optional), and a U-shaped vaginal flap mobilized at least 2–3 cm beyond the repair is advanced over the closure as a separate final layer. A pediatric or Fogarty catheter placed through the tract aids identification and gentle traction. This is the chapter authors' preferred sequence, not a validated comparative technique or a universal minimum mobilization margin.<sup>[[22]](#ref22)</sup>

Mörgeli & Tunn included 34 VVF, 11 urethrovaginal, three ureterovaginal and two neobladder-vaginal fistula patients. Forty-seven received modified Sims-Simon repair and all closed; the 14% complication rate belongs to the overall series. The selected nonradiogenic series provides no general VVF success estimate or comparison of flap benefit.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup>

## Sims-Simon vs Latzko

| Feature | Sims-Simon Multilayered | Latzko Partial Colpocleisis |
|---|---|---|
| Principle | Anatomic tissue-plane separation + layered closure | Denuding + imbrication (partial colpocleisis) |
| Fistula tract excision | Traditionally yes (debated) | No |
| Vesicovaginal-space dissection | Wide mobilization required | Minimal |
| Closure layers | Bladder (1–2) + fascia + vaginal epithelium | Imbricated fibromuscular tissue + vaginal epithelium |
| Vaginal anatomy | No planned colpocleisis; scarring/shortening remain possible | Partial colpocleisis; discuss residual length and sexual goals |
| Applicable fistula locations | Any transvaginally accessible | Primarily apical / cuff |
| Ureteral protection | Required during dissection and closure | Required despite limited dissection |
| Comparative operative time | Case mix and technique differ | No matched evidence establishes a universal advantage |
| Interposition | Select by tissue, prior repair and complexity | Select by tissue, prior repair and complexity |
| Comparative efficacy | No high-quality head-to-head evidence establishes superiority | Closure and continence must be reported separately |

A 2024 systematic review of 46 studies of VVF, urethrovaginal and bladder neck–vaginal fistula repair found that high-quality evidence to guide technique is lacking. Route of surgery (vaginal or abdominal) was determined mainly by surgeon preference and showed no difference in repair success. Interpositional flaps, trimming of fistula edges, fibrin glue and fascial sling did not significantly improve fistula cure. Layered closure and intraoperative antibiotics seemed to improve success, although the data were insufficient.<sup>[[19]](#ref19)</sup>

The 2026 Cochrane publication is a protocol, which plans comparisons of surgical interventions including partial colpocleisis and multilayer closure; it contains no completed comparative results.<sup>[[6]](#ref6)</sup>

## Advantages

- The operation **aims to preserve vaginal anatomy** without planned colpocleisis.
- **Broad applicability.** It can be used at any vaginal fistula location, not just the apex.
- **Anatomic reconstruction.** It restores normal tissue planes between bladder and vagina.
- **Flap harvest** may be avoided in appropriately selected cases, reducing donor-site exposure.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup>
- **Short operating time.** The median was 40 min in the Mörgeli and Tunn series.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup>
- **High closure rate.** Mörgeli and Tunn reported 47/47 closures in selected nonradiogenic fistulas.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup>

---

## Disadvantages and Limitations

- **Mobilization near ureters** requires careful identification and protection; comparative injury rates versus Latzko are not established.
- **Technically demanding.** The operation requires comfort with deep pelvic dissection and layered closure in a confined space.
- **Learning curve.** The reported improvement after approximately 20 cases came from one surgeon’s retrospective experience; it is not a validated credentialing threshold or a guarantee of proficiency.<sup>[[15]](#ref15)</sup>
- **Risk of enlarging the defect.** Excising the fistula edges may make the defect larger and harder to close.

---

## Predictors and Limits of Generalization

Adinata's 2026 retrospective series of 80 women associated successful repair with early intervention (less than four weeks), smaller defects and minimal fibrosis. “First attempt” was not the third independent predictor reported in the abstract. Earlier intervention was associated with success in this retrospective series; assess tissue readiness individually.<sup>[[14]](#ref14)</sup>

A 1,185-repair multinational report included mixed urinary and rectovaginal fistulae, heterogeneous adjuncts and incomplete centralized follow-up. Its outcomes do not establish efficacy of a particular Sims-Simon modification, biologic adjunct or postrepair continence procedure.<sup>[[17]](#ref17)</sup>

---

## Postoperative Care

Maintain unobstructed bladder drainage and assess persistent leakage before catheter removal. Duration and the choice of dye testing or imaging depend on repair complexity and tissue quality; use the [source-specific guidance](/docs/surgical-techniques/04h-fistula-repair/fistula-repair-principles#diversion-and-stenting), rather than requiring 14–21 days for every repair. Routine follow-up cystoscopy at a fixed interval is not established for all uncomplicated repairs.<sup>[[18]](#ref18)</sup>

Treat troublesome bladder spasms after checking catheter function, discuss activity and intercourse as healing permits, and document continence, emptying and sexual symptoms separately from anatomical closure. For obstetric VVF repair, the 2026 IUGA/ICS proposal’s consensus dye-test statement advises testing at catheter removal; its consensus on residual incontinence calls for separate evaluation including postvoid residual and a cough test.<sup>[[25]](#ref25)</sup>

No Sims-Simon-specific VTE recommendation exists; this is vaginal reconstructive surgery, so apply the general risk framework (age, obesity, prior VTE, malignancy, prolonged operation or immobility) on the [VTE Prophylaxis](/docs/foundations/pharmacology/perioperative-eras/vte-prophylaxis) page rather than a fixed regimen.
