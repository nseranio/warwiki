# Principles of Fistula Repair

Genitourinary fistula repair succeeds or fails on a small number of principles that transcend the individual tract or technique. Each principle exists because the operation that ignored it failed. The same framework — sepsis control, optimization, anatomic delineation, and tension-free, watertight, multilayered closure with vascularized interposition when indicated — applies whether the fistula is urinary, enteric, or vascular-urinary.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[2]](#ref2)[[3]](#ref3)[[4]](#ref4)</sup>

---

## Why Fistulae Persist

Understanding why fistulae fail to close spontaneously is the foundation of all management. Four classical mechanisms prevent spontaneous healing:<sup>[[5]](#ref5)</sup>

1. **Distal obstruction** maintaining flow through the tract
2. **Foreign body or calculus** physically blocking closure (suture material, ureteral stent, mesh)
3. **Granulomatous tissue or malignancy** in the tract (TB, Crohn's disease, cancer)
4. **Epithelialization** of the tract — the fistula develops its own lining and becomes self-sustaining

A fifth, increasingly recognized in pelvic reconstruction, is **radiation-induced tissue ischemia** — irradiated tissue has poor vascularity and impaired healing capacity.<sup>[[6]](#ref6)[[7]](#ref7)</sup>

The mnemonic **FRIEND** captures the same ideas for enterocutaneous fistulae and applies equally to urinary fistulae: **F**oreign body, **R**adiation, **I**nfection / inflammation, **E**pithelialization, **N**eoplasm, **D**istal obstruction.<sup>[[3]](#ref3)[[8]](#ref8)</sup>

## Phased Management

### Phase 1 — Recognition, resuscitation, and sepsis control (days 0–10)

Sepsis is the leading cause of death in fistula patients, accounting for **~68%** of fistula-related mortality.<sup>[[9]](#ref9)</sup>

- **Fluid and electrolyte resuscitation** — high-output fistulae (&gt; 500 mL/day) cause large losses of fluid, electrolytes, and protein.<sup>[[3]](#ref3)[[4]](#ref4)[[10]](#ref10)</sup>
- **Sepsis control** — the single most important determinant of outcome:
  - **CT-guided percutaneous drainage** of abscesses; undrained sepsis is the primary barrier to healing.<sup>[[11]](#ref11)</sup>
  - **Targeted broad-spectrum antibiotics**.
  - More than **90%** of fistulae that close spontaneously do so within **1 month of infection eradication**.<sup>[[9]](#ref9)</sup>
- **Controlled drainage** of fistula effluent to protect skin and quantify losses.<sup>[[11]](#ref11)</sup>
- **Skin protection** with barrier creams, ostomy appliances, and NPWT.<sup>[[11]](#ref11)[[12]](#ref12)</sup>
- **Urinary diversion** for urinary fistulae — Foley catheter, ureteral stent, or percutaneous nephrostomy to decompress the tract and divert urine away from the fistula.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[2]](#ref2)</sup>

### Phase 2 — Anatomic definition and optimization (days 10 onward)

- **Delineate the fistula anatomy** with CT, fistulography, MRI, cystoscopy / vaginoscopy / endoscopy as appropriate.<sup>[[2]](#ref2)</sup>
- **Optimize nutrition** — malnutrition is nearly universal and a major predictor of outcome:<sup>[[3]](#ref3)[[4]](#ref4)[[10]](#ref10)</sup>
  - Caloric needs **25–30 kcal/kg/day**; protein **1.5–2.0 g/kg/day**.<sup>[[3]](#ref3)</sup>
  - **Parenteral nutrition** for high-output fistulae when enteral feeding is not feasible.<sup>[[10]](#ref10)</sup>
  - **Enteral nutrition** preferred when possible to maintain mucosal integrity; can be delivered distal to the fistula (fistuloclysis).<sup>[[3]](#ref3)[[4]](#ref4)</sup>
  - Serum albumin &lt; 3.0 g/dL signals risk and an opportunity for optimization.<sup>[[13]](#ref13)</sup>
- **Reassess for spontaneous closure** — many fistulae close with conservative management alone if the FRIEND factors are absent.<sup>[[3]](#ref3)[[9]](#ref9)</sup>

### Phase 3 — Definitive management

If spontaneous closure does not occur within **4–6 weeks** of conservative management (enteric fistulae) or after an appropriate trial of urinary diversion (urinary fistulae), definitive surgical repair is indicated.<sup>[[10]](#ref10)[[11]](#ref11)</sup>

## Factors Predicting Spontaneous Closure

| Factor | Favors closure | Against closure |
|---|---|---|
| **Output** | Low (&lt; 200 mL/day) | High (&gt; 500 mL/day)<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[2]](#ref2)</sup> |
| **Tract length** | Long (&gt; 2 cm) | Short (&lt; 2 cm)<sup>[[3]](#ref3)</sup> |
| **Defect size** | Small (&lt; 1 cm) | Large (&gt; 1 cm)<sup>[[3]](#ref3)</sup> |
| **Number** | Single | Multiple<sup>[[2]](#ref2)[[13]](#ref13)</sup> |
| **Etiology** | Surgical / traumatic | Radiation, malignancy, Crohn's<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[2]](#ref2)[[6]](#ref6)</sup> |
| **Sepsis** | Absent / controlled | Uncontrolled<sup>[[2]](#ref2)[[6]](#ref6)</sup> |
| **Distal obstruction** | Absent | Present<sup>[[3]](#ref3)</sup> |
| **Foreign body** | Absent | Present<sup>[[3]](#ref3)</sup> |
| **Epithelialization** | Absent | Present<sup>[[14]](#ref14)</sup> |

Multivariate analysis confirms that **high output, jejunal site, multiple fistulae, and sepsis** are independent adverse factors for non-closure, operative requirement, and death.<sup>[[13]](#ref13)</sup>

## Approach Selection

The approach follows the fistula — not the surgeon's comfort.

- **Low, small, non-irradiated VVFs** → transvaginal
- **High, complex, concomitant-ureter VVFs** → transabdominal (open or robotic)
- **Mid-to-high RUFs** → perineal gracilis or transabdominal exposure
- **Low non-irradiated RUFs** → transanal (MITAR / TAMIS) or York-Mason
- **Urethrovaginal fistulae** → almost always transvaginal
- **Pediatric UCF** → Fahmy algorithm
- **Refractory or tissue-poor cases** → consider permanent dual diversion as a legitimate reconstructive endpoint

Picking the wrong approach produces tension, poor exposure, and recurrent fistula.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[2]](#ref2)</sup>

## Timing

The optimal timing of fistula repair has been debated for decades.<sup>[[6]](#ref6)[[15]](#ref15)[[16]](#ref16)[[17]](#ref17)</sup>

### Traditional — delayed repair

The classic teaching has been to wait **8–12 weeks** for urinary fistulae or **≥ 3 months** for enteric fistulae after fistula formation, allowing inflammation to subside and tissue planes to mature.<sup>[[3]](#ref3)[[10]](#ref10)[[15]](#ref15)</sup>

### Contemporary — early repair may be equivalent

- A 2026 transvaginal VVF series found early repair (&lt; 3 months) was not inferior to delayed repair.<sup>[[16]](#ref16)</sup>
- Wang and Hadley 1990 reported **100% success** with non-delayed transvaginal VVF repair in selected high-lying fistulae.<sup>[[17]](#ref17)</sup>
- A 14-year monocentric experience found that **prolonged delay (12 vs. 6 months)** was a significant predictor of failure (p = 0.027) — excessive delay is harmful.<sup>[[6]](#ref6)</sup>
- For **ureterovaginal fistulae**, ureteral stenting within **2 weeks** achieves up to 95% success, falling sharply beyond 6 weeks.<sup>[[18]](#ref18)</sup>

### Current consensus

Repair when the patient is **optimized** (sepsis controlled, nutrition adequate, inflammation resolved) and the **tissue is healthy** — early or delayed depending on tissue quality, patient condition, and fistula characteristics, not a fixed waiting period.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[2]](#ref2)</sup>

## Tension-free, Watertight, Multilayered Closure

Every successful repair is multilayer, every layer is tension-free, every suture line is offset from adjacent layers, and every repair drains freely.<sup>[[19]](#ref19)</sup>

- **Tension-free** — adequate mobilization to eliminate tension on the suture line; tension is one of the most common causes of repair failure.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[19]](#ref19)</sup>
- **Watertight** — tested intraoperatively (e.g., bladder filling with dye for urinary fistulae).<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[2]](#ref2)[[19]](#ref19)</sup>
- **Multilayered with non-overlapping suture lines** — for urinary fistulae: bladder mucosa → muscularis / detrusor → interposition tissue → vaginal / skin closure; for enteric fistulae: mucosal layer → seromuscular layer → interposition tissue.<sup>[[11]](#ref11)[[19]](#ref19)</sup>
- **Excision of the fistula tract** — back to healthy, well-vascularized tissue. Latzko-style colpocleisis is an exception in which closure is achieved without formal excision.<sup>[[19]](#ref19)</sup>
- **Resection over oversewing** — for enterocutaneous fistulae, resection of the involved bowel with primary anastomosis recurred in **16%** vs. **36%** for simple oversewing (p = 0.006).<sup>[[11]](#ref11)</sup>

## Tissue Interposition

Interposing healthy, well-vascularized tissue between the closed layers prevents recurrence in:<sup>[[7]](#ref7)[[19]](#ref19)</sup>

- Recurrent fistulae (failed prior repair)
- Radiation-induced fistulae (poorly vascularized tissue)
- Complex fistulae with significant tissue loss
- Cases where tension-free layered closure is otherwise difficult

| Flap | Indication | Approach | Reported success | Notes |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **[Martius (labial fat pad)](/docs/foundations/surgical-principles/flaps/martius)** | Transvaginal VVF, urethral fistulae, radiation fistulae | Vaginal | **80–97%** | Seroma, hematoma, labial distortion, numbness; low morbidity overall<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[2]](#ref2)[[7]](#ref7)</sup> |
| **[Omental flap](/docs/foundations/surgical-principles/flaps/omental)** | Transabdominal VVF, RVF, complex pelvic fistulae | Abdominal | **&gt; 90%** | Postoperative pain, ileus<sup>[[3]](#ref3)[[20]](#ref20)</sup> |
| **[Gracilis muscle flap](/docs/foundations/surgical-principles/flaps/gracilis)** | Complex perineal / vaginal / RUF / radiation fistulae | Perineal / vaginal | **80–93%** | Donor-site morbidity, wound infection<sup>[[3]](#ref3)</sup> |
| **[Peritoneal flap](/docs/foundations/surgical-principles/flaps/peritoneal)** | Transabdominal VVF | Abdominal | **&gt; 90%** | Minimal donor morbidity<sup>[[3]](#ref3)</sup> |
| **Rectus abdominis flap** | Large abdominal-wall / pelvic defects | Abdominal | Variable | Hernia, wound complications<sup>[[3]](#ref3)</sup> |

Interposition is **not universally necessary**. A 2024 review of Martius use found that for simple, non-irradiated obstetric fistulae, graftless repair achieves closure rates &gt; 90% even for complex cases. Martius is most valuable when there is difficulty achieving tension-free closure, significant tissue loss, urethral involvement, or poorly vascularized tissues after radiotherapy.<sup>[[7]](#ref7)</sup>

## Diversion and Stenting

Urinary diversion (suprapubic tube, Foley, bilateral ureteral stents, or in extremis nephrostomy) is built into every repair to keep the suture line dry during healing.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[19]](#ref19)</sup>

- **Urinary fistulae** — continuous bladder drainage for **2–4 weeks** postoperatively to prevent intravesical pressure from disrupting the repair.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[19]](#ref19)</sup>
- **Enteric fistulae and RVF / RUF** — proximal fecal diversion (loop ileostomy or colostomy) is selectively used for complex repairs and irradiated tissue, though it has not improved outcomes in every comparative series.<sup>[[21]](#ref21)</sup>
- Removing diversion or stents too early re-fails a properly constructed repair.

## Pharmacologic Adjuncts — Somatostatin and Octreotide

For enterocutaneous fistulae, somatostatin and its analogues reduce GI secretions and fistula output:<sup>[[3]](#ref3)[[22]](#ref22)[[23]](#ref23)</sup>

- **Spontaneous closure** — significantly increased (OR 2.82, 95% CI 1.77–4.51, p &lt; 0.001).<sup>[[22]](#ref22)</sup>
- **Time to closure** — reduced by ~**6.4 days** (WMD −6.45, 95% CI −9.67 to −3.23).<sup>[[3]](#ref3)</sup>
- **Mortality** — no significant effect.<sup>[[22]](#ref22)[[23]](#ref23)</sup>
- **ASPEN-FELANPE recommendation** — somatostatin analogue for **high-output ECF (&gt; 500 mL/day)** to reduce effluent and enhance closure (moderate-quality evidence). Typical dosing: octreotide **100 µg SC TID for 10–20 days**.<sup>[[3]](#ref3)</sup>

For urinary fistulae no analogous pharmacologic agent exists; output reduction depends on adequate diversion.

## Negative Pressure Wound Therapy (NPWT / VAC)

NPWT is increasingly used as an adjunct in complex fistula management:<sup>[[11]](#ref11)[[12]](#ref12)[[24]](#ref24)</sup>

- **Mechanism** — continuous negative pressure removes fluid, reduces edema, promotes granulation, approximates wound edges, and protects perifistular skin.
- **Enterocutaneous fistulae** — median closure **64.6%** (range 7.7–100%) within ~58 days across a 10-study, 151-patient SR.<sup>[[12]](#ref12)</sup>
- **Enteroatmospheric fistulae** — NPWT can convert an enteroatmospheric fistula into an enterocutaneous fistula by promoting granulation tissue growth over exposed bowel.<sup>[[24]](#ref24)</sup>
- **Urinary fistulae** — successfully used for vesicocutaneous fistula closure and as a bridge to definitive reconstruction.<sup>[[11]](#ref11)</sup>
- **Limitations** — evidence is predominantly Level IV; no RCTs vs. standard surgical closure.<sup>[[12]](#ref12)</sup>

## Radiation-Bed Considerations

Radiated tissue is non-compliant, hypovascular, and slow to heal. Radiation-era fistulas demand:

- **Vascularized interposition** with non-irradiated tissue (gracilis, omentum, Martius).<sup>[[7]](#ref7)</sup>
- **Staged reconstruction** in which the first step is optimization rather than closure.
- **Honest pre-op counseling** that failure rates are higher and that **permanent dual diversion** is a legitimate reconstructive answer — not a failure — for the most hostile cases.
- A 14-year urogenital fistula series showed prior radiotherapy carried a **75% surgical failure rate** vs. **10.8%** in non-irradiated patients (p = 0.012).<sup>[[6]](#ref6)</sup>

## The Failed Repair

Every failed repair is a teaching case. The sequence is:

1. Confirm the diagnosis.
2. Image the tract.
3. Reassess the tissue bed.
4. Decide whether the next operation adds what the previous one lacked — better approach, vascularized flap, protective diversion, or simply more time.
5. Obtain patient agreement that further attempts are reasonable.

Escalating to **permanent dual diversion** is sometimes the correct next step rather than another attempt at closure.

## Approach-Specific Outcomes

### Urinary fistulae

- Conservative trial first: Foley drainage ± ureteral stent for 2–6 weeks. Vesical fistulae close conservatively in ~42%; ureterovaginal fistulae stented within 2 weeks close in up to 95%.<sup>[[18]](#ref18)</sup>
- First-repair success in specialized centers **89.7–98%** for VVF; definitive closure ultimately **92.5%**.<sup>[[6]](#ref6)[[15]](#ref15)[[19]](#ref19)[[25]](#ref25)</sup>
- Predictors of failure: prior radiotherapy, oncologic etiology, prolonged delay, large fistula size.<sup>[[6]](#ref6)[[16]](#ref16)</sup>

### Enterocutaneous fistulae

- Spontaneous closure **32–37%** with conservative management.<sup>[[9]](#ref9)[[11]](#ref11)</sup>
- Mortality **9–15%**, driven by sepsis and malnutrition.<sup>[[9]](#ref9)[[11]](#ref11)[[13]](#ref13)</sup>

### Anorectal fistulae

- **Simple fistulae** — fistulotomy with &gt; 90% success and low incontinence risk.<sup>[[21]](#ref21)</sup>
- **Complex fistulae** — endorectal advancement flap (66–87% healing) or LIFT (~76% overall success).<sup>[[21]](#ref21)</sup>
- **Crohn's disease fistulae** — control luminal disease with anti-TNF before surgical closure.<sup>[[21]](#ref21)[[26]](#ref26)</sup>

## Universal Principles — Summary

| Principle | Detail |
|---|---|
| **Sepsis control** | Drain abscesses, treat infection; spontaneous closure cannot occur with uncontrolled sepsis<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[3]](#ref3)[[9]](#ref9)</sup> |
| **Nutritional optimization** | 25–30 kcal/kg/day, 1.5–2.0 g/kg/day protein; albumin &gt; 3.0 g/dL<sup>[[3]](#ref3)[[4]](#ref4)</sup> |
| **Anatomic delineation** | CT, fistulography, MRI, endoscopy to define the tract and plan repair<sup>[[2]](#ref2)</sup> |
| **Address persistence factors** | Relieve obstruction; remove foreign bodies; excise malignant or granulomatous tissue<sup>[[5]](#ref5)</sup> |
| **Tension-free closure** | Adequate mobilization to eliminate tension on suture lines<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[19]](#ref19)</sup> |
| **Watertight closure** | Tested intraoperatively; prevents leakage through the repair<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[19]](#ref19)</sup> |
| **Multilayered, offset closure** | Each layer separate; non-overlapping suture lines<sup>[[19]](#ref19)</sup> |
| **Tissue interposition** | Well-vascularized flap (Martius / omental / gracilis / peritoneal) for complex, recurrent, or irradiated fistulae<sup>[[7]](#ref7)</sup> |
| **Postoperative diversion** | Urinary catheter (2–4 weeks) ± fecal diversion as indicated<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[19]](#ref19)[[21]](#ref21)</sup> |
| **Individualized timing** | Repair when the patient is optimized and the tissue is healthy — neither premature nor excessively delayed<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[6]](#ref6)[[16]](#ref16)</sup> |
