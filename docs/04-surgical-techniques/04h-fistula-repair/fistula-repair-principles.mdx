# Principles of Fistula Repair

Fistula repair requires control of infection, definition of anatomy, optimization and a reconstruction appropriate to the tissue and organs involved. Urinary, bowel and vascular-urinary fistulae differ substantially: an enterocutaneous fistula nutrition protocol or a VVF closure technique should not be applied to every fistula. Suspected ureteroarterial fistula with major hematuria warrants urgent vascular assessment and hemorrhage control before urinary reconstruction.<sup>[[32]](#ref32)</sup><sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[2]](#ref2)[[3]](#ref3)[[4]](#ref4)</sup>

---

## Why Fistulae Persist

Four classical mechanisms prevent spontaneous healing of a fistula:<sup>[[5]](#ref5)</sup>

1. **Distal obstruction** maintaining flow through the tract
2. **Foreign body or calculus** physically blocking closure (suture material, ureteral stent, mesh)
3. **Granulomatous tissue or malignancy** in the tract (TB, Crohn's disease, cancer)
4. **Epithelialization** of the tract: the fistula develops its own lining and becomes self-sustaining

A fifth, increasingly recognized in pelvic reconstruction, is radiation-induced tissue ischemia, in which irradiated tissue has poor vascularity and impaired healing capacity.<sup>[[6]](#ref6)[[7]](#ref7)</sup>

The mnemonic **FRIEND** captures the same ideas for enterocutaneous fistulae and provides a useful checklist for some urinary fistulae: **F**oreign body, **R**adiation, **I**nfection or inflammation, **E**pithelialization, **N**eoplasm, **D**istal obstruction.<sup>[[3]](#ref3)[[8]](#ref8)</sup>

## Phased Management

### Recognition, resuscitation and source control

Treat sepsis, drain collections, relieve obstruction and replace fluid and electrolyte losses when present. Use antimicrobials for infection, with cultures and source control guiding treatment. Skin protection and controlled effluent collection are particularly important for external bowel fistulae. Urinary drainage may use a bladder catheter, ureteral stent or nephrostomy according to the level of the leak.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[2]](#ref2)[[11]](#ref11)</sup>

The often-quoted 68% figure is from a 1978 external gastrointestinal fistula cohort: that proportion of deaths occurred with uncontrolled sepsis.<sup>[[9]](#ref9)</sup> It is not a mortality estimate for uncomplicated VVF. Likewise, the high-output threshold of 500 mL/day is used in enterocutaneous fistula care, not as a universal urinary-fistula classification.<sup>[[3]](#ref3)[[9]](#ref9)</sup>

### Define anatomy and optimize

Select imaging and endoscopy to establish the involved organs, obstruction, collections and tissue condition. Assess nutrition and functional reserve individually. Patients with enterocutaneous fistulae can have substantial protein, fluid and electrolyte losses; specialist nutrition support may include enteral feeding, fistuloclysis or parenteral nutrition according to anatomy and tolerance. Malnutrition is not nearly universal in all GU fistula populations.<sup>[[2]](#ref2)[[3]](#ref3)[[4]](#ref4)[[10]](#ref10)</sup>

Low albumin was associated with mortality in a postoperative enterocutaneous fistula cohort, but a threshold of 3.0 g/dL is not a nutritional diagnosis or a stand-alone clearance target for repair.<sup>[[13]](#ref13)</sup>

### Reassess the need for definitive treatment

Persistence despite drainage and correction of reversible factors prompts a reconstructive assessment. No fixed 4–6-week interval applies; timing depends on tissue recovery. Particularly after an open abdomen or enteroatmospheric fistula, definitive reconstruction may need to wait until recovery and wound healing. Timing depends on tissue condition, sepsis, anatomy and the patient's goals.<sup>[[10]](#ref10)[[23]](#ref23)[[30]](#ref30)</sup>

## Factors Predicting Spontaneous Closure

Foreign material, distal obstruction, active infection or inflammation, malignancy, radiation injury and an epithelialized tract can make spontaneous healing less likely.<sup>[[5]](#ref5)[[8]](#ref8)[[14]](#ref14)</sup> Prediction is anatomy-specific:

- **Enterocutaneous fistulae:** high output, jejunal origin, multiple tracts and sepsis were adverse factors in a 174-patient postoperative cohort. Tract length and intestinal continuity also matter.<sup>[[9]](#ref9)[[13]](#ref13)</sup>
- **VVF:** a recent, small defect with viable tissue may merit a trial of bladder drainage; closure is uncommon overall and the evidence is mainly retrospective.<sup>[[26]](#ref26)</sup>
- **Ureterovaginal fistula:** early stenting can succeed in selected cases. A meta-analysis of 17 retrospective studies reported 32% pooled success for endoscopic stenting in the 12 studies reporting that approach, with substantially different results by timing; this is not evidence that 42% of *vesical* fistulae close conservatively.<sup>[[18]](#ref18)</sup>

---

## Approach Selection

Choose the exposure that permits safe access, ureteral protection and a tension-free repair. For VVF, vaginal accessibility and associated reconstruction matter more than a simple low or high label; selected high fistulae are accessible vaginally. An abdominal approach may be needed for inaccessible defects or ureteral reimplantation.<sup>[[17]](#ref17)[[26]](#ref26)</sup>

Use the separate [female](/docs/surgical-techniques/04h-fistula-repair/female-fistula), [male](/docs/surgical-techniques/04h-fistula-repair/male-fistula) and [all-patient](/docs/surgical-techniques/04h-fistula-repair/all-patients-fistula) atlases for anatomy-specific options. Extensive tissue loss, irradiation, associated outlet disease and prior repairs may require vascularized tissue, staged reconstruction or diversion.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[2]](#ref2)</sup>

A 2024 systematic review included 46 studies of vesicovaginal, urethrovaginal and bladder neck–vaginal fistulas; its 17 nonrandomized VVF vaginal-versus-abdominal-laparotomy comparisons (n=1,143) found insufficient evidence to determine a difference in reported repair success (RR 0.98, 95% CI 0.94–1.03). Interposition, edge trimming, fibrin glue and fascial sling showed no statistically significant improvement in fistula closure in the available comparisons.<sup>[[31]](#ref31)</sup>

---

## Timing

Older practice often delayed urinary-fistula repair for several months to let inflammation settle. Current EAU guidance individualizes timing once edema, inflammation, necrosis and infection have resolved; no fixed waiting period fits all patients.<sup>[[15]](#ref15)[[26]](#ref26)</sup>

Recent observational reports support feasibility of selected early repairs but not equivalence or a causal benefit of earlier surgery:

- Adinata's 2026 retrospective series of 80 women defined early transvaginal VVF repair as less than four weeks, not less than three months. Its higher early-group success was not a randomized noninferiority comparison.<sup>[[16]](#ref16)</sup>
- In Zhang's 2026 VVF subgroup, longer delay was associated with failure (12 versus 6 months); confounding by complexity and referral history prevents concluding that delay itself caused failure.<sup>[[6]](#ref6)</sup>
- The ureterovaginal meta-analysis found 95% pooled success for stenting within two weeks versus 20% after six weeks, but every included study was retrospective. Prompt recognition and drainage are reasonable; the figures are not a guarantee for an individual patient.<sup>[[18]](#ref18)</sup>

---

## Tension-free Closure and Tissue Preservation

Aim for viable tissue, adequate mobilization, secure closure and appropriate drainage. For bladder repairs, test watertightness without overdistending the reconstruction. Separate adjacent organ repairs and offset suture lines where feasible.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[26]](#ref26)</sup>

There is no rule that every successful fistula repair must have the same number of layers. The 2026 Cochrane publication is a review protocol and not a completed comparative review; its background describes one- or two-layer bladder closure. It cannot establish superiority of a particular operative method.<sup>[[19]](#ref19)</sup>

Remove foreign material and nonviable tissue, while preserving tissue needed for closure. Latzko repair closes selected apical defects without formal excision, and EAU’s review of a trimming trial did not establish better closure from trimming.<sup>[[19]](#ref19)[[26]](#ref26)</sup>

For enterocutaneous fistula surgery, a 205-patient retrospective series found more recurrence after oversewing or wedge repair than resection and reanastomosis (36% versus 16%). The series supports feasible enterocutaneous resection; urinary fistulae were not studied.<sup>[[11]](#ref11)</sup>

---

## Tissue Interposition

Vascularized tissue can separate closures and supplement a compromised bed. Consider it for selected recurrent, irradiated or tissue-deficient repairs; it does not guarantee closure. Evidence for routine interposition in simple VVF is limited, and success rates from different flap series cannot rank the flaps.<sup>[[7]](#ref7)[[26]](#ref26)</sup>

| Tissue | Common use | Planning considerations |
|---|---|---|
| **[Martius fat pad](/docs/foundations/surgical-principles/flaps/martius)** | Vaginal or urethral repairs | Reach, pedicle integrity, labial wound and sensory effects |
| **[Omentum](/docs/foundations/surgical-principles/flaps/omental)** | Abdominal or combined pelvic repairs | Available length, prior operations and abdominal morbidity |
| **[Gracilis](/docs/foundations/surgical-principles/flaps/gracilis)** | Complex perineal or rectourinary repairs | Vascular supply, reach and donor morbidity |
| **[Peritoneum](/docs/foundations/surgical-principles/flaps/peritoneal)** | Selected abdominal VVF repairs | Local tissue quality and a tension-free pedicle |

Technique-specific pages give the relevant cohorts and limitations. For recurrent or otherwise complex RVF, ASCRS typically recommends a gracilis or bulbocavernosus (Martius) flap (strong recommendation, low-quality evidence, 1C); the guideline does not prescribe a fixed sequence.<sup>[[20]](#ref20)</sup>

---

## Diversion and Stenting

Provide reliable low-pressure drainage suited to the repair. After ureteric fistula repair, use anatomical and functional imaging to detect stricture and deterioration in renal function.<sup>[[26]](#ref26)</sup> Nephrostomy is an appropriate planned option for upper-tract leakage or failed retrograde drainage, not merely an intervention of last resort.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[18]](#ref18)</sup>

Postoperative bladder-catheter duration is not universally 2–4 weeks:

- **WHO:** 7–10 days after selected simple obstetric urinary fistula repairs.<sup>[[27]](#ref27)</sup>
- **IUGA/ICS 2026 obstetric consensus:** a Delphi consensus of fistula surgeons (statement 37) sets a minimum of 10–14 days of continuous drainage after obstetric VVF repair. The group acknowledges WHO's 7–10 days and that 7 days may suffice for simple cases.<sup>[[28]](#ref28)</sup>
- **EAU 2026 (weak recommendation):** expert opinion suggests 10–14 days for simple or postsurgical VVF and 14–21 days for complex or postradiation repair.<sup>[[26]](#ref26)</sup>

Use the operative findings, drainage function and assessment for persistent leakage to plan removal. For RVF endorectal advancement-flap repair, ASCRS advises individualizing diversion because a stoma has not been shown to significantly improve outcomes; it generally recommends a diverting ostomy with Martius or gracilis flap repair despite limited supporting evidence.<sup>[[20]](#ref20)</sup> In a retrospective series of 30 men with RUF, selective fecal diversion was possible.<sup>[[33]](#ref33)</sup>

## Pharmacologic Adjuncts — Somatostatin and Octreotide

Somatostatin analogues have been studied to reduce gastrointestinal fistula secretions. Older reviews and ASPEN-FELANPE guidance were more favorable about closure, but they should be read alongside the updated evidence.<sup>[[3]](#ref3)[[21]](#ref21)[[22]](#ref22)</sup>

A 2025 meta-analysis of nine randomized trials (442 participants) found no statistically significant increase in closure across seven trials: 134/193 versus 99/170 with standard care (RR 1.11, 95% CI 0.95–1.28).<sup>[[29]](#ref29)</sup> Time to closure was about six days shorter in the trials reporting that outcome; mortality, complications and need for surgery did not differ significantly. Regimens and fistula types varied, and certainty was mostly low. The meta-analysis did not pool measured output reduction. One included open-label randomized trial enrolled 40 adults (17 with intestinal fistulas and 23 with small-bowel enterostomies); for the eight-week primary endpoint, 9/20 assigned to lanreotide and 2/20 controls had at least 25% output reduction in the intention-to-treat analysis using last observation carried forward.<sup>[[34]](#ref34)</sup> These data suggest a possible output-management adjunct for selected high-output intestinal losses.<sup>[[29]](#ref29)[[34]](#ref34)</sup>

## Negative Pressure Wound Therapy (NPWT / VAC)

For an open abdominal wound with an enteroatmospheric fistula, the immediate goals are effluent isolation, protection of exposed bowel and wound healing. NPWT can help achieve those goals, but should not be applied directly to exposed viscera. Definitive fistula reconstruction is usually delayed until recovery and wound healing.<sup>[[30]](#ref30)</sup>

An older review of ten level-IV studies including 151 patients reported a median study-level VAC closure rate of 64.6%; this is not a controlled estimate of benefit.<sup>[[12]](#ref12)</sup> In a later 77-patient enteroatmospheric series, 14 healed without surgery and 56 required intestinal reconstruction.<sup>[[23]](#ref23)</sup>

---

## Radiation-Bed Considerations

Radiated tissue is non-compliant, hypovascular, and slow to heal. A fistula arising after radiotherapy as primary cancer treatment may indicate tumour recurrence; assess for recurrence before reconstruction.<sup>[[26]](#ref26)</sup> Radiation-associated fistula planning includes:

- EAU recommends an interposition graft when repairing a radiation-associated fistula (weak recommendation). Use suitable non-irradiated tissue when repair is feasible; some patients need diversion instead. When urinary or fecal diversion is required, EAU recommends avoiding an irradiated bowel segment (weak recommendation).<sup>[[7]](#ref7)[[26]](#ref26)</sup>
- **Staged reconstruction** may be needed, with optimization rather than closure as the first step.
- **Pre-operative counseling** covers the higher failure rates and the fact that permanent dual diversion is a legitimate reconstructive outcome for the most hostile cases.
- In the VVF subgroup (39 patients) of a 67-patient mixed urogenital fistula series, failure was reported in 75% with prior radiotherapy versus 10.8% without it; the irradiated subgroup size and referral case mix limit generalization.<sup>[[6]](#ref6)</sup>

## The Failed Repair

After a failed repair, the sequence is:

1. Confirm the diagnosis.
2. Image the tract.
3. Reassess the tissue bed.
4. Decide whether the next operation adds what the previous one lacked: better approach, vascularized flap, protective diversion, or simply more time.
5. Obtain patient agreement that further attempts are reasonable.

Escalating to permanent dual diversion is sometimes the correct next step rather than another attempt at closure.

For obstetric fistula, the IUGA/ICS 2026 consensus considers a fistula incurable only after three failed repairs despite adequate technique and postoperative care, with independent assessment by two expert surgeons (statements 7 and 38). Diversion can be offered to these women, weighing device availability, acceptance and long-term follow-up.<sup>[[28]](#ref28)</sup>

---

## Interpreting Outcomes

Report anatomical closure, continence, complications, quality of life and reoperation separately. When reporting outcomes, identify the fistula type, etiology, prior repairs, denominator and follow-up. The cited IUGA/ICS consensus defines success specifically after obstetric urinary pelvic floor fistula repair.<sup>[[28]](#ref28)</sup>

- The IUGA/ICS 2026 consensus for obstetric fistula defines surgical success as anatomical closure without residual urinary incontinence (statement 3). A woman with residual leakage is not classified as cured, and a dye test at catheter removal, 10–14 days after surgery, is the recommended objective check of closure (statement 2). A fistula that breaks down within 3–6 months is counted as a failed repair rather than a new fistula (statement 15, near consensus). These are expert consensus statements for obstetric fistula.<sup>[[28]](#ref28)</sup>

- Zhang's 67-patient mixed GU-fistula cohort reported 80.6% success after first repair and 92.5% ultimately; VVF success was 89.7%. The overall final rate is not a VVF-only estimate.<sup>[[6]](#ref6)</sup>
- Singh's 638-patient mixed GU cohort reported first-repair success at three months—cure of urinary incontinence with complete restoration of bladder and vaginal function—of 93.95%, 94.24% and 96.55% in selected abdominal, vaginal and laparoscopic groups.<sup>[[24]](#ref24)</sup>
- Historical external gastrointestinal or enterocutaneous cohorts reported spontaneous closure of 32%<sup>[[9]](#ref9)</sup> and 37%<sup>[[13]](#ref13)</sup>, respectively, and substantial sepsis-related morbidity. Those rates should not be quoted as urinary-fistula outcomes.<sup>[[9]](#ref9)[[13]](#ref13)</sup>
- In Crohn-related anorectal fistulae, treatment combines control of sepsis and luminal and perianal inflammation with appropriate medical and surgical therapy. Drain an abscess before relying on immune-directed treatment or definitive closure.<sup>[[20]](#ref20)[[25]](#ref25)</sup>

## Practical Checklist

| Principle | Application |
|---|---|
| **Control infection and losses** | Drain collections, treat infection, relieve obstruction and replace losses appropriate to the fistula. |
| **Assess nutrition and reserve** | Individualize support; do not use an albumin target or an ECF feeding prescription for every GU fistula. |
| **Define anatomy** | Establish involved organs, tissue condition, obstruction and associated disease. |
| **Preserve viable tissue** | Remove foreign material and nonviable tissue; avoid unnecessary tissue sacrifice. |
| **Plan exposure and closure** | Aim for a tension-free repair with secure organ closure; layers and interposition depend on anatomy. |
| **Plan drainage and follow-up** | Specify catheter/stent function, duration, assessment before removal and the patient's functional goals. |
| **Individualize timing** | Use recovery, tissue condition and the consequences of waiting; no universal calendar interval applies. |

These principles require the anatomy-specific guidance and evidence limitations described above.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[3]](#ref3)[[20]](#ref20)[[26]](#ref26)[[28]](#ref28)</sup>
