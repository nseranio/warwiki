# Principles of Fistula Repair

Genitourinary fistula repair succeeds or fails on a small number of principles that transcend the individual tract or technique. Each principle exists because the operation that ignored it failed.

## Approach selection

The approach follows the fistula — not the surgeon's comfort. Low, small, non-irradiated VVFs are transvaginal. High, complex, concomitant-ureter VVFs are transabdominal. Mid-to-high RUFs need perineal gracilis or transabdominal exposure; low non-irradiated RUFs can be done transanal (MITAR / TAMIS) or via York-Mason. Urethrovaginal fistulas are almost always transvaginal. UCFs in children are typically managed per the Fahmy algorithm. Picking the wrong approach produces tension, poor exposure, and recurrent fistula.

## Timing

Immediate recognition and repair during the index operation has the best outcomes. Early delayed repair (2–3 months) is the default for most iatrogenic urinary fistulas once inflammation has settled. Long radiation-delay (12+ months) applies to radiation-induced fistulas, where premature repair in unhealed tissue fails. Obstetric fistulas are repaired once the patient is nutritionally and functionally optimized. Bahuguna time-dependent stenting for UVF is the canonical example of how timing alone can change the answer from 95% to 20%.

## Tension-free multilayer closure

Every repair is multilayer, every layer is tension-free, every suture line is offset from adjacent layers, and every repair drains freely. Non-overlapping suture lines prevent point-to-point leaks. Water-tight closure is tested intraoperatively whenever possible.

## Interposition flaps — when and which

Interposition flaps are added when (1) the fistula is complex, recurrent, large, or irradiated; (2) the tissue planes are hostile; or (3) non-overlapping closure is not achievable without additional vascularized bulk. Martius is the low-morbidity default for transvaginal work. Gracilis is the workhorse for transperineal RUF / RVF / complex UCF / UPF, especially in radiated fields. Omentum is reached at transabdominal operations and is uniquely useful for high VVFs and pelvic salvage. Peritoneal flaps support select robotic repairs.

## Diversion and stenting

Urinary diversion (suprapubic tube, Foley, bilateral ureteral stents, or in extremis nephrostomy) is built into every repair to keep the suture line dry during healing. Bowel diversion (proximal ostomy) is added for RVF and complex RUF where fecal contamination across the repair would otherwise guarantee failure. Removing these adjuncts too early re-fails a properly constructed repair.

## Radiation bed considerations

Radiated tissue is non-compliant, hypovascular, and slow to heal. Radiation-era fistulas need vascularized coverage (gracilis, omentum, Martius), staged reconstruction where the first step is optimization rather than closure, and honest discussion with the patient that failure rates are higher and that permanent dual diversion is a legitimate reconstructive answer — not a failure — for the most hostile cases.

## The failed repair

Every failed repair is a teaching case. The sequence is: confirm the diagnosis, image the tract, reassess the tissue bed, decide whether the next operation adds what the previous one lacked (better approach, vascularized flap, protective diversion, time), and obtain patient agreement that further attempts are reasonable. Escalating to permanent dual diversion is sometimes the correct next step.
