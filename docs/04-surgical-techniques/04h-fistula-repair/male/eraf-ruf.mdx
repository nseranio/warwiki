# Endorectal Advancement Flap (ERAF) for Rectourethral Fistula

The **endorectal advancement flap (ERAF)** covers the rectal side of a rectourethral fistula (RUF) with mobilized viable rectal tissue. A purely transanal approach avoids deliberate external anal-sphincter division and a distant muscle donor site. It can suit an accessible defect with healthy local tissue, and the evidence consists mainly of small series. Anatomical closure, urinary continence, bowel function and reversal of diversion are distinct outcomes.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[2]](#ref2)</sup>

Related pages: [Transperineal RUF Repair](/docs/surgical-techniques/04h-fistula-repair/male/transperineal-ruf-repair), [York-Mason Repair](/docs/surgical-techniques/04h-fistula-repair/male/york-mason), [Transanal Minimally Invasive Repair](/docs/surgical-techniques/04h-fistula-repair/transanal-minimally-invasive-repair), and the separate [ERAF for RVF](/docs/surgical-techniques/04h-fistula-repair/female/eraf) page.

## Selection and preoperative planning

Assess rectal access, fistula level and size, urethral stricture, residual continence mechanisms, infection and tissue damage from radiation or ablation. Active infection needs control before elective closure. Local flap repair is less attractive with severe proctitis, ischemia, a large tissue deficit or a urethra requiring substantial reconstruction.<sup>[[3]](#ref3)[[4]](#ref4)</sup>

There is no validated universal 1.5-cm ERAF cutoff. That threshold was an exclusion criterion in Nicita's 12-patient MITAR series, a distinct transanal repair using laparoscopic instruments and separate urinary and rectal suturing. It should not be transferred to every advancement-flap technique.<sup>[[5]](#ref5)</sup>

Fecal diversion is individualized according to contamination, symptoms, tissue and planned repair. Two of Dreznik's three patients underwent a purely transanal flap without urinary or fecal diversion; the third underwent a posterior transsphincteric repair after both diversions. This experience shows feasibility in selected patients. It does not show that diversion is generally unnecessary.<sup>[[6]](#ref6)</sup> In Joshi's five-patient series, diversion was less likely with localized sepsis than with severe intra-abdominal sepsis. The comparison was not randomized.<sup>[[2]](#ref2)</sup>

:::info[ASCRS guidance concerns fistula-in-ano]
The 2022 ASCRS recommendation for advancement flaps, its statement that diversion usually adds no benefit, its failure predictors and its reported incontinence rates concern fistula-in-ano. The guideline has a separate RVF section. None of these is a validated RUF-specific recommendation or complication rate.<sup>[[7]](#ref7)</sup>
:::

## Operative concept and its limits

Expose the anterior rectal-wall opening and establish its relationship to the urethra. The conventional advancement-flap concept is to prepare the tract or opening, mobilize a broad viable rectal flap, close the underlying opening as appropriate and advance the flap without tension over that closure. Mucosa, submucosa and variable muscle may be incorporated. The exact flap thickness and urinary-side repair depend on anatomy and exposure; descriptions of anal-fistula flap construction are technical background rather than RUF comparative evidence.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[6]](#ref6)[[7]](#ref7)</sup>

Tension, thermal injury and a poorly perfused distal flap are avoided, the anal sphincter is preserved, and an already tissue-deficient defect is not enlarged. There is no evidence that the rectum is always the high-pressure side or that the urinary defect can always be left to heal without attention. When safe exposure or urinary reconstruction cannot be achieved transanally, another approach is selected.<sup>[[3]](#ref3)[[4]](#ref4)</sup>

### Typical operative steps

The RUF series report few operative details. The sequence below is the conventional transanal advancement-flap technique as taught for anorectal fistula, adapted to the anterior rectal wall. It is technique description and not comparative evidence.<sup>[[2]](#ref2)[[6]](#ref6)[[7]](#ref7)</sup>

1. Position the patient in lithotomy or prone jackknife.
2. Expose the anterior rectal wall with a Parks or similar anal retractor and identify the fistula opening; cystoscopic identification with a catheter passed through the tract has been used in transanal repairs.<sup>[[5]](#ref5)</sup>
3. Curette or excise the tract and fibrotic margins to remove granulation and epithelialized lining.
4. Design a broad-based flap of mucosa and submucosa with a variable thickness of muscularis, with its base proximal. A base wider than the apex, commonly about twice as wide, helps preserve perfusion.
5. Mobilize the flap far enough to reach the closed opening without tension.
6. Close the internal opening with interrupted absorbable suture, including the urethral defect when it is accessible.
7. Advance the flap over the closure and suture it with interrupted absorbable stitches so that the flap and internal-opening suture lines do not overlie one another.
8. Avoid electrocoagulation near the repair; Nicita's transanal repair did not use it.<sup>[[5]](#ref5)</sup>

Purely transanal ERAF and posterior transsphincteric advancement are different operations. The latter divides the posterior sphincter and rectal complex in the midline to expose the anterior wall, and the sphincter complex is reapproximated in layers afterward. Al-Ali's traumatic-fistula series used it with prior double diversion; all 11 patients treated with that method had fistula closure, and three developed urethral stricture. Their outcomes do not describe a sphincter-avoiding transanal flap.<sup>[[8]](#ref8)</sup>

## What the primary RUF series show

| Study | Patients and approach | Closure and follow-up |
|---|---|---|
| Garofalo 2003 | 12 advancement-flap repairs within a 23-patient RUF series | **8/12 primary**, then **10/12 after repeat repair**; average follow-up 31 months. One reported complication. Quality-of-life data were collected, but the small cohort cannot establish a general low-risk guarantee.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup> |
| Joshi 2011 | Five patients receiving six transanal flaps; four had laparoscopic prostatectomy without radiation | **4/5 primary**, all five after a second procedure in one patient; mean follow-up 11 months.<sup>[[2]](#ref2)</sup> |
| Dreznik 2003 | Two purely transanal repairs and one posterior transsphincteric repair | All three healed with reported normal urinary/fecal continence; route-specific denominators are only two and one.<sup>[[6]](#ref6)</sup> |

These cohorts are small and selected, and they differ in technique, repeat operations and follow-up. Their results are therefore not pooled.

## Comparison with transperineal interposition

A transperineal approach permits wider urethral exposure, concomitant urethral reconstruction and vascularized interposition, which often favors it in complex or irradiated defects. An advancement flap avoids a distant donor incision. It may provide limited exposure, particularly for the anterior rectal wall, and it places no distant vascularized barrier between the urinary and bowel repairs.<sup>[[3]](#ref3)[[4]](#ref4)</sup>

Park's retrospective comparison found recurrence in 2/24 gracilis repairs versus 6/12 controls (8% versus 50%, P = 0.009). ERAF was not randomized against gracilis, and the abstract does not state that all controls had ERAF. In the control group, prior radiation and urinary incontinence were associated with recurrence. The study supports further evaluation of interposition and gives no comparative ERAF failure rate.<sup>[[9]](#ref9)</sup>

In the 2013 systematic review of 416 patients in retrospective series, transanal repair accounted for 5.9% of repairs and transperineal repair for 65.9%. Centers treating at least 25 patients used transperineal repair with a tissue flap in 100% of cases.<sup>[[3]](#ref3)</sup> In Harris's multi-institutional series of 201 men, an interposition flap or graft was used in 91% to 92% and ultimate closure was 92% overall.<sup>[[11]](#ref11)</sup> The 2026 review likewise found transperineal gracilis common and outcomes more difficult after radiation.<sup>[[10]](#ref10)</sup> Practice frequency and between-series success rates do not establish superiority for every simple fistula.

After recurrence, the cause, perfusion, infection and urinary defect are reassessed. A repeat flap succeeded in two Garofalo patients, and the others needed a different route or interposition. Evidence does not support routine repeat ERAF or routine conversion to gracilis after every recurrence.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup>

## Postoperative assessment

Urinary drainage and catheter removal follow the actual repair and confirmation of healing. Joshi used postoperative cystography to document closure. No comparative trial establishes a fixed catheter duration for ERAF repairs.<sup>[[2]](#ref2)</sup>

Before reversing fecal diversion, confirm closure and assess bowel and urinary function. Persistent leakage needs localization, because SUI, obstruction and another fistula can remain despite successful repair. Counseling covers recurrence, urinary and anal continence problems and the possibility of later procedures. The ASCRS “up to 35%” incontinence estimate comes from anal-fistula flaps and is not an RUF-specific risk.<sup>[[3]](#ref3)[[7]](#ref7)</sup>
