# Simple Closure with Skin Advancement Flap for Urethrocutaneous Fistula

Simple closure with a skin advancement flap repairs a selected urethrocutaneous fistula (UCF) with local tissue. The urethral defect is closed first, and a second layer of de-epithelialized or full-thickness advancement skin covers it so that the urethral and skin suture lines do not overlap. In Santangelo and colleagues' series of 99 patients and 94 repairs at Children's National Medical Center, 66 of 69 simple repairs (95.7%) succeeded with this coverage, and the overall failure rate was 6.4%. This is a selected case-series result, not a guaranteed success rate or proof of superiority to other operations.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup>

See the [male fistula atlas](/docs/surgical-techniques/04h-fistula-repair/male-fistula), [PATIO](/docs/surgical-techniques/04h-fistula-repair/male/ucf-patio-repair), [Scrotal Flaps](/docs/surgical-techniques/04h-fistula-repair/male/ucf-scrotal-flaps) and [Tunica Vaginalis Flap](/docs/surgical-techniques/04h-fistula-repair/male/ucf-tunica-vaginalis-flap) for alternative repairs.

## Assessment and selection

Assess the fistula or fistulas, urinary stream, meatus and distal urethra, tissue quality and associated dehiscence. A fistula accompanied by stenosis or a substantial urethral defect may require meatoplasty or urethroplasty rather than closure alone. The ability to obtain a viable, tension-free repair matters alongside size and location.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[2]](#ref2)[[10]](#ref10)</sup>

Local advancement and de-epithelialized flaps have been used at coronal and shaft sites, including selected recurrent fistulas. A small fistula is not automatically suitable for simple closure, and a larger or coronal fistula does not automatically mandate a particular flap. Size-based recommendations in older series reflect their selection strategies. In Elbakry's series, simple closure succeeded in 30 of 42 small fistulas (71%), and 78% of recurrences were coronal. Elbakry concluded that simple closure suits small fistulas of the penile shaft and that rotational or advancement flaps suit large and coronal fistulas. In Shankar's 113 children, primary repair succeeded in 71%, and fistulas larger than 2 mm recurred more often than those of 2 mm or less (52% against 24%).<sup>[[2]](#ref2)[[6]](#ref6)[[7]](#ref7)[[11]](#ref11)</sup>

A concurrent distal obstruction, such as meatal stenosis, is a major reason for recurrence and is excluded before repair. Cimador excluded an outflow impairment in every child.<sup>[[10]](#ref10)</sup>

## Reconstruction options

The objectives are a sound urethral closure, vascularized coverage where appropriate, and skin closure without excessive tension. Overlapping suture lines can be avoided, but recurrence also depends on obstruction, tissue quality and the overall reconstruction; no single maneuver eliminates failure.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[4]](#ref4)[[5]](#ref5)</sup>

### Operative sequence

The following sequence combines the techniques described by Santangelo, Geltzeiler and Belman, Ahuja and Dekalo. Details vary by author.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[6]](#ref6)[[7]](#ref7)[[8]](#ref8)</sup>

1. Circumscribe the fistula, dissect the tract to its base and excise it.
2. Close the urethral defect with fine absorbable suture. Ahuja closed the inner skin edges with an inverting subcuticular stitch as the urethral layer, and Dekalo closed the defect with one continuous layer.
3. Cover the closure. In the midshaft variant, a de-epithelialized flap of adjacent penile skin is raised on a hinge with its dartos, turned over the urethral closure and secured. In the coronal variant, adjacent full-thickness penile skin is mobilized and advanced over the closure.
4. Close the remaining penile skin over the flap so that the skin suture line is offset from the urethral closure, or as a pants-over-vest closure.

### De-epithelialized coverage

After defining and repairing the urethral defect, a viable local flap can be de-epithelialized and positioned over the closure while retaining its blood supply. Superficial skin is then approximated or advanced with its suture line displaced from the underlying repair. This is distinct from simple closure without a planned interposed flap.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[3]](#ref3)[[6]](#ref6)</sup>

The older Belman 1988 report describes coverage during primary hypospadias urethroplasty, not isolated UCF repair. Its result should not be substituted for the outcomes of treating established fistulas.<sup>[[3]](#ref3)</sup>

### Skin advancement

Adjacent skin is mobilized sufficiently to cover the urethral repair without traction or devascularization. Adequate mobilization does not mean indiscriminate undermining. Reassess whether the available tissue can close safely before completing the reconstruction.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[4]](#ref4)[[9]](#ref9)</sup>

When direct closure would be under tension, Chen and colleagues made a longitudinal relaxing incision 0.5 to 1.5 cm from the wound edge after closing the fistula, in 34 patients with 46 fistulas. The relaxing incisions healed in 7 to 21 days. This is a specific adjunct supported by an uncontrolled series, not a mandatory incision at a fixed distance from every closure or proof that it is the single determinant of success.<sup>[[4]](#ref4)</sup>

### Hinged turnover dartos variant

Ahuja described closing the inner fistula edges with an inverting subcuticular stitch, raising adjacent de-epithelialized skin with dartos on a preserved tissue hinge, and turning this flap over the first layer. A penile or scrotal skin flap, or an overlapping skin closure, completed coverage. Thus, the exact tissue retained or excised depends on the variant; routine complete tract excision followed by this skin-edge inversion should not be prescribed as one universal sequence.<sup>[[8]](#ref8)</sup>

Use fine absorbable sutures suited to the tissue and the chosen technique; select the material and filament structure deliberately. Preserve perfusion and avoid unnecessary cautery or traction. For the broader material principles, see [Sutures](/docs/foundations/surgical-principles/sutures).

## Clinical outcomes

| Source | Finding | Interpretation |
|---|---|---|
| Santangelo 2003 | Six failures among 94 repairs overall; three failures among 69 simple repairs with local advancement or de-epithelialized coverage. | The 25 complex repairs included urethroplasty incorporating the fistula (18 repairs, 2 failures), meatoplasty with distal repair (2, 1 failure) and urethroplasty with separate fistula repair (2, no failures). The denominators are 94 repairs in 99 patients.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup> |
| Geltzeiler and Belman 1984 | No recurrence in 19 midshaft repairs using an overlapping de-epithelialized technique; 10/11 coronal advancement repairs succeeded. | Small historical subgroups; 13/19 and 10/11, respectively, were performed without diversion as outpatient procedures.<sup>[[6]](#ref6)</sup> |
| Cimador 2003 | Retrospective comparison reported first-attempt success of 74% with simple closure and 94% with layered repair. | Groups were not randomized. The abstract lists 72 children reviewed, 39 with simple closure and 32 with a pants-over-vest layered repair. Second-attempt success was 80% and 100%.<sup>[[10]](#ref10)</sup> |
| Dekalo 2020 | Seventy-two children underwent 81 local-tissue repairs; 17 (21%) recurred at mean follow-up 7.9 years. Recurrence did not differ by fistula size, coronal or more proximal site, or prior repair. | This “simple” technique included subcutaneous coverage. Patients needing revision urethroplasty, other fistula techniques or concomitant meatoplasty were excluded.<sup>[[7]](#ref7)</sup> |
| Ahuja 2009 | Nine of ten healed without complications; one residual pinpoint fistula closed spontaneously. | Final closure in ten is different from ten uncomplicated initial repairs.<sup>[[8]](#ref8)</sup> |

A pooled “skin-flap” category in a heterogeneous systematic review (54.5%, alongside 94.6% for scrotal flaps, 94.3% for tunica vaginalis and 73.2% for simple primary closure) is not interchangeable with one particular advancement technique. Conversely, favorable single-center results do not establish that meticulous technique will reliably produce a success rate over 90% for every patient.<sup>[[5]](#ref5)</sup>

## Urinary drainage and recovery

Some uncomplicated repairs can be performed as day surgery without a stent, as in the Santangelo, Geltzeiler and Dekalo series. Those selected populations do not establish a catheter-free rule for every fistula, particularly when another urethral procedure is needed.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[6]](#ref6)[[7]](#ref7)</sup>

In children with a small (2 mm or less), early-onset fistula, a 2026 systematic review of non-operative approaches (tissue adhesive, fibrin glue or recatheterization) found closure in 53.8% to 62.5% in the primary series. The five studies (73 patients) were heterogeneous and mostly at high risk of bias, so the findings are hypothesis-generating.<sup>[[12]](#ref12)</sup>

Elbakry's retrospective series observed recurrence in 4/25 patients receiving suprapubic diversion versus 10/22 without it. However, diversion was selected for large or multiple fistulas, and repair techniques differed. The comparison cannot establish a causal reduction from 45% to 16% or mandate suprapubic drainage solely because a fistula exceeds 4 mm.<sup>[[2]](#ref2)</sup>

Tailor urinary drainage, dressings, analgesia and recovery restrictions to the operation. Assess wound healing and recurrence, and evaluate a weak stream, straining or other signs of distal obstruction. Early closure is not the only outcome: the long-follow-up Dekalo cohort also included later meatoplasty and additional penile reconstruction.<sup>[[7]](#ref7)</sup>
