# Scrotal Flaps for UCF Repair

Scrotal flaps supply vascularized tissue from outside the penile operative field for a urethrocutaneous fistula (UCF) when local penile tissue is scarred or inadequate. The scrotal dartos flap, the de-epithelialized scrotal skin flap, the turnover dartos flap and the septocutaneous flap are different reconstructions. Their indications and complications differ, and they should not be combined into one expected outcome.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[2]](#ref2)[[3]](#ref3)[[4]](#ref4)</sup>

The 2023 UCF-repair review reported 94.6% success for scrotal flaps and 94.3% for tunica vaginalis. Those pooled proportions come from heterogeneous studies and do not prove that scrotal flaps are superior or that either rate applies to every recurrent fistula.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup>

See [Simple Closure and Skin Advancement](/docs/surgical-techniques/04h-fistula-repair/male/ucf-simple-closure-skin-flap), [Double Dartos](/docs/surgical-techniques/04h-fistula-repair/male/ucf-double-dartos), [PATIO](/docs/surgical-techniques/04h-fistula-repair/male/ucf-patio-repair) and [Tunica Vaginalis Flap](/docs/surgical-techniques/04h-fistula-repair/male/ucf-tunica-vaginalis-flap) for related options.

## Tissue choice and anatomy

A cadaver study of 15 scrotums described two main vascular systems, the inferior external pudendal arteries and the perineal arteries, supplying three widely anastomosed cutaneous territories. Each lateral territory receives an inferior external pudendal artery that enters at the midpoint of the scrotal root and fans out over the hemiscrotum. The central territory is supplied by the continuation of the perineal arteries, which run deep on both sides of the septum. This redundancy supports flap design, but it does not guarantee an undamaged pedicle after prior surgery, trauma or infection.<sup>[[5]](#ref5)</sup>

| Tissue | Reconstructive role | Distinction |
|---|---|---|
| **Scrotal dartos** | Vascularized interposition over a closed urethra. | Fibroadipose tissue between scrotal skin and tunica vaginalis, with a pedicle at the penoscrotal angle that reaches the distal shaft without tension. It is not the tunica vaginalis surrounding the testis.<sup>[[2]](#ref2)[[6]](#ref6)</sup> |
| **De-epithelialized scrotal skin** | Reinforcement outside the urethral lumen. | Retains vascularized underlying tissue after removing the surface epithelium.<sup>[[3]](#ref3)</sup> |
| **Septocutaneous skin flap** | Selected reconstruction of urethral lining and/or an external defect. | This introduces different concerns, including intraluminal hair when skin forms the urethra.<sup>[[4]](#ref4)[[8]](#ref8)</sup> |
| **Local turnover dartos flap** | Hinged tissue beside the fistula is turned over the urethral closure. | In Ahuja's description the overlying skin may come from the penile shaft or scrotum. The interposition flap is not necessarily a scrotal flap.<sup>[[7]](#ref7)</sup> |

Assess fistula number and size, distal obstruction, associated urethral loss, local and donor tissue quality, flap reach and prior operative scars. A flap should reach without traction or twisting. Previous inguinal or scrotal surgery warrants assessment of both dartos and tunica options. It does not automatically establish that one is unavailable and the other unaffected.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[6]](#ref6)[[10]](#ref10)</sup>

## Operative principles for interposition

1. **Plan the urethral repair first.** Define the tract and any associated obstruction or breakdown. Calibration or cystoscopy excludes distal stricture, which Landau's series did before every repair. Primary fistula closure and reconstruction of a larger urethral defect are different procedures.<sup>[[10]](#ref10)</sup>
2. **Select and mobilize viable tissue.** Preserve the chosen flap's vascular base and donor skin perfusion. Scrotal dartos can provide a pedicled layer when local penile dartos is scarred or deficient.
3. **Transfer with adequate reach.** Bring the flap to the urethral closure without pedicle compression, twist, penile rotation or excessive traction.
4. **Cover the repair.** Secure vascularized tissue over the relevant urethral suture line with fine absorbable sutures. Extend the flap beyond the suture line and offset the urethral, flap and skin closures. Avoid unnecessary bulk or luminal compression.
5. **Close and reassess.** Obtain tension-free superficial coverage, evaluate donor and recipient perfusion, and choose drainage according to the underlying repair.<sup>[[2]](#ref2)[[3]](#ref3)[[6]](#ref6)[[10]](#ref10)</sup>

Scrotal dartos harvest usually avoids opening the tunica vaginalis. Tunica vaginalis harvest involves a different dissection around the testis and cord. Neither distinction establishes zero donor-site risk or a universal need for orchiopexy in every technique. See the dedicated tunica vaginalis page for its operative approach.<sup>[[2]](#ref2)[[10]](#ref10)</sup>

### Scrotal dartos flap (Churchill)

The dartos flap is fibroadipose tissue between the scrotal skin and the tunica vaginalis, based on a pedicle at the penoscrotal angle. Churchill and colleagues used it in eight children with repeat hypospadias or complex urethral repairs.<sup>[[2]](#ref2)</sup> The usual sequence in this and similar techniques is:

1. Excise the fistula tract and close the urethral defect in an inverting fashion with fine absorbable suture.
2. Make a transverse or oblique incision on the ipsilateral hemiscrotum, elevate the scrotal skin off the dartos, and raise a tongue-shaped flap that keeps the penoscrotal pedicle.
3. Transpose the flap to the penile shaft, lay it over the urethral suture line and fix it with fine absorbable sutures.
4. Close the scrotal and penile skin over the flap, keeping the suture lines offset.

### De-epithelialized scrotal skin flap (Lee)

Lee and colleagues reinforced the urethral closure with a de-epithelialized scrotal skin flap. The urethral defect is closed primarily. Adjacent scrotal skin large enough to cover the site is de-epithelialized with its dermis and dartos intact, rotated or advanced over the closure, and the donor site is closed by advancing scrotal skin.<sup>[[3]](#ref3)</sup>

### De-epithelialized turnover dartos flap (Ahuja)

Ahuja circumscribed the fistula and closed the inner skin edges with an inverting subcuticular stitch as the urethral layer. A flap marked next to the incision was de-epithelialized, raised with its dartos fascia, turned over the first layer on a hinge of tissue around the defect, and secured. A long skin flap from the shaft or scrotum, or a pants-over-vest skin closure, completed the repair. The urethral catheter stayed for 3 to 4 days.<sup>[[7]](#ref7)</sup>

## Results with their actual populations

| Source | Population and outcome | Limit |
|---|---|---|
| Lee 1990 | Eight UCF closures, mostly penoscrotal; no recurrence reported. Three were performed without urinary diversion or a urethral stent. | The same report also included 15 hypospadias reconstructions, three of which developed a fistula distal to the original opening. Do not combine all procedures as zero-fistula repairs.<sup>[[3]](#ref3)</sup> |
| Churchill 1996 | Eight complex reconstructions included six repeat hypospadias operations; no fistula was reported in those six at one year. | The other two cases had different diagnoses. This is not eight successful isolated UCF closures, and reported cosmetic success was not universal.<sup>[[2]](#ref2)</sup> |
| Muruganandham 2010 | Twenty-one patients with 2–4 mm fistulas received scrotal dartos coverage; two recurred during the study's mean 3.5-year follow-up. | Technique was selected by defect size. Comparison with smaller fistulas receiving simple closure or larger defects receiving tunica vaginalis is confounded.<sup>[[6]](#ref6)</sup> |
| Shankar 2002 | In 113 children, primary fistula repair succeeded in 71%. Tunica vaginalis or scrotal dartos was used only from the third repair onward and succeeded in five of six. | The authors advised covering recurrent fistulas with tissue from an unscarred area. The six cases are too few to compare flaps.<sup>[[14]](#ref14)</sup> |
| Ahuja 2009 | Ten patients received a local de-epithelialized turnover flap. Nine healed without complications; one residual pinpoint fistula subsequently closed spontaneously. | Final closure in all ten is not ten immediately uncomplicated repairs, nor evidence specific to scrotal harvest.<sup>[[7]](#ref7)</sup> |

These studies support feasibility in selected patients. They do not establish a universal size-based hierarchy, superiority to tunica vaginalis, or freedom from testicular or scrotal complications.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup>

## Scrotal septum flaps and skin used as urethral lining

Scrotal septum flaps were developed for complex urethral reconstruction and are a different operation from interposition over a closed urethra.

- **Sakai septocutaneous island flap.** The flap is supplied by septal vascular networks from both perineal and posterior scrotal arteries and is divided into a component for the urethral lining and one for the skin defect. Hairless scrotal skin must be used for the lining.<sup>[[4]](#ref4)</sup>
- **Yachia pedicled scrotal flap.** A one-stage bulbomembranous reconstruction in which the septum used as a pedicle gives the flap good mobility, so the method appears applicable anywhere along the urethra.<sup>[[13]](#ref13)</sup>
- **Mokhless scrotal septum pedicled flap.** Used in seven patients with complex hypospadias. Results were satisfactory in five. Distal stricture developed in two, one of whom had a subcoronal fistula, and minor hair growth occurred in one.<sup>[[8]](#ref8)</sup>
- **Gil-Vernet biaxial epilated scrotal flap.** A perineum-based flap with biaxial supply from both superficial perineal arteries and a possible length of up to 20 cm. Thirty-seven men with complex urethral stenosis had one of three urethroplasty techniques based on the flap, and 86% achieved normal voiding after one stage at a mean follow-up of 39.5 months. It is not a UCF-closure cohort.<sup>[[9]](#ref9)</sup>
- **Scrotal island flap urethroplasty.** In 20 men with bulbomembranous stricture, 16 had satisfactory results and 4 needed revision for stricture, diverticulum or fistula. The authors stressed hairless skin and aggressive tailoring of the flap.<sup>[[16]](#ref16)</sup>

Jordan argued that scrotal and perineal skin do not differ from other genital skin in permeability, and that properly prepared islands are no more likely to form diverticula. Hair is imported into the urethra if the flap is raised from hirsute skin, and hairless scrotal areas can be used instead.<sup>[[15]](#ref15)</sup>

The EAU urethral-stricture guideline (2026) gives a strong recommendation not to use hair-bearing perineal or scrotal flaps unless no other option is feasible, because they are associated with hairball formation and chronic infection. Epilation techniques do not make untreated hair-bearing skin a routine urethral substitute. A dartos layer placed outside an intact closed urethra serves a different purpose and does not line the urinary lumen, and hair is not a concern for it.<sup>[[12]](#ref12)[[15]](#ref15)</sup>

## Pooled results and comparison with other layers

The 2023 systematic review of UCF repair after hypospadias (71 studies, 2,886 patients, 539 failures) reported these pooled success proportions. The studies are heterogeneous, and the authors concluded that no technique could be labeled ideal.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup>

| Technique | Pooled success |
|---|---|
| Scrotal flaps | 94.6% |
| Tunica vaginalis | 94.3% |
| PATIO repair | 93.5% |
| Biomaterials or dermal substitutes | 92% |
| Double dartos flaps | 81% |
| Dartos | 78.8% |
| Simple primary closure | 73.2% |
| Biocompatible adhesives | 56.5% |
| Skin-based flaps | 54.5% |

The scrotal and tunica vaginalis figures are close, and neither establishes superiority. The tunica vaginalis flap needs testicular delivery and fixation, and in a randomized trial of primary hypospadias repair testicular ascent occurred in 3 of 41 children at 12 months with the tunica vaginalis flap and none with a dartos flap. That trial excluded prior orchiopexy and inguinal surgery and did not test scrotal dartos. See the dedicated page for details.<sup>[[17]](#ref17)</sup>

## Where scrotal flaps fit

- A 2–4 mm fistula: Muruganandham used a flip flap with scrotal dartos wrapping, with two recurrences in 21 patients.<sup>[[6]](#ref6)</sup>
- A recurrent fistula or a hypospadias cripple with fibrotic penile tissue: unscarred tissue from outside the penile field is preferred, and Shankar's series reserved tunica vaginalis or scrotal dartos for the third and later repairs.<sup>[[14]](#ref14)</sup>
- A penoscrotal or proximal fistula: the pedicle lies next to the site.<sup>[[2]](#ref2)[[6]](#ref6)</sup>
- Prior orchiopexy or inguinal surgery: dartos is often chosen over tunica vaginalis, though the choice rests on surgeon judgment and no comparison has tested it.<sup>[[10]](#ref10)</sup>
- Long or panurethral reconstruction: septum flaps are used, not dartos interposition.<sup>[[9]](#ref9)[[13]](#ref13)</sup>

Limits of the evidence: dartos and scrotal flap data for UCF are case series without randomized comparisons. Cosmesis was rated excellent in 84% of Churchill's six evaluable patients, and scrotal skin moved to the shaft may differ in color, texture and thickness.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[2]](#ref2)</sup>

## Donor-site risk and follow-up

Discuss hematoma, infection, skin or flap compromise, tethering, cosmetic change, recurrent fistula and possible further surgery. Small UCF series that observed no testicular injury cannot exclude that risk. A separate 22-patient study of bilateral scrotal flaps for penile shaft skin replacement reported testicular ascent in 22.7%; that rate must not be assigned to scrotal dartos interposition for UCF.<sup>[[6]](#ref6)[[11]](#ref11)</sup>

Drainage and recovery restrictions depend on the urethral repair, contamination, tissue condition and surgeon's protocol. Some small repairs have been managed without diversion; neither a compulsory catheter duration nor routine suprapubic drainage follows from the scrotal-flap literature. Follow healing, urinary stream, donor-site recovery and symptoms of recurrence or obstruction.<sup>[[3]](#ref3)[[6]](#ref6)[[10]](#ref10)</sup>
