# Transperineal Approach to Rectourethral Fistula

Transperineal repair provides access to the urinary–rectal defect while preserving the anal sphincter complex, and permits simultaneous urethral reconstruction and vascularized interposition. It is commonly used at specialist centers, including for selected radiation-associated and recurrent rectourethral fistulas (RUFs). A 2013 systematic review of 416 patients reported 65.9% perineal, 15.7% transsphincteric, 12.5% transabdominal and 5.9% transanal repairs in its categorized series.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup> Interposition flaps, predominantly gracilis, were used in 72% of repairs, the fistula closed in 87.5%, and most high-volume centers (25 or more patients) used transperineal repair with a flap in every case.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup> Contemporary reviews continue to describe frequent use of perineal repair with gracilis.<sup>[[2]](#ref2)[[3]](#ref3)</sup> In a 2026 four-center retrospective cohort, transperineal repair with gracilis interposition (n = 30) had higher 12-month freedom from reintervention than York-Mason repair (n = 27; 94.7% versus 68.2%) and higher first-attempt closure (77% versus 44%). Fistulas &gt;2 cm were more frequent with York-Mason repair (16/27 [59%] versus 10/30 [33%] with transperineal gracilis repair); median follow-up was 27 versus 53 months, respectively.<sup>[[29]](#ref29)</sup>

For flap anatomy and harvest see [gracilis](/docs/foundations/surgical-principles/flaps/gracilis). Other options include [York-Mason](/docs/surgical-techniques/04h-fistula-repair/male/york-mason), [endorectal advancement](/docs/surgical-techniques/04h-fistula-repair/male/eraf-ruf), [transanal MIS](/docs/surgical-techniques/04h-fistula-repair/transanal-minimally-invasive-repair), [abdominal or combined repair](/docs/surgical-techniques/04h-fistula-repair/male/transabdominal-ruf-rvf) and [conservative management](/docs/surgical-techniques/04h-fistula-repair/male/conservative-ruf-management).

## Selection and Preparation

Perineal repair is an option for postsurgical, radiation- or ablation-associated, traumatic and recurrent RUF when the defect can be exposed and viable urinary and rectal reconstruction is possible. Associated posterior stenosis or tissue loss can often be addressed concurrently, although combined approaches may also reconstruct the outlet. Concurrent stricture was present in 11% of nonirradiated and 28% of radiation- or ablation-associated fistulas in the Vanni series, and a stricture or bladder-neck contracture in 14% after prostatectomy alone and 26% after energy ablation in the multicenter Harris series.<sup>[[4]](#ref4)</sup><sup>[[6]](#ref6)</sup> The reported incidence of rectourethral fistula after radical prostatectomy ranges from under 0.01% to 1.5%.<sup>[[3]](#ref3)[[4]](#ref4)[[5]](#ref5)[[6]](#ref6)</sup>

Evaluate fistula location and size, cavitation, bladder and urethral function, continence, rectal disease, radiation injury and previous repairs. Use cystoscopy, urethrography or cystography and rectal examination or endoscopy to define anatomy; MRI can assist in complex irradiated disease. Investigate or biopsy suspicious tissue when recurrent malignancy is a concern. Assess urinary drainage, infection, nutritional status, functional reserve and the patient's goals before definitive repair.<sup>[[3]](#ref3)[[7]](#ref7)</sup> Lin and colleagues recommend a recent screening colonoscopy before staged repair to rule out neoplasm; treat this as their preoperative step rather than a validated requirement for every patient.<sup>[[26]](#ref26)</sup>

Diversion and timing are individualized. Severe fecal contamination, sepsis, damaged tissue or major colorectal reconstruction often favor fecal diversion. Thomas' small series associated fecaluria with failure without diversion. Keller used selective diversion (67% of 30 patients; suprapubic catheter in 47%); its 8/30 spontaneous closures without fecal diversion and 6/30 after diversion are proportions of the whole cohort, not comparative treatment-success rates.<sup>[[8]](#ref8)</sup> In the Harris multicenter series, 84% of the ablation group and 65% of the prostatectomy group were diverted before repair.<sup>[[6]](#ref6)[[8]](#ref8)[[9]](#ref9)</sup>

Allow inflammation to settle and optimize tissues, with planned reassessment rather than a mandatory three-to-six-month wait for every patient. Gupta's minimum 12-week interval and other centers' longer catheter or diversion intervals are institutional protocols in selected cohorts. Do not prolong observation through uncontrolled sepsis or inadequate drainage.<sup>[[3]](#ref3)[[10]](#ref10)[[7]](#ref7)</sup>

For a very high or inaccessible defect, severe cavitation, nonfunctional bladder or rectum or extensive tissue loss, discuss combined abdominal access, resection or permanent diversion. Radiation alone does not preclude repair, and a failed repair does not automatically mandate extirpation.<sup>[[4]](#ref4)[[11]](#ref11)[[6]](#ref6)</sup>

## Operative Framework

### Exposure and separation

Lithotomy facilitates perineal exposure and gracilis harvest; some centers use prone positioning or change position after flap harvest. Positioning and incision should suit the fistula and planned reconstruction. A midline perineal incision is commonly described, but its exact length and configuration are not standardized.<sup>[[12]](#ref12)[[7]](#ref7)</sup> Duldulao describes an alternative X- or H-shaped incision placed in the perineal body about 1 cm anterior to the anal verge and posterior to the scrotal base, cautioning against extending the lateral dissection too far, where distal pudendal branches enter; Lin and colleagues describe an inverted-U incision for lateral rectal-wall exposure. Both are author-described alternatives to a midline incision rather than a standardized configuration.<sup>[[27]](#ref27)[[26]](#ref26)</sup>

Solomon's published technique illustrates one sequence. The patient is in a modified Lloyd-Davies position, and a vertical midline incision runs from the base of the scrotum to 2 cm anterior to the anal verge. Dissection follows the anterior rectal wall through Denonvilliers fascia to the fistula and continues 1 to 2 cm above it. The urethral opening is closed vertically and the rectal opening horizontally with interrupted 3/0 and 4/0 polyglactin.<sup>[[13]](#ref13)</sup>

Expose the bulbar or posterior urethral region and develop the plane along the anterior rectal wall, preserving the anal sphincter. Mobilize enough healthy tissue above and around the fistula to permit separate tension-free closures. Fibrosis can obscure normal planes; avoid treating a fixed dissection distance or a named fascial plane as a substitute for direct identification.<sup>[[13]](#ref13)[[7]](#ref7)[[14]](#ref14)</sup> When localization is difficult, passing a guidewire or small open-ended catheter through the fistula, and a guidewire with a Council-tip catheter in the urethra, can help mark the lumen; cystoscopic transillumination into the field, a digital rectal examination or flexible sigmoidoscopy are further author-described aids for identifying the urethra, fistula and tissue plane while sparing the sphincter, though no comparison shows they improve closure or reduce injury.<sup>[[26]](#ref26)</sup>

Excise the tract and nonviable margins, preserving usable tissue. Radiation exposure does not mean all irradiated tissue should be removed. Assess the separate rectal and urinary defects before committing to the closure method.<sup>[[4]](#ref4)[[7]](#ref7)</sup>

### Rectal closure

Close viable rectal tissue without tension or luminal narrowing. Transverse (horizontal) closure with interrupted absorbable suture, sometimes in two layers, is described in perineal series; orientation and layers depend on the actual defect. Large defects, poor rectal function or diseased bowel may require a different colorectal reconstruction or diversion rather than forced primary closure.<sup>[[13]](#ref13)[[7]](#ref7)[[14]](#ref14)</sup> Submerging the field and performing flexible sigmoidoscopy to check for an air leak after closure has been described as an intraoperative integrity check, without diagnostic-accuracy or outcome data establishing it as required for every repair.<sup>[[26]](#ref26)</sup>

### Urinary reconstruction

| Finding | Option and qualification |
|---|---|
| **Small defect without important stenosis** | Primary closure in viable tissue, maintaining urethral caliber |
| **Short posterior stenosis or obliteration** | Excision and anastomotic urethroplasty when healthy ends can meet without tension |
| **Larger defect or stenosis suitable for augmentation** | Selective buccal-mucosal graft reconstruction with an adequate vascular bed/support; a graft does not compensate for nonviable tissue |
| **PFUI with urethrorectal fistula** | Trauma-specific posterior reconstruction; a progressive perineal approach may suffice below the verumontanum, with corporal separation or inferior pubectomy when needed for reach. The 2026 EAU guideline recommends a midline perineal incision (strong) and a local tissue flap after concomitant fistula repair (weak), reserves combined abdominoperineal reconstruction for complicated cases (weak), and advises against total pubectomy (strong).<sup>[[30]](#ref30)</sup> |
| **Major cavitation or prostatic/outlet destruction** | Consider more extensive reconstruction, combined access, selective prostatectomy or diversion according to the remaining bladder and outlet |

Published choices vary with tissue integrity, defect extent and previous treatment. Neither BMG nor pubectomy is mandatory for every irradiated or long defect. See the [urethral reconstruction atlas](/docs/surgical-techniques/urethral-reconstruction) for urethroplasty techniques.<sup>[[4]](#ref4)[[5]](#ref5)[[7]](#ref7)[[15]](#ref15)[[16]](#ref16)</sup>

### Vascularized interposition

Strongly consider healthy tissue between urinary and rectal closures for radiation or ablation injury, recurrent repairs, dead space and tenuous tissue. Gracilis is a common option, not the sole acceptable flap. Its use does not substitute for source control, viable margins and a tension-free repair.<sup>[[4]](#ref4)[[12]](#ref12)[[7]](#ref7)</sup>

Protect the dominant pedicle during harvest and bring the muscle through a tunnel that avoids compression, twisting or excessive tension. Position it to separate and cover the repair. Exact pedicle anatomy and flap reach must be identified in the patient; see the [gracilis atlas](/docs/foundations/surgical-principles/flaps/gracilis) rather than relying on a fixed distance from a bony landmark.<sup>[[12]](#ref12)[[17]](#ref17)</sup> Securing the distal muscle end to the repair has been described with a nonabsorbable suture, or with several interrupted 3-0 absorbable braided sutures, including fixation to a buccal graft when one is used; neither fixation method has been compared against the other for closure outcome.<sup>[[28]](#ref28)[[26]](#ref26)</sup>

The comparative evidence is limited:

- **Park 2022:** recurrence was 8% in 24 gracilis cases versus 50% in 12 controls. This was retrospective, and risk factors were examined using single-variable, not multivariable, regression.<sup>[[18]](#ref18)</sup>
- **Voelzke 2013:** among 13 energy-ablative cases, success was 3/5 with a flap and 5/8 without on the reported intent-to-treat analysis. Two flap patients died early and were counted as failures; the three surviving flap patients succeeded. This cannot support an uncomplicated causal “100% versus 63%” benefit or prove equivalence. The authors still strongly favored considering interposition in damaged tissue.<sup>[[7]](#ref7)</sup>
- **Garoufalia 2023:** pooled weighted success was 79.4% across 658 complex perineal fistulas, only 33.7% of which were rectourethral; heterogeneity was substantial. This is not an RUF-specific expected cure rate.<sup>[[19]](#ref19)</sup>

Selected nonirradiated postoperative fistulas have healed without a flap (in the Voelzke series a dartos flap was used in 2 of 10 postoperative repairs, all 10 healed), but omission should follow the actual tissue and suture-line assessment. The 72% interposition figure in the older review included all flap types, predominantly gracilis, rather than 72% gracilis use alone.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[7]](#ref7)</sup>

### Other flaps

Dartos, bulbospongiosus, rectus and local options can be considered according to available vascularized tissue. Small reports cannot establish interchangeable outcomes. Solomon's bilateral puborectalis technique involved four patients, with three bowel-diversion reversals and one permanent ileostomy; this limited experience does not remove the need to assess sphincter function.<sup>[[13]](#ref13)[[7]](#ref7)</sup>

Hou's 2025 BSM series included 19 traumatic fistulas and 17 cases of anterior rectal-wall weakness. Its 94.4% overall reconstructive success is not a 36-patient fistula-closure rate. Nonsignificant functional comparisons were reported against selected non-BSM urethroplasty controls.<sup>[[20]](#ref20)</sup>

Dartos can also be used with a transsphincteric York-Mason repair: Dafnis reported five such cases. Thus, the presence of interposition alone does not distinguish a perineal repair from every alternative route.<sup>[[21]](#ref21)</sup>

## Outcomes in the Relevant Population

| Cohort | Result | Qualification |
|---|---|---|
| **Vanni 2010, 74 patients** | One-procedure closure at mean follow-up 20 months: 35/35 nonirradiated and 84% of 39 radiation/ablation-associated cases | All had muscle interposition, with BMG used selectively; 31% of the radiation/ablation group required permanent fecal diversion <sup>[[4]](#ref4)</sup> |
| **Kaufman 2016, 98 patients** | One-procedure closure at median follow-up 14.5 months (range 3–144): 48/49 nonirradiated and 42/49 radiation/ablation-associated cases | Bowel continuity restored in 45/48 and 30/46, respectively. These are separate denominators from all repaired patients <sup>[[22]](#ref22)</sup> |
| **Sbizzera 2022, 21 patients** | Fistula closure in 20/21 at median follow-up 27 months; digestive stoma closure in 10/12 diverted patients | Two grade ≥IIIb complications; 18 completed questionnaires, including 11/18 with significant urinary incontinence <sup>[[12]](#ref12)</sup> |
| **Wexner 2008, 36 male RUF cases within 53 perineal fistulas** | Initial male RUF success 78%, overall clinical healing 97% after further procedures | Seventeen men had postoperative complications; initial versus final healing must remain distinct <sup>[[23]](#ref23)</sup> |

Sources: original reports.<sup>[[4]](#ref4)[[22]](#ref22)[[12]](#ref12)[[23]](#ref23)</sup>

Kaufman also reported 30-day complication rates of 29% in both groups and 90-day rates of 2% in nonirradiated and 24% in irradiated patients.<sup>[[22]](#ref22)</sup> Gupta's 15 patients (7 iatrogenic, 5 congenital, 3 traumatic) all closed with perineal gracilis repair after diversion; two had minimal leakage that resolved after 8 weeks of catheterization, and one had a gracilis extrusion. Muñoz-Duyos closed 9 of 9 postprostatectomy fistulas in nonirradiated patients (median follow-up 54 months), and fecal diversion was closed in 6 of the 7 patients who had a stoma.<sup>[[10]](#ref10)[[17]](#ref17)[[22]](#ref22)</sup>

Vanni and Kaufman share an institution and overlapping recruitment periods. Their selected reconstructive results also differ from Linder's severe radiation or ablation referral population, in which 25/29 needed permanent colostomy and 27/29 permanent urinary diversion.<sup>[[11]](#ref11)</sup> The differing case mixes limit technique comparisons and prognosis estimates for irradiated patients.<sup>[[4]](#ref4)[[22]](#ref22)[[11]](#ref11)</sup> A later 53-man prostate-treatment gracilis series from Cleveland Clinic Florida reported 28/53 (52.8%) primary healing and 43/53 (81.1%) after further procedures; its 2000–2018 enrollment may overlap Wexner's 1995–2007 cohort and should not be pooled with it.<sup>[[23]](#ref23)[[32]](#ref32)</sup>

### Concurrent posterior urethroplasty

Khouri reported 23 concurrent reconstructions within 130 RUF repairs: 22 used a perineal approach and one a prone Kraske approach; 18 had anastomotic urethroplasty and five BMG. At median 55.7 months, 20/23 had successful fistula closure, and no isolated stricture recurrence requiring instrumentation was reported. Fourteen reported incontinence and 7/23 received an AUS (30% of the full cohort, or half of the 14 incontinent men).<sup>[[5]](#ref5)</sup>

For PFUI, Guo reported 29/32 overall successful reconstructions with gracilis: 17 of 18 with perineal anastomosis and corporal separation, and 12 of 14 with the addition of inferior pubectomy.<sup>[[16]](#ref16)</sup> All 22 patients without a previous repair succeeded, but only 7 of 10 with a prior failed urethroplasty or fistula repair. Two developed isolated recurrent strictures and one a recurrent fistula with stricture. Xu's 31 traumatic strictures with urethrorectal fistula were repaired by a simple perineal (4/4 closed), inferior pubectomy (16/18) or combined transpubic-perineal approach (7/9), with bulbospongiosus and dartos flaps in 22 patients, rectus flaps in eight and a gracilis flap in one.<sup>[[15]](#ref15)</sup> Wang's separate 2024 proximal-transection series reported 36/40 success at median 45 months, with postoperative ED in 75%.<sup>[[24]](#ref24)</sup> Its definition combined urethral patency, absence of leakage and no further intervention; it was not a comparison with distal transection and does not quantify new ED caused by repair.<sup>[[15]](#ref15)[[16]](#ref16)[[24]](#ref24)</sup>

## Function, Follow-up and Patient Expectations

Plan urinary drainage and assessment of healing according to the repair. Voelzke's transperineal series and Yankelevich's gracilis series used both urethral and suprapubic catheters.<sup>[[7]](#ref7)[[31]](#ref31)</sup> Reported catheter protocols vary, including three-week assessment and four-to-six-week cystography. Persistent leakage may require continued drainage and reassessment; in a retrospective 22-patient gracilis series, 5/12 with leakage on a four-week cystogram had no leak after four more weeks of drainage.<sup>[[31]](#ref31)</sup> Bowel continuity should be restored only after healing and function have been evaluated, rather than automatically at a fixed postoperative month.<sup>[[7]](#ref7)[[25]](#ref25)[[14]](#ref14)</sup>

No RUF-specific VTE recommendation exists. Assess VTE and bleeding risks for the actual procedure and patient, including age, obesity, prior VTE, active malignancy, prolonged operation and immobility; see [VTE Prophylaxis](/docs/foundations/pharmacology/perioperative-eras/vte-prophylaxis) for regimen selection. The 2024 European urology guideline identifies prior VTE, age and BMI as primary patient VTE factors and states that risk for most other urologic procedures varies with procedure and patient factors.<sup>[[33]](#ref33)</sup>

Anatomic closure and functional recovery are different outcomes. Counsel about urinary incontinence, bowel dysfunction, erectile dysfunction, perineal or donor-site symptoms, recurrent stenosis, catheter needs and later procedures. Prior cancer treatment or trauma may already have impaired function; studies without baseline measures cannot attribute all postoperative impairment to the fistula operation.<sup>[[5]](#ref5)[[12]](#ref12)[[25]](#ref25)[[14]](#ref14)</sup>

In Hampson's 21-patient series, 15 had postoperative urinary incontinence and 11 of those 15 received an AUS. Fifteen of the 21 were contacted for the long-term survey: 12/15 reported some urinary leakage, 8/15 perineal pain and 12/15 a positive life change. None of those respondents would have chosen complete urinary diversion instead, but responder selection and the absence of a standardized preoperative quality-of-life comparison limit generalization. Hampson also reported 95% success after the initial repair, a 19% 30-day complication rate (3 of 4 grade III or higher), and problems related to the gracilis flap in 6/15 long-term survey respondents (40%). Do not present these as the preferences or outcomes of every repair candidate.<sup>[[14]](#ref14)</sup>

Among the 18 of 21 Sbizzera patients who completed the postoperative questionnaire, the mean St Mark's fecal incontinence score was 5/24 and mean patient satisfaction was 9/10.<sup>[[12]](#ref12)</sup> Among 17 Wagner patients who completed postoperative questionnaires, median Wexner score was 3 and median Decision Regret Scale score was 0; median operating time in the 29-patient open-repair cohort was 90 minutes.<sup>[[25]](#ref25)</sup>

Wagner 2026 adds long-term follow-up with validated questionnaires: 29 open repairs included 26 perineal and three abdominal operations. Six patients were lost to follow-up; among the 23 included in survival analysis, estimated five-year freedom from fistula recurrence was 96%, while freedom from any disease-related reintervention was 75%. Seventeen completed PROMs, reporting high satisfaction but residual urinary and bowel symptoms. These data support assessing both closure and subsequent treatment burden; they are not a 29-patient, pure-gracilis or pure-perineal cure rate.<sup>[[25]](#ref25)</sup>
