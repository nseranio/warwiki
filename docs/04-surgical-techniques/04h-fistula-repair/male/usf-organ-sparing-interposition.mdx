# Organ-Sparing USF Repair with Interposition Flap

Organ-sparing repair of a urosymphyseal fistula (USF) or anterior prostatosymphyseal fistula closes the urinary defect and debrides the pubic symphysis while retaining the bladder and prostate. A vascularized flap is placed between the urinary repair and the debrided bone. Kaufman and colleagues described the operation with a rectus abdominis muscle flap in four irradiated men with pubic osteomyelitis. The series shows feasibility in selected patients. It does not show that preservation is the least morbid option or that voiding will be normal afterward.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup>

See [USF / PPF Bladder-Sparing Approaches](/docs/surgical-techniques/04h-fistula-repair/male/usf-ppf-bladder-sparing) for selection, [salvage prostatectomy](/docs/surgical-techniques/04h-fistula-repair/male/salvage-prostatectomy-usf) for a more extensive reconstruction, and the [male fistula atlas](/docs/surgical-techniques/04h-fistula-repair/male-fistula) for diversion options.

## Rationale

The fistula connects the prostatic urethra or bladder neck to the symphysis. Three problems are addressed in one operation: infected bone is removed, the urinary defect is closed, and healthy tissue is interposed to separate the two in a radiation-compromised field. In a series of urorectal fistulas after prostate cancer treatment, post-irradiation fistulas and fistulas with cavitation required an interposition flap.<sup>[[9]](#ref9)</sup>

The surgical literature for USF consists of small retrospective series and case reports. A 2025 review found no professional-society guidance on evaluation, antimicrobial regimens or technique, and it considered debridement with cystectomy, pubectomy and urinary diversion the most definitive cure.<sup>[[3]](#ref3)</sup> In a Leuven series of 26 men treated for USF with pubic osteomyelitis after prostate cancer treatment, 19 had cystectomy, 5 had bladder-sparing surgery and 2 had conservative treatment. Conservative treatment succeeded only in men who had not been irradiated. Reinterventions or severe complications occurred in 56.5%, and all patients were free of fistula and osteomyelitis after up to four treatment attempts.<sup>[[13]](#ref13)</sup> Organ preservation therefore applies to a selected minority.

## Patient Selection

Assess bladder capacity and symptoms, urethral patency, baseline continence, fistula extent, tissue viability, prior treatment and the patient's ability to tolerate reconstruction. A patent or reconstructable outlet and a useful bladder are the main requirements for preservation. Extensive necrosis, severe radiation cystitis or a poorly functioning outlet may favor another reconstruction or cystectomy with diversion. No single percentage or prostate-cancer treatment history defines eligibility.<sup>[[2]](#ref2)[[3]](#ref3)</sup>

Favorable features in the described series:

- A localized, well-defined anterior tract without extensive cavitation or multiple tracts.
- Adequate bladder function, without severe radiation cystitis.
- A sphincter that can maintain continence after repair.
- No distal urethral obstruction.
- A urinary defect small enough to close in layers without tension.

In the Escandón series, patients with distal urethral obstruction, radiation cystitis or a non-viable sphincter underwent anterior exenteration with flap transfer instead of a bladder-sparing repair.<sup>[[4]](#ref4)</sup> Cavitation, bladder neck contracture and ischemia from sequential energy sources (external beam, brachytherapy, cryotherapy or HIFU) mark complex fistulas in the urorectal literature, and the same features generally argue against preservation.<sup>[[9]](#ref9)</sup>

Preserving the prostate does not treat every radiation-associated stenosis or sphincter problem. Discuss possible persistent incontinence, repeat outlet procedures and eventual diversion even if the fistula closes.<sup>[[2]](#ref2)[[6]](#ref6)</sup>

## Operative Principles

1. **Define the urinary and bone disease.** MRI, urinary-tract imaging and endoscopic assessment guide exposure and the feasibility of preserving the outlet. Involve orthopedic, infectious-disease and reconstructive colleagues as needed.<sup>[[2]](#ref2)[[3]](#ref3)</sup>
2. **Debride infected and nonviable tissue.** The extent of pubic resection follows the disease, from partial symphysectomy to near-complete pubectomy. Pay particular attention to stability after prior pelvic-ring injury or insufficiency fracture. Send bone and tissue for microbiology and pathology. In Andrews's cohort of 25 irradiated men, urine and bone cultures were discordant in 21 of 22 paired samples, so bone cultures guide antibiotic therapy.<sup>[[3]](#ref3)[[7]](#ref7)</sup>
3. **Repair a viable urinary defect without tension.** Confirm that distal obstruction and major tissue loss have been addressed. Close the defect in layers with absorbable suture and choose urinary drainage to fit the remaining anatomy.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[2]](#ref2)</sup>
4. **Interpose vascularized tissue.** In the Kaufman series a rectus muscle flap separated the urinary repair from the debrided symphysis. Plan a viable pedicle, sufficient reach and obliteration of dead space, together with donor-site closure.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[3]](#ref3)</sup>

### Sequence as usually described

The following sequence is the approach of the Kaufman and Vanni group as described in reviews. Exposure and closure details vary by surgeon.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[3]](#ref3)</sup>

1. Position the patient supine, with lithotomy access if a perineal step may be needed. Use a lower midline incision, divide the anterior rectus sheath and separate the rectus muscles to reach the retropubic space.
2. Enter the retropubic space, which is often obliterated by fibrosis or contains a urinoma. Identify the tract from the anterior prostatic urethra or bladder neck to the symphysis and define its relation to the bladder and prostate.
3. Debride the symphysis with rongeurs, curettes or osteotomes to healthy bleeding bone. Send cultures and pathology.
4. Excise the tract back to healthy tissue and close the prostatic urethra or bladder neck in layers. Confirm a watertight closure and leave a urethral catheter.
5. Mobilize the interposition flap (see below) and secure it between the urinary closure and the bone bed, obliterating dead space.
6. Place closed-suction drains beside the flap, close the rectus sheath and close the wound in layers.

### Rectus abdominis muscle flap

The rectus is mobilized from the posterior sheath on the inferior epigastric pedicle, divided superiorly, rotated inferiorly and transposed into the retropubic space. It lies next to the operative field, needs no extra incision and provides a large volume of vascularized muscle. It can be harvested without a skin paddle. Robertson and colleagues proposed the rectus flap as an alternative to omentum when prior abdominal surgery precludes omental mobilization and reported no adverse functional or cosmetic abdominal-wall result in four patients.<sup>[[5]](#ref5)</sup>

A muscle-only rectus flap differs from a skin-bearing vertical rectus abdominis myocutaneous (VRAM) flap, which leaves a larger abdominal-wall defect. In a retrospective series of VRAM reconstructions after radical pelvic surgery, the donor-site hernia rate was 16.7% and the perineal hernia rate 3.3%.<sup>[[8]](#ref8)</sup> Those data come from a different population, and a hernia rate for the muscle-only flap is not reported. For flap anatomy, see [Rectus Abdominis / VRAM](/docs/foundations/surgical-principles/flaps/vram). Assess prior incisions, pedicle integrity and available tissue before harvest.

## Choosing an Interposition

Omentum, rectus muscle and gracilis have all been used in anterior urinary fistula reconstruction. The choice depends on exposure, tissue availability and the cavity to fill. The TURNS cohort included both flap and no-flap bladder-preserving repairs, so it does not describe one standardized operation.<sup>[[2]](#ref2)</sup>

| Flap | Setting | Reported result |
|---|---|---|
| Omentum | Retropubic exposure with omentum available | In 34 USF repairs, 90-day sepsis was 3% and fistula recurrence was lower than after primary repair (odds ratio 0.012, P = 0.011). The advantage over VRAM was not shown for late recurrence.<sup>[[4]](#ref4)</sup> |
| Rectus muscle | Prior abdominal surgery, or omentum unavailable | Four irradiated patients closed after one operation at median 27 months.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup> |
| Gracilis | Perineal or posterior exposure | In 12 patients with complex, recurrent urogenital fistulas, mean operating time was 3 h 11 min with gracilis and 5 h 31 min with VRAM. Continence was restored in 7 of 7 evaluable gracilis patients and 3 of 3 evaluable VRAM patients had slight incontinence.<sup>[[10]](#ref10)</sup> |
| Perivesical fat | No omentum or rectus available | Described in three patients with other difficult fistulas, followed for 1 month to 3 years. It has not been reported for USF with osteomyelitis.<sup>[[12]](#ref12)</sup> |

The Escandón comparison is retrospective and includes local reconstruction and extirpative surgery, and it does not establish a universal flap hierarchy. Its 56 patients received primary repair (11), omentum (34) or VRAM (11). Sepsis at 90 days occurred in 18%, 3% and 27% (P = 0.041), and recurrence in 30% of primary repairs and 27.3% of VRAM repairs, with a lower rate after omentum (P = 0.04). A muscle-only rectus flap was not analyzed separately.<sup>[[4]](#ref4)</sup> See the [comparative-evidence discussion](/docs/surgical-techniques/04h-fistula-repair/male/usf-primary-repair-no-flap#comparative-evidence-2026).

## Outcomes and Follow-Up

| Outcome | Kaufman 2016 (n = 4) | TURNS 2017, bladder-sparing arm (n = 12) |
|---|---|---|
| Fistula closure | 4/4 with one procedure | 11/12; the recurrence was managed with cystectomy |
| Follow-up | Median 27 months | Pain assessed at 6 months in the whole cohort |
| Pain | Not stated in the abstract | Resolved in 26/31 (84%) of the whole cohort |
| Complications | Not stated in the abstract | Grade 3 events in four men at 30 days and two at 90 days, whole cohort |
| Prostatectomy or diversion | None required | One cystectomy, for the recurrence |

<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[2]](#ref2)</sup>

All four men in the Kaufman series had prior pelvic radiation and pubic osteomyelitis. The small size prevents conclusions on comparative morbidity, late durability or the probability of success in an individual patient. In TURNS, 12 of 31 men had bladder-preserving repair and 19 had cystectomy with diversion. Pain improvement applies to the overall cohort, not to the rectus-flap subgroup. Bladder preservation, closure, continence and avoidance of future procedures are separate outcomes.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[2]](#ref2)</sup>

Use operative cultures to guide antibiotic treatment. Antibiotic duration and route, catheter and imaging plans, drains, mobilization and abdominal-wall restrictions are individualized. A fixed six-to-eight-week antibiotic or weight-bearing schedule is not a validated protocol.<sup>[[3]](#ref3)[[7]](#ref7)</sup>

## Advantages and Limitations

Advantages of the organ-sparing approach:

- The bladder and prostate are kept, so diversion, an external appliance and prostatectomy in an irradiated field are avoided.
- The operation does not require bowel surgery.
- Native voiding is retained if the outlet functions.

Limitations:

- **Small experience.** The index series has four patients over 10 years at one high-volume center, and follow-up is a median of 27 months.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup>
- **Outlet dysfunction after closure.** The repair does not treat the radiation-damaged prostatic urethra. In a series of gracilis repairs of rectourinary and urinary-cutaneous fistulas, bladder outlet dysfunction (bladder neck contracture or stress incontinence) developed in 18 of 20 irradiated patients (90%) and 1 of 7 non-irradiated patients (P = 0.0006). The population was not USF.<sup>[[6]](#ref6)</sup>
- **Radiation and repair failure.** In 86 patients with enterourinary or urinary-cutaneous fistulas, radiation was associated with repair failure (P = 0.0002) and with permanent diversion at final follow-up in 72% of irradiated patients versus 7% of non-irradiated patients. These fistulas were not exclusively USF, and the repairs did not use muscle flaps.<sup>[[11]](#ref11)</sup>
- **Conservative management.** Andrews concluded that conservative management does not resolve symptoms in USF after radiation.<sup>[[7]](#ref7)</sup>

Counsel patients about possible eventual urinary diversion even when the fistula closes.<sup>[[6]](#ref6)[[11]](#ref11)</sup>
