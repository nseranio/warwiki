# Primary Repair (No Tissue Interposition) for USF

This page considers closure of a urosymphyseal fistula without vascularized tissue interposition. Terminology varies: the TURNS study used “primary repair” for bladder-preserving closure or urethroplasty with or without a flap. It is therefore incorrect to identify every published primary repair as a no-flap operation.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup>

For the treatment decision, see [USF / PPF Bladder-Sparing Approaches](/docs/surgical-techniques/04h-fistula-repair/male/usf-ppf-bladder-sparing). The flap-based alternative is [organ-sparing repair with interposition](/docs/surgical-techniques/04h-fistula-repair/male/usf-organ-sparing-interposition).

## What the Operation Includes

Closure without interposition still includes debridement of the symphysis (pubectomy when needed), excision of the fistula tract and multilayer closure of the urinary defect. The difference from flap-based repair is the absence of a vascularized tissue barrier between the urinary closure and the debrided bone bed.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[6]](#ref6)</sup>

## Clinical Role

USF often combines a urinary leak, infected pubic bone and radiation-damaged tissue. Reconstruction must address the urinary source, infected or nonviable tissue, drainage and any distal obstruction. Flap coverage can separate the urinary repair from the bone bed and fill dead space; expert series commonly advocate it. Evidence does not establish that every repair without a flap inevitably fails, or that a fixed recurrence percentage applies to an individual patient.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[2]](#ref2)</sup>

A small, closable defect in viable tissue is different from a contracted irradiated bladder with cavitation or an unsalvageable outlet. If a safe, functional reconstruction cannot be achieved, omitting a flap does not make local closure an adequate substitute for more extensive source control. Reconstruction and diversion options require specialist assessment.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[2]](#ref2)</sup>

## Escandón 2026 — Comparative Evidence and Its Limits

This retrospective 56-patient study included 11 primary repairs, 34 omental reconstructions and 11 VRAM reconstructions, with at least three months of follow-up. The abstract reports 90-day sepsis rates of 18%, 3% and 27%, respectively, and lower recurrence with omentum. Its adjusted association for recurrence at 90 days was OR 0.012 versus primary repair; it did not establish superiority over VRAM.<sup>[[3]](#ref3)</sup>

| Outcome (Escandón 2026) | Primary repair (n = 11) | Omental flap (n = 34) | VRAM flap (n = 11) | P |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 90-day sepsis | 18% | 3% | 27% | 0.041 |
| Fistula recurrence | 30% | Lower (rate not given in the abstract) | 27.3% | 0.04 |
| Adjusted odds of recurrence at 90 days | Reference | OR 0.012 | Not significant | 0.011 (omentum) |

These groups included different underlying operations: patients with obstruction, radiation cystitis or a nonviable sphincter underwent anterior exenteration and flap transfer. This is not a randomized comparison of otherwise identical bladder-preserving closures. The abstract's reported 30% primary-repair recurrence does not specify an evaluable denominator; it should not become a universal counseling estimate. The odds ratio does not prove a 98.8% causal reduction in recurrence risk.<sup>[[3]](#ref3)</sup>

## Why Interposition Is Usually Added

The rationale rests on the tissue bed rather than on a comparative trial of otherwise identical closures.

- **Irradiated tissue heals poorly.** In a Washington University series of enterourinary and urinary-cutaneous fistulae, radiation was associated with more repair failure (P = 0.0002), more postsurgical incontinence and more permanent diversion (72% of irradiated versus 7% of nonirradiated patients at final follow-up). These were not USF-only data.<sup>[[7]](#ref7)</sup>
- **Bone is often infected.** Pubic bone cultures showed active infection in 80% of Andrews' 25 patients, and 93% of USF patients in the systematic review had prior pelvic radiation.<sup>[[4]](#ref4)[[5]](#ref5)</sup>
- **Pubectomy leaves dead space.** A flap can obliterate the cavity and separate the urinary closure from the bone bed. This is a reconstructive rationale, and Campbell and Vanni state that interposition of healthy muscle allows repair in a high proportion of appropriately selected patients, especially rectourethral fistula.<sup>[[6]](#ref6)</sup>

Raup's gracilis series adds that pelvic radiation was associated with persistent incontinence and worse quality of life even after fistula closure.<sup>[[8]](#ref8)</sup>

When a flap is not available because of prior abdominal surgery or prior flap harvest, other interposition options are considered before a no-flap closure; see [organ-sparing repair with interposition](/docs/surgical-techniques/04h-fistula-repair/male/usf-organ-sparing-interposition). Kaufman's rectus flap series closed all four fistulas with one procedure at median 27 months.<sup>[[13]](#ref13)</sup>

## Operative and Postoperative Principles

The surgical plan should specify debridement of infected bone and soft tissue, management of the fistula and urinary defect, whether the bladder and outlet are preservable, and the reason any interposition is omitted. The incision, catheter and closure layers are tailored to the anatomy and urinary repair; omission of a flap does not define a standardized operative protocol.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[2]](#ref2)</sup>

A typical sequence, as general operative teaching rather than a protocol from the USF series:

1. Expose the retropubic space through a lower midline incision and identify the tract from the prostatic urethra or bladder neck to the symphysis.
2. Resect infected and necrotic symphyseal bone to healthy bleeding margins, and send bone for culture (and pathology when malignancy is a question).
3. Excise the tract back to healthy tissue and freshen the edges of the urinary defect.
4. Close the defect in multiple layers with absorbable suture and confirm the closure is watertight.
5. Drain the urine with a urethral or suprapubic catheter, place retropubic drains and close the wound in layers. No flap is interposed.

A cystogram before catheter removal is commonly obtained to confirm the closure. Long courses of culture-directed antibiotics are used for the osteomyelitis; the review by Haas and colleagues describes this as part of definitive treatment.<sup>[[2]](#ref2)</sup>

Obtain operative bone and tissue cultures where debridement is performed. In Andrews' 25 irradiated patients, urine and bone cultures differed in 21/22 paired samples; the figure describes that cohort, not the accuracy of every patient's urine culture. Antimicrobial selection, route and duration should follow the organisms, source control and infectious-disease assessment. Catheter removal and imaging depend on the urinary reconstruction and healing.<sup>[[4]](#ref4)[[2]](#ref2)</sup>

New pelvic pain, fever, recurrent drainage or deteriorating mobility warrants reassessment for infection, persistent leakage or recurrent fistula. Successful initial closure does not ensure a functional bladder outlet.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[4]](#ref4)</sup>

## Conservative Care Is a Separate Decision

Catheter drainage and antibiotics are not synonymous with surgical primary repair. A 2023 systematic review of published USF cases reported failure of conservative care in 96% after radiation and 72% without radiation; these are selected, heterogeneous reports rather than prospective individual-risk estimates. Conservative or palliative care can still be appropriate when major surgery is unsuitable or declined, with explicit goals and monitoring.<sup>[[5]](#ref5)[[2]](#ref2)</sup>

For sepsis or poor physiological reserve, prioritize stabilization and appropriate drainage and source control. There is no established evidence for deliberately performing an inadequate no-flap closure as a routine bridge to a later flap operation. Likewise, successful glue treatment of other urinary fistulas does not establish treatment of USF-associated bone infection.

## Minimally Invasive Options

- **Robotic repair with holmium laser debridement.** Hebert and colleagues described robotic dissection of the fistula, holmium laser debridement of the symphysis (2 J, 50 Hz), reconstruction or diversion, antibiotic beads in the defect and an interposition flap when available. The four patients had no recurrence at 7 to 16 months. The authors' stated indications were prior sacral insufficiency fractures, which make partial pubectomy a risk to pelvic ring stability, and mild osteomyelitis manageable by debridement.<sup>[[9]](#ref9)</sup>
- **Robot-assisted cystectomy with laser debridement.** Navaratnam's 12 patients (11 with prior radiation) had a median operative time of 270 minutes and a median stay of 5 days. Eleven (91.7%) had complete fistula resolution at median 29 months.<sup>[[10]](#ref10)</sup>
- **Cyanoacrylate glue.** In Muto's 13 patients with mixed urinary fistulas, 11 sealed with no relapse at median 35 months. The two failures were wider fistulas (over 1 cm). USF with osteomyelitis was not studied.<sup>[[11]](#ref11)</sup>

Moring and colleagues conclude that many less invasive and conservative options ultimately lead to cystectomy, and that cystectomy with urinary diversion is a safe and viable option in selected irradiated patients.<sup>[[12]](#ref12)</sup>
