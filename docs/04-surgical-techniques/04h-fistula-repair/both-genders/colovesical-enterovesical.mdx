# Colovesical and Enterovesical Fistula Repair

**Enterovesical fistula (EVF)** — pathological communication between bowel and bladder — arises most commonly from **diverticular disease (41–75%)**, followed by **colorectal cancer (16–20%)**, **Crohn's disease (7–17%)**, and less commonly radiation.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[2]](#ref2)[[3]](#ref3)</sup> **Colovesical fistula (CVF)** — the colon-specific subset — is the most common fistula complicating diverticulitis (~65% of diverticular fistulas).<sup>[[4]](#ref4)</sup> Strong male predominance (M:F ~ 6:1) reflects the protective interposition of the uterus.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[5]](#ref5)</sup>

For broader context see [Colovesical / Small-Bowel Fistulas (clinical)](/docs/clinical-conditions/03f-fistulas/in-both-genders/colovesical-small-bowel). For other transabdominal urology-side approaches see [Transabdominal RUF / RVF Repair](/docs/surgical-techniques/04h-fistula-repair/male/transabdominal-ruf-rvf).

---

## Clinical Presentation

| Symptom | Frequency |
|---|---|
| **Pneumaturia** (most pathognomonic) | 71–95%<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[2]](#ref2)[[5]](#ref5)</sup> |
| **Fecaluria** | 36–82% |
| **Recurrent UTI** (often polymicrobial / enteric) | 57–88% |
| Dysuria, frequency, hematuria, orchitis | Variable |

Urine cultures grow enteric organisms (*E. coli*, mixed bowel flora).<sup>[[7]](#ref7)</sup>

---

## Diagnosis

Primarily **clinical** — history + urine culture. Imaging confirms diagnosis, identifies etiology (especially malignancy), and plans surgery.

| Modality | Sensitivity | Role |
|---|---|---|
| **Poppy-seed test** | **~95%** | Highest sensitivity to confirm fistula<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup> |
| **CT abdomen / pelvis** | 55–90% | Best for etiology, anatomy, surgical planning — intravesical air, bladder-wall thickening, fistulous tract<sup>[[2]](#ref2)[[3]](#ref3)</sup> |
| **Cystoscopy** | 10–89% | Identifies bladder-side pathology, rules out bladder cancer<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[4]](#ref4)</sup> |
| **Colonoscopy** | 8.5–65% | Essential to exclude colorectal malignancy<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[4]](#ref4)</sup> |
| **Barium enema** | 20–65% | Variable; may show tract<sup>[[2]](#ref2)[[4]](#ref4)</sup> |
| **Cystography** | 11–27% | Low sensitivity; presurgical planning<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[2]](#ref2)</sup> |
| **MRI** | ~60% | Useful in Crohn's for tract characterization<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[9]](#ref9)</sup> |

**ACR Appropriateness Criteria** — CVF can usually be diagnosed on contrast-enhanced CT alone (enhancing tracts ± gas extending from colon to bladder wall).<sup>[[8]](#ref8)</sup> Colonoscopy and cystoscopy remain essential to rule out malignancy.<sup>[[2]](#ref2)[[5]](#ref5)[[10]](#ref10)</sup>

---

## Indications for Surgery

**ASCRS 2020** — elective colectomy for diverticulitis complicated by fistula (Strong, 1B).<sup>[[11]](#ref11)</sup>

Indications:

- Symptomatic fistula causing recurrent UTI, pneumaturia, fecaluria
- Failure of medical management
- Underlying malignancy
- Associated abscess, obstruction, or stricture
- Recurrent pyelonephritis (ACG relative indication)<sup>[[12]](#ref12)</sup>

In **Crohn's disease**, fistula presence does not mandate surgery in the absence of malabsorption, intractable diarrhea, or recurrent infection.<sup>[[13]](#ref13)</sup> Anti-TNF ± immunomodulators achieve complete durable response in **45–66%** of EVF; surgery is avoided in &gt; 50% of patients at 5 yr.<sup>[[13]](#ref13)[[14]](#ref14)</sup> Sigmoid-originated fistulas and concurrent CD complications (obstruction, abscess, enterocutaneous fistula) predict eventual surgery.<sup>[[15]](#ref15)</sup>

---

## Preoperative Optimization

Particularly important in Crohn's:

- Nutritional support (malnutrition, albumin)
- Treat active infection / UTI
- Medical therapy (anti-TNF, immunomodulators) to reduce inflammation
- Bowel rest when appropriate

Chen 2023 (n = 74 CD entero-urinary fistulas): preoperative optimization resolved urinary symptoms in **57%**, reduced need for bladder repair, and improved surgical outcomes.<sup>[[6]](#ref6)</sup>

---

## Surgical Technique

### 1. One-stage resection with primary anastomosis (procedure of choice)

The default when no abscess, obstruction, or significant contamination:<sup>[[3]](#ref3)[[11]](#ref11)[[16]](#ref16)</sup>

1. **Exploration and mobilization** — identify fistula and inflammatory phlegmon; mobilize sigmoid; separate inflammatory mass from bladder
2. **Bowel resection** — diseased segment (typically sigmoid colectomy for diverticular CVF; ileocecal / segmental for Crohn's); fistula tract taken down with the specimen
3. **Primary anastomosis** — colorectal or ileocolic; typically without protective stoma
4. **Bladder management** — key decision point:

   | Bladder finding | Management |
   |---|---|
   | **No overt defect / negative leak test** | **Foley catheter alone** — sufficient in **68%** of cases; defect heals within 1 wk with drainage alone (Ferguson 2008)<sup>[[17]](#ref17)</sup> |
   | **Overt large defect or positive leak test** | **Formal 2-layer bladder repair** with absorbable suture<sup>[[16]](#ref16)[[17]](#ref17)[[18]](#ref18)</sup> |

   Systematic review confirms bladder repair is not necessary after a negative intraoperative leak test.<sup>[[16]](#ref16)</sup>
5. **Tissue interposition** — **omental flap** between bowel anastomosis and bladder closure to reduce recurrence; particularly in irradiated fields or complex cases. **Perivesical fat rotational flap** is a described alternative when omentum is unavailable (Hwang 2023).<sup>[[18]](#ref18)[[19]](#ref19)[[20]](#ref20)</sup>
6. **Foley catheter** in place **7–14 days** postoperatively.<sup>[[17]](#ref17)[[21]](#ref21)</sup>

### 2. Multi-stage procedures

For complicated presentations:<sup>[[3]](#ref3)[[22]](#ref22)</sup>

- **Two-stage (Hartmann)** — resection with end colostomy → later reversal — for significant contamination, abscess, poor nutritional status
- **Three-stage** — diverting colostomy → definitive resection → colostomy reversal — for emergent peritonitis presentations

**Diverting colostomy alone (without resection) is not recommended** — persistent fistula and ongoing urinary sepsis in all patients.<sup>[[3]](#ref3)</sup>

### 3. Open vs minimally invasive

**MIS (laparoscopic or robotic) is increasingly preferred:**

| Parameter | Open | MIS | Source |
|---|---|---|---|
| **LOS** | 7.3–8 d | 5–6.9 d (significantly shorter) | Volkert 2025, Froiio 2022, Trejo-Avila 2021<sup>[[23]](#ref23)[[24]](#ref24)</sup> |
| **Overall complications** | Higher | Lower (OR 0.55) | Volkert<sup>[[23]](#ref23)</sup> |
| **Anastomotic leak** | No difference | No difference | |
| **Mortality** | 1% | 1% (no difference) | |
| **Conversion (lap)** | N/A | **27–36%** | Trejo-Avila<sup>[[24]](#ref24)</sup> |
| **Conversion (robotic)** | N/A | **~1%** | Sassun 2025<sup>[[26]](#ref26)</sup> |

**Robotic** appears particularly advantageous — Sassun 2025 Mayo (n = 89): conversion **1%**, AL 3%, fistula recurrence **1%** at median 16.5 mo; ureteral ICG fluorescence in 44%.<sup>[[26]](#ref26)</sup> Posterior bladder fistula location is a significant lap-conversion risk factor.<sup>[[25]](#ref25)</sup>

### 4. Endoscopic OTS clip

**Over-the-scope clip** is an emerging option for poor surgical candidates — initial technical success **80%**, long-term success **~50%**.<sup>[[27]](#ref27)</sup>

---

## Outcomes

Excellent overall:

- **Clavien-Dindo ≥ 3 complications:** 7.4%<sup>[[16]](#ref16)</sup>
- **30-day mortality:** 0–1.5%<sup>[[5]](#ref5)[[16]](#ref16)</sup>
- **Fistula recurrence:** 0–1%<sup>[[5]](#ref5)[[16]](#ref16)[[26]](#ref26)</sup>
- **Anastomotic leak:** 3%<sup>[[26]](#ref26)</sup>
- **Bladder leak:** ~1%<sup>[[6]](#ref6)</sup>

Long-term (median 68 mo): no recurrent fistulas after definitive resection.<sup>[[5]](#ref5)</sup> In Crohn's, recurrence is uncommon but associated with postoperative sepsis and multiple fistulas at the time of surgery.<sup>[[21]](#ref21)</sup>

---

## Medical Management (Crohn's Disease)

When surgery is not immediately indicated:

- **Anti-TNF agents** (infliximab, adalimumab) — complete response in **57%** of EVF<sup>[[13]](#ref13)[[14]](#ref14)</sup>
- **Immunomodulators** (azathioprine, 6-MP) — often combined
- **Antibiotics** (metronidazole, ciprofloxacin) — adjunctive
- **Mesalamine and corticosteroids ineffective** for fistulizing CD<sup>[[12]](#ref12)</sup>

ACG: recurrent symptomatic UTI, especially with pyelonephritis, is a relative indication for surgery even in patients on medical therapy.<sup>[[12]](#ref12)</sup>

---

## Special Considerations

- **Malignancy** — exclude preoperatively in all cases; malignancy was not diagnosed preoperatively in 50% of cancer-related fistulas in one series.<sup>[[3]](#ref3)</sup>
- **Radiation-induced fistulas** — more complex, higher complications; often require tissue interposition (omental or myocutaneous flap) and may require multi-stage procedures.
- **Ureteral protection** — preoperative ureteral stent in complex cases; ICG fluorescence increasingly used in robotic approaches.<sup>[[26]](#ref26)</sup>

---

## Key Takeaways

- **One-stage resection + primary anastomosis** is the procedure of choice for benign EVF / CVF without abscess.
- **Foley alone** manages the bladder in 68% — formal repair is reserved for overt defects or positive leak test.
- **Robotic** approach offers very low conversion (~ 1%) vs lap (27–36%); ICG fluorescence aids ureteral identification.
- **Anti-TNF first** for Crohn's EVF — > 50% avoid surgery at 5 yr; pyelonephritis is a relative surgical indication.
- **Diverting colostomy alone** does not resolve the fistula — always combine with definitive resection.
