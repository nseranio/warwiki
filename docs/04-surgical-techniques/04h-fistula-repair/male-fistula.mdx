# Male Fistula Repair

**Male fistula repair** covers rectourethral, bowel–bladder, urosymphyseal and urethrocutaneous fistulae, and this atlas links a technique page for each. The organs involved, the cause, tissue quality, obstruction, and baseline urinary and bowel function determine the choice of repair. Prostate cancer treatment is a major cause of acquired rectourethral and urosymphyseal fistulae. Bowel–bladder communications also arise from diverticulitis, Crohn's disease and malignancy.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[2]](#ref2)[[3]](#ref3)</sup>

## Identify the Communication

| Clinical clue | Evaluation focus |
|---|---|
| Pneumaturia, fecaluria or urine per rectum after prostate treatment | Evaluate for RUF with directed urinary and rectal endoscopy and urethrography; assess prior radiation, outlet stenosis and continence.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup> |
| Pneumaturia or recurrent UTI with bowel disease | Define the bowel source, abscess or obstruction and exclude malignancy; symptoms alone do not distinguish all bowel–urinary fistulae.<sup>[[3]](#ref3)[[4]](#ref4)</sup> |
| Pubic/groin pain and difficulty walking after radiation or outlet instrumentation | Consider urosymphyseal fistula and osteomyelitis; MRI is particularly useful for defining the communication and soft-tissue/bone involvement.<sup>[[2]](#ref2)</sup> |
| Urine through penile, scrotal or perineal skin | Map the urethrocutaneous tract and assess associated stricture, prior reconstruction, skin quality and infection.<sup>[[5]](#ref5)</sup> |

## Rectourethral Fistula: Selecting an Approach

No single route is best for every RUF. A monitored drainage trial may suit a small uncomplicated fistula, while significant symptoms, infection or tissue damage change the urgency and the diversion plan. Fecaluria marks a more severe fistula and often prompts fecal diversion. It does not by itself mandate a colostomy followed by a three-to-six-month wait.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup>

| Option | Features relevant to selection |
|---|---|
| **Transperineal repair** | Provides access for tissue interposition and simultaneous urethral reconstruction; often used for complex or irradiated fistulae. |
| **York–Mason** | Transsphincteric access for selected fistulae with suitable tissue; limited urethral exposure and sphincter-related morbidity matter. |
| **Transanal advancement / minimally invasive repair** | Selected accessible defects; working space, tissue viability and limited ability to reconstruct the outlet constrain use. |
| **Transabdominal / combined repair** | High communication, abdominal pathology, unusable bladder or need for complex bowel/urinary reconstruction. |
| **Definitive diversion with or without extirpative surgery** | May best match severe tissue destruction, poor organ function, prior unsuccessful reconstruction, operative fitness and patient preferences. |

The route considerations above derive from reconstructive reviews and retrospective series. No randomized comparison exists.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[6]](#ref6)</sup> Radiation raises the risk of failure but does not make failure inevitable, and reported results differ widely between selected reconstructive series and cohorts with severe tissue destruction. The fecal-diversion page tabulates [populations and denominators](fecal-diversion.mdx#rectourethral-fistula).

### Selected reported outcomes

| Series | Population and approach | Reported outcome |
|---|---|---|
| Vanni 2010 | 74 RUF repaired through a transperineal approach with a muscle interposition flap (68 gracilis) and selective buccal mucosa onlay; 35 nonradiated and 39 radiated or ablated | Closed with one procedure in 100% of nonradiated and 84% of radiated or ablated fistulae at mean follow-up of 20 months. Permanent fecal diversion was required in 31% of the radiated or ablated group.<sup>[[8]](#ref8)</sup> |
| Harris 2017 | 201 men from four reconstructive centers; 97 after prostatectomy alone, 104 after energy ablation; interposition flap or graft in 91% to 92% | Ultimate closure 99% after prostatectomy and 87% after ablation (92% overall). Postoperative urinary incontinence was 16% and 35%, and complications 11% and 25%, respectively.<sup>[[9]](#ref9)</sup> |
| Khouri 2024 | 23 men with posterior urethral stenosis or defect who had urethroplasty at the time of RUF repair (anastomotic in 18, buccal graft in 5; gracilis in 21) | Closure in 20/23 (87%) at median follow-up of 55.7 months. Urinary incontinence occurred in 14/23 (61%), and 7 (30%) had an artificial urinary sphincter placed. Posterior urethral stenosis did not by itself preclude restorative repair.<sup>[[10]](#ref10)</sup> |
| Long Depaquit 2026 | 57 patients at four French centers (27 York–Mason, 30 transperineal gracilis); retrospective | First-attempt success 44% with York–Mason and 77% with gracilis; overall success 70% and 94%. Complex fistula morphology predicted York–Mason failure. The authors conclude York–Mason may remain suitable for selected simple, nonirradiated fistulae.<sup>[[11]](#ref11)</sup> |
| Linder 2013 | 45 patients; 29 after radiation or ablation | Permanent colostomy in 86% and permanent urinary diversion in 93% of the radiation or ablation group, against 0% and 6% in the others.<sup>[[12]](#ref12)</sup> |
| Hechenbleikner 2013 | Systematic review of 416 patients in retrospective series | Transperineal repair was used in 65.9% and transanal repair in 5.9%. Fistula closure was 87.5%, and permanent fecal and urinary diversion rates were 10.6% and 8.3%.<sup>[[13]](#ref13)</sup> |

These series differ in selection, definitions of success and follow-up, so their percentages are not directly comparable.

Repair does not guarantee urinary or fecal continence. Baseline function is documented and later continence treatment is discussed separately. See [fistula principles](fistula-repair-principles.mdx) and [male SUI procedures](/docs/surgical-techniques/04f-incontinence-procedures/male-sui/male-stress-incontinence-database).

## Enterovesical / Colovesical Fistula

The underlying bowel disease and any associated sepsis are treated first. The ASCRS 2020 guideline recommends elective colectomy for diverticulitis complicated by fistula (strong recommendation, 1B); timing, anastomosis and diversion depend on clinical circumstances.<sup>[[4]](#ref4)</sup> Bladder treatment depends on the actual defect. In Ferguson's 74-patient cohort of benign bowel disease without prior abdominal cancer or pelvic radiation, the bladder was managed by Foley catheter alone in 68% after bowel resection and by surgical repair in 32%. Formal repair was reserved for overt bladder defects, and the series excluded cancer and radiation injury.<sup>[[3]](#ref3)</sup>

Crohn's entero-urinary disease is planned jointly by gastroenterology and surgery. Selected patients respond to anti-TNF treatment, but abscess, obstruction or sepsis changes the sequence. In Taxonera's 97-patient retrospective cohort, 45% of the 33 anti-TNF-treated patients achieved sustained remission without surgery, and more than 80% of the entire cohort ultimately required surgery.<sup>[[7]](#ref7)</sup>

## Urosymphyseal / Puboprostatic Fistula

This communication often coexists with pubic osteomyelitis after radiation and subsequent outlet instrumentation. Patel's review included 248 cases; 93% had prior prostate radiotherapy, but nonirradiated cases also occurred. Conservative management failed in 96% of irradiated cases and 72% of radiation-naive cases, and among irradiated patients cystectomy with diversion (86%) was used far more often than bladder-sparing surgery (14%). These selected reports support early multidisciplinary reconstructive assessment.<sup>[[2]](#ref2)</sup>

Infection control and urinary drainage, debridement with bladder or outlet preservation, and extirpative surgery with diversion are the options. The choice follows the remaining organ function, bone and soft-tissue damage, and patient fitness. Pubic, thigh and cutaneous extensions can overlap, and their location alone does not determine the operation.<sup>[[2]](#ref2)</sup>

| Series | Population | Reported outcome |
|---|---|---|
| Bugeja 2016 | 16 patients treated for USF after prostate cancer treatment; conservative management succeeded in 1 | 7/15 operated patients had reconstruction with salvage prostatectomy and substitution or augmentation cystoplasty and 8 had cystectomy with ileal conduit. Symptoms resolved in all, with pain resolving almost immediately.<sup>[[14]](#ref14)</sup> |
| Andrews 2021 | 25 irradiated patients treated with pubectomy and urinary reconstruction | Pain resolved in 24/25 (96%), median ECOG performance status improved from 3 to 0, and 8/25 (32%) had major 90-day complications. Bone cultures showed active infection in 80%.<sup>[[15]](#ref15)</sup> |
| Osterberg 2017 (TURNS) | 31 men with anterior urinary fistula after prostate radiotherapy (pubic symphysis in 19, thigh in 12) | 19 had cystectomy and 12 had fistula repair. Pain had resolved in 26/31 (84%) at 6 months, with one fistula recurrence.<sup>[[16]](#ref16)</sup> |

In a retrospective cohort of 58 patients who had decompression, pubic bone resection and reconstruction, repair within 100 days of diagnosis was associated with less recurrent pain (11.1% versus 46.7%), recurrent osteomyelitis (0% versus 20%) and fistula recurrence (3.7% versus 23.3%) than later repair. Ninety-day complication rates were similar.<sup>[[17]](#ref17)</sup>

## Urethrocutaneous Fistula

Separate an isolated tract from broader complications of hypospadias repair or urethral reconstruction. Inspect the urethral plate, distal outlet, skin and available vascularized tissue. A fistula does not automatically require staged urethroplasty; associated stricture and poor tissue may make a larger reconstruction necessary.<sup>[[5]](#ref5)</sup>
