# Ureteroarterial Fistula — Repair

This operative atlas covers control of ureteroarterial (arterio-ureteral) fistula, abbreviated UAF or AUF. See [Vascular-Urinary Fistula](/docs/clinical-conditions/03f-fistulas/all-patients/vascular-urinary) for risk factors and diagnostic evaluation. Treatment requires coordinated vascular surgery, interventional radiology, urology and resuscitation support; the evidence consists mainly of retrospective series and case reports.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[2]](#ref2)[[3]](#ref3)</sup>

## Immediate Priorities

Suspected UAF with major hematuria is a vascular emergency. Resuscitation and preparation for arterial hemorrhage control proceed together. A negative angiogram does not exclude an intermittently bleeding fistula; persistent strong suspicion requires multidisciplinary reassessment and no unprotected stent exchange.<sup>[[2]](#ref2)[[3]](#ref3)</sup>

| Priority | Practical approach |
|---|---|
| **Resuscitate** | Restore perfusion with blood products and other support according to hemorrhage severity; activate the team able to obtain immediate arterial control. |
| **Avoid disrupting tamponade** | An existing ureteral stent may tamponade the tract. Avoid unnecessary manipulation before vascular support is ready. |
| **Control arterial bleeding** | Covered stent-grafting is the usual initial option when anatomy and the infection or contamination context permit; open or hybrid control may be necessary. |
| **Plan urinary drainage and source control** | Assess obstruction, renal function, infection and any bowel communication alongside the arterial repair. |

In the Heers multicenter series of 26 fistulas in 24 patients, 92.3% of fistula episodes required transfusion and 46.2% required inotropic support. These are selected-series observations and do not define thresholds for intervention.<sup>[[4]](#ref4)</sup> Bladder catheter drainage and irrigation can address clot retention; irrigation itself does not identify the bleeding ureter.

:::danger[Ureteral manipulation can precipitate catastrophic bleeding]
Do not treat suspected UAF as a routine difficult stent exchange. Malgor reported massive bleeding during intentional ureteral manipulation in 3 of 14 cystoscopic evaluations. If removal, exchange, provocation or ureteral balloon tamponade is needed, perform it in a setting with immediate endovascular or operative arterial control and a plan to preserve access. An instruction to blindly reinsert a stent immediately after bleeding starts is unsafe.<sup>[[5]](#ref5)[[7]](#ref7)</sup>
:::

## Endovascular Repair

Covered stent-grafting can exclude the communication while maintaining iliac arterial flow and avoiding hostile pelvic dissection. It is generally the preferred initial treatment in suitable cases; it does not eliminate every source of infection or the need for later reconstruction.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[2]](#ref2)</sup>

### Planning and deployment

- Define the likely fistula level, arterial anatomy and landing zones. Trace the actual ureteral course, especially after diversion or vascular reconstruction: a 2024 location review found fistulas involving the aorta, iliac branches and bypass anastomoses as well as the common iliac artery.<sup>[[19]](#ref19)</sup> Diagnostic angiography and treatment can occur in the same session; proof of extravasation is not always obtainable.<sup>[[2]](#ref2)[[10]](#ref10)</sup> In one covered-stent case report, completion angiography showed no extravasation, maintained common-to-external iliac flow and no contralateral cross-filling.<sup>[[20]](#ref20)</sup>
- Select a covered stent appropriate to vessel diameter and anatomy. Published experience includes both self-expanding and balloon-expandable devices. Guntau's series used a balloon-expandable covered stent (ADVANTA) for precise deployment.<sup>[[8]](#ref8)</sup>
- Determine whether the internal iliac artery needs exclusion to prevent continued fistula filling. Balance hemorrhage control against pelvic perfusion; selective embolization or a branched reconstruction depends on the anatomy.<sup>[[9]](#ref9)[[10]](#ref10)</sup> In Malgor's series, 11 patients had iliac stent grafts and the internal iliac artery was excluded in nine (by stent-graft coverage in five and by coil embolization in four). The cost is buttock claudication, which occurred in one stent-graft patient.<sup>[[7]](#ref7)</sup> Sachsamanis described an iliac-branched device in one patient and internal iliac coil embolization alone in another.<sup>[[9]](#ref9)</sup>
- Common iliac occlusion requires an explicit distal and pelvic perfusion plan. Embolization of an isolated internal iliac source and occlusion of the common iliac artery are not interchangeable procedures.<sup>[[10]](#ref10)[[14]](#ref14)</sup>

### Failure and complications

Recurrent hematuria, stent-graft infection, thrombosis, embolic or ischemic events and device migration can require urgent reintervention. A reported case of late thrombosis with migration into the bladder illustrates a possible complication and gives no population frequency.<sup>[[12]](#ref12)</sup> In Sachsamanis's nine-patient series, three of six initially endovascularly treated patients later required open conversion for recurrent bleeding or infection; this cannot be presented as a universal recurrence rate.<sup>[[9]](#ref9)</sup>

## Open and Hybrid Repair

Consider open or hybrid treatment for enteric contamination, abscess or infected graft requiring source control, failed endovascular exclusion, recurrent bleeding, or anatomy that cannot be treated while preserving necessary perfusion. A pre-existing arterial graft increases planning complexity but is not, by itself, an absolute reason to reject endovascular hemorrhage control.<sup>[[7]](#ref7)[[9]](#ref9)[[13]](#ref13)[[15]](#ref15)</sup>

| Reconstruction | Selection considerations |
|---|---|
| **Arterial exclusion with extra-anatomic bypass** | Percutaneous common-iliac embolization followed by femoral crossover or another suitable bypass can restore distal perfusion without laparotomy in selected patients.<sup>[[14]](#ref14)</sup> Open arterial ligation is a separate approach that may require pelvic exposure, particularly when drainage or debridement is needed.<sup>[[7]](#ref7)</sup> Plan limb and pelvic circulation before arterial exclusion. |
| **Direct repair or patch** | Selected accessible defects with suitable arterial tissue; extensive radiation injury or infection may make local repair inappropriate. |
| **Interposition reconstruction** | Replacement of an affected segment when required; material and route depend on infection and perfusion requirements. |
| **Staged endovascular-to-open treatment** | Initial hemorrhage control followed by definitive drainage, debridement or reconstruction when the patient's condition permits. |

These choices are individualized vascular reconstructions and are not obligatory sequential stages.<sup>[[7]](#ref7)[[13]](#ref13)</sup> In Malgor's selected series, enterocutaneous fistula followed three of five laparotomy or flank-incision repairs involving direct arterial repair or ligation. Across all eight open repairs, five patients had complications: three enterocutaneous fistulas and two superficial wound infections.<sup>[[7]](#ref7)</sup> Hybrid treatment has been described, with a stent-graft for acute hemorrhage control followed by delayed open repair when the patient is stable, or with percutaneous iliac embolization plus extra-anatomic bypass to avoid laparotomy in the irradiated pelvis.<sup>[[13]](#ref13)[[14]](#ref14)</sup> Vandersteen's historical experience included five fistulas in four patients treated with provocative arteriography, common iliac embolization and extra-anatomic bypass, without death, limb loss or renal loss. It is a small series, not a comparison proving superiority.<sup>[[14]](#ref14)</sup> Limited prognosis alone does not make arterial ligation without a perfusion assessment appropriate.

In a Bricker ileal-conduit anastomosis, the ureteroenteric junction is commonly positioned directly ventral to the right common iliac artery, an anatomical relationship that has been described as a contributor to fistula formation at that site; positioning a new conduit away from the artery has been suggested as a preventive measure at the index operation. For open repair in an irradiated or scarred field, interposing omentum between the artery and the ureter or conduit has been described as one way to separate the reconstructed structures.<sup>[[17]](#ref17)</sup>

### Concomitant urinary procedures

Assess ipsilateral renal function, obstruction, ureteral viability and the burden of future exchanges. Options include continued ureteral drainage, nephrostomy, ureteral ligation or exclusion with permanent nephrostomy, or nephrectomy in selected circumstances (for example, a nonfunctioning kidney). After arterial control, address the underlying ureteral obstruction and select continued stenting, nephrostomy or ureteral exclusion according to renal function, infection and the risk of future exchanges. Plan any stent removal or exchange with the vascular team after securing urinary drainage.<sup>[[5]](#ref5)[[6]](#ref6)[[7]](#ref7)</sup> An unsalvageable ureter does not automatically require nephrectomy. Fox reported ureteral ligation or nephrectomy in four of six open repairs; that combined category must not be described as four nephrectomies.<sup>[[6]](#ref6)</sup>

## Urinary Drainage After Arterial Control

A urinary drainage plan is necessary, but nephrostomy is not obligatory after every stent-graft. In Fox's retrospective series of 20 fistulas in 19 patients, 14/20 fistulas received percutaneous iliac arterial stenting or embolization; long-term ureteral stenting was reported in 12 of these treated cases.<sup>[[6]](#ref6)</sup> Balance reliable drainage, renal preservation, infection and the hazards of repeated instrumentation. Plan future exchanges jointly with the vascular or interventional team.<sup>[[5]](#ref5)[[6]](#ref6)</sup>

Diversion or ureteral exclusion alone does not reliably treat the arterial lesion; if used without arterial repair as palliation, explicitly acknowledge the risk of further bleeding.<sup>[[7]](#ref7)</sup> Antimicrobial and antithrombotic plans depend on contamination, cultures, implanted material, thrombosis risk and ongoing bleeding; the cited small series cannot specify a standard regimen.<sup>[[9]](#ref9)[[11]](#ref11)</sup>

## Interpreting Outcomes

| Source and population | Observed result | Limit |
|---|---|---|
| **Kamphorst systematic review:** 445 patients, 470 fistulas | UAF-specific mortality was 19% before 2000 and 7% thereafter. | The 19% and 7% figures compare eras amid increased endovascular treatment, without isolating open versus endovascular mortality or causation.<sup>[[3]](#ref3)</sup> |
| **Guntau:** eight patients, ten fistulas | No 30-day deaths after balloon-expandable covered stent treatment. | Small selected series; no device or open-surgery comparator.<sup>[[8]](#ref8)</sup> |
| **Fox:** 19 patients, 20 fistulas | Ten patients developed lower-extremity morbidity during follow-up. | The 53% figure belongs to this cohort across treatments.<sup>[[6]](#ref6)</sup> |
| **Matsunaga:** 40 patients, 43 fistulas after **ileal conduit** | Stent-graft infection occurred in 4/28 (14.3%) combined endovascular and endoureteral treatment cases (26 had stent-grafts; two had coil embolization alone). UAF-related mortality was 2/28 (7.1%) in that group and 2/15 (13.3%) with definitive surgical management, which included six single-step open and nine staged hybrid repairs. | Heterogeneous, nonrandomized reports in a specific diversion population, with median follow-up of 9.5 and 14.0 months, respectively.<sup>[[11]](#ref11)</sup> |

Published patency, survival and freedom-from-bleeding estimates use different follow-up periods and small denominators. They should not be assembled into an apparently comparative endovascular-versus-open prognosis table.

## Special Situations and Follow-up

**Ileal conduit.** Contamination and stent-graft infection deserve particular attention; Matsunaga supports close follow-up, especially in the first year, while acknowledging uncertainty about antibiotic strategy.<sup>[[11]](#ref11)</sup>

**Prior arterial reconstruction.** Review graft infection, bowel involvement and feasible perfusion-preserving options. Treatment may require removal or bypass of infected material; a temporary endovascular bridge remains a case-specific decision.<sup>[[13]](#ref13)[[15]](#ref15)</sup>

**Metallic ureteral stents.** Song's selected seven-patient complication series included three UAFs: two after polymeric stents and one after a metallic mesh stent. The authors advised caution with high-pressure balloon deployment in patients with prior pelvic surgery or radiation. This series provides no incidence estimate or class-wide metallic-stent contraindication.<sup>[[16]](#ref16)</sup>

Arrange vascular and urologic follow-up for recurrent bleeding, infection, patency and urinary drainage. New hematuria can signal recurrent UAF even after a technically successful repair; fever or new limb ischemic symptoms also require prompt evaluation.<sup>[[2]](#ref2)[[9]](#ref9)</sup>
