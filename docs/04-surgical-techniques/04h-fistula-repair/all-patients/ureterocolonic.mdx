# Ureterocolonic Fistula Repair

Ureterocolonic fistula repair addresses the bowel pathology and the urinary tract together, with attention to kidney salvage, ureteral viability and sepsis. Resection of diseased colon may be necessary, but does not guarantee that an injured or obstructed ureter will heal without repair. See [Ureterocolonic Fistula](/docs/clinical-conditions/03f-fistulas/all-patients/ureterocolonic) for presentation and diagnostic workup.

## Initial Management

Define the tract, associated abscess, obstruction, renal function and any malignant or inflammatory cause. Coordinate urology, colorectal surgery and interventional radiology. Control sepsis with antibiotics and drainage, relieve clinically significant urinary obstruction and address nutrition. Ureteral stenting or nephrostomy can provide diversion; a bowel stoma is a selected adjunct and is not mandatory treatment for every fistula.<sup>[[2]](#ref2)[[3]](#ref3)</sup>

For an iatrogenic injury, treatment should start promptly. When repair is feasible, timing depends on tissue, physiological stability and expertise; selected incomplete injuries can be managed with retrograde or antegrade stenting. Complete disruption, a long devitalized segment or failed drainage may require reconstruction. In unstable patients, ureteral closure with nephrostomy and delayed reconstruction is a described damage-control option. There is no general requirement to wait for a fixed interval while leakage or infection continues.<sup>[[10]](#ref10)</sup>

### When Drainage May Be Definitive

Evidence specific to ureterocolonic fistulas is mostly case reports, not comparative trials:

- Lang's 15-patient experience combined ureteroenteric, cutaneous, vaginal and retroperitoneal fistulas. It supports feasibility of nephrostomy with antegrade stenting, not a ureterocolonic-specific closure probability.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup>
- Krishna described an 88-year-old whose diverticular fistula closed after proximal colostomy; closure was documented when she returned for planned sigmoid colectomy. This single case does not establish that fecal diversion alone definitively treats the underlying diverticular disease.<sup>[[4]](#ref4)</sup>
- Tramontano's 2025 postoperative ureterocolic fistula resolved after an endoscopic or percutaneous approach. It illustrates a selected successful pathway and does not show superiority over reconstruction.<sup>[[5]](#ref5)</sup>

Document closure and unobstructed drainage before ending treatment, and monitor for later ureteral stenosis. Drainage-device exchanges and removal require an explicit follow-up plan.<sup>[[2]](#ref2)[[10]](#ref10)</sup>

## Selecting an Operation

### Bowel Resection and Ureteral Assessment

For fistulizing diverticulitis, ASCRS 2020 recommends elective colectomy when appropriate. Primary anastomosis, protected anastomosis or staged reconstruction depends on contamination, inflammation, tissue and patient condition.<sup>[[15]](#ref15)</sup>

Cirocco's 1994 report reviewed only five reported diverticular ureterocolic cases. Favorable results after bowel-directed surgery and its advice to avoid unnecessary urinary dissection should not be generalized to an ischemic ureter, a complete iatrogenic disruption or malignant involvement. Assess ureteral patency and viability directly when deciding whether it needs repair.<sup>[[6]](#ref6)[[10]](#ref10)</sup>

Remove the diseased bowel segment when indicated and preserve uninvolved tissue where possible. Separate adjacent repairs with healthy tissue when feasible; the rarity of this fistula precludes a reliable estimate of the independent benefit of omental interposition.<sup>[[10]](#ref10)[[14]](#ref14)</sup>

### Ureteral Reconstruction When the Kidney Is Salvageable

The following applies established ureteral-injury principles; published general reconstruction results are not ureterocolonic-fistula success rates.<sup>[[10]](#ref10)</sup>

| Anatomy | Potential reconstruction |
|---|---|
| Distal defect with adequate reach | Ureteroneocystostomy |
| Distal defect with insufficient reach | Psoas hitch (bladder mobilized and fixed to the psoas tendon) or Boari flap (tubularized bladder flap) if bladder tissue and function permit |
| Short mid or proximal defect with viable ends | Ureteroureterostomy over a stent |
| Extensive loss not amenable to standard repair | Selected transureteroureterostomy, bowel substitution or renal autotransplantation in an experienced center |

The WSES guideline describes a repair with good vascular supply, adequate drainage and a wide spatulated, tension-free mucosa-to-mucosa anastomosis.<sup>[[10]](#ref10)</sup> The steps are:

1. Debride all devitalized tissue.
2. Preserve periureteral adventitia and blood supply.
3. Spatulate the ureteral ends and close them watertight without tension.
4. Place a double-J stent and a retroperitoneal drain.
5. Separate the ureteral repair from the colonic suture line with omentum or peritoneum when feasible.

The interposition step is common surgical practice, and its independent benefit in this rare fistula is not established. Defect length alone does not establish bladder reach; prior radiation, bladder capacity and tissue quality matter. Transureteroureterostomy exposes the opposite urinary tract to risk, and bowel substitution may be unsuitable in active bowel disease or limited bowel reserve.<sup>[[10]](#ref10)</sup>

See [Ureteral Reimplantation](/docs/surgical-techniques/04d-upper-tract-reconstruction/ureteral-reimplantation), [Boari Flap & Psoas Hitch](/docs/surgical-techniques/04d-upper-tract-reconstruction/boari-flap-psoas-hitch) and [Ureteroureterostomy](/docs/surgical-techniques/04d-upper-tract-reconstruction/ureteroureterostomy).

### Nephrectomy / Nephroureterectomy

Removal may be appropriate for a chronically infected, nonfunctional or otherwise unsalvageable renal unit; the extent of ureteral removal depends on the cause and involved tissue. Assess contralateral function and reversible obstruction rather than treating any low differential function as an automatic indication. Pediatric iatrogenic, diverticular and stone or XGP reports illustrate selected nephroureterectomies, not a validated renal-function threshold for every patient.<sup>[[7]](#ref7)[[8]](#ref8)[[9]](#ref9)</sup>

## Crohn's Disease: Keep the Evidence in Context

Identify and control abscess or sepsis before escalating immunosuppression. ASCRS recommends considering surgery for enteric fistulas that persist despite appropriate medical therapy; uncontrolled penetrating complications also favor resection. Diseased bowel and a secondarily involved urinary organ may need different treatment.<sup>[[14]](#ref14)</sup>

The AGA biologic-plus-antibiotic recommendation concerns active perianal fistulas without perianal abscess. It does not establish a standard 12-week ciprofloxacin regimen for ureterocolonic fistula. Medical therapy for internal urinary fistulas should be selected with the IBD team and reassessed against infection, obstruction and kidney function.<sup>[[13]](#ref13)[[14]](#ref14)</sup>

| Original study | Relevant finding and limitation |
|---|---|
| Taxonera, 2016 | Among 97 entero-urinary fistula patients, 33 received anti-TNF and 45% of those 33 achieved sustained remission without surgery. More than 80% of the full cohort underwent surgery; 99% of surgical patients achieved remission. Retrospective selection and mixed anatomy limit comparisons<sup>[[12]](#ref12)</sup> |
| Chen, 2023 | All 74 patients underwent surgery. After preoperative optimization, 42 had no urinary-fistula symptoms; bladder repair was needed in 9/42 versus 19/32 with persistent symptoms. This is symptom improvement before surgery and is not a 57% nonsurgical closure rate. Three recurrent bladder fistulas occurred<sup>[[11]](#ref11)</sup> |

These studies predominantly inform broader entero-urinary or bladder disease, not a proven ureterocolonic-specific drug or repair success rate.

## Endoscopic and Other Limited-Evidence Options

Clips, suturing or sealants may be considered by an experienced multidisciplinary team for selected defects, usually after source control and assessment of surrounding tissue. General GI leak literature does not establish which device reliably closes a ureterocolonic fistula.<sup>[[17]](#ref17)[[18]](#ref18)</sup>

Sharma's fibrin-glue report included eight urinary pathologies, one of which was a caliceal diverticulum and seven fistulas of different sites, including one ureterorectal tract. Six of eight succeeded overall; that 75% is not a ureterocolonic closure rate. Repeated procedures and eventual operative salvage may be necessary.<sup>[[16]](#ref16)</sup>

## Counseling and Follow-Up

Discuss the possibility of persistent fistula, urinary infection, ureteral stricture, bowel-anastomotic complications, diversion and renal loss. Confirm both urinary and bowel recovery; absence of external leakage alone does not prove unobstructed drainage. A successful small series cannot supply an individual 91–100% guarantee.<sup>[[2]](#ref2)[[10]](#ref10)</sup>
