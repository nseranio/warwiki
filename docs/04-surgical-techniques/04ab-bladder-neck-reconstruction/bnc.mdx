# Bladder Neck Contracture (BNC)

**Bladder neck contracture (BNC)** is stenosis of the bladder neck after benign prostate surgery, such as TURP or HoLEP. EAU reports bladder neck stenosis in 0.3–9.7% of transurethral prostate surgery, and it is more frequent after resection of smaller glands and with excessive or circumferential resection.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup> Post-prostatectomy [VUAS](/docs/surgical-techniques/04ab-bladder-neck-reconstruction/vuas) involves a different anastomosis and remaining sphincter anatomy. Select treatment by lumen, length, prior procedures or radiation, bladder function, continence and patient goals.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup> The AUA 2023 amendment notes that no studies compare the treatment strategies for BNC after endoscopic prostate procedures, and that repeat endoscopic treatment may be necessary.<sup>[[2]](#ref2)</sup>

## Decision framework

| Clinical situation | Main considerations |
|---|---|
| Nonobliterative post-BPO BNC | EAU recommends transurethral resection or hot-knife incision as first-line treatment (strong); reported patency was 58.3% and 72%, respectively (level of evidence 3).<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup> A single-center series of 123 patients used 24 Fr balloon dilation followed by deep incisions at 3 and 9 o'clock with a Collins knife. Patency at 12 months was 101/123 (82.1%) after one procedure and 116/123 (94.3%) after two, and two or more prior endoscopic treatments was the only factor associated with failure.<sup>[[3]](#ref3)</sup> |
| Recurrent but patent lumen | Repeat endoluminal treatment is recommended to stabilize the stenosis (weak).<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup> In a 50-patient series of deep lateral incisions, mostly in refractory disease, 72% needed no further surgery for obstruction at mean 12.9 months and 86% after a second incision; more than 10 pack-years of smoking and two or more previous endoscopic procedures were associated with failure.<sup>[[4]](#ref4)</sup> Repeated recurrence or unacceptable treatment burden warrants a discussion of reconstruction, and there is no fixed two-failure rule |
| Refractory to endoscopic treatment | EAU recommends bladder-neck reconstruction with Y-V or T-plasty (weak), citing patency of 83–100% and de novo incontinence of 0–14%, and it recommends warning patients about de novo incontinence (strong).<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup> In an open Y-V plasty series of 18 men after TURP with two or more failed endoscopic treatments, success was 100% at mean 14.8 months, but 61% reported persistent storage symptoms.<sup>[[5]](#ref5)</sup> See [Y-V plasty](/docs/surgical-techniques/04ab-bladder-neck-reconstruction/bnc-vuas/yv-plasty), [T-plasty](/docs/surgical-techniques/04ab-bladder-neck-reconstruction/bnc-vuas/t-plasty-bnc), [primary reanastomosis](/docs/surgical-techniques/04ab-bladder-neck-reconstruction/bnc-vuas/primary-reanastomosis) and [subtrigonal BMG inlay](/docs/surgical-techniques/04ab-bladder-neck-reconstruction/bnc-vuas/subtrigonal-inlay) |
| Complete obliteration | EAU advises against endoluminal treatment (strong). Assess reconstructive access, tissue availability and drainage alternatives.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup> |
| Concurrent SUI | Establish stable patency and assess bladder and sphincter function before continence surgery. EAU advises deferring AUS implantation, usually at least three to six months, because of the risk of recurrent stenosis with the cuff in place.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup> In 63 men referred with BNC after radical prostatectomy (a different population from post-BPO BNC), deep cold-knife incision was followed at 3–4 months by an [AUS](/docs/surgical-techniques/04f-incontinence-procedures/procedures/artificial-urinary-sphincter) or [male sling](/docs/surgical-techniques/04f-incontinence-procedures/procedures/male-urethral-slings) once healed. Of the 46 men with successful BNC management, 91.3% were satisfied with continence, and 66% overall achieved continence.<sup>[[6]](#ref6)</sup> |
| Orthotopic neobladder BNC | In 71 patients with BNC in an orthotopic neobladder, 155 endoscopic procedures had a per-procedure success of 37% (50/136 with adequate follow-up), and the rate did not fall with repeated treatment. Success was 58% (14/24) with adjuvant clean intermittent catheterization and 32% (36/112) without (p = 0.02). Overall, 50/71 patients (73%) had a successful outcome and two needed neobladder removal.<sup>[[7]](#ref7)</sup> |
| Radiation, necrosis or fistula | Reassess tissue viability and bladder salvageability. In patients with prior radiotherapy, failed reconstruction, a densely fibrotic, necrotic or calcified posterior urethra, refractory incontinence or severe comorbidity, reconstruction may not be feasible, and urinary diversion with or without cystectomy is usually required.<sup>[[8]](#ref8)</sup> |
| Devastated outlet | Consider chronic suprapubic drainage, selected [bladder-neck closure](/docs/surgical-techniques/04ab-bladder-neck-reconstruction/outlet-continence/bladder-neck-closure) with a dependable alternate route such as a [catheterizable channel](/docs/surgical-techniques/04b-bladder-reconstruction/catheterizable-channels), or [diversion](/docs/surgical-techniques/04c-urinary-diversion), according to bladder function and goals |

Deep posterior-stenosis incisions at 6 and 12 o'clock should be avoided (EAU strong). Mitomycin C for posterior stenosis is recommended only within a clinical trial, and posterior urethral stents are discouraged (EAU weak). Drug-coated balloons in BNC are off-label, and novel graft or mucosal-realignment procedures are not established first-line replacements.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup>

The database links to the detailed techniques. Reported outcomes identify selected cohorts and should not be compared as if they were trials in equivalent patients. Patency, dryness and freedom from additional procedures are different endpoints.
