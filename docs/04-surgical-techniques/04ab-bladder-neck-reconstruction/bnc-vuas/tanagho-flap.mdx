# Tanagho Anterior Bladder Tube

The **Tanagho anterior bladder tube** uses tubularized anterior bladder wall to reconstruct the bladder neck and proximal urethra. Tanagho described it for total urinary incontinence; the 1981 series of 50 anterior tube repairs (plus 6 after retropubic prostatectomy) was compared with 25 posterior (Leadbetter) tube repairs. Post-prostatectomy incontinence was the main indication (44 patients). Other indications were trauma, congenital anomalies including epispadias, urethrotomy and urethral excision. Success with the anterior repair was about 70%.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup> It is a historical technique with selected salvage uses and is not a standard first-line treatment for modern BNC or VUAS.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[2]](#ref2)</sup>

## Concept and operative principles

A vascularized strip of anterior bladder wall is fashioned into a mucosa-lined tube and connected to the remaining outlet or urethral target.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[2]](#ref2)</sup> The general sequence is as follows.

1. Expose the bladder through a lower midline or Pfannenstiel incision, develop the space of Retzius and mobilize the anterior bladder wall.
2. Open the bladder anteriorly to expose the bladder neck and trigone.
3. Outline a strip of full-thickness anterior wall, based proximally at the bladder neck and extending cephalad.
4. Tubularize the strip over a catheter with the mucosa inward.
5. Direct the tube caudally and anastomose it to the urethral stump, or bring it to the perineum.
6. Close the donor defect and leave a urethral catheter through the neourethra, usually with suprapubic drainage.

Protect the ureteral orifices and avoid tension or rotation. Tube orientation, dimensions, caliber and catheter regimen vary across the named modifications and are not one universal protocol.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[2]](#ref2)[[3]](#ref3)[[4]](#ref4)</sup> The technique steps above are a summary from the published descriptions; the abstracts do not give each detail.

The tube restores outlet length and resistance. It is not a normal striated sphincter. In a canine two-stage bladder-flap neourethra, closure pressure was related to the tone of the smooth muscle tube plus compression by the pelvic floor muscles.<sup>[[10]](#ref10)</sup> Clinical reports do not establish that the tube opens reliably whenever the detrusor contracts. Continence and emptying depend on the whole reservoir and outlet system, and obstruction or a need for intermittent catheterization may result.

## Original experience and modifications

| Report | Scope | Interpretation |
|---|---|---|
| Tanagho 1981 | 25 posterior and 50 anterior bladder-tube repairs, plus six additional post-prostatectomy applications described | Approximately 70% success in the anterior group. The title's “10 years' experience” is the treatment period, not ten-year follow-up of all 50 patients.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup> |
| Koraitim 1985 | 27 patients: 10 standard anterior tubes, 17 treated with three other configurations | 85% reported overall success. Tailoring was the authors' rationale; no comparator proves that a particular configuration caused a better result.<sup>[[2]](#ref2)</sup> |
| Beck 1979 | Five difficult urethrovesical anastomoses during radical prostatectomy | The tube was anastomosed to the transected urethra. The authors report that it facilitated the anastomosis and improved transient postoperative incontinence. There was no control group.<sup>[[3]](#ref3)</sup> |
| Gallagher 1995 | 8 patients with complex adult incontinence (7 women with epispadias or severe urethral damage after obstetric or gynecologic procedures); 3 were judged unsuitable for AUS because of scarring and tissue loss | 5/8 (63%) completely continent and satisfied; 2 had ileal conduit diversion; 1 was awaiting further treatment. 4/5 (80%) potentially suitable for AUS were satisfied, against 1/3 (33%) of those unsuitable. Results were best with a healthy bladder and periurethral tissue.<sup>[[9]](#ref9)</sup> |
| Seaman/Benson 1996 | 29 tubularized bladder-neck reconstructions, compared with 30 selected controls | A 5 cm-base/3 cm-apex flap was tubularized over a 30 Fr catheter. In the reconstruction group, 7/29 were dry at 24 hours, 27/29 at 3 months and 28/29 at 6 months; one BNC occurred. The 30 comparison patients were randomly selected from those without a bladder flap, not randomized to treatment. Continence was higher at all follow-up times.<sup>[[5]](#ref5)</sup> |

Those dimensions belong to the Seaman modification and should not be presented as mandatory dimensions of Tanagho's original operation.

## Female urethral reconstruction

Radwan's 16-patient retrospective experience compared six anterior bladder-tube repairs with ten labial-flap repairs; 15 patients had follow-up. Complete continence was 4/6 and 6/9, respectively, at mean follow-up 42 months; two patients had partial continence across the combined evaluable cohort. The labial-flap group had one meatal stenosis and two cases of transient retention; anatomical repair succeeded in all patients. The tube was placed through a combined vaginal and abdominal approach. Equal observed proportions in these tiny selected groups do not establish equivalence, and the partial-continence figure applies to the combined cohort, not to each group.<sup>[[6]](#ref6)</sup>

Nayyar and colleagues cite three limitations of the classic Tanagho repair: the bladder neck is shifted anterosuperiorly, which may impair voiding; the posteriorly directed suture line risks vaginal fistula; and rotational tug on the bladder puts tension on the suture line. Their U-shaped modification was reported in three women with traumatic bladder-neck obliteration, with or without urogenital fistula. It keeps the native bladder neck fibers at the bladder neck. Tube lengths were 3.5, 3 and 3.5 cm, catheters were removed at 3 weeks, and follow-up was 15, 7 and 3 months. Favorable voiding and continence in these cases do not establish durable effectiveness or a preferred operation for all female outlet defects.<sup>[[4]](#ref4)</sup>

## Distinguish other bladder-flap operations

| Procedure | Difference |
|---|---|
| [Y-V plasty](/docs/surgical-techniques/04ab-bladder-neck-reconstruction/bnc-vuas/yv-plasty) | Advances a bladder flap into an opened stenosis without constructing a long tube |
| [T-plasty](/docs/surgical-techniques/04ab-bladder-neck-reconstruction/bnc-vuas/t-plasty-bnc) | Uses a modified two-flap configuration to widen the outlet |
| Pippi Salle urethral lengthening | Sutures an anterior bladder wall flap to the posterior wall in onlay fashion to create a flap valve, not a circumferential tube. It was reported in children with neurogenic incontinence or exstrophy; in 17 patients, 12 (70%) were continent for more than 4 hours, 2 had urethrovesical fistula and 3 had catheterization problems<sup>[[7]](#ref7)[[8]](#ref8)</sup> |
| Tubularized bladder-flap catheterizable channel | Creates a route for catheterization; its continence mechanism and outlet differ from a Tanagho neourethra. In a retrospective series of 173 channels (median follow-up 12.4 years), revision-free survival did not differ between appendicovesicostomy, Monti and tubularized bladder flap channels; 53% of all channels needed at least one revision, and revisions were more common with Monti channels<sup>[[11]](#ref11)</sup> |

See [catheterizable channels](/docs/surgical-techniques/04b-bladder-reconstruction/catheterizable-channels) for that separate application. Anterior bladder tissue is shared material, but the procedures are not interchangeable.

## Current clinical use

Bladder-tube reconstruction has been reported for female urethral reconstruction after trauma with total urethral loss, complex incontinence in patients unsuitable for AUS because of scarring or tissue loss, and epispadias in women.<sup>[[6]](#ref6)[[9]](#ref9)</sup> It is considered only after defining the defect, reservoir function, tissue quality, continence mechanism and feasible alternatives. Historical percentages cannot be used to rank it against current AUS, sling, graft or diversion strategies. The reports are small, heterogeneous and often include additional procedures; neither reliable spontaneous emptying nor freedom from further continence surgery is guaranteed.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[2]](#ref2)[[6]](#ref6)[[4]](#ref4)</sup>
