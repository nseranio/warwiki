# Transurethral Incision of Bladder Neck Contracture (TUIBNC)

**Transurethral incision of bladder neck contracture (TUIBNC)** is an initial endoscopic treatment for non-obliterative bladder neck stenosis after benign prostate surgery and for vesicourethral anastomotic stenosis (VUAS) after radical prostatectomy. These are different anatomical populations, and their results should not be pooled into one expected success rate. EAU guidelines recommend transurethral resection or hot-knife incision (strong) for bladder neck stenosis after benign outlet surgery, and visually controlled dilation or direct vision internal urethrotomy (weak) for non-obliterative VUAS.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup> AUA guidelines allow dilation, incision or resection according to surgeon preference (Expert Opinion for bladder neck contracture after endoscopic prostate surgery; Conditional Recommendation, Evidence Level C, for VUAS) and expect comparable outcomes.<sup>[[10]](#ref10)</sup>

See [BNC](/docs/surgical-techniques/04ab-bladder-neck-reconstruction/bnc), [VUAS](/docs/surgical-techniques/04ab-bladder-neck-reconstruction/vuas), the [resection technique](/docs/surgical-techniques/04ab-bladder-neck-reconstruction/transurethral-bladder-neck-resection), and, for open or robotic reconstruction, [Y-V plasty](/docs/surgical-techniques/04ab-bladder-neck-reconstruction/bnc-vuas/yv-plasty), [T-plasty](/docs/surgical-techniques/04ab-bladder-neck-reconstruction/bnc-vuas/t-plasty-bnc) and [combined abdominoperineal repair](/docs/surgical-techniques/04ab-bladder-neck-reconstruction/bnc-vuas/combined-abdominoperineal).

## Definition and etiology

BNC is fibrotic narrowing of the bladder neck that presents with obstructive symptoms, reduced flow and raised post-void residual. After transurethral prostate surgery it presented at a median of 5.8 months in a single-center review of 1008 patients (20 cases, 2%). Incidence was 4.7% after bipolar TURP and 1.3% after transurethral enucleation.<sup>[[18]](#ref18)</sup> After radical prostatectomy with or without radiation, the same problem is termed VUAS and dominates referral series.<sup>[[2]](#ref2)</sup>

## Selection and operative safety

Confirm the prior operation, radiation exposure, stenosis location and length, residual lumen, baseline continence, and bladder function. Identify fistula, radionecrosis or complete obliteration before attempting treatment. Do not attempt endoluminal treatment of a completely obliterated posterior stenosis (EAU strong recommendation). Releasing obstruction can reveal or worsen sphincteric incontinence.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup>

:::warning[Posterior incision depth]
Avoid deep incisions at 6 and 12 o'clock because of rectal injury and urosymphyseal fistula risk, particularly after radiation. Lateral incisions do not eliminate sphincter injury, extravasation or fistula risk. The desired endpoint is controlled opening of the stenosis within the patient's anatomy, not exposure of fat.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup>
:::

## Technique

1. Assess the lumen cystoscopically and establish safe access under direct vision. Antegrade assessment through a suprapubic tract can help when anatomy is uncertain.
2. In the published Nealon/Ramirez protocol, a 24 Fr balloon permits resectoscope access, followed by bilateral 3 and 9 o'clock incisions with an electrocautery Collins knife.<sup>[[2]](#ref2)[[3]](#ref3)</sup> Both series divide the scar deeply; depth must be adapted to the location, prior reconstruction and radiation injury. A Collins electrocautery knife is distinct from a cold knife.
3. Confirm a patent channel, hemostasis and catheter drainage. Catheter size and duration depend on tissue injury, the procedure performed and the drainage plan.

| Variant | Notes |
|---|---|
| Holmium laser incision | Reported with balloon dilation in day-surgery mode in 41 patients: six had urge incontinence and one recurred at 12 months. No comparison with knife incision exists.<sup>[[22]](#ref22)</sup> |
| Radial (tri- or quadrant) cold-knife incision | Used in mitomycin C protocols<sup>[[12]](#ref12)</sup> |
| Incision with transverse mucosal realignment | An endoscopic Y-V type repair; see [transverse mucosal realignment](/docs/surgical-techniques/04ab-bladder-neck-reconstruction/bnc-vuas/transurethral-incision-transverse-mucosal-realignment). Supported by small observational cohorts<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[4]](#ref4)</sup> |

Current posterior-stenosis evidence does not establish one incision energy as superior.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup>

## Outcomes and their limits

| Study | Population and design | Reported outcome |
|---|---|---|
| Nealon 2022 | Retrospective, 123 patients with heterogeneous BNC/VUAS; balloon plus Collins incisions, no injection | 101/123 (82.1%) patent after one procedure; 116/123 (94.3%) after a second; median follow-up 12 months. Patency assessment included passage of a 16 Fr cystoscope at approximately 2 months.<sup>[[2]](#ref2)</sup> |
| Ramirez 2013 | 50 patients; 78% had failed previous TUIBNC | 72% success after one procedure, 86% after two; mean follow-up 12.9 months.<sup>[[3]](#ref3)</sup> |
| Rosenbaum 2021 | 60 patients after benign prostate surgery; VUAS excluded; 49 resections and 11 incisions | 32/60 (53%) overall success. The small nonrandomized comparison does not establish equivalence of incision and resection.<sup>[[5]](#ref5)</sup> |
| Abramowitz 2021 | 19 patients, one surgeon; incision plus mucosal realignment | 89% success after one procedure and 100% after a repeat procedure; median follow-up 6 months. No new incontinence was reported, but the sample and follow-up cannot establish a negligible risk.<sup>[[4]](#ref4)</sup> |
| Quarta 2025 | 110 patients after BPE surgery, 91.8% with holmium laser incision | At 3 months, IPSS was mild in 64.0%, moderate in 30.7% and severe in 5.3%.<sup>[[11]](#ref11)</sup> |

A 2024 systematic review of 40 studies and 1,452 patients with post-prostatectomy anastomotic stenosis estimated endoscopic success at 72.8% (95% CI 64.4–79.9%); adjustment for publication bias lowered the estimate to 62.9% (53.6–71.4%). Radiotherapy was associated with lower success. Techniques, definitions and follow-up varied, and the pooled estimates do not establish superiority of a particular method.<sup>[[6]](#ref6)</sup>

## Risk factors for failure

| Factor | Evidence |
|---|---|
| Two or more prior endoscopic BNC procedures | Associated with failure in Ramirez (p = 0.03) and Nealon (the only factor on multivariable analysis)<sup>[[2]](#ref2)[[3]](#ref3)</sup> |
| More than 10 pack-years of smoking | Associated with failure in Ramirez (p = 0.039)<sup>[[3]](#ref3)</sup> |
| Pelvic radiation | Lower success; prospective comparisons are lacking<sup>[[6]](#ref6)[[10]](#ref10)</sup> |
| Shorter interval from prostate surgery to BNC | Recurrence was associated with a shorter interval in Rosenbaum (p = 0.01 and 0.046); onset under 6 months was associated with failure in Quarta (OR 3.87)<sup>[[5]](#ref5)[[11]](#ref11)</sup> |
| Small prostate at the original operation | Under 50 g was associated with failure in Quarta (OR 3.47)<sup>[[11]](#ref11)</sup> |

These are counseling factors from individual retrospective cohorts, not validated rules that mandate reconstruction after exactly two failures.

## Complications

Sphincteric incontinence is the main concern. Any endoluminal treatment may cause de novo incontinence (up to 25%) or worsen existing incontinence (up to 15%).<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup> Recurrence after a single procedure was 18% in Nealon, 28% in Ramirez and 47% in the mixed resection and incision cohort of Rosenbaum.<sup>[[2]](#ref2)[[3]](#ref3)[[5]](#ref5)</sup> Bleeding and infection are standard endoscopic risks.

## Intralesional adjuncts

Mitomycin C should not be used for posterior stenosis outside a clinical trial under EAU guidance. The TURNS cohort reported serious adverse events in 4/55 patients (7%), including osteitis, bladder-neck necrosis and rectourethral fistula; 3 needed or were planning cystectomy. Other uncontrolled series have more favorable safety reports, but they do not establish a reliably safe dose or incremental benefit.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[7]](#ref7)</sup> The AUA amendment notes conflicting data on mitomycin C for recurrent VUAS.<sup>[[10]](#ref10)</sup>

Reported mitomycin C protocols use cold-knife radial incisions and injection of 0.3 to 0.4 mg/mL at each site, or 2 mg in 5 mL sterile water, sometimes with one month of daily clean intermittent catheterization. These are individual surgeons' protocols.<sup>[[12]](#ref12)[[16]](#ref16)[[23]](#ref23)</sup>

|---|---|---|---|---|---|

| Rozanski 2021<sup>[[13]](#ref13)</sup> | 86 | 91% had a prior DVIU | 65% | 90% overall | Median 21.1 months |
| Sourial 2017<sup>[[14]](#ref14)</sup> | 29 | Recurrent VUAS after prostatectomy (injection plus dilation) | 79% | 86% with salvage injection | 12 months |
| Hacker 2022<sup>[[15]](#ref15)</sup> | 51 | BNC or VUAS after prostate cancer treatment | 45% | 84% (up to four procedures) | Median 32 months |
| Farrell 2015<sup>[[16]](#ref16)</sup> | 37 | Recurrent strictures and BNC | 75.7% needed no further surgery | | Median 23 months |
| TURNS 2015<sup>[[7]](#ref7)</sup> | 55 | 80% failed prior dilation or incision | 58% | 75% after repeat | Median 9.2 months |

Radiation was associated with lower success. In Rozanski, success was 94% in nonradiated and 76% in radiated patients (p = 0.04).<sup>[[13]](#ref13)</sup> In Hacker, patients treated with prostatectomy followed by radiation had the shortest interval to recurrence (p = 0.018).<sup>[[15]](#ref15)</sup>

Triamcinolone has been used with incision or resection in recurrent cases; the first report was of 7 patients in 1979.<sup>[[19]](#ref19)</sup> Zhang's 28-patient series reported 26/28 patent outlets after resection plus intraoperative and serial postoperative injections (2 mL of 40 mg/mL at 8 sites, then every 4 weeks for three doses); 25/28 had adequate voiding, with one patient limited by detrusor underactivity. This uncontrolled result cannot separate the effects of surgery, repeated instrumentation and steroid injection.<sup>[[8]](#ref8)</sup>

| Steroid series | n | Population | Result |
|---|---|---|---|
| Zhang 2021<sup>[[8]](#ref8)</sup> | 28 | Highly recurrent post-TURP BNC | 92.9% patent at median 2.8 years |
| Sun 2022<sup>[[17]](#ref17)</sup> | 46 | BNC after TURP or PVP; GreenLight vaporization plus weekly triamcinolone for three doses | No recurrence in follow-up; immunohistochemistry showed lower collagen I, MMP-3 and TGF-β after treatment |
| Wu 2024<sup>[[18]](#ref18)</sup> | 20 | BNC after TURP or enucleation; incision plus betamethasone | 7/20 (35%) recurred at median 1.8 months; median follow-up 35.8 months |
| Kravchick 2013<sup>[[20]](#ref20)</sup> | 14 of 29 | Recurrent anastomotic stenosis after prostatectomy; dilation plus TRUS-guided steroid | Lower re-treatment rate and no incontinence, against a 57.1% incontinence rate after resection |

A meta-analysis of randomized trials found that local steroids with internal urethrotomy prolonged the time to recurrence but did not change the high recurrence rate.<sup>[[21]](#ref21)</sup>

The Pang meta-analysis addressed male urethral strictures across heterogeneous settings. It found that any adjunct was associated with less recurrence (OR 0.37) and that mitomycin C had the lowest odds (intralesional OR 0.23, 95% CI 0.11-0.48). Its estimates do not directly compare adjuncts for BNC and do not establish mitomycin C as the best BNC adjunct. Urinary infection (2.9% to 14%), bleeding (8.8%) and extravasation (5.8%) were reported with steroid injection. Absence of necrosis in small steroid series does not prove zero risk.<sup>[[9]](#ref9)</sup>

For drug mechanisms, evidence and safety, use the [antimitotic/antifibrotic hub](/docs/foundations/pharmacology/dermatologic-topical-urethral/antimitotics-antifibrotics) and [intralesional corticosteroid hub](/docs/foundations/pharmacology/dermatologic-topical-urethral/intralesional-corticosteroids). The no-injection Nealon series shows that favorable outcomes can occur without an adjunct; cross-series comparisons do not establish that incision technique accounts for most of the benefit.<sup>[[2]](#ref2)</sup>

## Continence and further treatment

Record continence before treatment. In Ramirez's referral cohort, 39/50 already had concomitant SUI; 78% is that cohort's baseline prevalence, not an expected complication rate for all post-prostatectomy patients. Twenty-six subsequently underwent AUS placement at a mean of 2.9 months, and 2 of those 26 needed repeat transurethral treatment for recurrent stenosis. The authors reported more than 90% urethral patency. This selected cohort does not prescribe a universal safe implantation interval or guarantee long-term device safety.<sup>[[3]](#ref3)</sup> EAU advises waiting three to four months after endoluminal treatment before incontinence surgery, with cystoscopy to exclude recurrence first.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup>

Establish stable patency before continence surgery and reassess symptoms, storage function and suitability for an AUS. Repeated endoscopic treatment can stabilize selected non-obliterative stenoses (EAU weak recommendation). For recalcitrant stenosis, AUA allows robotic or open reconstruction (Conditional Recommendation, Evidence Level C), and cites robotic patency of 72.7% to 75% with a risk of incontinence that may require an AUS. The approach is chosen by location, tissue quality, radiation, bladder function and the patient's goals. EAU advises against posterior urethral stents (weak).<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[10]](#ref10)</sup>
