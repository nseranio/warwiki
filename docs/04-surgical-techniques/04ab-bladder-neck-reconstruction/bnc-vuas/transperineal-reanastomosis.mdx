# Transperineal Reanastomosis (TPRA)

**Transperineal reanastomosis (TPRA)** uses perineal access, scar excision and proximal urethral mobilization to reconnect the urethra to the bladder outlet. It has been used for highly recurrent or obliterative VUAS after radical prostatectomy, as a last option before urinary diversion, and when prior abdominal surgery makes transabdominal access difficult. It mirrors the perineal approach to PFUI repair.<sup>[[2]](#ref2)[[3]](#ref3)</sup> See [primary reanastomosis](/docs/surgical-techniques/04ab-bladder-neck-reconstruction/bnc-vuas/primary-reanastomosis) for operative principles, the retropubic and robotic approaches and the original cohorts.

## Patency and continence

In the Hamburg series, patency was 14/15 (93.3%) at a mean of 20.5 months, and 20/23 (87%) at a median of 45 months in the extended cohort. The 23-patient series extends the earlier 15-patient experience and does not add to it. Recurrences were treated endoscopically.<sup>[[2]](#ref2)[[3]](#ref3)</sup> Almost all patients were already incontinent: 14 of 15 in the first series, and all 23 in the extended series. Incontinence worsened in 9 of 15 (60%) in the first series, and no de novo incontinence occurred. AUS was implanted in 10 of 15 and 17 of 23 (73.9%).<sup>[[2]](#ref2)[[3]](#ref3)</sup>

Redo vesicourethral anastomosis differs from PFUI repair because the continence mechanism is already vulnerable. EAU guidelines make a strong recommendation to warn patients that incontinence is inevitable after transperineal redo VUA, because the approach disrupts the external sphincter, and that anti-incontinence surgery may be needed later. AUS placement is deferred at least three to six months so that patency is confirmed.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup> EAU weakly recommends a retropubic approach when the patient is continent before surgery, and notes that the two approaches have not been compared directly.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup> The AUA amendment cites an 83.3% incontinence rate after perineal reconstruction in preoperatively continent patients, drawn from small cohorts, so that figure is not a universal risk.<sup>[[4]](#ref4)</sup>

## Selection

| Favors TPRA | Favors another approach |
|---|---|
| Already incontinent, with delayed AUS acceptable | Continent before surgery, when preserving continence is central |
| Prior abdominal surgery limits retropubic access | Short VUAS in which a robotic or retropubic approach is feasible |
| Obliterative or highly recurrent VUAS needing perineal mobilization | Radiation-associated VUAS in which non-transecting dorsal BMG is feasible |
| A last outlet option before diversion | Patient unwilling to accept a staged AUS pathway |

The table summarizes selection logic used in the published series and is not a guideline algorithm. Other factors are stenosis length and location, available urethra, prior operations or radiation, bladder function and the patient's priorities. EAU advises redo VUA only with adequate bladder function and in the absence of urethral necrosis, calcification or fistula, and only in experienced high-volume centers, particularly after radiotherapy.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup>

## Operative logic

The operation borrows from posterior urethroplasty: perineal exposure, proximal urethral mobilization, scar excision and tension-free mucosa-to-mucosa reanastomosis. Longer gaps may require progressive perineal maneuvers such as corporal separation or partial inferior pubectomy, or a combined approach. In the post-prostatectomy patient the operation is aimed at outlet patency first and continence restoration later. Graft, flap, retropubic, transvesical and diversion options address different defects and are not ranked by comparing percentages from unrelated case series.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup>
