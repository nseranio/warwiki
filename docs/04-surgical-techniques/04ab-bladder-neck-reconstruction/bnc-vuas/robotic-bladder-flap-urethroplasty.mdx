# Robotic Bladder Flap Posterior Urethroplasty

**Robotic bladder flap posterior urethroplasty** uses an anterior bladder-wall flap to form a new vesicourethral anastomosis after division of a diseased outlet and removal of unsuitable scar. Zhao, Shakir and Zhao described it for selected recalcitrant BNC/VUAS in a nine-patient retrospective technical series. Follow-up was short, and the operation has not been shown to avoid incontinence or diversion.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup>

## Anatomical role

The flap supplies bladder tissue when direct reanastomosis may otherwise be difficult. It differs from [Y-V plasty](/docs/surgical-techniques/04ab-bladder-neck-reconstruction/bnc-vuas/yv-plasty), which widens an incised stenosis, and from a [Tanagho tube](/docs/surgical-techniques/04ab-bladder-neck-reconstruction/bnc-vuas/tanagho-flap), which creates a longer tubularized conduit. Viable bladder tissue, adequate mobility, bladder function and the urethral target determine whether this approach is feasible.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup>

Recurrent stenosis is not necessarily complete obliteration. Evaluate its location, length, residual lumen, radiation damage, fistula and baseline continence. The EAU guideline supports individualized reconstruction for appropriate refractory posterior disease. No guideline names this flap as preferred over other reconstructions.<sup>[[2]](#ref2)</sup>

## Operative sequence

The published operation uses transperitoneal robotic access.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup>

1. Mobilize the anterior bladder and expose the diseased outlet, protecting adjacent pelvic structures.
2. Localize the stenosis with combined endoscopic and robotic assessment. Cystoscopic transillumination is light localization; it does not require ICG injection. Near-infrared viewing of a cystoscope's light and fluorescence after administration of ICG are distinct techniques.<sup>[[3]](#ref3)</sup>
3. Divide the diseased bladder neck and remove unsuitable scar until viable tissue is reached.
4. Fashion an anterior bladder-wall flap that reaches the urethral target without tension and preserves its blood supply.
5. Create a mucosa-to-mucosa vesicourethral anastomosis and close the remaining bladder defect. Confirm hemostasis, watertight closure and catheter drainage.
6. Add tissue interposition or associated fistula repair when indicated. Individualize drainage and healing assessment.

The original report and operative video give the flap design. Tissue viability and available length determine the final construction, and the series does not define a fixed flap size or catheter duration.

## Index-series outcomes

| Outcome | Zhao 2022, n=9 |
|---|---|
| Mean operative time | 295.5 minutes overall; 264.5 minutes excluding three patients with ancillary procedures |
| Complications | No intraoperative complications reported; two postoperative complications |
| Follow-up | Mean 21.1 weeks |
| Freedom from recurrent stenosis | 7/9 (78%) without recurrence; both recurrences underwent balloon dilation |
| New stress incontinence | **0/3 patients continent before surgery** developed new SUI |

Only 3 of the 9 patients were continent before surgery, so the continence result does not mean all nine were continent or used at most one pad. Short follow-up in a small selected cohort does not show durable patency or comparative morbidity.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup>

## Alternatives and evidence limits

[Primary reanastomosis](/docs/surgical-techniques/04ab-bladder-neck-reconstruction/bnc-vuas/primary-reanastomosis), [Y-V plasty](/docs/surgical-techniques/04ab-bladder-neck-reconstruction/bnc-vuas/yv-plasty), [transvesical reconstruction](/docs/surgical-techniques/04ab-bladder-neck-reconstruction/bnc-vuas/transvesical-approaches), graft augmentation and urinary diversion address different anatomical situations, and percentages from their separate case series are not comparable. Patients are counseled about further stenosis treatment and staged continence surgery, with patency and bladder function reassessed first.<sup>[[2]](#ref2)</sup>
