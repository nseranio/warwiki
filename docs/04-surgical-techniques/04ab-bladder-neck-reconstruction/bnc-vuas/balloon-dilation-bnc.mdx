# Balloon Dilation for Bladder Neck Contracture

Balloon dilation treats selected nonobliterative posterior stenoses. It is used as a standalone treatment and as the first step before incision or resection. In the series below, standalone dilation succeeded in 59% of patients, and dilation followed by incision achieved 82% patency after one procedure and 94% after two. Bladder-neck stenosis after benign prostate surgery and VUAS after radical prostatectomy should be distinguished. The EAU guideline recommends visually controlled dilation or direct vision internal urethrotomy as first-line treatment of nonobliterative VUAS (weak) and resection or hot-knife incision as first-line treatment of bladder-neck stenosis after benign outlet surgery (strong). It recommends against endoluminal treatment of complete posterior obliteration (strong).<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup>

See [TUIBNC](/docs/surgical-techniques/04ab-bladder-neck-reconstruction/transurethral-incision-bnc), [TURBN](/docs/surgical-techniques/04ab-bladder-neck-reconstruction/transurethral-bladder-neck-resection), [BNC](/docs/surgical-techniques/04ab-bladder-neck-reconstruction/bnc), and the [drug-coated balloon evidence page](/docs/surgical-techniques/04a-urethral-reconstruction/drug-coated-balloon-pointer).

## Technique and selection

Define the stenosis, prior treatment, radiation injury and baseline continence. Establish the true lumen under vision, and use a guidewire and imaging as needed for safe positioning. Select the balloon and inflation parameters for the device and anatomy. Do not force access through an uncertain lumen. Dilation can injure the sphincter or create a false passage.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup>

Dilation can be repeated in selected nonobliterative recurrence after discussion of durability and alternatives. Increasing obstruction, recurrent procedures, fistula or tissue necrosis should prompt reassessment rather than further dilation.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup>

## Standalone balloon dilation

Ramchandani evaluated fluoroscopically guided transurethral balloon dilation for VUAS after radical retropubic prostatectomy in a series of 45 consecutive patients. Balloon dilation was the primary treatment in 27, and 16 (59%) responded. Ten of the 11 who did not respond underwent bladder-neck incision, and 7 of those 10 needed repeat attempts or subsequent balloon dilation. New incontinence developed in one patient treated primarily with incision and in none treated with balloon dilation. The authors suggested that refractoriness may relate to dense scar.<sup>[[2]](#ref2)</sup>

The EAU guideline summarizes dilation series for VUAS and radiation-induced bulbomembranous stenosis as patency rates between 0% and 89%, a risk of de novo incontinence of 0% to 11% and no other reported complications (level of evidence 3). Most series report visually controlled dilation in stenosis without complete obliteration.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup> The AUA guideline states that first-time VUAS is treated successfully in about 50% to 80% of cases, that all endoscopic techniques have similar success rates and that no study directly compares them. It lists dilation, vesicourethral incision and transurethral resection as options for post-prostatectomy VUAS (Conditional Recommendation, Evidence Level C) and dilation, incision or resection for bladder-neck contracture after endoscopic prostate procedures (Expert Opinion).<sup>[[9]](#ref9)</sup>

## Balloon dilation as a preparatory step

Dilation of the contracture to admit the resectoscope, followed by deep incision or resection, is a common combined approach.

| Combined approach | Series | Outcome |
|---|---|---|
| 24 Fr balloon, then bilateral Collins-knife incisions at 3 and 9 o'clock (Nealon 2022) | 123 patients, retrospective; success defined as a patent bladder neck and 16 Fr cystoscope passage at 2 months | 101/123 (82.1%) patent at 12 months after one procedure and 116/123 (94.3%) after a second. Two or more prior endoscopic procedures were associated with failure. This is the outcome of the combined procedure, not the independent effect of balloon dilation<sup>[[3]](#ref3)</sup> |
| Transurethral columnar balloon dilation of the prostate, then holmium laser incision, in a day-surgery model (Sun 2023) | 41 patients, retrospective | All completed within 24 h. Six patients had urge incontinence and one had recurrence at 12 months. IPSS, Qmax, quality of life and PVR were better at 1 and 6 months<sup>[[7]](#ref7)</sup> |

These cohorts differ in selection, era, intervention and outcome definition. Comparing their percentages, or comparing them with the standalone dilation series above, does not show that one technique is intrinsically more effective or which scar mechanism explains a difference. For the TUIBNC technique including the dilation step, see [TUIBNC](/docs/surgical-techniques/04ab-bladder-neck-reconstruction/transurethral-incision-bnc).

## Drug-coated balloons in posterior stenosis

Treatment of posterior BNC or VUAS with a paclitaxel-coated balloon is off-label. The FDA anterior-urethral indication and the ROBUST anterior-stricture trials are not evidence of posterior efficacy. Current guideline-supported selection for anterior disease is narrower than all urethral stenoses; see the [device pharmacology hub](/docs/foundations/pharmacology/dermatologic-topical-urethral/drug-coated-balloon-therapy) for labeling and paclitaxel precautions.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup>

Berg's retrospective comparative study included 65 DCB and 76 standard-treatment patients with posterior stenosis (VUAS and bladder-neck contracture), matched for age, initial treatment and radiotherapy history. DCB treatment was associated with better recurrence-free survival (log-rank p = 0.013). The adjusted HR was 0.40 (95% CI 0.19–0.87), a 60% lower estimated hazard, not an absolute success rate. In the DCB group PVR fell from 55 mL to 0 mL and Qmax rose from 9 to 22 mL/s. Matching does not remove residual confounding, and long-term comparative durability is uncertain.<sup>[[4]](#ref4)</sup>

A 2025 narrative review reports success rates of 40% to 72% for traditional endoscopic treatment of posterior urethral stenosis, with 90% of patients needing repeat intervention within 2 years, and short-term success approaching 85% to 90% for newer endoscopic techniques including drug-coated balloon dilation and transurethral mucosal realignment. These are pooled descriptions of heterogeneous short-term series.<sup>[[8]](#ref8)</sup>

An international survey of 102 Optilume users found that 65% used it off-label for bladder-neck stenoses. Users treating 10 or more patients a year were more likely to use it for bladder-neck stricture (OR 4.66, 95% CI 1.55–14.03). Adoption and its association with operator volume do not show effectiveness or define a learning curve.<sup>[[5]](#ref5)</sup>

For any DCB use, counsel using the current IFU: abstinence or condom use for 30 days, and avoidance of pregnancy with effective contraception for 6 months when a partner can become pregnant. These precautions apply even when the anatomical use is off-label. Drug and label details remain in the [pharmacology hub](/docs/foundations/pharmacology/dermatologic-topical-urethral/drug-coated-balloon-therapy).<sup>[[6]](#ref6)</sup>

## Complications and counseling

Discuss recurrence, bleeding, infection, extravasation and new or worsened incontinence. In the EAU summary, endoluminal treatment of posterior stenosis may be associated with de novo incontinence in up to 25% and worsening of existing incontinence in up to 15%.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup> No available comparison shows that balloon dilation has the lowest complication rate for every BNC or VUAS population. Radiation injury and the need for subsequent continence surgery require particular planning. Refractory stenosis warrants specialist reconstructive assessment, and the choice depends on anatomy, bladder function and patient goals.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup>
