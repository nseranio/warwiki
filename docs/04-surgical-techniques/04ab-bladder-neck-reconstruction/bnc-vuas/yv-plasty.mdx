# Y-V Plasty for BNC and VUAS

**Y-V plasty** advances a vascularized bladder-wall flap into an opened stenotic outlet. It can be performed open, laparoscopically or robotically. It is a reconstructive option for selected refractory bladder-neck contracture (BNC) or vesicourethral anastomotic stenosis (VUAS). Recurrent scarring and incontinence remain possible.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[2]](#ref2)[[3]](#ref3)</sup>

The rationale is that a well-vascularized flap replaces fibrotic tissue with healthy mucosa at the opened outlet. After endoscopic incision, by contrast, the scar heals by secondary intention. No randomized trial compares Y-V plasty with repeat endoscopic treatment.<sup>[[4]](#ref4)</sup>

## Guideline position

EAU recommends bladder-neck reconstruction with Y-V or T-plasty for refractory bladder-neck stenosis (weak recommendation). It reports patency of 83–100% and de novo incontinence of 0–14% in these series (level of evidence 3), and it recommends warning patients about de novo incontinence (strong). EAU also notes that a minimally invasive approach is increasingly used.<sup>[[5]](#ref5)</sup>

The AUA 2023 amendment states that surgeons may perform robotic or open reconstruction for recalcitrant BNC or post-prostatectomy VUAS (Conditional Recommendation; Evidence Level Grade C). It reports robotic patency of 72.7–75% and, among men continent before surgery, 82% continence afterward. Open reconstruction had patency of 70–100%. Among preoperatively continent men, 10% were incontinent after a retropubic approach and 83.3% after a perineal approach. It advises tailoring treatment to patient preference, prior radiotherapy and baseline incontinence, and it notes that reconstruction may cause incontinence requiring a later [artificial urinary sphincter](/docs/surgical-techniques/04f-incontinence-procedures/procedures/artificial-urinary-sphincter).<sup>[[4]](#ref4)</sup> EAU notes that retropubic and perineal approaches have not been compared directly for incontinence.<sup>[[5]](#ref5)</sup>

## Selection

Define stenosis location, length, residual lumen, prior surgery or radiation, tissue quality, bladder function and baseline continence. Reported series usually enroll men after about two failed endoscopic treatments (a mean of two prior attempts in Granieri's cohort), but no fixed rule requires reconstruction after exactly two failures. Repeated endoscopic treatment remains an option for selected nonobliterative recurrence.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[5]](#ref5)</sup>

A viable bladder flap must reach the opened outlet without tension. Obliteration, inadequate tissue or a long gap may call for a different reconstruction. The Y-V and transvesical series have mostly enrolled short stenoses (Zheng 2 cm or less; Lee under 2 cm), and no cutoff has been validated for choosing a flap. BNC after benign prostate surgery and VUAS after prostatectomy have different relationships to the remaining sphincter, so counsel about new or worsened SUI and possible later AUS treatment.<sup>[[6]](#ref6)[[7]](#ref7)[[5]](#ref5)</sup>

Magnetic resonance urethrography (MRU) measured obliteration length more accurately than RUG/VCUG in a multicenter series: 0.91 cm on MRU and 1.72 cm on RUG/VCUG against an intraoperative length of 0.96 cm. MRU also detected false passages and diverticula.<sup>[[7]](#ref7)</sup>

## Robotic anterior Y-V sequence

The Granieri/Zhao description uses transperitoneal robotic exposure.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup>

1. Place standard robotic ports. Open the space of Retzius and mobilize the bladder while protecting adjacent structures.
2. Localize the stenosis with cystoscopy. Firefly imaging is used to identify the stenosis from the abdominal side.
3. Open the stenosis anteriorly into viable urethra and extend the incision proximally onto the bladder to create the Y and its V-shaped bladder flap. Excise fibrotic tissue.
4. Advance the flap's distally directed apex into the distal urethrotomy and suture viable mucosal edges without tension. The apex does not point proximally while being advanced distally.
5. Close the remaining defect, assess hemostasis and watertightness, and establish catheter drainage. The index protocol placed a closed suction drain, removed before discharge, and a 22 Fr catheter, removed in the office at about two weeks (median 15 days). The median operative time was 240 minutes. Adapt catheter duration to healing and operative findings.

Y-V plasty widens an opened outlet by tissue advancement. It is not synonymous with circumferential excision and primary reanastomosis. Scar excision and flap geometry depend on the chosen modification.

In the open technique of Masumoto, a midline lower abdominal incision exposes the bladder neck. A V-shaped incision in the anterior bladder wall forms the flap, and the bladder neck is incised from the apex of the V to healthy urethra, forming the Y. Fibrotic tissue is excised, the posterior urethra and bladder are anastomosed, and the flap is sutured to healthy urethra with 4-0 PDS interrupted sutures.<sup>[[3]](#ref3)</sup>

## Variants

Abo Youssef and John described an extraperitoneal robotic "inverted" Y-V plasty that avoids intraperitoneal entry. Median skin-to-skin time was 131 minutes and the catheter was removed at a median of 10 days.<sup>[[2]](#ref2)</sup> This series and Granieri's are separate cohorts, so the times do not show that one approach is faster.

Zang and colleagues describe an extraperitoneal laparoscopic modification with two absorbable barbed sutures. The mucosa and submucosa are closed continuously in a V shape, and serosa and muscularis are then sutured to reinforce the closure. The authors describe this as reducing anastomotic leakage and operative time compared with interrupted sutures, without a comparative test.<sup>[[8]](#ref8)</sup> Zheng's 31-patient multicenter series used the same modification with incisions at 5 and 7 o'clock and layered barbed closure. The median operative time was 75 minutes (range 62–192). The series selected obliteration segments of 2 cm or less without false passages or diverticula on MRU. It does not show that barbed sutures reduce leaks or that MRU is universally superior to RUG/VCUG.<sup>[[7]](#ref7)</sup>

## Results with study-specific definitions

| Study | Patients and follow-up | Findings |
|---|---|---|
| Granieri 2018 | 7 patients: 6 BNC, 1 VUAS; median 8 months | All patent. Two had **persistent** incontinence at one pad daily.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup> |
| Shamout 2022 (open) | 18 patients with refractory BNC after TURP; mean 14.8 months | Success (no further instrumentation or surgery) 100%. Persistent storage symptoms in 61%; higher BMI and baseline IPSS predicted a worse OAB-V8 change.<sup>[[9]](#ref9)</sup> |
| Abo Youssef 2023 | 30 patients; median 27 months | Five treatment failures at 24 months, of which **two were restrictures**. The 83.3% treatment-success endpoint is not simply anatomic patency. Two major complications (Clavien IIIa and IV).<sup>[[2]](#ref2)</sup> |
| Viegas 2024 | 21 patients: 15 BNC, 6 VUAS | Five patients had SUI, all in the six-patient VUAS subgroup. One already had an AUS and three had concomitant AUS implantation.<sup>[[6]](#ref6)</sup> |
| Zheng 2024 | 31 selected short obliterative outlets | Patency 28/31 (90.3%); four had persistent SUI with leakage of 80–120 g/day. Three recurrences were treated by direct vision internal urethrotomy.<sup>[[7]](#ref7)</sup> |
| Masumoto 2025 | 5 open post-TURP repairs; median 13 months | All patent; three used one to two pads postoperatively.<sup>[[3]](#ref3)</sup> |

In Abo Youssef's 30 patients, IPSS fell from 17 to 11 in the short term (1–6 months) and 6 in the intermediate term (6–24 months). Qmax rose from 7.4 to 13 and then 17 mL/s, and PVR fell from 90 mL to 0 mL. SF-8 quality-of-life improvement was not significant.<sup>[[2]](#ref2)</sup>

These observational series differ in approach, anatomy and endpoints. Do not combine them into a ranking against BMG or direct reanastomosis. A systematic review of ten robotic bladder-neck reconstruction series (119 men; techniques and etiologies varied) reported pooled patency of 80% and pooled de novo incontinence of 17% (8/47).<sup>[[10]](#ref10)</sup> The TURNS cohorts and Zhang's 105-patient robotic series also mix reconstruction types. In the 105-patient series, 57.1% had prior pelvic radiation, 6.7% had a Clavien grade 3 or higher complication within 30 days, and 24.8% needed at least one reintervention. The 28.6% who received an AUS is a whole-cohort rate and not a BMG-specific new-SUI rate.<sup>[[11]](#ref11)[[12]](#ref12)[[13]](#ref13)</sup>

## BNC versus VUAS

Viegas compared robotic Y-V plasty in BNC after endoscopic BPH treatment and in VUAS. Patency was similar. Perioperative outcomes were worse in VUAS, consistent with its position next to the external sphincter mechanism. Groups were small (15 BNC, 6 VUAS), so this is a subgroup signal and not a universal risk.<sup>[[6]](#ref6)</sup>

| Parameter | BNC (n = 15) | VUAS (n = 6) | p |
|---|---|---|---|
| Operative time | 146.7 min | 277.5 min | 0.008 |
| Hospital stay | 1.7 days | 3.2 days | 0.03 |
| Postoperative complications | 26.7% | 66.7% | 0.14 |
| SUI (including existing or concomitant AUS) | 0% | 83.3% | less than 0.0001 |
| Long-term reoperation | 0 | 3 patients | 0.05 |
| Improved (PGI-I 1–2) | 80% | 83.3% | 0.31 |

Stricture recurrence occurred in 9.5% of the whole cohort with no difference between groups (p = 0.50).<sup>[[6]](#ref6)</sup>

## Robotic versus open perineal reconstruction

In a 28-patient retrospective comparison of VUAS reconstruction (18 open perineal, 10 robot-assisted), patency was similar (77.8% vs 80.0%; p = 0.944). De novo incontinence was lower in the robotic group (16.6% vs 100%; p = 0.031), with less blood loss (100 vs 200 mL) and a shorter hospital stay (3 vs 4 days). Operative times were comparable (120 vs 150 min; p = 0.175).<sup>[[14]](#ref14)</sup> The study is small and nonrandomized, so it does not show that robotic access itself preserves continence. EAU advises warning patients that incontinence is inevitable after transperineal redo anastomosis, and it offers a retropubic approach to men who are continent before surgery (weak).<sup>[[5]](#ref5)</sup>

## Radiation and additional reconstruction

Radiation injury affects tissue selection and healing. In a 21-man single-surgeon series of robotic posterior reconstruction, the five irradiated men had reintervention in 80% (vs 0% and 10% in the benign and prostatectomy groups), functional success of 60% (vs 100% and 100%) and AUS placement in 80% (vs 0% and 30%).<sup>[[15]](#ref15)</sup> In a retrospective cohort of 809 men after robotic prostatectomy (VUAS in 2.3%, urethral stenosis in 1.2%), radiotherapy was independently associated with stenosis formation (estimated 5-year risk 7.3% vs 2.1%).<sup>[[16]](#ref16)</sup> In the Savun comparison, radiotherapy and preoperative incontinence were associated with patency failure (p = 0.007 and p = 0.041).<sup>[[14]](#ref14)</sup> EAU notes that flaps such as gracilis or peritoneum may help protect the anastomosis in irradiated patients, and that ReDo anastomosis needs adequate bladder function and no urethral necrosis, calcification or fistula.<sup>[[5]](#ref5)</sup> Small cohorts cannot establish a single strongest predictor across techniques.

When local tissue is inadequate, consider [dorsal BMG](/docs/surgical-techniques/04ab-bladder-neck-reconstruction/bnc-vuas/dorsal-bmg-bnc), [subtrigonal inlay](/docs/surgical-techniques/04ab-bladder-neck-reconstruction/bnc-vuas/subtrigonal-inlay), [transvesical repair](/docs/surgical-techniques/04ab-bladder-neck-reconstruction/bnc-vuas/transvesical-approaches), [reanastomosis](/docs/surgical-techniques/04ab-bladder-neck-reconstruction/bnc-vuas/primary-reanastomosis), or combined reconstruction according to anatomy.

- **Buccal mucosal graft.** A multi-institutional retrospective series of dorsal onlay BMG urethroplasty after prostatectomy and radiation (45 men) had 7 recurrences and no de novo incontinence. Median follow-up in men without recurrence was 21 months, and 28 men were incontinent both before and after surgery.<sup>[[17]](#ref17)</sup> A robotic subtrigonal BMG inlay for refractory BNC improved Qmax from 2 to 27 mL/s in a single case report.<sup>[[18]](#ref18)</sup>
- **Vascularized tissue.** Rectus abdominis, omental and gracilis flaps were used as ancillary procedures in a nine-patient single-port robotic BMG series. Omental, rectus and gracilis transfers need different harvest approaches, and a robotic urethroplasty does not imply robotic gracilis harvest.<sup>[[19]](#ref19)</sup> A perivesical fat rotational flap has been described in three cases as an alternative when omentum is unavailable.<sup>[[20]](#ref20)</sup>
- **Transperineal urethral advancement.** When a tension-free anastomosis cannot be achieved, robotic anastomosis assisted by transperineal urethral mobilization preserves the orthotopic tract. Incontinence is expected, and an AUS can be placed in the following months. This is a technique report.<sup>[[21]](#ref21)</sup>

## Complications and follow-up

Discuss recurrent stenosis, leakage or fistula, infection, bleeding and incontinence. Osteitis pubis with pubovesical fistula (Clavien IIIb) occurred in 1 of 12 patients in the first TURNS robotic series and required vesicopubic fistula repair with pubic bone debridement. Two of 30 patients in the Abo Youssef series had major complications.<sup>[[11]](#ref11)[[2]](#ref2)</sup> SUI requiring AUS is the most frequent sequela, particularly after VUAS reconstruction.<sup>[[6]](#ref6)[[13]](#ref13)</sup>

Confirm healing and stable patency, then reassess storage function and continence before any AUS surgery. EAU advises deferring AUS implantation, usually for at least three to six months, because of the risk of recurrent VUAS with the cuff in place.<sup>[[5]](#ref5)</sup>
