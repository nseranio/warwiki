# Combined Abdominoperineal Reconstruction

Combined abdominal and perineal access is an option for selected complex posterior defects when a single route cannot provide adequate exposure, tissue reach or associated repair. It can be entirely open or combine robotic abdominal dissection with open perineal work. It is not synonymous with every urethral "pull-through" or progressive perineal urethroplasty.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[2]](#ref2)[[3]](#ref3)</sup>

## Definition

Transperineal urethral advancement, or urethral pull-through, mobilizes the bulbar urethra extensively through a perineal incision. The scarred or obliterated segment is excised, and the healthy distal urethra is advanced to the proximal stump (prostatic urethra or bladder neck) for a spatulated, mucosa-to-mucosa, tension-free anastomosis over a catheter. Standard end-to-end anastomotic urethroplasty relies on more limited mobilization and direct re-approximation. The operation can be performed entirely open or as a hybrid with robotic abdominal dissection.<sup>[[11]](#ref11)[[12]](#ref12)</sup>

## Indications

- Pelvic fracture urethral distraction defects (PFUDD), the most common indication. Defect lengths in these series ranged from 1.5 to 7 cm.<sup>[[4]](#ref4)[[11]](#ref11)[[12]](#ref12)</sup>
- Recalcitrant posterior stenosis, including [VUAS](/docs/surgical-techniques/04ab-bladder-neck-reconstruction/vuas) after radical prostatectomy when endoscopic treatment has failed.<sup>[[2]](#ref2)[[3]](#ref3)</sup>
- Failed prior urethroplasty or endoscopic management. Of the patients in the Wang and Yin series, 46% and 41% had had previous failed management.<sup>[[11]](#ref11)[[12]](#ref12)</sup>
- Posterior urethral stricture with urethrorectal fistula, combined with [fistula repair](/docs/surgical-techniques/04h-fistula-repair) and tissue interposition.<sup>[[17]](#ref17)[[18]](#ref18)</sup>
- Pediatric and adolescent posttraumatic posterior stricture.<sup>[[19]](#ref19)[[20]](#ref20)</sup>
- Defunctionalized bladder with recalcitrant stenosis, treated by cystectomy, neobladder formation and urethral pull-through as a salvage orthotopic reconstruction.<sup>[[10]](#ref10)</sup>

## Distinguish PFUI from post-prostatectomy VUAS

For most delayed PFUI repairs, progressive perineal anastomotic urethroplasty is the standard reconstructive approach. A combined route is reserved for complexity such as associated rectourethral fistula, bladder-neck or prostatic injury or difficult recurrent anatomy. Corporal separation or inferior pubectomy can sometimes be performed through the perineum alone; long radiographic length by itself does not mandate abdominal access.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[4]](#ref4)[[5]](#ref5)</sup>

After prostatectomy, the anastomotic defect, residual sphincter and radiation injury create a different situation. Continence outcomes from traumatic bulboprostatic repairs should not be applied to redo vesicourethral reconstruction. See [primary reanastomosis](/docs/surgical-techniques/04ab-bladder-neck-reconstruction/bnc-vuas/primary-reanastomosis) for VUAS evidence.

## Operative principles

The repair is performed through a perineal incision in lithotomy (standard, high or exaggerated), through an inverted-Y incision in Wang's suspension technique. After pelvic fracture urethral injury, lithotomy may be limited until orthopedic and soft-tissue injuries have resolved.<sup>[[21]](#ref21)[[25]](#ref25)</sup> The steps of the open operation are:

1. Divide the bulbospongiosus muscle and expose the bulbar urethra circumferentially.
2. Mobilize the urethra proximally and distally to gain length, distally as far as the penoscrotal junction in some techniques.<sup>[[11]](#ref11)[[21]](#ref21)</sup>
3. Transect and excise the scar. In Sa's progressive strategy the urethra is transected first at the proximal margin of the stenosis. If the proximal end is too deep to mobilize fully, transection is made at the distal margin and the scar is cored out to expose the proximal lumen.<sup>[[13]](#ref13)</sup>
4. Identify the proximal (prostatic) lumen with a sound or catheter passed through the suprapubic tract.
5. Advance the mobilized distal urethra and suture it to the proximal stump with interrupted absorbable sutures over a catheter.<sup>[[11]](#ref11)[[13]](#ref13)</sup>

The principles that apply to both open and combined repair are:

1. Map the urethral and bladder ends, defect length, fistula, tissue quality and baseline continence. Confirm the intended reservoir and drainage plan.
2. Position for safe perineal and abdominal access, accounting for operative duration and pressure and nerve injury risk.
3. Use the abdominal component to expose the proximal target and mobilize bladder tissue when needed. Perineal dissection exposes the distal urethra.
4. Mobilize only as much as needed for a viable, tension-free repair. Routine maximal mobilization beyond the penoscrotal junction can jeopardize vascularity or length.
5. Excise unsuitable scar, identify viable mucosal edges and construct the anastomosis. Add length-gaining maneuvers or tissue transfer according to anatomy.
6. Repair associated fistula and consider vascularized interposition when appropriate. Confirm watertightness and durable drainage.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[2]](#ref2)[[3]](#ref3)[[4]](#ref4)</sup>

"Pull-through" publications describe several modifications, many performed perineally. They do not establish that more extensive mobilization is simpler, safer or superior to standard tension-free anastomotic principles. Detailed PFUI techniques belong in the urethral-reconstruction domain.

### Adjunctive maneuvers for gaining length

When mobilization alone is insufficient, the maneuvers are added sequentially, as Webster and Ramon described in 74 patients with a 96% rate of excellent results (defects 1.5 to 7 cm).<sup>[[4]](#ref4)</sup>

- **Corporal separation** divides the intercorporal septum so that the urethra passes between the corpora.
- **Inferior pubectomy** removes the inferior margin of the pubic symphysis.
- **Supracrural rerouting** passes the urethra around one side of the corpora cavernosa. Li reported this in 37 patients with long-segment complex stricture, with 31 of the 34 followed achieving unobstructed voiding (mean follow-up 41 months).<sup>[[22]](#ref22)</sup>
- **Urethral suspension** suspends the proximal urethra and surrounding tissues with sutures at the 2, 5, 7 and 10 o'clock positions to expose the proximal mucosa. Wang reported unobstructed flow in 22 of 24 patients (91.7%) with a median stricture length of 5.1 cm, at a median follow-up of 13.6 months.<sup>[[21]](#ref21)</sup>

In the multicenter TURNS series of 122 posterior urethroplasties after PFUI, 95% were performed perineally and 5% abdominoperineally. Corporal splitting was used in 36%, pubectomy in 13% and supracrural rerouting in 2%. Younger age, longer defects and angioembolization were associated with the need for ancillary maneuvers, and 91% needed no repeat intervention.<sup>[[5]](#ref5)</sup> In Fu's series of 301 patients, success was 89.3% with simple perineal anastomosis, 86.5% with corporal separation, 84.2% with inferior pubectomy and 85.7% with rerouting. The groups were not randomized, and the authors concluded that the maneuvers are useful for achieving a tension-free anastomosis.<sup>[[14]](#ref14)</sup>

### Pull-through and standard anastomotic urethroplasty

- Wang's group describes the pull-through as simple and less demanding, and especially suitable in patients with previous failed treatment. Yin's group suggests it might be less demanding and less time-consuming. Neither series compared it with standard end-to-end repair.<sup>[[11]](#ref11)[[12]](#ref12)</sup>
- Koraitim advised attempting anastomosis through the perineum first in every case, with the transpubic route used only when a tension-free bulboprostatic anastomosis could not be achieved from below.<sup>[[15]](#ref15)</sup>
- Netto (1985) preferred the transpubic approach for complex strictures, damaged urethral lengths above 3 cm and small children, reserving perineal pull-through for low-lying strictures under 3 cm. Good results were reported in 69% of adults with the transpubic approach and 66% with perineal pull-through.<sup>[[16]](#ref16)</sup>

## Combined robotic/perineal evidence

The AUA guideline states that surgeons may perform robotic or open reconstruction for recalcitrant bladder-neck or vesicourethral anastomotic stenosis (Conditional Recommendation; Evidence Level C). It reports robotic patency of 72.7% to 75% and open retropubic or perineal patency of 70% to 100%. Among men continent before surgery, 82% remained continent after robotic reconstruction, 10% became incontinent after an open retropubic approach and 83.3% after a perineal reconstruction.<sup>[[25]](#ref25)</sup>

The robotic abdominal component gives visualization and ergonomics for deep suture placement. Unterberg described robotic-assisted proximal suture placement during perineal urethroplasty in 10 patients: robotic set-up took 15 minutes, suture placement 30 to 45 minutes, and no patient had extravasation on the postoperative VCUG.<sup>[[23]](#ref23)</sup> The robotic approach also allows adjunctive procedures in the same setting, including prostatectomy for residual obstructing tissue, bladder-neck resitting, omental or gracilis interposition and buccal graft placement, as in a single-port series of nine patients.<sup>[[3]](#ref3)[[24]](#ref24)</sup>

Cavallo's retrospective multi-institutional series comprised 12 complex posterior reconstructions, with mean follow-up 596 days. Eight required prostatectomy, four gracilis interposition and one corporal splitting. These selected patients were not a uniform post-radical-prostatectomy VUAS population.<sup>[[3]](#ref3)</sup>

| Outcome | Cavallo 2021 |
|---|---|
| Recurrent stenosis | 2/12; mean time to recurrence 187.5 postoperative days, not overall follow-up |
| Reported new SUI | 4 patients; this is distinct from the number subsequently receiving an AUS |
| AUS placement | 9/12, at mean 359.2 days after reconstruction; no erosion reported during observed follow-up |
| Other complications | One urinary leak, one thromboembolic event, two wound abscesses; two reported new ED cases |

The cohort demonstrates feasibility in selected complex anatomy. It has no contemporaneous open-only control proving equal complication rates or lower morbidity.<sup>[[3]](#ref3)</sup>

Rodriguez's technical report describes perineal urethral advancement when robotic retropubic dissection alone cannot achieve a tension-free anastomosis. The distal urethra is mobilized through a perineal incision and advanced to create a tension-free vesicourethral anastomosis, preserving an orthotopic voiding configuration. Incontinence is expected, and an AUS is planned in the following months. It illustrates an option, not a requirement that every patient follow the same staged AUS pathway.<sup>[[2]](#ref2)</sup>

## Broader robotic studies are not combined-approach trials

Zhang's 105-patient robotic series included 41 combined approaches and several reconstruction types. Overall reintervention was approximately one quarter; 30 patients received an AUS. Those whole-cohort results are not the patency or new-SUI rates of a specific combined repair. Shakir's 32-patient cohort likewise mixed techniques and reported 24 patent outlets versus 26 patients voiding per urethra.<sup>[[6]](#ref6)[[7]](#ref7)</sup>

Savun compared only 18 open perineal and 10 robotic cases retrospectively. Similar observed patency (77.8% and 80%) does not establish equivalence, and differences in incontinence cannot be assigned causally to access route without adequate control of baseline anatomy and function. Blood loss and hospital stay were significantly lower in the robot-assisted group (p = 0.001 for each), and operative time and complication rates did not differ significantly. Pelvic radiotherapy, preoperative incontinence and postoperative complications were associated with patency failure.<sup>[[8]](#ref8)</sup>

Radiation affects selection and healing. In Zhang's series 57.1% of patients had prior pelvic radiation, and in the TURNS series 50% did.<sup>[[6]](#ref6)[[7]](#ref7)</sup> Bearrick's irradiated subgroup contained only five patients: four required reintervention, four ultimately achieved anatomic success, three functional success and four an AUS. These small denominators should not be presented as universal radiation-risk estimates.<sup>[[9]](#ref9)</sup>

## Complications

- **Urinary incontinence.** In Sa's series, 8.5% had some degree of incontinence after final repair.<sup>[[13]](#ref13)</sup> Wang and Yin reported all patients continent. Combined robotic and perineal series after prostatectomy report higher new SUI (33.3% in Cavallo).<sup>[[3]](#ref3)[[11]](#ref11)[[12]](#ref12)</sup>
- **Erectile dysfunction.** New erectile dysfunction was 1.6% in Sa's series, 3.7% in Wang's, and 5% of potent men in Yin's. Koraitim attributed impotence mainly to the original injury (15%) and rarely to the urethroplasty (2.5%).<sup>[[11]](#ref11)[[12]](#ref12)[[13]](#ref13)[[15]](#ref15)</sup>
- **Recurrent stricture.** Wang and Yin reported failures only within the first 8 and 6 months respectively, mostly managed endoscopically or by repeat urethroplasty.<sup>[[11]](#ref11)[[12]](#ref12)</sup>
- **Penile deformity.** No chordee, penile shortening or urethral diverticula were reported in the Wang, Yin and Sunay series.<sup>[[11]](#ref11)[[12]](#ref12)[[19]](#ref19)</sup>

## Special contexts

- **Urethrorectal fistula.** Transperineal urethroplasty with gracilis interposition achieved success in 29 of 32 patients (91%) in Guo's series, and 36 of 40 (90%) in a later series of transperineal anastomosis with proximal transection and fistula repair, in which 75% had postoperative erectile dysfunction and 77.5% normal continence.<sup>[[17]](#ref17)[[18]](#ref18)</sup>
- **Children and adolescents.** In Sunay's series of 75 patients the primary success rate was 69.3% and overall success 93.3% after secondary procedures. Wang reported 20 of 22 boys (90.9%) successfully treated, most by a transperineal inferior pubic approach.<sup>[[19]](#ref19)[[20]](#ref20)</sup>

## Counseling and follow-up

Discuss recurrent stenosis, leakage or fistula, infection, wound problems, thromboembolism, sexual effects and substantial new or worsened SUI risk. A patient may require several procedures to achieve acceptable voiding and continence; distinguish single-operation patency from later functional success. Establish stable patency and acceptable bladder function before continence surgery.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup>

For an unsalvageable bladder or outlet, chronic drainage or diversion may better match the patient's goals. Patil's eight-patient salvage cystectomy and neobladder pull-through experience required a median of two AUS revisions to achieve social continence; it is an exceptional reconstruction, not a guarantee of complete dryness.<sup>[[10]](#ref10)</sup>
