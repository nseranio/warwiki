# Bladder Neck Closure

**Bladder neck closure** (BNC) is an irreversible salvage operation that permanently excludes the urethral outlet in selected patients with refractory incontinence or a devastated, nonreconstructible outlet. It requires a dependable alternative drainage route: a [continent catheterizable channel](/docs/surgical-techniques/04b-bladder-reconstruction/catheterizable-channels), an incontinent diversion or a permanent suprapubic catheter.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup> Here "closure" is distinct from bladder neck contracture, which shares the abbreviation BNC.

The AUA/SUFU NLUTD guideline states that clinicians may offer bladder neck closure with concomitant bladder drainage to select patients with NLUTD and refractory SUI (Expert Opinion). The guideline text cites continence rates of 75% to 100%, with fistulization and recurrent incontinence in up to 25%.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup> Selection considers the bladder, urethra, hand and cognitive function, caregiver support, prior surgery and the patient's goals. Every lesser operation need not be attempted first when anatomy or function makes it unsuitable. BNC is the endpoint of the [bladder neck reconstruction](/docs/surgical-techniques/04ab-bladder-neck-reconstruction/bladder-neck-reconstruction-principles) escalation ladder.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup>

## Indications and alternatives

- **Neurogenic bladder.** Chronic catheterization can erode the urethra, and prior bladder neck procedures may have failed. All 39 patients in Shpall's series and all 29 in Ginger's had neurogenic bladder.<sup>[[4]](#ref4)[[20]](#ref20)</sup>
- **Bladder exstrophy and other congenital outlet disease.** BNC follows multiple failed outlet reconstructions. In Kavanagh's 28 patients, 19 (68%) had undergone 20 unsuccessful bladder neck procedures beforehand.<sup>[[3]](#ref3)[[18]](#ref18)[[22]](#ref22)</sup>
- **Recalcitrant post-prostatectomy vesicourethral anastomotic stenosis with incontinence.** Spahn's series included 10 such men. [VUAS reconstruction](/docs/surgical-techniques/04ab-bladder-neck-reconstruction/vuas) is considered first when the tissue allows it.<sup>[[2]](#ref2)[[24]](#ref24)</sup>
- **Severely compromised female outlet.** Causes include urethral erosion from indwelling catheters, failed anti-incontinence surgery with severe intrinsic sphincter deficiency, and post-vulvectomy or radiation injury.<sup>[[2]](#ref2)[[5]](#ref5)[[16]](#ref16)</sup>
- **Complex urethrovesical fistula or radiation-damaged urethra in men.** Ullrich described BNC combined with prostatectomy and intestinal interposition in five men.<sup>[[23]](#ref23)</sup>

A bladder that is painful, infected, poorly compliant or otherwise unsalvageable may need a different diversion strategy rather than closure alone.<sup>[[2]](#ref2)[[3]](#ref3)[[4]](#ref4)[[1]](#ref1)</sup>

The AUA/SUFU female SUI guideline states that, in women with severe outlet dysfunction or recurrent or persistent SUI after surgery, clinicians may offer an obstructing pubovaginal sling or bladder neck closure with urinary drainage (Expert Opinion). Its text lists the options for a severely compromised outlet as an autologous pubovaginal sling, an obstructing autologous sling, closure with a catheterizable stoma, an AUS, and total urinary diversion. These are alternatives chosen by anatomy and goals. An AUS does not follow a formally closed bladder neck. The tension needed for an obstructing configuration should preclude a synthetic sling.<sup>[[5]](#ref5)</sup>

Chancellor's 14-woman series reported dryness at mean 24 months after an obstructing pubovaginal sling. Drainage and concomitant reconstruction varied: 2 had sling plus CIC alone, 5 enterocystoplasty, 5 a bladder chimney, and 2 suprapubic drainage. The authors suggested the sling may be superior to formal closure because it carries no fistula risk from bladder neck transection. The series was small and uncontrolled.<sup>[[6]](#ref6)</sup>

In selected men with an end-stage urethra after AUS failure, [permanent perineal urethral ligation](/docs/surgical-techniques/04a-urethral-reconstruction/permanent-urethral-ligation) with chronic suprapubic drainage is another salvage option. It is not a required step before closure.<sup>[[7]](#ref7)[[8]](#ref8)</sup>

## Reservoir and drainage planning

| Drainage plan | Requirements and consequences |
|---|---|
| Continent catheterizable channel | Safe storage pressures, adequate reservoir, reliable intermittent catheterization by patient or caregiver, and durable access |
| Permanent suprapubic catheter | Dependable continuous drainage, catheter-change support and a plan for blockage or dislodgement; the tube remains the bladder's outlet |
| Ileovesicostomy or another incontinent route | Individual assessment of drainage, obstruction, appliance needs and possible reoperation |
| Augmentation with reconstruction | Selected when storage capacity or compliance cannot safely support the intended regimen; it adds bowel, metabolic, stone and perforation risks |

Bladder storage is assessed before outlet resistance is increased, particularly when the planned regimen requires intermittent emptying. Continuous suprapubic drainage is a different physiological plan from a continent storage reservoir. Augmentation is not automatically required for every closure.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup> In Jayanthi's 28 patients (mean follow-up 29 months), 11 had closure and Mitrofanoff diversion without augmentation, and 4 patients who did not initially have augmentation later needed it, 2 for hydronephrosis and 2 for persistent incontinence.<sup>[[18]](#ref18)</sup>

Grimsby's 109-child study concerned mixed bladder neck reconstruction, closure and sling procedures without augmentation, not closure alone. Estimated ten-year rates were 30% for subsequent augmentation, 70% for additional continence procedures and more than 50% for upper-tract changes. Patients with a prior vesicostomy or reflux surgery were at higher risk. These data support long-term surveillance when augmentation is deferred.<sup>[[9]](#ref9)</sup>

## Operative principles

### Abdominal approach

1. Obtain exposure appropriate to prior surgery and any planned augmentation or channel (lower midline or Pfannenstiel incision). Identify and protect the ureteral orifices.
2. Divide the bladder neck completely, separating the bladder from the urethral outlet and preserving viable tissue for a tension-free closure.
3. Close the bladder side securely in two layers with absorbable suture and oversew the urethral stump. Separate suture lines when feasible. Verify watertightness and unobstructed alternative drainage.<sup>[[4]](#ref4)[[10]](#ref10)</sup>
4. Interpose vascularized tissue between the closed bladder neck and the urethral stump, particularly in scarred or previously operated tissue.<sup>[[10]](#ref10)[[11]](#ref11)</sup>

Hensle reported initial success in 12 of 13 patients, and eventual success in all 13, with bladder neck division, two-layer closure and omental interposition.<sup>[[10]](#ref10)</sup> Interposition options include:

| Interposition | Reported experience |
|---|---|
| Omental flap | Pedicled omentum placed between the suture lines; the usual choice in Hensle's series<sup>[[10]](#ref10)</sup> |
| Rectus abdominis muscle flap | Inferior epigastric-based; used when omentum is unavailable. Smith described 6 patients, all continent with no fistulas at mean 45.5 months<sup>[[11]](#ref11)</sup> |
| Human acellular dermis | In a retrospective exstrophy cohort of 147 patients, fistulas occurred in 5.8% with acellular dermis or native-tissue flaps and 20.8% without interposition (P = 0.039). The association is not proof of causation, and acellular dermis is not a vascularized flap<sup>[[12]](#ref12)</sup> |
| Intestinal patch | Ileocecal or sigmoid segment at the closure site, with concomitant prostatectomy for unsalvageable urethral disease. Ullrich described 5 men with mean follow-up of 6 months<sup>[[23]](#ref23)</sup> |

No comparative trial establishes one material or a flap as mandatory.

### Transvaginal approach

In selected women, vaginal access avoids laparotomy. It has been used particularly in women with a urethra destroyed by chronic catheterization.<sup>[[5]](#ref5)[[13]](#ref13)[[14]](#ref14)</sup>

1. Make an anterior vaginal wall incision and develop the plane between the vaginal wall and the bladder neck and urethra.
2. Dissect the bladder neck circumferentially and separate the urethra from the bladder.
3. Close the bladder neck in multiple layers with viable tissue.
4. Close the vaginal wall as a separate layer over the repair.<sup>[[13]](#ref13)[[14]](#ref14)[[15]](#ref15)</sup>

In Rovner's posterior urethral flap technique, the dorsally bivalved urethra is rotated cephalad onto the incised anterior bladder wall. The suture line then lies high in the retropubic space, away from the ureteral orifices and the vaginal incision. The technique was designed to lower the risk of ureteral injury and fistula. It was reported in 11 women with mean follow-up of 9.6 months, and one failed at 6 weeks.<sup>[[15]](#ref15)</sup>

In Willis's retrospective comparison of 64 women with suprapubic drainage, initial continence was 85.7% after transvaginal and 81.5% after retropubic closure (P = 0.74). The transvaginal group had shorter operative time (78 vs 138 minutes), shorter stay (1.5 vs 4.9 days) and fewer short-term complications (5.7% vs 31.0%). The design was nonrandomized, so selection differences may contribute.<sup>[[16]](#ref16)</sup> In Ginger's 29 debilitated patients, all 3 who had a non-retropubic closure (2 transvaginal, 1 perineal) developed a fistula (P = 0.01).<sup>[[20]](#ref20)</sup>

### Combined abdominal and vaginal approach

Levy reported that 2 of 5 transvaginal closures (40%) in 4 women were successful. The group then used a combined abdominal and transvaginal repair, which succeeded in 10 consecutive patients (follow-up 6 to 40 months, mean 15.6). The abdominal component allows tissue interposition and better visualization, and the vaginal component helps dissect the destroyed outlet.<sup>[[19]](#ref19)</sup>

## Outcomes

Closure success, continence and freedom from further surgery are distinct outcomes.

| Series | Patients and follow-up | Result |
|---|---|---|
| Spahn 2010 | 17 adults: 10 post-prostatectomy, 1 post-vulvectomy, 6 neurogenic; median follow-up 68 months | Primary closure in all 17; initial continence 82%. Three needed further continence reconstruction and four had stomal stenosis.<sup>[[2]](#ref2)</sup> |
| Kavanagh 2012 | 28 patients: 15 exstrophy, 13 neurogenic; augmentation plus Mitrofanoff; median follow-up 69 months | Initial closure 27/28; one fistula repaired. Eleven patients (39.3%) required further surgery, including three augment-rupture repairs.<sup>[[3]](#ref3)</sup> |
| Shpall 2004 | 39 adults with neurogenic dysfunction and varied reconstruction; mean follow-up 36.9 months | Overall complications 31%; six vesicourethral fistulas (15%), four requiring repair. High pressures, previous bladder neck surgery and noncompliance with catheter care were risk factors for prolonged leakage.<sup>[[4]](#ref4)</sup> |
| O'Connor 2005 | 35 patients; mean follow-up 79 months | 29 initial successful closures (83%); 33 (94%) after one additional closure procedure.<sup>[[17]](#ref17)</sup> |
| Jayanthi 1995 | 28 patients aged 1 to 20 years; closure with Mitrofanoff; mean follow-up 29 months | 27 (96%) totally continent; closure primarily successful in 24 (86%). Five had bladder calculi and five needed stomal revision.<sup>[[18]](#ref18)</sup> |
| Nguyen 2003 | 20 children after multiple failed outlet operations; mean follow-up 5.4 years | 40% dry at 3 months and 85% continent after additional surgery. Of 15 followed beyond 3 years, only 40% remained dry, and stomal leakage developed in 47% after a dry year. Stomal stenosis occurred in 30% and stones in 40%.<sup>[[22]](#ref22)</sup> |
| Landau 2009 | 12 children followed at least 10 years (mean 12.4) | Closure cured incontinence in all 6 patients with a modified posterior bladder neck flap; 2 of the earlier 6 needed revision. CIC compliance was good and upper tracts were preserved.<sup>[[21]](#ref21)</sup> |
| Ginger 2010 | 29 debilitated patients (24 women) with suprapubic tube; retropubic closure in 26 | Early complication rate 52%. Persistent leakage in 8; all 3 non-retropubic closures developed fistulas.<sup>[[20]](#ref20)</sup> |

## Complications

Percentages differ with population, reservoir, drainage route, follow-up and whether additional operations count as success. Series report:

- **Vesicourethral or vesicovaginal fistula.** This is the signature complication: 15% in Shpall's adults and 40% at 3 months in Nguyen's children. Reported risk factors are high-pressure bladder systems, prior bladder neck surgery, noncompliance with catheter care and absent interposition.<sup>[[4]](#ref4)[[20]](#ref20)[[22]](#ref22)[[12]](#ref12)</sup>
- **Stomal stenosis.** It occurred in 23% (Spahn) and 30% (Nguyen). In Spahn's series, 3 of 8 abdominal stomas and 1 of 9 umbilical stomas stenosed.<sup>[[2]](#ref2)[[22]](#ref22)</sup>
- **Bladder calculi.** They occurred in 40% of Nguyen's children and 5 of 28 in Jayanthi's series.<sup>[[22]](#ref22)[[18]](#ref18)</sup>
- **Augment rupture.** Kavanagh repaired 3 augment ruptures among 28 patients, and Ullrich reported 1 among 5.<sup>[[3]](#ref3)[[23]](#ref23)</sup>
- **Stomal incontinence.** It developed late in 47% of Nguyen's children after a dry year, often related to catheterization compliance.<sup>[[22]](#ref22)</sup>
- **Upper-tract change.** Kavanagh observed no progressive or de novo hydronephrosis, and Spahn no ureteral obstruction. Without augmentation, the ten-year cumulative incidence of upper-tract changes in Grimsby's cohort exceeded 50%.<sup>[[2]](#ref2)[[3]](#ref3)[[9]](#ref9)</sup>

## Postoperative drainage and lifelong follow-up

:::warning[Preserve the established outlet]
After formal closure, a urethral Foley does not traverse the closed bladder neck. Temporary healing catheters through a channel or suprapubic tract are managed according to the reconstruction and confirmation of healing. Do not remove a permanent suprapubic catheter because a 2–3-week cystogram is satisfactory: it remains the drainage route unless a working alternative has deliberately been established. Catheter blockage or dislodgement requires prompt assessment because the urethral escape route has been closed.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[15]](#ref15)</sup>
:::

Cystography at approximately 2–3 weeks is described in some operative protocols, including Rovner's. It assesses healing and does not remove the need for permanent drainage. Maintain dependable decompression, assess leakage and infection, and distinguish fistula from catheter bypass or stomal incontinence. Poor catheter care in the postoperative period was associated with leakage in Ginger's series.<sup>[[4]](#ref4)[[15]](#ref15)[[20]](#ref20)</sup>

Follow renal function, upper tracts and bladder storage according to the underlying condition and reconstruction. Teach catheterization or catheter care and provide an emergency drainage plan. Long-term CIC compliance was good beyond 10 years in Landau's small pediatric series.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[21]](#ref21)</sup> Augmented patients need the additional long-term surveillance described in [augmentation cystoplasty](/docs/surgical-techniques/04b-bladder-reconstruction/bladder-augmentation).<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup>

## Guideline positioning

- **AUA/SUFU NLUTD 2021 (Statement 47).** Clinicians may offer bladder neck closure and concomitant bladder drainage to select patients with NLUTD and refractory SUI (Expert Opinion).<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup>
- **AUA/SUFU female SUI 2023 (Statement 22).** Clinicians may offer an obstructing pubovaginal sling or bladder neck closure with urinary drainage to women with severe outlet dysfunction or surgical failure (Expert Opinion).<sup>[[5]](#ref5)</sup>
- **AUA urethral stricture guideline amendment 2023 (Statement 29).** Surgeons may perform robotic or open reconstruction for recalcitrant bladder neck stenosis or post-prostatectomy VUAS (Conditional Recommendation; Evidence Level: Grade C). The text cites robotic patency of 72.7% to 75%.<sup>[[24]](#ref24)</sup>

## Technical pearls

- Interpose vascularized tissue (omentum or rectus muscle) when feasible. In the exstrophy cohort, fistulas were 5.8% with a flap or acellular dermis and 20.8% without.<sup>[[10]](#ref10)[[11]](#ref11)[[12]](#ref12)</sup>
- Use autologous tissue when the goal is bladder neck occlusion with a sling. The tension required should preclude synthetic mesh.<sup>[[5]](#ref5)</sup>
- Plan the drainage route before transection. The catheterizable channel, ileovesicostomy or suprapubic tube must already be designed.<sup>[[3]](#ref3)[[4]](#ref4)</sup>
- Consider concurrent augmentation when reservoir capacity or compliance is borderline. Without augmentation, Grimsby's cohort estimated a 30% ten-year rate of later augmentation.<sup>[[9]](#ref9)</sup>
- Maintain bladder drainage for 2–3 weeks with confirmatory cystography before catheter removal.<sup>[[4]](#ref4)[[15]](#ref15)</sup>
