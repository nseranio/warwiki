# Bladder Neck Closure

Bladder neck closure (BNC) is an **irreversible salvage procedure** that permanently closes the bladder outlet at the level of the bladder neck and commits the patient to an alternative form of urinary drainage — typically a [continent catheterizable channel](/docs/surgical-techniques/04b-bladder-reconstruction/catheterizable-channels) (Mitrofanoff appendicovesicostomy or Yang-Monti ileal channel), an [ileovesicostomy](/docs/surgical-techniques/04b-bladder-reconstruction/bladder-augmentation), or a permanent suprapubic catheter.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[2]](#ref2)[[3]](#ref3)</sup> It is a last-resort intervention for the **devastated or non-reconstructible bladder outlet** — the endpoint of the [Principles of Bladder Neck Reconstruction](/docs/surgical-techniques/04ab-bladder-neck-reconstruction/bladder-neck-reconstruction-principles) escalation ladder, after every lesser outlet operation has failed or is anatomically impossible.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[2]](#ref2)</sup>

> BNC abandons the urethral outlet entirely. It is not a failure of reconstruction — it is the most honest reconstructive endpoint when the native outlet can no longer be salvaged.

---

## Indications

BNC is performed across sexes for overlapping but distinct clinical scenarios:

- **Neurogenic bladder** (spinal cord injury, myelomeningocele, sacral agenesis) — the most common indication across all patients, particularly when the urethra has been destroyed by chronic indwelling catheterization or when prior bladder-neck procedures have failed.<sup>[[2]](#ref2)[[3]](#ref3)[[4]](#ref4)</sup>
- **Bladder exstrophy** — a major indication in pediatric reconstructive practice, often after multiple failed bladder-neck reconstructions in the exstrophy-epispadias complex.<sup>[[2]](#ref2)[[5]](#ref5)[[6]](#ref6)</sup>
- **Recalcitrant post-prostatectomy vesicourethral anastomotic stenosis (VUAS) with severe incontinence** (men) — when repeated endoscopic management and open / robotic [VUAS reconstruction](/docs/surgical-techniques/04ab-bladder-neck-reconstruction/vuas) have failed.<sup>[[7]](#ref7)[[1]](#ref1)[[8]](#ref8)</sup>
- **Severely compromised female bladder outlet** — urethral erosion from chronic indwelling catheterization, failed anti-incontinence operations with severe intrinsic sphincter deficiency (ISD), and post-vulvectomy or post-radiation urethral destruction. The **AUA/SUFU 2023 SUI Guideline** explicitly recommends considering BNC with a catheterizable stoma in women with a severely compromised outlet.<sup>[[9]](#ref9)[[10]](#ref10)</sup>
- **Complex urethrovesical fistula and radiation-damaged urethra** (men) — when urethral pathology is unsalvageable, BNC combined with prostatectomy and intestinal interposition has been described.<sup>[[11]](#ref11)</sup>

---

## The Continuum: Obstructing PVS to Formal BNC

In women, BNC sits at the irreversible end of a graded escalation that begins with the [autologous pubovaginal sling (PVS)](/docs/surgical-techniques/04f-incontinence-procedures/procedures/autologous-pubovaginal-sling). The **AUA/SUFU 2023 Guideline** explicitly positions these as a single continuum for the severely compromised outlet:<sup>[[10]](#ref10)</sup>

1. **Traditional autologous PVS** at the bladder neck — standard tension for SUI / ISD.<sup>[[12]](#ref12)[[13]](#ref13)</sup>
2. **Obstructing autologous PVS** ("functional urethral closure") — intentional urethral occlusion with planned lifelong CIC (Chancellor 1994: 14/14 women dry at 24 mo with destroyed urethra).<sup>[[14]](#ref14)</sup>
3. **Formal BNC with continent catheterizable channel.**<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[2]](#ref2)[[3]](#ref3)</sup>
4. **Artificial urinary sphincter (AUS)** — feasible at the bladder neck in selected women but with revision ~15% / explantation ~13% / vaginal erosion ~9%.
5. **Total urinary diversion** ([ileal conduit](/docs/surgical-techniques/04c-urinary-diversion/ileal-conduit) or continent diversion).

The guideline specifically warns that **the degree of bladder-neck occlusion required for the devastated outlet "could require a degree of tension that should preclude the use of synthetic slings"** — only autologous tissue (rectus fascia or fascia lata) should be used in the obstructing-PVS configuration.<sup>[[10]](#ref10)</sup> Chancellor's argument is that obstructing PVS may be **superior to formal BNC** for selected patients because it preserves some option of reversibility and avoids the fistula risk inherent to bladder-neck transection — at the cost of being feasible only when some residual urethral tissue exists for sling support.<sup>[[14]](#ref14)</sup>

| Feature | Obstructing PVS | Formal BNC |
|---|---|---|
| Reversibility | Potentially reversible (sling incision) | Irreversible |
| Fistula risk | Low (no bladder-neck transection) | 8–20% (vesicourethral / vesicovaginal) |
| Continence rate | ~100% in small series | 82–96% |
| Requires CIC / SPT | Yes | Yes |
| Suitable for completely destroyed urethra | Limited — needs some residual tissue | Yes — definitive option |
| Concomitant augmentation | May be needed | Often needed |
| AUA/SUFU 2023 support | Expert Opinion | Expert Opinion |

## Surgical Approaches and Techniques

### Retropubic (transabdominal) BNC

The most commonly described approach, particularly in men and in complex pediatric cases.<sup>[[3]](#ref3)[[15]](#ref15)</sup>

1. **Exposure** — lower midline or Pfannenstiel incision; bladder mobilized and opened if augmentation or stoma creation is concomitant.<sup>[[3]](#ref3)</sup>
2. **Bladder-neck division** — the bladder neck is completely transected, separating the bladder from the urethra. Ureteral orifices must be identified and protected.<sup>[[15]](#ref15)</sup>
3. **Layered closure** — the bladder side is closed in **at least two layers** (inner mucosal-muscular + outer seromuscular) using absorbable sutures. The urethral stump is similarly oversewn.<sup>[[3]](#ref3)[[15]](#ref15)</sup>
4. **Tissue interposition** — a vascularized flap is interposed between the closed bladder neck and the urethral stump to prevent fistula formation. Hensle 1995 reported initial success in 12 / 13 cases (and ultimately 13 / 13) using a strict protocol of bladder-neck division + 2-layer closure + omental interposition.<sup>[[15]](#ref15)</sup>

**Tissue interposition options:**

| Interposition | Evidence / outcome |
|---|---|
| **Omental flap** (most common) | Pedicled omental flap brought into the pelvis and secured between suture lines.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[15]](#ref15)</sup> |
| **Rectus abdominis muscle flap** | Inferior-epigastric-based; used when omentum is unavailable. Smith 2010 — 6 patients, 100% continence, no fistulas at 45.5 mo.<sup>[[16]](#ref16)</sup> |
| **Human acellular dermis (HAD)** | Benz 2018 exstrophy series of 147 patients — fistula rate **20.8% → 5.8%** (P = 0.039) with HAD or native-tissue interposition vs no interposition.<sup>[[17]](#ref17)</sup> |
| **Intestinal patch interposition** | Ileocecal or sigmoid segment used at the BNC site, particularly when prostatectomy is concomitant for unsalvageable urethral disease.<sup>[[11]](#ref11)</sup> |

### Transvaginal BNC (women)

Avoids laparotomy and is particularly suited to women with a destroyed urethra from chronic catheterization.<sup>[[9]](#ref9)[[18]](#ref18)[[19]](#ref19)</sup>

1. **Incision** — anterior vaginal-wall incision; the plane between vaginal wall and bladder neck / urethra is developed.<sup>[[18]](#ref18)[[19]](#ref19)</sup>
2. **Dissection** — circumferential dissection of the bladder neck; urethra is separated from the bladder.<sup>[[20]](#ref20)</sup>
3. **Closure** — multi-layered closure of the bladder neck. In Rovner's **posterior urethral flap technique**, the dorsally bivalved urethra is rotated cephalad onto the incised anterior bladder wall, placing the suture line **high in the retropubic space — away from the ureteral orifices and the vaginal incision** — to minimize fistula risk.<sup>[[20]](#ref20)</sup>
4. **Vaginal-wall closure** — closed as a separate layer over the repair.<sup>[[18]](#ref18)</sup>

In Willis 2015 (n = 64 women, transvaginal vs retropubic with concomitant SPT), **continence rates were equivalent (85.7% vs 81.5%)** but the transvaginal approach offered significantly **shorter operative time (78 vs 138 min)**, **shorter hospital stay (1.5 vs 4.9 d)**, and **fewer short-term complications (5.7% vs 31.0%)**.<sup>[[9]](#ref9)</sup> Ginger 2010 noted that non-retropubic approaches (transvaginal, perineal) have been associated with **higher fistula rates** in some debilitated-patient series (P = 0.01).<sup>[[21]](#ref21)</sup>

### Combined abdominal-vaginal approach

Levy 1994 described a combined approach after experiencing a 40% failure rate with purely transvaginal BNC. The combined technique was successful in **10 / 10 consecutive patients** — the abdominal component allows tissue interposition and improved visualization, while the vaginal component facilitates dissection of the destroyed outlet.<sup>[[22]](#ref22)</sup>

## Concomitant Procedures

BNC is almost never performed in isolation. The reservoir, drainage route, and continence mechanism must all be addressed simultaneously:

- **Augmentation cystoplasty** ([enterocystoplasty](/docs/surgical-techniques/04b-bladder-reconstruction/bladder-augmentation)) — when bladder capacity or compliance is reduced (most neurogenic and exstrophy patients).<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[2]](#ref2)[[23]](#ref23)</sup>
- **Continent catheterizable channel** — [Mitrofanoff appendicovesicostomy](/docs/surgical-techniques/04b-bladder-reconstruction/catheterizable-channels) or Yang-Monti ileal channel for continent abdominal-stoma drainage.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[2]](#ref2)[[5]](#ref5)</sup>
- **Ileovesicostomy** — incontinent low-pressure cutaneous diversion for patients who cannot perform CIC.<sup>[[3]](#ref3)</sup>
- **Suprapubic catheter (SPT)** placement — a simpler option for debilitated patients with low functional status or limited life expectancy.<sup>[[21]](#ref21)</sup>

## Complications

- **Vesicourethral / vesicovaginal fistula** — the signature complication of BNC, occurring in **15–40%** of cases depending on the series. Risk factors: high-pressure bladder systems, prior bladder-neck surgery, premature catheter removal, and absence of tissue interposition.<sup>[[3]](#ref3)[[21]](#ref21)[[26]](#ref26)</sup>
- **Stomal stenosis** — **23–30%** of patients; umbilical stomas may have lower stenosis rates than abdominal-wall stomas.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[26]](#ref26)</sup>
- **Bladder calculi** — **30–40%** of pediatric series, particularly in augmented bladders.<sup>[[5]](#ref5)[[26]](#ref26)</sup>
- **Augment rupture** — serious late complication in approximately 10% of augmented patients.<sup>[[2]](#ref2)</sup>
- **Upper-tract changes** — generally well preserved with appropriate diversion, but when bladder-neck procedures are performed **without augmentation**, the 10-year cumulative incidence of upper-tract changes exceeds 50%, and ~30% will eventually need augmentation.<sup>[[23]](#ref23)</sup>
- **Stomal incontinence** — late-onset leakage via the stoma in up to 47% of pediatric patients after initially being dry, often related to CIC compliance.<sup>[[26]](#ref26)</sup>

## Special Considerations

### Augmentation cystoplasty — with or without?

Performing BNC without concurrent augmentation remains controversial. Grimsby 2016 — a large pediatric series — found that at 10 years, the cumulative incidence of **needing augmentation was 30%**, additional continence procedures **70%**, and upper-tract changes **&gt; 50%**.<sup>[[23]](#ref23)</sup> Patients with prior vesicostomy or VUR surgery were at highest risk. This supports careful patient selection and intensive follow-up if augmentation is deferred — the burden of secondary procedures is high.

### Patient selection and counseling

BNC is irreversible and commits the patient to lifelong alternative bladder drainage. The **AUA/SUFU NLUTD Guideline** emphasizes that hand and cognitive function necessary for CIC must be assessed preoperatively, either in the patient or in a reliable caregiver.<sup>[[4]](#ref4)</sup> Long-term CIC compliance has been shown to be good even at &gt; 10 years of follow-up.<sup>[[24]](#ref24)</sup>

### Postoperative catheter management

Premature catheter removal is a recognized risk factor for fistula. Most protocols maintain bladder drainage (Foley + SPT or SPT alone) for **2–3 weeks** with a cystogram before catheter removal.<sup>[[3]](#ref3)[[20]](#ref20)</sup>

### Guideline positioning

- **AUA Urethral Stricture Guideline (2023):** robotic or open reconstruction may be performed for recalcitrant VUAS / bladder-neck contracture (robotic patency 72–75%); BNC with diversion is reserved for cases refractory to reconstruction.<sup>[[7]](#ref7)</sup>
- **AUA/SUFU Female SUI Guideline (2023):** consider BNC with a catheterizable stoma, AUS, or total urinary diversion for women with a severely compromised outlet.<sup>[[10]](#ref10)</sup>
- **AUA/SUFU Adult NLUTD Guideline (2021):** continent catheterizable channels with or without augmentation are supported for patients requiring BNC due to a devastated urethra.<sup>[[4]](#ref4)</sup>

## Key Technical Pearls

- **Tissue interposition is essential** — the fistula rate falls from ~21% without interposition to ~6% with omental, rectus, or HAD interposition.<sup>[[15]](#ref15)[[16]](#ref16)[[17]](#ref17)</sup>
- **Synthetic mesh must not be used** when the goal is bladder-neck occlusion — only autologous tissue (rectus fascia, fascia lata) tolerates the tension required.<sup>[[10]](#ref10)</sup>
- **Plan the drainage route before transection** — the catheterizable channel, ileovesicostomy, or SPT must already be designed; BNC without a planned outflow is not a complete operation.<sup>[[2]](#ref2)[[3]](#ref3)</sup>
- **Consider concurrent augmentation** when reservoir capacity or compliance is borderline — deferred augmentation carries a 30% subsequent-augmentation rate at 10 years.<sup>[[23]](#ref23)</sup>
- **Maintain bladder drainage for 2–3 weeks** with confirmatory cystography before catheter removal to minimize fistula risk.<sup>[[3]](#ref3)[[20]](#ref20)</sup>
