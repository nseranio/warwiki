# Salvage Prostatectomy — Reconstructive Indications

**Salvage prostatectomy** in this setting is removal of an unsalvageable prostate as part of reconstruction for severe treatment-related necrosis, fistula or a devastated outlet. The prostate must still be present. Post-prostatectomy VUAS requires outlet reconstruction or diversion, not another prostatectomy. Salvage surgery for recurrent cancer is a separate oncologic decision.

## Identify the problem before choosing resection

Radiation and ablation can impair vascularity and healing. The EAU urethral stricture guideline (2026) notes that radiotherapy and high-energy modalities (cryoablation, HIFU) can cause prostatic necrosis, sloughing and obstruction. Cases refractory to transurethral resection with good bladder capacity may be salvaged by prostatectomy, taking into account the morbidity (rectal injury, VUAS, incontinence). The guideline weakly recommends offering salvage prostatectomy to motivated, fit patients with adequate bladder function and a prostatic stricture after irradiation or high-energy treatment. It cites patency in 67% (level of evidence 4) with substantial morbidity.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup>

Histologic necrosis is an expected finding after HIFU: 24 of 33 post-hemiablation biopsies (73%) showed necrosis in one series.<sup>[[13]](#ref13)</sup> It does not by itself indicate a devastated outlet or the need for radical removal. Assess symptoms, infection, tissue viability, fistula, bladder capacity and compliance, sphincter function, remaining prostate anatomy and cancer status as relevant.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[2]](#ref2)</sup>

Prostatic abscess is usually managed with antimicrobial treatment and drainage when indicated, not routine prostatectomy. The EAU infections guideline describes both drainage and conservative strategies as feasible. In one study, conservative treatment succeeded for cavities under 1 cm, and larger abscesses were better treated by single aspiration or continuous drainage.<sup>[[3]](#ref3)</sup> Prostatectomy is an exceptional multidisciplinary decision when limited source control or reconstruction is unsuitable. Abscess has been reported as a complication of salvage cryoablation after radiotherapy (single case report).<sup>[[14]](#ref14)</sup>

Complex fistula with necrotic tissue may require removal of the diseased prostate, urinary or fecal diversion and vascularized interposition. An end-stage bladder changes the plan toward cystectomy and diversion rather than reconnection to the urethra.<sup>[[2]](#ref2)[[4]](#ref4)[[1]](#ref1)</sup> In Faris's 30 patients who underwent urinary diversion after prostate radiation, the indications were fistula (37%), devastated outlet (27%), end-stage bladder (20%) and end-stage bladder with devastated outlet (17%), after an average of 4.4 procedures to preserve native voiding.<sup>[[15]](#ref15)</sup>

## Operative risks and planning

Prior treatment can obscure planes near the rectum and sphincter and leave poor tissue for anastomosis. Counsel about rectal injury, urinary leakage or fistula, recurrent stenosis, infection, sexual dysfunction and incontinence. Most reported figures come from oncologic salvage cohorts, whose anatomy and selection may differ from patients with severe benign necrosis, abscess or fistula.<sup>[[5]](#ref5)[[6]](#ref6)</sup>

| Series | Population | Reported findings |
|---|---|---|
| Perera 2022<sup>[[5]](#ref5)</sup> | 293 salvage prostatectomies after radiotherapy failure (232 open, 61 minimally invasive) | Bladder neck contracture in 31% within one year. Continence 16% open and 18% minimally invasive; grade 3 complications about 10% within 30 days |
| Gontero 2019<sup>[[16]](#ref16)</sup> | 395 salvage prostatectomies (186 open, 209 robotic), 18 centers | Rectal injury 1.6%, fistula 2.0%. Anastomotic stricture 16.6% open vs 7.7% robotic (P &lt; 0.01). Severe incontinence (3 or more pads a day) in 24.6% |
| Ogaya-Pinies 2019<sup>[[17]](#ref17)</sup> | 96 salvage robotic prostatectomies | Anastomotic leak 14.6%; no rectal injuries; 57.3% pad-free at 12 months |
| Covas Moschovas 2025<sup>[[6]](#ref6)</sup> | 397 salvage robotic prostatectomies from nine centers | Clavien grade 3 or higher under 2%. Three-year cumulative continence 67% after primary radiation, 92% after focal ablation and 71% after whole-gland ablation |
| Chade 2012<sup>[[18]](#ref18)</sup> | Systematic review of 40 articles | Anastomotic stricture 7–41% and rectal injury 0–28%; continence 21–90% |

Continence definitions differ across these series, and none used a common standard. The 2025 multicenter authors concluded that functional outcomes are inferior to primary surgery and that patients should be counseled accordingly.<sup>[[6]](#ref6)</sup>

Plan the reservoir, outlet or drainage route and any tissue transfer before starting irreversible dissection. The alternatives below are chosen according to function and anatomy, not by ranking continence percentages from unrelated series.

## Reconstructive options

### Direct vesicourethral anastomosis

Use it when viable bladder and urethral tissue can meet without tension and the retained bladder is suitable. A new anastomosis can leak or stenose, and sphincter function remains a separate problem. The bladder neck contracture rate of 31% in Perera's cohort is one estimate from an oncologic series, not a fixed expectation for every reconstructive salvage operation.<sup>[[5]](#ref5)</sup>

In a propensity-matched study, 15 salvage robotic prostatectomies with urinary bladder extracellular matrix reinforcement were compared with 45 salvage controls sutured without reinforcement and 45 primary prostatectomies. Clinically significant leak or bladder neck disruption occurred in 1 of 15 (6.7%) with reinforcement versus 16 of 45 (35.5%) without (P = 0.045). Median catheter time was 11.2 versus 17.4 days, and no primary-prostatectomy control had a leak. The design was nonrandomized and the reinforced group was small.<sup>[[7]](#ref7)</sup>

### Bladder-neck closure with catheterizable reconstruction

When urethral reconstruction is unsuitable but the bladder can be retained, [closure](/docs/surgical-techniques/04ab-bladder-neck-reconstruction/outlet-continence/bladder-neck-closure) with a [continent catheterizable channel](/docs/surgical-techniques/04b-bladder-reconstruction/catheterizable-channels), with augmentation when necessary, can bypass the damaged outlet. This approach was developed at MD Anderson (Pisters and colleagues) for patients with preexisting voiding symptoms. Reliable catheterization and safe reservoir pressures are needed. Closure removes urethral voiding and does not eliminate stomal leakage, stenosis, fistula or the need for further surgery.<sup>[[8]](#ref8)[[9]](#ref9)</sup>

- **Pisters 2000.** In 13 patients with closure and an appendicovesicostomy (9) or ileovesicostomy (4), 10 of 12 were dry day and night and 2 used pads. Four had serious complications requiring reoperation, including one death from sepsis after a small-bowel anastomotic leak.<sup>[[8]](#ref8)</sup>
- **De 2007.** In 11 patients with closure, ileal augmentation and a catheterizable stoma (mean follow-up 32 months), 8 of 11 (73%) were dry by physician assessment. Three (27%) needed stomal revision, and 7 of 9 respondents would repeat the procedure.<sup>[[9]](#ref9)</sup>
- **Zafirakis 2010.** In 12 such patients followed for a mean of 61 months, 10 were dry (83.3%) and four (33%) required revision. 86% of respondents would choose the surgery again. The authors compared this experience with historical patients and did not directly compare matched vesicourethral anastomosis patients. Their term "Monti ileovesicostomy" refers to a catheterizable ileal channel, distinct from an incontinent ileovesicostomy or bladder chimney.<sup>[[10]](#ref10)</sup>

### Cystectomy with urinary diversion

When the bladder is also unsalvageable, options are [ileal conduit](/docs/surgical-techniques/04c-urinary-diversion/ileal-conduit), selected continent cutaneous reconstruction and, exceptionally, an orthotopic reservoir. Orthotopic reconstruction requires a usable urethral target, an appropriate continence plan and the ability to manage the reservoir. It is not an automatic choice for an end-stage bladder. Diversion carries substantial operative and long-term burdens.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[11]](#ref11)</sup>

Bochner reported orthotopic neobladders in 18 patients after failed radical radiotherapy for bladder or prostate cancer. Good day and night continence was reported by 67% and 56% of irradiated patients, and poor continence was treated with an artificial urinary sphincter.<sup>[[19]](#ref19)</sup> Gheiler's 8 salvage cystoprostatectomies included 3 orthotopic neobladders, all continent by day, and 5 ileal conduits.<sup>[[20]](#ref20)</sup> In patients with prior radiation, failed reconstruction, or a densely fibrotic or necrotic posterior urethra, a review found that diversion with or without cystectomy is usually required.<sup>[[21]](#ref21)</sup>

### Total lower-tract reconstruction

Patil's retrospective eight-patient series combined salvage cystectomy, ileal neobladder and urethral pull-through, with staged AUS placement. A median of two AUS revisions (range 0–4) was needed to establish social continence. At median 58 months, all maintained storage, urethral patency and social continence, and none needed cutaneous diversion. The EAU guideline adds that 4 of 8 patients had cuff erosion requiring removal and reimplantation, and that the technique needs further validation before it can be recommended.<sup>[[11]](#ref11)[[1]](#ref1)</sup>

### Salvage prostatectomy within fistula repair

When prostatic necrosis or abscess causes a urorectal fistula, prostatectomy removes the necrotic tissue and allows interposition of vascularized tissue (tunica vaginalis, peritoneal or omental flap, or rectus muscle) between the urinary and rectal tracts. Gözen reported laparoscopic salvage prostatectomy in 2 of 4 men with complex urorectal fistula, with a tunica vaginalis flap in one and peritoneal interposition flaps in others.<sup>[[2]](#ref2)</sup> Ullrich described bladder neck closure with prostatectomy and ileocecal or sigmoid interposition in five men with fistula, radiation injury or neurogenic dysfunction, with no fistula or recanalization at mean 6 months.<sup>[[4]](#ref4)</sup>

## Tissue interposition

Omental or rectus muscle tissue can fill dead space and separate repaired structures in selected compromised fields. The MD Anderson group describes a well-vascularized rectus flap as a way to reduce perioperative morbidity. The need, tissue source and harvest approach depend on anatomy and previous surgery. An acellular scaffold does not replace viable tissue or source control, and the favorable scaffold data are from one small study.<sup>[[12]](#ref12)[[7]](#ref7)[[4]](#ref4)</sup>

## Counseling and follow-up

Discuss the likely number of stages, catheterization or appliance needs, continence goals and the possibility that diversion is preferable to further attempts at orthotopic reconstruction. Follow renal function, drainage, infection and reconstruction-specific complications. Continence from large oncologic salvage cohorts does not predict the result in a different devastated-outlet population.<sup>[[6]](#ref6)[[1]](#ref1)</sup>
