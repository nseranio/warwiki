# OAB & Urgency Urinary Incontinence

Overactive bladder (OAB) and urgency urinary incontinence (UUI) are managed across a menu of treatment categories rather than a strict stepwise ladder. The [AUA/SUFU 2024 guideline](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1002/nau.25532) permits minimally invasive treatment without mandatory prior behavioral or drug trials through shared decision-making; this is an Expert Opinion option, not a requirement to start with a procedure. Reconstructive urologists are typically involved at the procedural and surgical end of this pathway.

---

## Decision Framework

The guideline organizes treatment into behavioral, non-invasive, pharmacologic, minimally invasive, invasive and indwelling-catheter categories. Behavioral therapy is offered to all; β3-agonists or antimuscarinics are oral options, with side-effect and cognitive-risk counseling. SNM, tibial nerve stimulation and intradetrusor botulinum toxin should be offered after inadequate response or intolerance to behavioral or drug therapy (Moderate Recommendation, Grade A); earlier procedural access without those trials is separately permitted through shared decision-making (Expert Opinion). Augmentation or diversion is reserved for severely impacted patients after other options fail. **Minimum initial office evaluation** is symptom-focused history, physical examination and urinalysis; PVR, questionnaires and a voiding diary are selective aids, not universal requirements. Urodynamics, cystoscopy and imaging are not routine initial tests. See [guideline statements 1–6 and 23–31](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1002/nau.25532).

The [EAU 2026 non-neurogenic female LUTS guideline](https://uroweb.org/guidelines/non-neurogenic-female-luts) is more stepwise. It recommends bladder training first-line (Strong); anticholinergics (extended-release preferred) or β3 agonists for women who fail conservative management (Strong), with early review (Strong); onabotulinumtoxinA 100 U for OAB/UUI refractory to conservative or drug treatment (Strong); SNM for UUI refractory to anticholinergic therapy (Strong); and PTNS as an option (Strong). It advises caution with long-term anticholinergics in older women, particularly with cognitive risk, and assessing anticholinergic burden (both Strong); it reports oxybutynin as likely to worsen cognition in older women (level 2) and darifenacin, fesoterodine, solifenacin and trospium as not shown to do so in short-term studies (level 1b). Vaginal laser is not offered for OAB outside research (Strong); augmentation cystoplasty is offered only after all other options fail (Weak), with lifelong follow-up.

### Treatment Selection by Clinical Scenario

| Clinical Scenario | Possible choice | Alternative(s) | Key Considerations |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Mild OAB, prefers non-pharmacologic** | [Behavioral therapy](/docs/surgical-techniques/04f-incontinence-procedures/procedures/behavioral-therapy-incontinence) + [PFMT](/docs/surgical-techniques/04f-incontinence-procedures/procedures/pelvic-floor-pt) ± TTNS | Bladder training alone | Adherence-dependent; safest profile |
| **OAB / UUI, initial pharmacotherapy** | [β-3 agonist (vibegron or mirabegron)](/docs/foundations/pharmacology/storage-oab/beta3-agonists) | [Antimuscarinic](/docs/foundations/pharmacology/storage-oab/anticholinergics) | A β3-agonist trial is typically preferred before an antimuscarinic when cognitive risk matters; choose by patient factors. **US-labeled vibegron dose is 75 mg once daily**, not 100 mg. See the [current US prescribing information](https://www.gemtesa.com/files/gemtesa-prescribing-information.pdf). |
| **Inadequate response to β-3 monotherapy** | Add antimuscarinic (mirabegron 50 + solifenacin 5 — SYNERGY II, BESIDE) | Switch to antimuscarinic | The guideline offers combination therapy from a different class (Conditional Recommendation, Grade B). SYNERGY II showed combination superior to either drug alone (adverse events 49% vs 41-44%); BESIDE showed superiority over solifenacin 5 mg but not 10 mg. Both trials tested mirabegron added to solifenacin; the panel considered the principle generalizable within each class. Monitor anticholinergic burden |
| **Older adult or cognitive concerns** | [β-3 agonist](/docs/foundations/pharmacology/storage-oab/beta3-agonists) often preferred | If an antimuscarinic is chosen, consider lower-CNS-penetration agents such as **trospium**, while counseling about anticholinergic exposure and monitoring cognition | Trospium **poorly** crosses the blood-brain barrier but is not proven dementia-risk-free; observational drug-specific rankings conflict. The Malcher 2022 **class-exposure** aOR was 1.48 at &gt;365 defined daily doses, not a tolterodine-specific estimate. |
| **Patient prefers to avoid daily medication** | [Intradetrusor onabotulinumtoxinA 100 U](/docs/surgical-techniques/04f-incontinence-procedures/procedures/intradetrusor-botox) **or** [SNM](/docs/surgical-techniques/04f-incontinence-procedures/procedures/sacral-neuromodulation) **or** [iTNM](/docs/surgical-techniques/04f-incontinence-procedures/procedures/implantable-tibial-nerve-stimulation) | [PTNS](/docs/surgical-techniques/04f-incontinence-procedures/procedures/percutaneous-tibial-nerve-stimulation) | May be offered without mandatory earlier therapy after counseling; this is the guideline's **Expert Opinion** early-access option, not a blanket first-line ranking. PTNS is the only procedure studied in treatment-naive patients (single-arm). Product labeling and coverage may differ. |
| **Inadequate response or intolerance** | [BTX 100 U](/docs/surgical-techniques/04f-incontinence-procedures/procedures/intradetrusor-botox) **or** [SNM](/docs/surgical-techniques/04f-incontinence-procedures/procedures/sacral-neuromodulation) **or** [tibial nerve stimulation](/docs/surgical-techniques/04f-incontinence-procedures/procedures/percutaneous-tibial-nerve-stimulation), including an implantable option in selected patients | [iTNM](/docs/surgical-techniques/04f-incontinence-procedures/procedures/implantable-tibial-nerve-stimulation) or office PTNS according to device eligibility and treatment burden | AUA's **Grade A** recommendation is to offer the treatment families after inadequate response/intolerance; it does not establish a universal efficacy ranking or equate every implantable device with the trials. |
| **Refractory to BTX and neuromodulation** | [Augmentation cystoplasty](/docs/surgical-techniques/04b-bladder-reconstruction/bladder-augmentation) | — | Last surgical resort; **may require CIC** and needs long-term surveillance. |
| **Intractable incontinence, all options exhausted** | [Urinary diversion](/docs/surgical-techniques/04c-urinary-diversion) or indwelling catheter | — | High morbidity; consider patient goals and comorbidities |
| **Male with OAB + BPH** | [Vibegron](/docs/foundations/pharmacology/storage-oab/beta3-agonists) (FDA-approved in this population) ± alpha-blocker | [Mirabegron](/docs/foundations/pharmacology/storage-oab/beta3-agonists) | Vibegron specifically studied in men on BPH pharmacotherapy |

### Minimally-Invasive Sub-Comparison

When pharmacotherapy fails or is declined, the choice among BTX, SNM, iTNM, and PTNS involves distinct trade-offs. The figures below come from different studies and populations and are not head-to-head rankings:

| Feature | [Intradetrusor BTX 100 U](/docs/surgical-techniques/04f-incontinence-procedures/procedures/intradetrusor-botox) | [Sacral Neuromodulation (SNM)](/docs/surgical-techniques/04f-incontinence-procedures/procedures/sacral-neuromodulation) | [Implantable TNS (iTNM)](/docs/surgical-techniques/04f-incontinence-procedures/procedures/implantable-tibial-nerve-stimulation) | [PTNS](/docs/surgical-techniques/04f-incontinence-procedures/procedures/percutaneous-tibial-nerve-stimulation) |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Reported response** | Improves UUI in trials of 100 U; not ranked against every device here | ≥50% UUI-episode response 71.8% (weighted single-arm average, [Amundsen 2025](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/39476308/)); 5-yr therapeutic success 67% (modified completers) to 82% (completers) in [Siegel 2018](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/28709886/) | ≥50% UUI-episode response 71.3% (weighted single-arm average, [Amundsen 2025](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/39476308/); OAB-symptom response 79.4% vs 73.9% for SNM); 79% at 24 months in the pivotal OASIS study of Revi ([Heesakkers 2025](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/39585851/)) | 68% pooled success in [Wang 2020](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/32681345/) meta-analysis |
| **Duration** | Repeat injections typically every 3–12 mo (AUA/SUFU); often ~6–9 mo | Continuous (5–15 yr battery) | Continuous (rechargeable) | Ongoing maintenance sessions |
| **Reversibility** | Wears off | Device removal | Device removal | Fully reversible |
| **Trial phase needed** | No | **Yes** ([PNE](/docs/surgical-techniques/04f-incontinence-procedures/procedures/percutaneous-nerve-evaluation) / staged implant) | **No** | No |
| **Key risks** | UTI and retention; **6.5% initiated CIC** versus 0.4% placebo in pooled US-label OAB trials; catheterization planning is required | Implant-site pain, lead issues and revision/removal risk; rates vary by device and cohort | Device-specific implant and follow-up risks; not proven identical to SNM | Usually low procedural burden, but repeated visits |
| **Office burden** | Repeat injections, typically every 3–12 mo | Low after implant | Low (home-based) | **High** — weekly + maintenance |
| **MRI compatibility** | N/A | Verify model-specific MRI conditions | Verify model-specific MRI conditions | N/A |

The [BOTOX US label](https://www.rxabbvie.com/pdf/botox_pi.pdf) reports CIC initiation after 100-U OAB treatment in 36/552 (6.5%) versus 2/542 (0.4%) with placebo (Table 10). This is not a universal patient-level prediction; measure baseline PVR and assess catheterization capacity before injection. The guideline advises caution when PVR exceeds roughly 100-200 mL relative to voided volumes, and recommends rechecking PVR (about 2 weeks after injection, when retention peaks) if symptoms do not improve.

The guideline makes no recommendation specific to the implantable tibial systems (eCoin, Revi) because direct comparisons or placebo-controlled data are lacking; the evidence base is single-arm studies.

### Antimuscarinic Cognitive-Risk Considerations

In the [Malcher 2022 case-control study](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/35686842/), greater combined OAB-anticholinergic exposure was associated with dementia (aOR 1.07 at 1–90 defined daily doses, 1.29 at 91–365, and 1.48 at &gt;365). This is an observational association, not a causal or agent-by-agent safety ranking. The [AUA/SUFU guideline](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1002/nau.25532) recommends discussing possible cognitive risk with patients taking or prescribed antimuscarinics. The [pharmacology hub](/docs/foundations/pharmacology/storage-oab/anticholinergics) holds agent-specific details.

| Consideration | Agents | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| **Lower expected CNS penetration, not proven dementia-safe** | **Trospium** (quaternary amine); **Fesoterodine** | Trospium poorly crosses the BBB; the guideline names fesoterodine, darifenacin and trospium as theoretically lower-risk on permeability grounds; observational dementia signals differ across cohorts. Neither agent is established as universally risk-free. |
| **Other agent-specific evidence** | Darifenacin, tolterodine ER, solifenacin | CNS penetration, short-term cognitive outcomes and observational dementia associations differ; do not infer a validated middle-to-low risk order from these data. |
| **Greater concern** | **Oxybutynin**, especially chronic oral use | Higher CNS penetration and observational dementia signals warrant particular caution; route and cumulative exposure matter. |

### Stepwise Escalation (Optional / When Patient Prefers a Graduated Approach)

The 2024 guideline eliminates mandatory step therapy, but many patients still prefer a graduated approach:

1. **Behavioral therapies:** bladder training, fluid, caffeine and alcohol modification, urgency suppression, [weight loss](/docs/surgical-techniques/04f-incontinence-procedures/procedures/weight-loss-incontinence)
2. **Non-invasive therapies:** [PFMT](/docs/surgical-techniques/04f-incontinence-procedures/procedures/pelvic-floor-pt) ± biofeedback, TTNS, transvaginal electrical stimulation
3. **Pharmacotherapy:** [β-3 agonist](/docs/foundations/pharmacology/storage-oab/beta3-agonists), then [antimuscarinic](/docs/foundations/pharmacology/storage-oab/anticholinergics), then combination
4. **Minimally invasive:** [BTX 100 U](/docs/surgical-techniques/04f-incontinence-procedures/procedures/intradetrusor-botox), [SNM](/docs/surgical-techniques/04f-incontinence-procedures/procedures/sacral-neuromodulation), [iTNM](/docs/surgical-techniques/04f-incontinence-procedures/procedures/implantable-tibial-nerve-stimulation), or [PTNS](/docs/surgical-techniques/04f-incontinence-procedures/procedures/percutaneous-tibial-nerve-stimulation)
5. **[Augmentation cystoplasty](/docs/surgical-techniques/04b-bladder-reconstruction/bladder-augmentation)**
6. **[Urinary diversion](/docs/surgical-techniques/04c-urinary-diversion)** or indwelling catheter
