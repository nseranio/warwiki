# Urethrolysis

**Urethrolysis** is a salvage operation that relieves iatrogenic bladder outlet obstruction (BOO) after anti-incontinence surgery. The causes are an overly tensioned midurethral sling (the most common), hypersuspension after retropubic bladder neck suspension, postoperative periurethral scar, and obstructing mesh.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup> The literature reports obstruction after 2.5–24% of anti-incontinence procedures, with 50–75% of those patients receiving surgical correction.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup>

The term "urethrolysis" historically covers a spectrum from simple sling transection to complete circumferential urethral mobilization. A 2025 review proposes reserving "urethrolysis" for extensive periurethral dissection and using "sling incision" or "sling excision" for the limited transvaginal procedures used after MUS. Most obstruction today follows a synthetic midurethral sling and can be treated with simple sling incision.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup>

---

## Indications and Diagnosis

The only absolute selection criterion in Carr and Webster's series was a clear temporal relationship between symptoms and the prior anti-incontinence procedure.<sup>[[2]](#ref2)</sup> Presenting symptoms include urinary retention, hesitancy, slow or interrupted stream, incomplete emptying, urgency or frequency, recurrent UTI and de novo urge incontinence. A low PVR does not exclude obstruction.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[2]](#ref2)</sup>

| Workup element | Finding suggestive of BOO |
|---|---|
| History and exam | Symptom onset temporally linked to prior sling / suspension; urethral tenderness; positive Q-tip deflection |
| Noninvasive uroflow | Slow or interrupted stream |
| Post-void residual | Elevated PVR |
| Multichannel UDS with pressure-flow | Low flow with high detrusor pressure. Female UDS lacks precision and **should not alone rule out obstruction**. Low pressure with low flow occurs with long-standing obstruction and detrusor underactivity<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup> |
| Cystoscopy | Mesh erosion; urethral angulation or kinking<sup>[[3]](#ref3)</sup> |

ACOG Committee Opinion 694 (2017) states that short-term voiding dysfunction after a synthetic midurethral sling, if improving, can be managed expectantly for up to 6 weeks. Retention, or small-volume voids with a large PVR, warrants earlier intervention.<sup>[[3]](#ref3)</sup> Gleich and Goldman advise that surgeons uncomfortable treating obstruction should refer to a specialist.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup>

## Surgical Approaches

The approach is primarily driven by the type of original anti-incontinence procedure.

| Approach | Best for | Success rate | Notes |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Simple sling incision** | MUS obstruction (first-line) | 70–90%<sup>[[5]](#ref5)</sup> | Transvaginal midline transection of the tape; ~20% recurrent SUI |
| **Transvaginal urethrolysis** | Post-retropubic suspension or failed sling incision | 71–85% (retrospective series)<sup>[[2]](#ref2)[[6]](#ref6)[[10]](#ref10)</sup> | Periurethral fascia dissection, endopelvic fascia perforation, sharp scar / suture release |
| **Retropubic (open) urethrolysis** | Prior retropubic surgery, hostile vaginal field | 78–86% (small series)<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[2]](#ref2)</sup> | Lower midline / Pfannenstiel; urethral mobilization from inferior pubis |
| **Suprameatal transvaginal urethrolysis** | Selected MUS / suspension obstruction without lateral perforation of the urethropelvic ligament | 65% of women in retention voided well; 67% of those with urgency improved<sup>[[4]](#ref4)</sup> | Dissection above the meatus; preserves lateral attachments |
| **Laparoscopic / robotic urethrolysis** | After retropubic bladder neck suspension; improved deep-pelvic visualization | Small series and technique videos (robotic: 5 of 6 with complete symptom resolution)<sup>[[7]](#ref7)[[8]](#ref8)</sup> | Useful when prior retropubic anatomy demands MIS access. Gleich and Goldman prefer a robotic transabdominal approach for post-suspension obstruction |

### Key Surgical Considerations

- **Martius fat-pad interposition** between the urethra and pubis may reduce recurrent scar formation. Gleich and Goldman consider it for repeat urethrolysis, heavy scarring or concomitant urethrotomy, and describe its value as limited in their experience.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[2]](#ref2)[[9]](#ref9)</sup>
- **No concomitant resuspension.** Gleich and Goldman do not place a new sling or resuspension at the time of urethrolysis or sling incision, because some patients remain continent and a persistent problem would be unclear afterward (author opinion).<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup>
- **Intraoperative cystourethroscopy** at completion to confirm no urethral or bladder injury.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup>

## Operative Technique by Approach

### 1. Simple Sling Incision (Transvaginal)

**First-line for midurethral sling obstruction**, with the least extensive dissection.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[5]](#ref5)</sup> In the first 7–10 days after a synthetic sling, the vaginal incision can be reopened and the sling gently pulled down ("loosening"). Published outcome data for loosening are sparse.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup>

- Small midline transvaginal incision over the sling.
- Light horizontal scalpel movement along the anterior surface of the dissection confirms sling location and exposes mesh fibers. Avoid going deep; urethral injury is a risk.
- If the sling is hard to locate: extend the incision, palpate the anterior vaginal wall between the bladder neck and meatus, or insert a cystoscope sheath into the urethra to facilitate palpation.
- Once identified, place a right-angle clamp between the sling and suburethral tissue. Spread the tips carefully, then transect sharply.
- The two sling portions retract laterally once tension is released.
- If additional release is needed: grasp the cut edge with an Allis, dissect laterally with Metzenbaum scissors (or blunt with a peanut sponge if the sling separates easily) to the lateral sulcus or endopelvic fascia, then cut each arm free with curved Mayo scissors.
- Close vaginal incision with absorbable suture. Patients often report immediate improvement in voiding and stream force.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup>
- If obstructive symptoms persist after sling incision, part of the sling may remain intact, since dense scar can hide it at the first operation. Gleich and Goldman report that operative reports in such patients often describe difficulty identifying the sling.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup>

**Fascial slings.** The suburethral portion must be addressed transvaginally. Within approximately 2 weeks of the original surgery, the sling ends scar in retropubically, so releasing retropubic sutures alone will not adequately relieve tension.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup>

### 2. Formal Transvaginal Urethrolysis

Appropriate after retropubic bladder neck suspension or when sling incision has failed.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup>

- **Positioning:** dorsal lithotomy with Trendelenburg; Foley placed; bladder neck identified.
- **Incision:** vertical midline or inverted-U incision along the anterior vaginal wall between midurethra and bladder neck.
- **Periurethral dissection** bilaterally until the endopelvic fascia is encountered, then perforated with heavy Mayo scissors.
- **Scar and suture removal.** Sharp incision of scar tissue and sutures. Imperative to remove all permanent sutures. If a suture is difficult to expose on the inferior pubis, place a right-angle clamp under finger guidance, insinuate between the suture and pubis, and pull down for visualization or removal.
- **Urethral mobilization.** Carefully dissect the anterior urethra off the undersurface of the pubis with Metzenbaum scissors. Palpate frequently (direct visualization is not possible); awareness of urethral location is necessary to avoid urethral or bladder neck entry.
- **Hemostasis.** Vascular field; heavy bleeding may occur. Best controlled with manual pressure to the underside of the pubis, expeditious completion, and vaginal packing.
- **Completion cystourethroscopy** with careful attention to the anterior urethra and bladder neck.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup>

### 3. Suprameatal Urethrolysis

Performed independently or as part of a traditional transvaginal urethrolysis; particularly helpful when dense scar is encountered or all sutures cannot be removed during traditional urethrolysis.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup>

- **Semilunar incision** 1 cm superior to the urethral meatus, extending from 3 o'clock to 9 o'clock (inverted-U shape).
- Allis clamps on superior and inferior incision edges for retraction.
- **Perineal membrane perforated sharply.**
- Place the index finger into the retropubic space and use a lateral, downward sweeping motion to bluntly free the space from its attachments.
- Sutures and dense scar are sharply incised with Metzenbaum scissors.
- If a sling is present, dissection continues laterally until the arms are identified and transected sharply.
- Further sweeping of the urethra from the pubis releases any residual scar.

**Advantage.** The endopelvic fascia remains intact, which may help maintain postoperative continence. Petrou et al. reported 13 of 20 women in urinary retention voided well after suprameatal urethrolysis.<sup>[[4]](#ref4)</sup>

### 4. Open Retropubic Urethrolysis

Preferred when the original surgery was retropubic (Burch, Marshall-Marchetti-Krantz).<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup>

- **Positioning:** dorsal lithotomy or supine with legs slightly spread for vaginal palpation.
- **Incision.** Lower midline or Pfannenstiel; enter the space of Retzius.
- Hand-held or self-retaining retractors for visualization.
- **Scar tissue and suspension sutures sharply incised**; mobilize the anterior urethra off the inferior pubis.
- Careful attention to Foley and bladder-neck location to avoid injury. If inadvertent cystotomy occurs, a finger may be placed into the bladder to palpate tissue planes; transvaginal palpation of the anterior vaginal wall may also aid dissection.
- Urethrolysis is complete when the urethra, bladder neck, and anterior vaginal wall are fully mobilized from the pubis; the created plane should accommodate the index finger.
- Fill the bladder through the Foley to assess for leaks. If cystorrhaphy is performed, maintain an indwelling Foley for 1–2 weeks.
- A peritoneal or omental flap may be interposed between the anterior urethra and pubis to prevent recurrent scar.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup>

Anger et al. compared the two approaches in 16 patients with post-Burch obstruction: 7 of 9 (78%) had symptom relief after the retropubic approach and 3 of 7 (43%) after the transvaginal approach. Gleich and Goldman have found residual permanent Burch sutures at retropubic surgery in several women after failed transvaginal urethrolysis.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup>

### 5. Martius Fat-Pad Flap Interposition

Used as an adjunct to prevent recurrent scar formation between urethra and pubis, particularly in repeat urethrolysis, heavy scarring or urethrotomy.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[9]](#ref9)</sup> The steps below combine the general Martius harvest described by Waterloos and Verla with the Oliver and Raz salvage technique.

- Sagittal incision at the most dependent line of the labium majus.
- Dissect until the deep fibrofatty tissue ("bright yellow") is identified.
- Mobilize the fibrofatty pad laterally and medially following a natural tissue plane under the subcutaneous fat.<sup>[[11]](#ref11)</sup>
- **Tunnel and transfer** the flap to the retropubic space inferior to the pubis.
- Secure between the bladder neck / urethra and the posterior-inferior pubic bone using pre-placed anchoring sutures in the superficial detrusor muscle.<sup>[[9]](#ref9)</sup>
- Restore urethral support by reattaching the pubourethral fascia to the periosteum.<sup>[[9]](#ref9)</sup>

**Outcomes.** Carey et al. reported resolution of obstruction in 20 of 23 women (87%) after urethrolysis with circumferential Martius wrapping (mean follow-up 15 months), with postoperative SUI in 6 of 23 (13%).<sup>[[12]](#ref12)</sup> In 11 women with refractory obstruction after failed transvaginal urethrolysis, suprameatal urethrolysis with a Martius flap succeeded in 9 (82%) at a median of 10.8 months, and SUI recurred in 1. Oliver and Raz believe Martius interposition is critical to prevent recurrent urethral fixation to the pubic bones.<sup>[[9]](#ref9)</sup>

## Postoperative Management

| Scenario | Catheter duration |
|---|---|
| **Sling incision** | Void trial before discharge; often immediate improvement<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup> |
| **Suprameatal urethrolysis with Martius flap** | Urethral catheter ~**5 days**<sup>[[9]](#ref9)</sup> |
| **Inadvertent bladder injury with cystorrhaphy** | Indwelling Foley **1–2 weeks**<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup> |
| **Urethral / bladder injury visualized on completion cystoscopy** | Catheter **2 weeks**<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup> |
| **Inaccessible area of injury (no repair possible)** | Foley **2–3 weeks** for secondary healing; cystourethrogram before removal<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup> |

---

## Outcomes

- **Voiding symptoms.** Voiding or obstructive symptoms improve in up to 87% of patients after surgery for obstruction. Retrospective series of primary urethrolysis report normal voiding or resolution in about 71–87% (Nitti 71%, Carr 73% vaginal and 86% retropubic, Cross 85%, Carey 87% with Martius).<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[2]](#ref2)[[6]](#ref6)[[10]](#ref10)[[12]](#ref12)</sup>
- **Recurrent SUI.** Simple sling incision is followed by recurrent SUI in about 20% (Moore and Goldman), and the reported range across series is 9–50%.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[5]](#ref5)</sup> Recurrence is less frequent after incision of a fascial sling than of a synthetic midurethral sling (author experience).<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup> Urethral bulking is one initial management option for recurrent SUI.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup>
- **Persistent urge incontinence.** Urge incontinence persists after sling incision in up to 70–80% in reported series, more often when treatment of the obstruction is delayed. It is managed with overactive bladder therapy.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup>
- **Preoperative PVR.** A higher PVR predicted failure in Nitti and Raz (p = 0.021). Petrou and colleagues found no association between PVR and success, and Carr and Webster found no predictive parameter.<sup>[[2]](#ref2)[[4]](#ref4)[[10]](#ref10)</sup>
- **Refractory obstruction.** Suprameatal urethrolysis with a Martius flap has been described after failed transvaginal urethrolysis (11 women, 82% success).<sup>[[9]](#ref9)</sup>

### Complications

Significant bleeding, urethral or bladder injury, and recurrent SUI are the principal complications.<sup>[[5]](#ref5)</sup>

---

## Timing

- Improving short-term voiding dysfunction after MUS placement can be managed expectantly for up to 6 weeks. Retention or a large PVR warrants earlier intervention.<sup>[[3]](#ref3)</sup>
- Timely treatment of obstruction gives symptom relief and may prevent long-term deterioration of the lower urinary tract.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup>
- In one cohort, resolution of lower urinary tract symptoms was higher when sling lysis was done within a year of the index surgery. In another, timing did not affect the risk of SUI requiring treatment.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup>

---

## Decision Pearls

- **MUS obstruction.** Perform simple sling incision first. Reserve formal urethrolysis for failure or for non-MUS etiologies.
- **Post-Burch or hypersuspension obstruction.** Perform retropubic or transvaginal urethrolysis, not sling incision (no sling to transect).
- **Martius flap interposition** if redo dissection, severely scarred field, or concomitant urethrotomy is required.
- Counsel about recurrent SUI (about 20% after sling incision, up to 50% in some series) before any sling release; have a plan for staged retreatment (bulking, repeat sling, [autologous PVS](/docs/surgical-techniques/04f-incontinence-procedures/procedures/autologous-pubovaginal-sling), or [AUS](/docs/surgical-techniques/04f-incontinence-procedures/procedures/artificial-urinary-sphincter)) if recurrence occurs.
- **Female UDS alone cannot exclude obstruction.** Clinical context plus uroflow, PVR, and cystoscopy carries equal weight.
