# Pudendal Neuromodulation

**Pudendal neuromodulation** places a stimulating lead at the pudendal nerve, usually near Alcock's canal or the ischial spine, rather than at the S3 sacral root used by [sacral neuromodulation (SNM)](/docs/surgical-techniques/04f-incontinence-procedures/procedures/sacral-neuromodulation).<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup> Implanted pudendal stimulation remains investigational for lower urinary tract indications and is not an established alternative to SNM. Human reports include small crossover trials and uncontrolled cohorts for OAB, painful bladder syndrome and other voiding dysfunction; the broader pudendal-stimulation literature also includes nonimplanted methods and cannot be pooled as evidence for an implanted lead.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[2]](#ref2)[[3]](#ref3)[[10]](#ref10)</sup>

For the family-level comparison with SNM, percutaneous tibial nerve stimulation, and implantable tibial systems, including the short-term randomized crossover trial of pudendal versus sacral stimulation for voiding dysfunction, see [Neuromodulation](/docs/surgical-techniques/04f-incontinence-procedures/procedures/neuromodulation). That trial's denominators and results are not repeated here.<sup>[[3]](#ref3)</sup>

---

## Rationale and Target

The pudendal nerve carries somatic afferent and efferent fibers from the external urethral and anal sphincters, perineal skin, and genitalia, and contributes to bladder, bowel, and pelvic-floor reflex circuits at a different level than the S3 sacral root used by SNM. Stimulating it has been proposed both for voiding-phase lower urinary tract dysfunction and, more often in reported series, for chronic pudendal-territory pain.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[4]](#ref4)</sup>

## Lead Placement

Reported techniques place a lead near the ischial spine or within Alcock's canal under fluoroscopic or anatomic-landmark guidance, usually prone with sedation and local anesthetic. One described approach uses perirectal electromyography and test stimulation at approximately 5 mA and 5 Hz, confirming proximity to the nerve by an anal "wink" response or compound muscle action potential before placing a long quadripolar lead; intraoperative test stimulation with manual pressure simulating sitting and lying has also been described to check for positional capture loss. This is the operative sequence as the chapter authors describe it, not a standardized protocol.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup> A separate anatomic-landmark technique ("STAR," using the ischial spine, ischial tuberosity, acetabulum, and anal rim) was developed in cadavers and tested in a 20-patient pilot to make lead placement more reproducible; after four weeks, pain fell from 85 to 40 mm on a 100-mm VAS in the bilateral Bock/STAR groups (p = 0.018), while the unilateral groups did not show a statistically significant decrease. In the ten STAR cases, mean bilateral operating time was 85 minutes with 3.5 attempts on average to localize each nerve.<sup>[[5]](#ref5)</sup>

## FDA Status and Current Evidence

Pudendal neuromodulation has no FDA-approved indication for overactive bladder, retention, pelvic-floor dysfunction, or underactive bladder.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup> Human evidence for urinary indications includes a 30-person, seven-day crossover trial against sacral stimulation (see [Neuromodulation](/docs/surgical-techniques/04f-incontinence-procedures/procedures/neuromodulation)) and an uncontrolled 84-person implanted-lead cohort, mainly with IC/BPS or OAB. In the cohort, 55 continued pudendal stimulation after the test phase and had a median follow-up of 24.1 months. Neither study establishes benefit for a specific urinary indication, and no implanted-lead human trial for underactive bladder is reported.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[3]](#ref3)[[11]](#ref11)</sup>

For refractory interstitial cystitis, a single-blind randomized crossover trial placed both an S3 tined lead and a posterior-approach pudendal lead in 22 patients and tested each for 7 days. Seventeen of 22 responded and received their preferred lead for permanent stimulation (13 pudendal, four sacral); during the randomized seven-day test, overall symptom reduction was 59% with pudendal versus 44% with sacral stimulation (p = 0.05), while subsequent six-month follow-up assessed selected leads rather than a randomized between-lead comparison. This small single-center trial does not establish a general preference for pudendal stimulation.<sup>[[9]](#ref9)</sup>

AUA 2025 identifies nerve blocks and neuromodulation for pudendal neuralgia as approaches requiring further research, in an ungraded Future Directions discussion.<sup>[[8]](#ref8)</sup>

Published pudendal-trunk stimulation studies span urinary, bowel, and pelvic-pain indications. A 2024 systematic review included 14 heterogeneous studies (292 patients): eight addressed urinary indications, three bowel indications, and three pelvic or perineal pain; lead placement and test-to-implant pathways varied.<sup>[[14]](#ref14)</sup> The series below concern selected patients with refractory pudendal neuralgia after medical therapy, pelvic-floor physical therapy, and/or nerve blocks.<sup>[[6]](#ref6)[[7]](#ref7)</sup>

| Series | Population | Reported outcome |
|---|---|---|
| Hoang Roberts 2021<sup>[[6]](#ref6)</sup> | 13 patients with pudendal neuralgia, wireless pudendal lead (StimWave), retrospective feasibility study | 10/13 (76.9%) had &gt;50% pain improvement after the trial period and 6/13 (46.1%) reported 100% improvement; 9 underwent permanent implantation, and 5/9 reported &gt;50% pain improvement at the last postoperative visit, 6–83 days after placement. Complications at follow-up included lead migration (2), a broken wire (1), and a nonfunctioning antenna (2) |
| Peters 2015<sup>[[7]](#ref7)</sup> | 19 patients with pudendal neuralgia and a tined lead already placed at the pudendal nerve, retrospective chart review plus mailed survey | Pain relief after lead placement was complete in 3, almost complete in 3, significant in 10, and small in 3; all 19 had a permanent generator placed, and 5 were later explanted at a mean of 2.95 years |

These are small, uncontrolled, single- or few-center reports without a comparator arm; they describe a selected refractory-pain population and do not establish an expected response rate for a general audience, a durability estimate beyond the reported follow-up, or a role in OAB, retention, or underactive bladder.<sup>[[6]](#ref6)[[7]](#ref7)</sup>

## Complications

Reported hardware problems include lead migration, lead or wire fracture, and loss of signal from a malfunctioning antenna in wireless systems; aberrant stimulation of a nearby nerve territory and local insertion-site discomfort have also been described.<sup>[[6]](#ref6)</sup> In a retrospective cohort of 55 patients who continued implanted pudendal stimulation after test lead placement (median follow-up 24.1 months), investigators reported seven complications requiring five revisions; four other reoperations occurred, and five patients underwent explantation.<sup>[[11]](#ref11)</sup> The selected cohort does not establish a generalizable procedure-specific complication rate.

## Practical Framing

For OAB and retention, pudendal neuromodulation remains an evidence-limited, off-label approach rather than an established alternative to SNM, PTNS, or implantable tibial neuromodulation.<sup>[[10]](#ref10)</sup> For refractory IC/BPS, the AUA 2022 guideline permits a neuromodulation trial after inadequate symptom control and quality-of-life improvement with other treatments (Option, Grade C); its discussion includes sacral and pudendal leads for carefully selected patients and cautions that frequency and urgency may respond more reliably than pain.<sup>[[12]](#ref12)</sup> EAU 2026 recommends neuromodulation before more invasive PBPS interventions (Weak) without specifying a target; its separate level 1b evidence statement favoring pudendal stimulation reflects a crossover trial in 22 patients that tested each lead for seven days.<sup>[[13]](#ref13)[[9]](#ref9)</sup> Pudendal implantation for neuralgia remains a selected, off-label approach in experienced programs.<sup>[[6]](#ref6)[[7]](#ref7)[[8]](#ref8)</sup> Counsel on the limited, small, uncontrolled evidence base before considering it, and reserve it for patients who have exhausted conservative, pharmacologic, and injection-based pudendal neuralgia therapy.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[6]](#ref6)[[7]](#ref7)</sup>
