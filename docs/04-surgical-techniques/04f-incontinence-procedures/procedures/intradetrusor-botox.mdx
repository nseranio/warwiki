# Intradetrusor OnabotulinumtoxinA

**Intradetrusor onabotulinumtoxinA (Botox)** is an FDA-approved, cystoscopically administered treatment for **overactive bladder (OAB) at 100 U** and **neurogenic detrusor overactivity (NDO) at 200 U** in patients inadequately managed by or intolerant of anticholinergic medications.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[2]](#ref2)</sup> It is also indicated for **NDO in pediatric patients ≥ 5 years old**, using a separate weight-based regimen.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup> First reported for urological use by Schurch and colleagues in 2000, it has since become an established minimally invasive treatment for lower urinary tract dysfunction, with contemporary single-center data continuing to confirm the durability of response in real-world practice.<sup>[[3]](#ref3)[[10]](#ref10)</sup>

This page is the **procedural counterpart** to the [Botulinum Toxin pharmacology hub](/docs/foundations/pharmacology/storage-oab/botulinum-toxin), which holds the broader drug-class evidence (mechanism, off-label uses, head-to-head data, novel delivery systems). The 2024 AUA/SUFU OAB Guideline groups it with minimally invasive options alongside [Sacral Neuromodulation](/docs/surgical-techniques/04f-incontinence-procedures/procedures/sacral-neuromodulation) and [PTNS](/docs/surgical-techniques/04f-incontinence-procedures/procedures/percutaneous-tibial-nerve-stimulation).<sup>[[8]](#ref8)</sup>

## Mechanism of Action

OnabotulinumtoxinA exerts a **dual efferent and afferent mechanism** in the bladder:<sup>[[4]](#ref4)[[5]](#ref5)[[6]](#ref6)</sup>

- **Efferent (motor) pathway** — Cleaves SNAP-25, a SNARE complex protein, thereby **blocking acetylcholine release** from peripheral cholinergic terminals, reducing detrusor contractile activity. It also blocks **ATP release** from purinergic efferent nerves.<sup>[[4]](#ref4)[[5]](#ref5)</sup>
- **Afferent (sensory) pathway** — Reduces urothelial ATP release, decreases expression of capsaicin (TRPV1) and purinergic (P2X3) receptors on suburothelial sensory nerves, and reduces substance P and nerve growth factor levels. These observations support a sensory contribution to clinical benefit.<sup>[[5]](#ref5)[[6]](#ref6)</sup>
- **Anti-inflammatory effects** — Decreases mast cell activation, reduces vascular endothelial growth factor expression, and attenuates neuroinflammation in the bladder wall.<sup>[[5]](#ref5)[[7]](#ref7)</sup>

These mechanisms help explain increased storage capacity and reduced urgency. Proposed sensory and inflammatory mechanisms should not be equated with proven central desensitization in patients.<sup>[[6]](#ref6)</sup>

## FDA-Approved Indications and Dosing

| Indication | Dose | Injection Sites | Dilution | References |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Idiopathic OAB** (adults) | **100 U** | 20 sites (0.5 mL each) | 100 U in 10 mL NS | <sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[8]](#ref8)[[14]](#ref14)[[15]](#ref15)</sup> |
| **NDO** (adults, SCI / MS) | **200 U** | 30 sites (1 mL each) | 200 U in 30 mL NS | <sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[11]](#ref11)[[12]](#ref12)</sup> |
| **NDO** (pediatric ≥ 5 years) | ≥34 kg: **200 U**; &lt;34 kg: **6 U/kg** | **20 sites**, 0.5 mL each | **10 mL total**; use label dilution table | <sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup> |

In the adult NDO pivotal program, 300 U offered no clinically relevant advantage over 200 U. In Ginsberg's trial, **among those not catheterizing at baseline**, CIC for retention began in 42% with 300 U, 35% with 200 U and 10% with placebo.<sup>[[11]](#ref11)[[12]](#ref12)[[13]](#ref13)</sup>

## Landmark Clinical Trials

### NDO — Pivotal Phase 3 Trials

The two trials enrolled **275** (Cruz 2011) and **416** (Ginsberg 2012) participants. Both included SCI and MS; the pooled 310 SCI / 381 MS counts are diagnoses across trials, not separate trial populations:<sup>[[11]](#ref11)[[12]](#ref12)</sup>

- OnabotulinumtoxinA 200 U reduced UI episodes by **21.8/week** vs 13.2/week for placebo at 6 weeks in Cruz's trial (p &lt; 0.01).<sup>[[12]](#ref12)</sup>
- Maximum cystometric capacity (MCC) increased by **141 mL** (200 U) vs placebo (p &lt; 0.001).<sup>[[13]](#ref13)</sup>
- Maximum detrusor pressure (MDP) decreased by **~33 cm H₂O** (p &lt; 0.001).<sup>[[13]](#ref13)</sup>
- Median time to retreatment request: **256 days** (200 U) vs 92 days (placebo).<sup>[[11]](#ref11)</sup>
- I-QOL scores improved significantly across all domains.<sup>[[11]](#ref11)[[12]](#ref12)</sup>

### OAB — Phase 3 Trial (2013)

Nitti's 557-patient RCT was one of the pivotal OAB trials (its 2017 publication is a reprint, not a separate trial):<sup>[[14]](#ref14)[[15]](#ref15)</sup>

- OnabotulinumtoxinA 100 U reduced daily UI episodes by **−2.65** vs −0.87 for placebo (p &lt; 0.001).<sup>[[15]](#ref15)</sup>

## Injection Technique

### Pre-procedure<sup>[[8]](#ref8)[[17]](#ref17)[[18]](#ref18)[[19]](#ref19)</sup>

- Measure **post-void residual (PVR)** — caution if PVR &gt; 100–200 mL.
- Counsel regarding risk of urinary retention and potential need for clean intermittent catheterization (CIC).
- Confirm willingness and ability to perform CIC if needed.
- The US label specifies non-aminoglycoside antibiotic prophylaxis 1–3 days before, on treatment day and 1–3 days afterward; follow the local antimicrobial protocol.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup>
- Local anesthetic instillation can be used; drain and irrigate before injection.<sup>[[17]](#ref17)</sup>

### Procedure<sup>[[9]](#ref9)[[17]](#ref17)[[18]](#ref18)</sup>

- Performed via **rigid or flexible cystoscope** in the office setting (preferred) or operating room.
- Reconstitute onabotulinumtoxinA in **10 mL preservative-free normal saline** for OAB 100 U or 30 mL for NDO 200 U.
- Use a cystoscopic injection needle appropriate for detrusor injection; needle length is not the intended insertion depth.<sup>[[9]](#ref9)</sup>
- Inject into the **detrusor muscle**, distributing across the posterior and lateral bladder walls.
- **Spare the trigone** (standard practice in pivotal trials, though some evidence suggests trigonal injection is safe).<sup>[[9]](#ref9)</sup>
- The labeled OAB template uses **20 sites**. Reduced-site protocols are technique variations discussed in expert consensus; do not present 1–20 sites as one universally validated standard.<sup>[[17]](#ref17)</sup>
- For **adult** NDO, 30 sites at 1 mL each is the labeled template; pediatric dosing differs as above.<sup>[[12]](#ref12)</sup>
- Procedure duration: approximately **15 minutes**.<sup>[[18]](#ref18)</sup>

### Post-procedure

- Follow-up at **7–14 days** to assess for urinary retention.<sup>[[18]](#ref18)</sup>
- In non-catheterizing patients, reassess PVR as clinically appropriate through 12 weeks.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup>

## Duration of Effect and Retreatment

- Onset of action: **1–2 weeks** post-injection.<sup>[[18]](#ref18)</sup>
- Duration of effect: typically **4–10 months** (median ~6–9 months for NDO; ~6 months for OAB).<sup>[[16]](#ref16)[[18]](#ref18)[[20]](#ref20)</sup>
- Retreatment interval: **no sooner than 12 weeks**; expert consensus recommends scheduling at **6-month intervals** with the option to retreat earlier if symptoms return.<sup>[[17]](#ref17)[[18]](#ref18)</sup>
- Patients do **not appear to become refractory** to repeat injections over multiple cycles, though one retrospective study suggested possible declining duration after the **5th treatment cycle** (from ~10 months to ~5.5 months).<sup>[[16]](#ref16)[[21]](#ref21)[[22]](#ref22)</sup>
- Across indications, the **adult limit is 400 U per 3 months**; pediatric limits differ. Units are formulation-specific.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup>

## Adverse Effects

The adverse effect profile is primarily **localized to the lower urinary tract**:<sup>[[13]](#ref13)[[14]](#ref14)[[19]](#ref19)[[23]](#ref23)</sup>

**Common**

- **Urinary tract infection** — in pooled OAB label trials, 18% versus 6% placebo within 12 weeks; definitions and follow-up differ across studies.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup> For NDO, the cited meta-analysis reported RR 1.47 (95% CI 1.29–1.67) versus placebo.<sup>[[13]](#ref13)[[14]](#ref14)[[24]](#ref24)</sup>
- **Urinary retention / CIC** — pooled OAB trials: 6.5% initiated CIC versus 0.4% placebo over the treatment cycle.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup> NDO risk depends on baseline catheterization and dose; see the non-catheterizing subgroup above rather than applying its percentage to all patients.<sup>[[3]](#ref3)[[11]](#ref11)[[12]](#ref12)[[13]](#ref13)[[14]](#ref14)</sup>
- **Dysuria** — common in OAB patients.<sup>[[14]](#ref14)</sup>
- **Hematuria** — RR 1.70 (95% CI 1.01–2.85).<sup>[[13]](#ref13)</sup>

**Less Common**

- **Muscle weakness** — RR 2.59 (95% CI 1.36–4.91), reflecting systemic spread of toxin.<sup>[[13]](#ref13)</sup>
- **Transient body weakness**.<sup>[[19]](#ref19)</sup>

**Male-Specific Considerations**

In one retrospective cohort of **146 men** with idiopathic OAB, **62.3%** reported a treatment response and **13%** had urinary retention. A higher bladder outlet obstruction index predicted both lower response rates and higher complication rates in that cohort; these figures are not a universal male risk estimate or directly comparable with the pooled label's CIC rate.<sup>[[25]](#ref25)</sup>

## Comparison with Other Minimally Invasive Therapies

The 2024 AUA/SUFU guideline supports selecting **SNM, PTNS, or intradetrusor BTX** through shared decision-making and permits minimally invasive therapy without requiring prior behavioral or medication trials (Expert Opinion). This does not imply equal efficacy or identical harms. The FDA-labeled OAB indication still specifies inadequate response to or intolerance of an anticholinergic; distinguish guideline options from product labeling and coverage requirements.<sup>[[8]](#ref8)[[18]](#ref18)[[26]](#ref26)</sup>

**ROSETTA is the key direct comparison with SNM.** It randomized 386 women with refractory UUI and analyzed 364 for the primary outcome. It used **200 U**, above the labeled idiopathic OAB dose, so its benefit/harms cannot be transferred unchanged to 100-U treatment.<sup>[[38]](#ref38)</sup>

| Six-month outcome | OnabotulinumtoxinA 200 U | SNM |
|---|---|---|
| Mean daily UUI reduction | 3.9 episodes | 3.3 episodes |
| Complete resolution, exploratory diary endpoint | 35/178 (20%) | 6/166 (4%) |
| UTI over six months | 35% | 11% |

The primary between-group difference was **0.63 episode/day (95% CI 0.13–1.14)**: statistically significant, with uncertain clinical importance. The complete-resolution analysis required at least four monthly diaries and was exploratory. Greater symptom reduction came with more UTI and catheterization; it does not establish universal superiority.<sup>[[38]](#ref38)</sup>

Systematic and network reviews combine different doses, populations, follow-up and outcome definitions; they should not be read as a definitive ranking of BTX, SNM and PTNS.<sup>[[27]](#ref27)[[28]](#ref28)</sup> Older real-world cost estimates are setting- and year-specific, not current patient prices.<sup>[[29]](#ref29)</sup>

<a id="mixed-incontinence-musa"></a>

### Mixed incontinence — MUSA

**MUSA (JAMA 2025)** randomized 150 women with bothersome stress and urgency incontinence despite conservative/medical treatment; 137 contributed to the primary analysis. At six months, UDI total-score change was −66.8 with onabotulinumtoxinA **100 U** versus −84.9 with midurethral sling (difference 18.1; 95% CI −4.6 to 40.7; p = 0.12). The trial **did not demonstrate Botox superiority**; it was not an equivalence trial. By 12 months, 22.6% had received both treatments. Discuss the likelihood of treating both symptom components and the different procedural risks.<sup>[[39]](#ref39)</sup>

## Off-Label / Emerging Indications

### Interstitial Cystitis / Bladder Pain Syndrome

The AUA guideline lists intradetrusor onabotulinumtoxinA as a treatment option for [IC/BPS](/docs/clinical-conditions/03h-pelvic-pain/ic-pbs) when other treatments have failed, though evidence is Grade C.<sup>[[30]](#ref30)</sup> A meta-analysis of 12 RCTs demonstrated significant improvements in ICSI, ICPI, VAS pain scores, and daytime frequency, with small-to-medium effect sizes.<sup>[[31]](#ref31)</sup> Injection sites vary across studies (trigonal vs lateral / posterior walls), and the true effect is difficult to determine given the dearth of placebo-controlled trials of BTX alone.<sup>[[30]](#ref30)</sup>

### Detrusor Sphincter Dyssynergia (DSD)

Injection of onabotulinumtoxinA into the **external urethral sphincter** (not intradetrusor) has been used for SCI patients with DSD, though this remains unlicensed.<sup>[[4]](#ref4)[[7]](#ref7)[[32]](#ref32)</sup>

### Other Investigational Uses

- Dysfunctional voiding (non-neurogenic).<sup>[[32]](#ref32)</sup>
- Benign prostatic hyperplasia — meta-analyses show **no benefit** over placebo for LUTS.<sup>[[32]](#ref32)[[33]](#ref33)</sup>
- Chronic prostatitis / pelvic pain (intraprostatic injection — emerging data).<sup>[[32]](#ref32)</sup>
- Novel delivery systems including **liposome-encapsulated** and **hydrogel intravesical instillation** are under investigation as less invasive alternatives to cystoscopic injection.<sup>[[32]](#ref32)</sup>

## Contraindications and Special Populations

Adult NLUTD care also requires attention to storage pressures, upper-tract risk and reliable emptying; see the [NLUTD guideline](https://doi.org/10.1097/JU.0000000000002239).<sup>[[23]](#ref23)</sup>

### Absolute Contraindications<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[18]](#ref18)[[19]](#ref19)</sup>

- Active UTI at time of injection.
- Urinary retention or **PVR &gt;200 mL without routine CIC**.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup>
- Known hypersensitivity to botulinum toxin or its components.

### Relative Contraindications

- Elevated PVR (&gt; 100–200 mL) — increased risk of retention.<sup>[[8]](#ref8)</sup>
- A workable catheterization plan is required if retention develops; resolve inability or unwillingness before treatment.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup>
- Impaired bladder emptying.<sup>[[30]](#ref30)</sup>

### Anticoagulation / Antiplatelet Therapy

Two retrospective studies (totaling &gt; 1,400 injection episodes) found **no clinically significant bleeding** in patients on antithrombotic therapy. In a cohort of 400 patients with 1,059 treatments, **zero cases of hematuria** were reported despite 27.8% being on antithrombotics with no discontinuation.<sup>[[34]](#ref34)</sup> A second study of 114 injection episodes on full-dose anticoagulation / antiplatelet therapy reported only **0.88%** spontaneously resolving hematuria.<sup>[[35]](#ref35)</sup> These observational cohorts support a low bleeding risk but do not establish that every antithrombotic should always continue. Individualize the plan with the prescriber and procedural protocol.<sup>[[34]](#ref34)[[35]](#ref35)</sup>

### Pregnancy

A 29-year pharmacovigilance analysis identified 913 reported pregnancies across indications; the **0.7%** major-defect estimate came from **1/152 prospectively reported live births**, not all 913 reports. This observational denominator does not establish pregnancy safety. Most exposures (94.6%) occurred before conception or during the first trimester.<sup>[[36]](#ref36)</sup> Data remain limited, however, and onabotulinumtoxinA is not formally recommended during pregnancy.<sup>[[36]](#ref36)[[37]](#ref37)</sup>

## Practical Pearls

- **Office-based procedure** is preferred over hospital / surgery center for OAB injections.<sup>[[17]](#ref17)</sup>
- Staff involvement from scheduling through post-procedure follow-up optimizes patient experience and retreatment compliance.<sup>[[17]](#ref17)</sup>
- Patients should be counseled that the procedure is **temporary and requires repeat treatments** — setting expectations early improves long-term adherence.<sup>[[17]](#ref17)</sup>
- It is reasonable to **bypass antimuscarinics** and proceed directly to BTX in patients who cannot take or do not wish to try oral medications.<sup>[[8]](#ref8)</sup>
- For NDO patients already performing CIC, the risk of urinary retention is less clinically significant.<sup>[[11]](#ref11)</sup>
- Cost-effectiveness analyses suggest onabotulinumtoxinA is **cost-effective compared with best supportive care** for both OAB and NDO.<sup>[[29]](#ref29)</sup>
