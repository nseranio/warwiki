# Intradetrusor OnabotulinumtoxinA

**Intradetrusor onabotulinumtoxinA (Botox)** is an FDA-approved, cystoscopically administered treatment for **overactive bladder (OAB) at 100 U** and **neurogenic detrusor overactivity (NDO) at 200 U** in patients inadequately managed by or intolerant of anticholinergic medications.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[2]](#ref2)</sup> It was also approved for **NDO in pediatric patients ≥ 5 years old** in 2020.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup> First reported for urological use by Schurch and colleagues in 2000, it has since become a third-line cornerstone in the management of refractory lower urinary tract dysfunction, with contemporary single-center data continuing to confirm the durability of response in real-world practice.<sup>[[3]](#ref3)[[10]](#ref10)</sup>

This page is the **procedural counterpart** to the [Botulinum Toxin pharmacology hub](/docs/foundations/pharmacology/neuromodulation-adjuncts/botulinum-toxin), which holds the broader drug-class evidence (mechanism, off-label uses, head-to-head data, novel delivery systems). It is one of three equivalent third-line options listed by the 2024 AUA/SUFU OAB Guideline alongside [Sacral Neuromodulation](/docs/surgical-techniques/04f-incontinence-procedures/procedures/sacral-neuromodulation) and [PTNS](/docs/surgical-techniques/04f-incontinence-procedures/procedures/percutaneous-tibial-nerve-stimulation).<sup>[[8]](#ref8)</sup>

## Mechanism of Action

OnabotulinumtoxinA exerts a **dual efferent and afferent mechanism** in the bladder:<sup>[[4]](#ref4)[[5]](#ref5)[[6]](#ref6)</sup>

- **Efferent (motor) pathway** — Cleaves SNAP-25, a SNARE complex protein, thereby **blocking acetylcholine release** from parasympathetic preganglionic and postganglionic nerve terminals at the neuromuscular junction, producing temporary detrusor paralysis. It also blocks **ATP release** from purinergic efferent nerves.<sup>[[4]](#ref4)[[5]](#ref5)</sup>
- **Afferent (sensory) pathway** — Reduces urothelial ATP release, decreases expression of capsaicin (TRPV1) and purinergic (P2X3) receptors on suburothelial sensory nerves, and reduces substance P and nerve growth factor levels. This produces a profound reduction in **urgency sensation** that exceeds what would be expected from simple muscle paralysis alone.<sup>[[5]](#ref5)[[6]](#ref6)</sup>
- **Anti-inflammatory effects** — Decreases mast cell activation, reduces vascular endothelial growth factor expression, and attenuates neuroinflammation in the bladder wall.<sup>[[5]](#ref5)[[7]](#ref7)</sup>

These combined effects result in decreased detrusor contractility, increased bladder capacity, reduced bladder hypersensitivity, and central desensitization through decreased uptake of substance P and neurotrophic factors.<sup>[[6]](#ref6)</sup>

## FDA-Approved Indications and Dosing

| Indication | Dose | Injection Sites | Dilution | References |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Idiopathic OAB** (adults) | **100 U** | 20 sites (0.5 mL each) | 100 U in 10 mL NS | <sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[8]](#ref8)[[14]](#ref14)[[15]](#ref15)</sup> |
| **NDO** (adults, SCI / MS) | **200 U** | 30 sites (1 mL each) | 200 U in 30 mL NS | <sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[11]](#ref11)[[12]](#ref12)</sup> |
| **NDO** (pediatric ≥ 5 years) | **200 U** | Per adult protocol | Per adult protocol | <sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[23]](#ref23)</sup> |

Importantly, **200 U and 300 U doses show no clinically significant difference in efficacy** for NDO, but 300 U is associated with higher rates of urinary retention and need for de novo catheterization (42% vs 35% vs 10% placebo).<sup>[[11]](#ref11)[[12]](#ref12)[[13]](#ref13)</sup>

## Landmark Clinical Trials

### NDO — Pivotal Phase 3 Trials (2011)

Two pivotal RCTs in patients with SCI (n = 310) and MS (n = 381) led to FDA approval in 2011:<sup>[[11]](#ref11)[[12]](#ref12)</sup>

- OnabotulinumtoxinA 200 U reduced UI episodes by **21.8/week** vs 13.2/week for placebo (p &lt; 0.001).<sup>[[12]](#ref12)</sup>
- Maximum cystometric capacity (MCC) increased by **141 mL** (200 U) vs placebo (p &lt; 0.001).<sup>[[13]](#ref13)</sup>
- Maximum detrusor pressure (MDP) decreased by **~33 cm H₂O** (p &lt; 0.001).<sup>[[13]](#ref13)</sup>
- Median time to retreatment request: **256 days** (200 U) vs 92 days (placebo).<sup>[[11]](#ref11)</sup>
- I-QOL scores improved significantly across all domains.<sup>[[11]](#ref11)[[12]](#ref12)</sup>

### OAB — Phase 3 Trial (2013)

A 557-patient RCT led to FDA approval for OAB in 2013:<sup>[[14]](#ref14)[[15]](#ref15)</sup>

- OnabotulinumtoxinA 100 U reduced daily UI episodes by **−2.65** vs −0.87 for placebo (p &lt; 0.001).<sup>[[15]](#ref15)</sup>

## Injection Technique

### Pre-procedure<sup>[[8]](#ref8)[[17]](#ref17)[[18]](#ref18)[[19]](#ref19)</sup>

- Measure **post-void residual (PVR)** — caution if PVR &gt; 100–200 mL.
- Counsel regarding risk of urinary retention and potential need for clean intermittent catheterization (CIC).
- Confirm willingness and ability to perform CIC if needed.
- Pre-treatment antibiotics may be administered (practice varies).
- Instill **viscous lidocaine** intravesically ~15 minutes prior to procedure for local anesthesia.<sup>[[17]](#ref17)</sup>

### Procedure<sup>[[9]](#ref9)[[17]](#ref17)[[18]](#ref18)</sup>

- Performed via **rigid or flexible cystoscope** in the office setting (preferred) or operating room.
- Reconstitute onabotulinumtoxinA in **10 mL preservative-free normal saline** for OAB 100 U or 30 mL for NDO 200 U.
- Use a **22–27 gauge needle, 4 mm in length**, with a stopper to prevent perforation.<sup>[[9]](#ref9)</sup>
- Inject into the **detrusor muscle**, distributing across the posterior and lateral bladder walls.
- **Spare the trigone** (standard practice in pivotal trials, though some evidence suggests trigonal injection is safe).<sup>[[9]](#ref9)</sup>
- Expert consensus supports **1–20 injection sites** for OAB, with fewer sites preferred for patient comfort; starting in the lower bladder with slower injection speed improves distribution.<sup>[[17]](#ref17)</sup>
- For NDO, **30 injection sites** at 1 mL each is standard.<sup>[[12]](#ref12)</sup>
- Procedure duration: approximately **15 minutes**.<sup>[[18]](#ref18)</sup>

### Post-procedure

- Follow-up at **7–14 days** to assess for urinary retention.<sup>[[18]](#ref18)</sup>
- PVR measurement at follow-up.

## Duration of Effect and Retreatment

- Onset of action: **1–2 weeks** post-injection.<sup>[[18]](#ref18)</sup>
- Duration of effect: typically **4–10 months** (median ~6–9 months for NDO; ~6 months for OAB).<sup>[[16]](#ref16)[[18]](#ref18)[[20]](#ref20)</sup>
- Retreatment interval: **no sooner than 12 weeks**; expert consensus recommends scheduling at **6-month intervals** with the option to retreat earlier if symptoms return.<sup>[[17]](#ref17)[[18]](#ref18)</sup>
- Patients do **not appear to become refractory** to repeat injections over multiple cycles, though one retrospective study suggested possible declining duration after the **5th treatment cycle** (from ~10 months to ~5.5 months).<sup>[[16]](#ref16)[[21]](#ref21)[[22]](#ref22)</sup>
- Total onabotulinumtoxinA dose should not exceed **360 U in a 3-month period** across all indications (including cosmetic, neurologic, etc.).<sup>[[18]](#ref18)</sup>

## Adverse Effects

The adverse effect profile is primarily **localized to the lower urinary tract**:<sup>[[13]](#ref13)[[14]](#ref14)[[19]](#ref19)[[23]](#ref23)</sup>

**Common**

- **Urinary tract infection** — most frequent AE; RR 1.47 (95% CI 1.29–1.67) vs placebo for NDO; 5.4–9.3% for OAB.<sup>[[13]](#ref13)[[14]](#ref14)[[24]](#ref24)</sup>
- **Urinary retention** — RR 5.58 (95% CI 3.53–8.83) vs placebo for NDO; 5.4% for OAB 100 U; de novo CIC rates of **35–42%** for NDO (200–300 U) vs 10% placebo; up to **42%** de novo self-catheterization reported in neurological patients.<sup>[[3]](#ref3)[[11]](#ref11)[[12]](#ref12)[[13]](#ref13)[[14]](#ref14)</sup>
- **Dysuria** — common in OAB patients.<sup>[[14]](#ref14)</sup>
- **Hematuria** — RR 1.70 (95% CI 1.01–2.85).<sup>[[13]](#ref13)</sup>

**Less Common**

- **Muscle weakness** — RR 2.59 (95% CI 1.36–4.91), reflecting systemic spread of toxin.<sup>[[13]](#ref13)</sup>
- **Transient body weakness**.<sup>[[19]](#ref19)</sup>

**Male-Specific Considerations**

In male patients with idiopathic OAB, treatment response was **62.3%**, with urinary retention in **13%**. A higher bladder outlet obstruction index predicted both lower response rates and higher complication rates.<sup>[[25]](#ref25)</sup>

## Comparison with Other Third-Line Therapies

The 2024 AUA/SUFU OAB Guideline recommends **sacral neuromodulation (SNM), tibial nerve stimulation (PTNS), and intradetrusor BTX** as equivalent third-line options with no treatment hierarchy, selected through shared decision-making — a positioning echoed by the 2023 EAU female non-neurogenic LUTS guidelines panel review.<sup>[[8]](#ref8)[[26]](#ref26)</sup>

| Feature | OnabotulinumtoxinA | Sacral Neuromodulation | PTNS | References |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Efficacy (UUI reduction) | Superior to placebo, comparable to SNM | Comparable to BTX; higher complete-resolution rate | Effective but less data | <sup>[[8]](#ref8)[[27]](#ref27)[[28]](#ref28)</sup> |
| UTI risk | Higher (24% vs 10% SNM) | Lower | Lowest | <sup>[[28]](#ref28)[[29]](#ref29)</sup> |
| Urinary retention risk | 5.4% (OAB); 35–42% (NDO) | Minimal | None | <sup>[[11]](#ref11)[[14]](#ref14)</sup> |
| Reversibility | Fully reversible (wears off) | Reversible (device removal) | Reversible | <sup>[[7]](#ref7)</sup> |
| Cost (1-year) | **$2,896** | **$15,343** | Variable | <sup>[[29]](#ref29)</sup> |
| Re-intervention rate | Repeat injections q6 months | 15.8% at 1 year; 26.1% at 3 years | Weekly office visits | <sup>[[29]](#ref29)</sup> |
| Patient satisfaction | Higher satisfaction rates | Comparable | Comparable | <sup>[[27]](#ref27)[[28]](#ref28)</sup> |

A 2025 meta-analysis found that BTX achieved a higher rate of **≥ 75% UUI reduction** compared with both placebo and SNM (p &lt; 0.001).<sup>[[27]](#ref27)</sup>

## Off-Label / Emerging Indications

### Interstitial Cystitis / Bladder Pain Syndrome

The AUA guideline lists intradetrusor onabotulinumtoxinA as a treatment option for [IC/BPS](/docs/clinical-conditions/03h-pelvic-pain/ic-pbs) when other treatments have failed, though evidence is Grade C.<sup>[[30]](#ref30)</sup> A meta-analysis of 12 RCTs demonstrated significant improvements in ICSI, ICPI, VAS pain scores, and daytime frequency, with small-to-medium effect sizes.<sup>[[31]](#ref31)</sup> Injection sites vary across studies (trigonal vs lateral / posterior walls), and the true effect is difficult to determine given the dearth of placebo-controlled trials of BTX alone.<sup>[[30]](#ref30)</sup>

### Detrusor Sphincter Dyssynergia (DSD)

Injection of onabotulinumtoxinA into the **external urethral sphincter** (not intradetrusor) has been used for SCI patients with DSD, though this remains unlicensed.<sup>[[4]](#ref4)[[7]](#ref7)[[32]](#ref32)</sup>

### Other Investigational Uses

- Dysfunctional voiding (non-neurogenic).<sup>[[32]](#ref32)</sup>
- Benign prostatic hyperplasia — meta-analyses show **no benefit** over placebo for LUTS.<sup>[[32]](#ref32)[[33]](#ref33)</sup>
- Chronic prostatitis / pelvic pain (intraprostatic injection — emerging data).<sup>[[32]](#ref32)</sup>
- Novel delivery systems including **liposome-encapsulated** and **hydrogel intravesical instillation** are under investigation as less invasive alternatives to cystoscopic injection.<sup>[[32]](#ref32)</sup>

## Contraindications and Special Populations

### Absolute Contraindications<sup>[[18]](#ref18)[[19]](#ref19)</sup>

- Active UTI at time of injection.
- Acute urinary retention (without catheterization).
- Known hypersensitivity to botulinum toxin or its components.

### Relative Contraindications

- Elevated PVR (&gt; 100–200 mL) — increased risk of retention.<sup>[[8]](#ref8)</sup>
- Inability or unwillingness to perform CIC.<sup>[[18]](#ref18)[[30]](#ref30)</sup>
- Impaired bladder emptying.<sup>[[30]](#ref30)</sup>

### Anticoagulation / Antiplatelet Therapy

Two retrospective studies (totaling &gt; 1,400 injection episodes) found **no clinically significant bleeding** in patients on antithrombotic therapy. In a cohort of 400 patients with 1,059 treatments, **zero cases of hematuria** were reported despite 27.8% being on antithrombotics with no discontinuation.<sup>[[34]](#ref34)</sup> A second study of 114 injection episodes on full-dose anticoagulation / antiplatelet therapy reported only **0.88%** spontaneously resolving hematuria.<sup>[[35]](#ref35)</sup> Intradetrusor BTX may therefore be classified as a **low-risk bleeding procedure** not requiring discontinuation of antithrombotic therapy.<sup>[[34]](#ref34)[[35]](#ref35)</sup>

### Pregnancy

A 29-year cumulative safety analysis of 913 pregnancies exposed to onabotulinumtoxinA (all indications) found a major fetal defect prevalence of **0.7%** (1/152 live births), consistent with background population rates of 3–6%. Most exposures (94.6%) occurred before conception or during the first trimester.<sup>[[36]](#ref36)</sup> Data remain limited, however, and onabotulinumtoxinA is not formally recommended during pregnancy.<sup>[[36]](#ref36)[[37]](#ref37)</sup>

## Practical Pearls

- **Office-based procedure** is preferred over hospital / surgery center for OAB injections.<sup>[[17]](#ref17)</sup>
- Staff involvement from scheduling through post-procedure follow-up optimizes patient experience and retreatment compliance.<sup>[[17]](#ref17)</sup>
- Patients should be counseled that the procedure is **temporary and requires repeat treatments** — setting expectations early improves long-term adherence.<sup>[[17]](#ref17)</sup>
- It is reasonable to **bypass antimuscarinics** and proceed directly to BTX in patients who cannot take or do not wish to try oral medications.<sup>[[8]](#ref8)</sup>
- For NDO patients already performing CIC, the risk of urinary retention is less clinically significant.<sup>[[11]](#ref11)</sup>
- Cost-effectiveness analyses suggest onabotulinumtoxinA is **cost-effective compared with best supportive care** for both OAB and NDO.<sup>[[29]](#ref29)</sup>
