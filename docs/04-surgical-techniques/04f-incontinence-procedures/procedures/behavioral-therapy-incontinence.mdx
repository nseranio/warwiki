# Behavioral Therapy for Urinary Incontinence

**Behavioral therapy** is a low-risk, low-cost first-line conservative treatment for overactive bladder (OAB), urgency urinary incontinence (UUI) and mixed urinary incontinence, and its lifestyle components also apply to stress urinary incontinence (SUI). The 2024 AUA/SUFU OAB guideline recommends that clinicians offer bladder training to all patients with OAB (Strong Recommendation; Evidence Level: Grade A) and offer behavioral therapies to all patients with OAB (Clinical Principle). It also recommends discussing incontinence management strategies such as pads and barrier creams with all patients who have urgency urinary incontinence (Expert Opinion).<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup> For SUI, the 2023 AUA/SUFU guideline directs counseling on observation, pelvic floor muscle training with or without biofeedback, and non-surgical options such as a continence pessary before surgery (Clinical Principle).<sup>[[15]](#ref15)</sup> Behavioral treatment is inexpensive, complements pharmacotherapy and procedures, and targets triggers that drugs do not address.

**Phenotype-specific emphasis.** Bladder training and urge suppression have the strongest evidence in OAB and UUI. In SUI the main components are weight loss, fluid and caffeine management and constipation control, and bladder training contributes less directly. The components below are used selectively by phenotype.

## Practice Guideline Position

The AUA/SUFU guideline separates behavioral therapy from broader non-invasive therapies.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup>

| Category | Examples | Practical interpretation |
|---|---|---|
| **Behavioral therapies** | Bladder training, fluid management, caffeine reduction, physical activity / exercise, dietary modification, mindfulness | Offer to all patients; few adverse effects; success depends on acceptance, coaching and adherence |
| **Select non-invasive therapies** | Pelvic floor muscle therapy, transcutaneous tibial nerve stimulation, transvaginal electrical stimulation, yoga | May offer; safety is favorable, but efficacy and evidence certainty vary by modality |
| **UUI management strategies** | Pads, briefs, barrier creams, skin care, bedside commode / urinal access | Discuss with all patients with leakage; these improve dignity and skin safety but are not disease-modifying |

Bladder training has the strongest evidence base of the behavioral therapies.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[2]](#ref2)</sup> PFMT overlaps with behavioral care when the goal is urge suppression and learned detrusor inhibition. It is best delivered as a formal pelvic-floor intervention when tone, strength, coordination or pain needs assessment.

## Components

Behavioral therapy comprises several components.<sup>[[2]](#ref2)[[3]](#ref3)[[4]](#ref4)</sup>

| Component | How it is delivered | Clinic target |
|---|---|---|
| **Bladder training / bladder drill** | Education, fixed voiding interval, diary review, gradual interval lengthening, positive reinforcement | Individualized, comfortable interval; 3-4 hours can be a goal for some patients, not a universal target |
| **Scheduled voiding / timed voiding** | Voids occur by clock rather than urgency | Frailty, cognitive impairment, caregiver-dependent toileting, high-volume urgency |
| **Urge suppression** | Stop moving, sit or stand still, relax abdomen / thighs / jaw, use rapid pelvic-floor contractions if appropriate, breathe, distract, then walk calmly once urgency fades | Break the urgency-rushing-leak loop |
| **Fluid management** | Avoid large boluses; distribute intake; reduce evening fluid when nocturia is diary-linked | Reduce frequency without causing dehydration |
| **Caffeine / alcohol / dietary trigger trial** | Time-limited elimination and rechallenge based on diary | Identify patient-specific urgency triggers |
| **Constipation management** | Stool softening strategy, fiber / osmotic laxative when needed, defecatory mechanics | Reduce rectal loading and pelvic-floor guarding |
| **Weight loss and activity** | Structured program when BMI is elevated; realistic exercise prescription | Reduce incontinence burden and improve mobility |
| **Mindfulness / CBT-informed strategies** | Urgency exposure, attention shifting, catastrophizing reduction, coping skills | Reduce fear-driven urgency amplification |

### Bladder Training Protocol

The following bladder-training example is adjustable. It is not a fixed protocol for retention, pain or impaired emptying.

1. Start with a 3-day bladder diary to identify the shortest safe interval.
2. Set the initial voiding interval just below the usual urgency interval.
3. Void by the clock while awake; avoid "just in case" voiding outside the plan.
4. When urgency arrives early, use urge suppression until the scheduled time.
5. Increase the interval by about 15 minutes after several successful days.
6. Continue for at least 6 weeks (NICE NG123), reassessing symptoms and safety. An 8-12-week course and a 3-4-hour interval may suit some patients but are not required targets.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[2]](#ref2)[[14]](#ref14)</sup>

Progress is easiest to sustain when the patient can see it in diary data: fewer urgency episodes, fewer leaks, longer intervals and fewer rushed bathroom trips.

### Urge Suppression Script

The patient is taught a short script:

- **Stop.** Do not rush to the toilet.
- **Still.** Sit or stand quietly, because rushing intensifies the urge and the risk of leakage.
- **Soften.** Relax abdomen, gluteals, thighs, shoulders, and jaw.
- **Squeeze only if appropriate.** Use several quick pelvic-floor contractions when the patient can contract without pain or guarding.
- **Shift attention.** Use slow breathing, counting backward, or another distraction until the urgency wave falls.
- **Walk calmly.** Go to the bathroom after the urge recedes.

For patients with pelvic-floor tenderness, pain, or guarding, route to [Pelvic Floor Physical Therapy](/docs/surgical-techniques/04f-incontinence-procedures/procedures/pelvic-floor-pt); repeated strengthening can worsen a high-tone phenotype.

## Evidence Snapshot

### Behavioral therapy vs. oxybutynin

In a randomized trial of 197 older women with urge or mixed incontinence, Burgio and colleagues compared biofeedback-assisted behavioral training, oxybutynin and placebo. Behavioral therapy reduced incontinence episodes by a mean 80.7%, compared with 68.5% with oxybutynin (P = .04, behavioral vs drug) and 39.4% with placebo.<sup>[[7]](#ref7)</sup>

A urodynamic companion analysis of 105 women reported mean reductions in incontinence frequency of 82.3% with behavioral training, 78.3% with oxybutynin and 51.5% with placebo. Bladder capacity rose by 68.9 mL with oxybutynin and 17.3 mL with behavioral training. Volume at strong desire to void rose by 69.9 mL with oxybutynin and 40.5 mL with behavioral training.<sup>[[6]](#ref6)</sup>

### Systematic reviews

| Evidence source | Finding |
|---|---|
| **Balk 2019 network meta-analysis** | For urgency UI, behavioral therapy was significantly more effective than anticholinergics for cure or improvement (high strength of evidence)<sup>[[8]](#ref8)</sup> |
| **Cochrane bladder training 2023** | Bladder training may be more effective than anticholinergics for cure or improvement (RR 1.37, 95% CI 1.10 to 1.70; 4 studies, 258 participants) with fewer adverse events; certainty was low to very low<sup>[[2]](#ref2)</sup> |
| **Cochrane conservative-interventions overview 2022** | For urgency UI, PFMT with feedback or biofeedback, electrical stimulation and bladder training were each more beneficial than control for cure or improvement (moderate to high certainty). Across all UI types, PFMT with bladder training was more beneficial than bladder training alone<sup>[[9]](#ref9)</sup> |

## Combining Behavioral and Drug Therapy

Behavioral therapy can be used as the first step, as a combination partner or as a rescue strategy when medications are stopped because of adverse effects.

| Population | Trial signal | Practical use |
|---|---|---|
| **OAB (75% women): tolterodine + simplified bladder training** | In 501 evaluable patients, median voiding frequency fell 33% with the combination vs 25% with tolterodine alone (P < 0.001); incontinence episodes fell 87% vs 81% (not significant)<sup>[[5]](#ref5)</sup> | Bladder training adds to drug therapy for frequency; the incontinence benefit was not shown |
| **Women: optimized oxybutynin ± behavioral therapy** | In 64 women, incontinence episodes fell 88.5% with drug alone and 78.3% with combined therapy (P = 0.16); the trial stopped early for futility<sup>[[10]](#ref10)</sup> | Optimized pharmacotherapy narrows the additive margin |
| **Men: behavioral vs drug vs combination** | At 6 weeks, combined therapy reduced voiding frequency more than drug therapy alone, but not more than behavioral therapy alone<sup>[[4]](#ref4)</sup> | A stepped strategy beginning with behavioral therapy alone is reasonable |

Medication partners are covered in [anticholinergic / antimuscarinic agents](/docs/foundations/pharmacology/storage-oab/anticholinergics), [β3-adrenergic receptor agonists](/docs/foundations/pharmacology/storage-oab/beta3-agonists), and the [OAB & UUI treatment database](/docs/surgical-techniques/04f-incontinence-procedures/oab-uui/oab-uui-database).

## CBT, Mindfulness, and Weight Loss

Structured cognitive behavioral therapy (CBT) is an emerging adjunct for urgency incontinence. A systematic review of 12 papers reported a high level of evidence for improved symptom severity and a moderate level for quality-of-life, psychological and satisfaction outcomes; effects on clinical signs were conflicting.<sup>[[11]](#ref11)</sup> In a 2024 randomized trial of 79 women with moderate-to-severe OAB, a multicomponent intervention incorporating CBT principles and exposure-based bladder training improved the OAB questionnaire quality-of-life score by 23.9 points, vs 11.3 points with a waiting list.<sup>[[12]](#ref12)</sup>

Weight loss is a lifestyle intervention when excess weight contributes to incontinence. In the PRIDE randomized trial of 338 overweight or obese women, a 6-month weight-loss program reduced weekly incontinence episodes by 47%, compared with 28% with a structured education program (P = 0.01). Stress-incontinence episodes fell more in the intervention group (P = 0.02); urge-incontinence episodes did not differ significantly in frequency (P = 0.14).<sup>[[13]](#ref13)</sup>

## Patient Selection and Delivery

Behavioral therapy is broadly appropriate. The delivery model is matched to the patient:

| Patient pattern | Best delivery model |
|---|---|
| Motivated, cognitively intact, mild-moderate OAB | Diary-guided bladder training with nurse / clinician follow-up |
| OAB with pelvic pain, dyspareunia, guarding, constipation, or poor muscle awareness | Pelvic health PT; avoid strengthening-first instructions until phenotype is known |
| Frailty, dementia, limited mobility, caregiver-dependent toileting | Timed voiding, prompted voiding, bedside commode access, clothing simplification, skin care |
| Heavy caffeine / evening fluid / edema-driven nocturia | Diary-driven trigger correction and nocturnal polyuria workup |
| Mixed UI | Pair bladder training and urge suppression with SUI-directed PFMT or SUI procedural counseling |

Adherence limits success, and the AUA/SUFU guideline notes that outcomes depend on patient acceptance and adherence.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup> Follow-up is built around reviewable data: diary intervals, leak counts, urgency scores, pad use, fluid timing, stool pattern and patient-defined goals.

## Operative and Clinic Pearls

- Offer behavioral therapy even when procedural treatment is likely; it improves baseline control and gives patients a non-drug rescue skill.
- Do not label every pelvic-floor intervention "Kegels"; urge suppression, relaxation, and coordination may matter more than strengthening.
- Use a bladder diary to avoid treating nocturnal polyuria, polydipsia, or edema as refractory OAB.
- In UUI, discuss pads, barrier creams and skin care early. Containment is quality-of-life care and does not signal treatment failure.
- If symptoms worsen during bladder training, check PVR, UTI, constipation, pain, and whether the starting interval was too ambitious.
