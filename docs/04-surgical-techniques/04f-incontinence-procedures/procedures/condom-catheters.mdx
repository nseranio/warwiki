# Condom Catheters &amp; External Urine Collection Devices

A condom catheter, also called a **penile sheath**, **external catheter**, **urisheath** or **Texas catheter**, is a non-invasive external collection device that fits over the penile shaft and passively drains spontaneously voided urine into an attached leg or bedside bag.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[2]](#ref2)</sup> It is distinct from a [penile clamp](/docs/surgical-techniques/04f-incontinence-procedures/procedures/penile-clamp), which mechanically occludes the urethra, the condom catheter does not drain the bladder; it simply collects urine as it exits the meatus. Both belong to the broader class of **containment or collection devices** for male urinary incontinence (alongside absorbent pads and body-worn urinals).

> Condom catheters contain leakage and do not treat its cause. They can bridge recovery, substitute for an indwelling collection device in selected men without retention or obstruction, or provide a chosen long-term containment option; [sling](/docs/surgical-techniques/04f-incontinence-procedures/procedures/male-urethral-slings) and [AUS](/docs/surgical-techniques/04f-incontinence-procedures/procedures/artificial-urinary-sphincter) are separate surgical options when appropriate.

---

## Mechanism and Design

A flexible sheath (latex or silicone) is rolled onto the penis and secured with an adhesive strip, self-adhesive inner lining, or external strap. The distal end connects to drainage tubing and a collection bag.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[2]](#ref2)</sup> Available types:

- **Latex sheaths.** Least expensive; contraindicated in latex allergy.
- **Silicone sheaths.** The default for latex-allergic patients.
- **Self-adhesive designs** (e.g., Conveen Optima). They have an integral adhesive in place of a separate strip. Fit, skin inspection and product-specific removal instructions still matter.<sup>[[3]](#ref3)[[4]](#ref4)</sup>

---

## Indications

- **Urinary incontinence with effective bladder emptying**, without retention or outlet obstruction. An AAFP review lists incontinence with a PVR &lt; 300 mL as an indication. The threshold is not a universally safe boundary. The clinical context and PVR trend are assessed.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup>
- **Hospital settings.** Accurate urine-volume measurement, non-sterile diagnostic urine sampling, fall prevention in high-risk patients, comfort in hospice or palliative care.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup>
- **Indwelling-catheter avoidance.** Consider an external device for cooperative hospitalized men who have no urinary retention or outlet obstruction requiring bladder drainage. CDC rates the comparative evidence low quality; the benefit in the small RCT concerned a composite outcome and did not prove reduction in every individual CAUTI outcome.<sup>[[5]](#ref5)[[18]](#ref18)</sup>
- **Selected neurogenic bladder / SCI patients** who empty spontaneously or use a clinically assessed reflex-voiding program; PVR, storage pressures and upper-tract risk are evaluated as indicated. The sheath does not make voiding safe.<sup>[[7]](#ref7)[[8]](#ref8)</sup> The [AUA/SUFU NLUTD guideline](https://www.auanet.org/documents/Guidelines/PDF/NLUTD.pdf) recommends [intermittent catheterization](/docs/foundations/tools/biomaterials/urinary-catheters/intermittent-catheter) over indwelling catheters to facilitate bladder emptying (Strong Recommendation; Evidence Level: Grade C). An external sheath collects urine and cannot replace needed emptying treatment.<sup>[[9]](#ref9)</sup>

The Ann Arbor Criteria (RAND/UCLA Appropriateness Method) rated 30 of 97 external-catheter scenarios as appropriate, 51 as inappropriate, and 16 as uncertain, recognizing that condom catheters can be pragmatically appropriate in select patients but should not be used reflexively.<sup>[[10]](#ref10)</sup>

---

## Contraindications

- **Urinary retention or clinically significant incomplete emptying.** A condom catheter does not drain the bladder. The 300-mL PVR cutoff in one general-practice review is not a universal safety threshold.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup>
- Significant **bladder outlet obstruction** (e.g., severe BPH).
- **Latex or adhesive allergy.**
- Significantly **retracted penis** (the sheath cannot be retained); consider a body-worn urinal (BWU) instead.<sup>[[11]](#ref11)</sup>
- Compromised **penile skin integrity** (lichen sclerosus, balanitis, post-circumcision wounds, prior radiation skin injury).

## Comparative Evidence

### Condom catheter vs indwelling urethral catheter

The randomized evidence is a single small trial, the Saint 2006 JAGS RCT (n = 75 hospitalized men ≥ 40 yr, unblinded):<sup>[[5]](#ref5)</sup>

- Adverse-outcome incidence 70 versus 131 per 1,000 patient-days (P = 0.07; adjusted P = 0.04).
- In men without dementia, indwelling catheter was associated with approximately 5× higher composite of bacteriuria, symptomatic UTI, or death (HR 4.84, 95% CI 1.46–16.02, P = 0.01).
- Patients reported condom catheters as more comfortable (P = 0.02) and less painful (P = 0.02).

The Saint 2019 prospective cohort (36 condom-catheter and 44 indwelling-catheter users, followed one month) found comparable rates of at least one catheter-related complication (80.6% versus 88.6%, P = .32) and fewer complications during placement with condom catheters (13.9% versus 43.2%, P < .001).<sup>[[13]](#ref13)</sup>

### Condom catheter vs absorbent pads

The Chartier-Kastler 2011 randomized crossover (n = 61 men with moderate-to-severe UI) compared Conveen Optima urisheaths to usual absorbent products:<sup>[[3]](#ref3)</sup>

- **69% preferred urisheaths** over their usual absorbent product (P = 0.002).
- All King's Health Questionnaire dimensions improved with urisheath; greatest reductions were Limitations of Daily Activities (−10.24, P = 0.01) and Incontinence Impact (−7.05, P = 0.045).
- Urisheaths scored higher for efficacy, self-image, odor management, discretion, and skin integrity, but not ease of use.

### 4-arm device trial (sheath / pad / clamp / BWU)

The Macaulay 2015 BJU trial (n = 56 men with post-prostatectomy incontinence) compared all four containment options head-to-head:<sup>[[11]](#ref11)</sup>

| Device | Best for | Strengths | Limitations |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Condom catheter (sheath)** | Extended activities (golf, travel) | Keeps skin dry; low odor; convenient for storage / travel | Requires adequate penile length; dislodgment |
| **Absorbent pad** | Everyday activities, nighttime | Easiest to use; most comfortable when dry | Most likely to leak; uncomfortable when wet |
| **[Penile clamp](/docs/surgical-techniques/04f-incontinence-procedures/procedures/penile-clamp)** | Short vigorous activities (swim, exercise) | Most secure; least likely to leak; most discreet | Painful or uncomfortable for almost all men |
| **Body-worn urinal** | Alternative when penis is retracted | Used when sheath cannot be retained | Poor for seated activities; rated worse than sheath |

Most men preferred pads at night. Around two-thirds were using a combination of pads and devices after testing, and most preferred a mixture of pads and devices during the day.<sup>[[11]](#ref11)</sup>

## Complications

### Infectious

- **Bacteriuria** can occur with prolonged collection. Cohort rates vary by setting and manipulation. No single monthly percentage applies to all users.<sup>[[6]](#ref6)</sup>
- **Long-term geriatric use** (mean 35 mo) was associated with UTI, particularly with Proteus and Providencia organisms, in a retrospective series of 64 patients.<sup>[[14]](#ref14)</sup>
- **Continuous nursing-home use.** In a prospective four-cohort study (30 continuous users), at least one episode of bacteriuria occurred in 87% and at least one UTI in 40% (0.08 episodes per patient-month), more than in continent men or incontinent men without an external catheter. Night-only use (19 men) had an intermediate frequency. The cohorts also differed in clinical and functional status.<sup>[[15]](#ref15)</sup>

### Non-infectious

- **Leaking, pain and discomfort.** Noninfectious complications were more common than infectious ones in both groups of the 2019 cohort.<sup>[[13]](#ref13)</sup>
- **Skin irritation or breakdown.** Adhesive contact, moisture and improper sizing contribute, and skin care is part of prevention.<sup>[[4]](#ref4)</sup>
- **Dislodgment.** The leading practical limitation reported by patients and nursing staff.<sup>[[16]](#ref16)</sup>
- **Leaking.** Frequently reported, especially with poor fit.<sup>[[16]](#ref16)</sup>
- **Penile strangulation and necrosis.** Only a few cases have been reported. A case report attributes it to incorrect sheath size or poor care in a debilitated patient; patients who cannot report discomfort (cognitive impairment, sensory neuropathy, SCI) are at particular risk.<sup>[[12]](#ref12)</sup>

In the 2019 cohort, patients reported approximately three times more noninfectious complications than were recorded in the medical record, which suggests under-recognition.<sup>[[13]](#ref13)</sup>

## Patient and Nursing Satisfaction

Saint 1999 (n = 104 patients + 99 nurses):<sup>[[16]](#ref16)</sup>

- In this convenience-sample survey, condom-catheter users were significantly more likely to report comfort (86% versus 58%, P = 0.04), less pain (14% versus 48%, P = 0.008), and less activity restriction (24% versus 61%, P = 0.002) than indwelling-catheter users.
- Nursing staff believed condom catheters were less painful and easier to apply, but also believed they fell off and leaked more often and required more nursing time.
- The authors concluded that a more secure sheath would greatly improve male incontinence management.

---

## Patient-Awareness Gap

A 2026 survey (Menzel et al.) of 99 men using at least 2 pads daily after prostatectomy found that 86% were unaware of condom catheters and penile clamps. Pads were the main aid (97%), condom catheters were used by 12% and clamps by 2%.<sup>[[17]](#ref17)</sup> Urologists were the main information source (88%).

---

## Practical Considerations

- **Sizing.** An oversized sheath dislodges, and an incorrectly sized sheath has been linked to strangulation.<sup>[[12]](#ref12)</sup>
- **Change according to the specific product instructions** and inspect penile skin for erythema, abrasion, or breakdown. For example, Coloplast's Conveen Optima fitting guide states single use and no longer than 24 hours; this does not establish one schedule for every model.<sup>[[4]](#ref4)[[12]](#ref12)[[19]](#ref19)</sup>
- Skin care and proactive infection control are needed.<sup>[[4]](#ref4)</sup>
- Counsel about expected dislodgment rates and have spare sheaths available.
- In neurogenic patients with impaired sensation, use extra caution. Compression injury and skin breakdown may not be reported reliably.<sup>[[12]](#ref12)</sup>

## Containment-Device Comparison

The [penile clamp](/docs/surgical-techniques/04f-incontinence-procedures/procedures/penile-clamp) and the condom catheter both belong to the conservative or containment tier of the male SUI ladder. Neither replaces the [male sling](/docs/surgical-techniques/04f-incontinence-procedures/procedures/male-urethral-slings) or [AUS](/docs/surgical-techniques/04f-incontinence-procedures/procedures/artificial-urinary-sphincter) for the surgical candidate. They differ on the axes below.

| Feature | [Penile clamp](/docs/surgical-techniques/04f-incontinence-procedures/procedures/penile-clamp) | Condom catheter |
|---|---|---|
| Mechanism | Occludes urethra via compression | Passively collects voided urine |
| Best use | Short vigorous activities (swim, exercise) | Extended activities (golf, travel), hospital |
| Leak protection | Most secure of all containment options | Good; dislodgment occurs |
| Comfort | Painful for most men; cannot wear all day | Generally well tolerated; more comfortable than indwelling |
| Discretion | Most discreet (no bag) | Requires leg bag |
| Skin / vascular risk | Pressure ischemia; reduced penile blood flow (Cunningham) | Skin irritation, rare strangulation if improperly sized |
| Infection risk | No collection system; skin/pressure concerns remain | Bacteriuria and UTI depend on duration, population and handling; an external sheath is not sterile bladder drainage |
| Patient selection | Cognitively intact, intact sensation, dexterity | Appropriate fit; effective emptying without retention/obstruction; PVR interpreted in context |
| Patient preference | Preferred for specific activities only | Preferred over pads by 69% (Chartier-Kastler 2011); preferred over indwelling (Saint 2006) |

In the Macaulay trial, about two-thirds of men were using a combination of pads and devices after testing.<sup>[[11]](#ref11)</sup>
