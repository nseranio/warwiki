# Pelvic Floor Physical Therapy

Pelvic floor physical therapy (PFPT) is the cornerstone first-line intervention for stress urinary incontinence, urgency urinary incontinence, overactive bladder, and pelvic organ prolapse across male and female patients. A specialized physiotherapist directs pelvic floor muscle training (PFMT), urge suppression strategies, and behavioral modification — with or without adjunct biofeedback or electrical stimulation. PFPT reduces incontinence episode frequency, improves quality of life, and delays or eliminates the need for pharmacotherapy or surgery.

---

## Indications

- **Stress urinary incontinence (male):** post-prostatectomy SUI — best initiated preoperatively and continued postoperatively; most effective for mild-to-moderate severity
- **Stress urinary incontinence (female):** hypermobility-predominant SUI; adjunct to and concurrent with surgical planning
- **Urgency urinary incontinence / OAB:** bladder training with urge suppression techniques; comparable to anticholinergic therapy in RCTs
- **Mixed incontinence:** addresses both urgency and stress components
- **Pelvic organ prolapse:** symptom management and post-repair rehabilitation
- **Post-pelvic surgery rehabilitation:** optimization before and after incontinence procedures or pelvic reconstructive surgery

## Technique Overview

### Assessment

A specialized pelvic floor physiotherapist performs:
- Internal (vaginal or rectal) and external palpation to assess resting tone, voluntary contraction, endurance, and coordination
- Identification of hypertonicity, hypotonicity, or dysynergia patterns
- Voiding diary review and functional assessment

### Pelvic Floor Muscle Training (PFMT)

- Targeted voluntary contraction and relaxation of the levator ani, pubococcygeus, and external urethral sphincter
- Protocol: 3 sets of 8–12 maximal contractions per day; endurance sets (10-second holds) combined with fast-twitch sets (1-second flicks)
- Preoperative PFMT (≥4 weeks before prostatectomy) reduces time to continence recovery
- Home exercise program reinforced by structured clinic visits (typically 6–12 sessions over 3 months)

### Urge Suppression (for OAB/UUI)

- Freeze-and-squeeze technique: rapid pelvic floor contractions at onset of urgency to inhibit detrusor contraction via pudendal-sacral reflex arc
- Bladder training: progressive voiding interval extension (by 15–30 min per week) to expand functional capacity
- Distraction and relaxation strategies

### Adjunct Modalities

- **Biofeedback:** surface EMG or manometric feedback to improve motor learning; beneficial for patients unable to identify the correct muscles
- **Electrical stimulation:** neuromodulatory current delivered via intravaginal or intrarectal probe; improves muscle recruitment in severely weakened pelvic floors and reduces urgency in OAB
- **Magnetic stimulation (chair-based):** non-invasive extracorporeal option for patients unable to tolerate internal probes

## Outcomes & Evidence

| Population | Outcome |
|---|---|
| Post-prostatectomy SUI | Cochrane review: PFMT accelerates return to continence; 1 fewer pad/day at 3 months vs. control |
| Female SUI | Cochrane review: PFMT participants 8× more likely to report cure vs. no treatment |
| OAB/UUI | PFMT comparable to anticholinergics for urgency episode reduction; superior tolerability |
| Mixed incontinence | Improvement in both stress and urgency components |

Success rates for mild-to-moderate SUI: 40–60% significant improvement; 20–30% cure with supervised PFMT programs.

## Pearls

- Supervised PFMT is significantly more effective than unsupervised home exercises alone
- Biofeedback adds measurable benefit in patients with poor kinesthetic awareness of the pelvic floor
- PFPT outcomes are durable — 5-year follow-up data supports maintained improvement in motivated patients
- PFPT should be offered and documented before surgical intervention for SUI (AUA guidelines requirement)
