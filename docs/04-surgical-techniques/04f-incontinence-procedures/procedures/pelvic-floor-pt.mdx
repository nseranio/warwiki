# Pelvic Floor Physical Therapy

Pelvic floor physical therapy (PFPT) is a first-line conservative treatment for many pelvic-floor disorders, including urinary or fecal incontinence, prolapse and pelvic pain; in reconstructive and survivorship care, its role depends on the condition treated. It includes pelvic floor muscle training (PFMT), coordination training, relaxation training, myofascial or manual therapy, biofeedback, electrical stimulation, dilators, bladder and bowel retraining, and education.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[2]](#ref2)[[3]](#ref3)</sup>

Treatment starts with phenotyping the pelvic floor:

| Pelvic-floor phenotype | Treatment goal | Example conditions |
|---|---|---|
| **Hypotonic / under-recruited pelvic floor** | Strength, endurance, power, timing, and "the Knack" before cough / lift | Female SUI, post-prostatectomy SUI, prolapse support, fecal incontinence |
| **Hypertonic / overactive pelvic floor** | Down-training, relaxation, trigger-point release, scar mobilization, graded exposure | IC/BPS with pelvic-floor tenderness, vulvodynia, vaginismus, dyspareunia, chronic pelvic pain |
| **Dyssynergic pelvic floor** | Coordination and learned relaxation during voiding or defecation | Dysfunctional voiding, defecatory dysfunction, pelvic-floor outlet obstruction |

Kegel-type strengthening does not suit every patient. In IC/BPS with pelvic-floor tenderness, pelvic-floor strengthening can worsen symptoms. The AUA IC/BPS guideline states that manual physical therapy should be offered to patients with pelvic-floor tenderness, if appropriately trained clinicians are available, and that pelvic floor strengthening exercises should be avoided (Standard; Evidence Strength Grade A).<sup>[[4]](#ref4)</sup>

See also: [Female SUI](/docs/clinical-conditions/03a-storage-incontinence/sui-female), [Male SUI](/docs/clinical-conditions/03a-storage-incontinence/sui-male), [OAB & Urgency Urinary Incontinence](/docs/clinical-conditions/03a-storage-incontinence/urgency-incontinence-oab), [Pelvic Organ Prolapse](/docs/clinical-conditions/03c-pelvic-support/pelvic-organ-prolapse), [IC/BPS](/docs/clinical-conditions/03h-pelvic-pain/ic-pbs), and [Fecal Incontinence](/docs/clinical-conditions/03i-defecatory-disorders/fecal-incontinence).

## Core Components

PFPT is more than an exercise sheet. A pelvic health therapist first identifies tone, strength, coordination, pain, scar restriction, bladder and bowel habits, and functional triggers, then builds a targeted program.

| Modality | Role |
|---|---|
| **Pelvic floor muscle training (PFMT)** | Repeated voluntary contraction and relaxation; improves strength, endurance, quick recruitment, and timing<sup>[[3]](#ref3)[[5]](#ref5)</sup> |
| **The Knack** | Pre-contraction before cough, sneeze, lift, or position change to reinforce urethral closure<sup>[[59]](#ref59)</sup> |
| **Biofeedback** | EMG, manometry, ultrasound, or pressure feedback to teach correct contraction, relaxation, and coordination<sup>[[6]](#ref6)</sup> |
| **Electrical stimulation** | Vaginal, rectal, surface, or perineal stimulation to assist recruitment or modulate urgency; adjunct, not a replacement for retraining<sup>[[7]](#ref7)</sup> |
| **Manual therapy** | Internal and external myofascial release, trigger-point pressure, scar mobilization, connective-tissue mobilization<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[4]](#ref4)[[8]](#ref8)</sup> |
| **Vaginal dilators / graded exposure** | Introital narrowing, vaginismus, vestibulodynia, post-radiation or post-surgical scar sensitivity<sup>[[9]](#ref9)</sup> |
| **Bladder and bowel retraining** | Timed voiding, urge suppression, defecatory mechanics, stool form optimization, dietary triggers<sup>[[10]](#ref10)</sup> |
| **Education** | Anatomy, symptom triggers, home program, pacing, sexual positioning, postoperative expectations |

For women with stress urinary incontinence whose examination calls for strengthening, no single PFMT regimen has been established as best; NICE recommends at least 8 contractions performed three times a day, and the prescription is set from examination findings.<sup>[[5]](#ref5)</sup>

## Biofeedback: When It Helps and When It Doesn't

Biofeedback (EMG, manometry, ultrasound, or pressure) is an adjunctive teaching tool. It is most useful when the patient cannot identify or correctly contract the pelvic floor through verbal instruction alone. The table below shows where it has added value and where it has not.<sup>[[6]](#ref6)[[29]](#ref29)</sup>

| Setting | Biofeedback benefit vs PFMT alone | Evidence quality |
|---|---|---|
| **Female stress / mixed UI** | The 2025 Cochrane review (33 studies, 3,031 women; chiefly stress or stress-predominant mixed UI) found little or no incontinence-specific QoL difference in 11 studies (1,169 women), a mean 0.29 fewer leakage episodes per 24 hours in 12 studies (932 women), and probably little or no difference in perceived cure or improvement in 14 studies (1,383 women). OPAL (600 randomized; 460 scored at 24 mo) found no meaningful ICIQ-UI SF difference (mean difference −0.09; 95% CI −0.92 to 0.75); it excluded urgency UI alone and women unable to contract | High for QoL; moderate for leakage and perceived cure/improvement<sup>[[6]](#ref6)[[30]](#ref30)</sup> |
| **Female urgency UI / OAB** | In older women with urge UI or urge-predominant mixed UI, 8-week mean incontinence-episode reductions were 63% with biofeedback-assisted behavioral training, 69% with verbal feedback, and 59% with self-help (p = 0.23); a meta-analysis in women with OAB found no statistically significant added QoL or cure-or-improvement benefit from biofeedback-assisted PFMT versus PFMT or bladder training | Moderate<sup>[[34]](#ref34)[[35]](#ref35)</sup> |
| **Early post-prostatectomy UI (0-3 mo)** | Guided biofeedback and/or electrical-stimulation programs improved continence recovery versus PFMT alone at 1–3 mo; a separate EMG-biofeedback meta-analysis found RR 1.78 for continence versus no treatment across follow-up ranging from 3 weeks to 12 months; ES plus biofeedback ranked highest for continence recovery in a network meta-analysis that preferentially used 6-mo results | Low to moderate<sup>[[21]](#ref21)[[31]](#ref31)[[32]](#ref32)[[33]](#ref33)</sup> |
| **Persistent post-prostatectomy UI (≥ 1 yr)** | **No added benefit** over behavioral therapy alone at 8 weeks (Goode 2011 JAMA: weekly incontinence episodes fell 55% with behavioral therapy and 51% with behavioral+BFB+ES vs 24% in controls) | Moderate<sup>[[22]](#ref22)</sup> |
| **Fecal incontinence (after failed conservative Rx)** | In one RCT (n = 45 biofeedback plus exercises; n = 63 exercises alone), 76% versus 41% reported adequate relief three months after training; ACG guidance also endorses biofeedback, and ASCRS notes 64–89% improvement in nonrandomized series | Moderate<sup>[[36]](#ref36)[[37]](#ref37)[[47]](#ref47)</sup> |
| **Dyssynergic defecation** | First-line — anorectal biofeedback focused on pelvic-floor relaxation during defecation outperforms generic PFMT | Moderate<sup>[[36]](#ref36)</sup> |

In women with stress or mixed UI who can learn a correct contraction, the OPAL RCT found no important 24-month benefit from adding EMG biofeedback to supervised PFMT, and the authors advised against routine use.<sup>[[30]](#ref30)</sup> NICE NG123 likewise advises against routine perineometry or pelvic floor EMG biofeedback with PFMT.<sup>[[38]](#ref38)</sup> OPAL does not answer the question for women who are unable to contract or who have urgency UI alone. Biofeedback can be useful for selected fecal incontinence or dyssynergic defecation<sup>[[36]](#ref36)</sup> and may aid early post-prostatectomy training.

## Female Stress Urinary Incontinence

PFMT is the best-supported conservative treatment for female SUI. Cochrane evidence shows women with SUI treated with PFMT are much more likely to report cure than women receiving no treatment or inactive control; at the end of treatment, four SUI trials (165 women) reported cure in 56% versus 6% of controls. The review also found cure or improvement in a pooled “any UI” category, but had no subgroup-specific cure or cure-or-improvement data for urgency-only or mixed UI.<sup>[[11]](#ref11)[[12]](#ref12)</sup>

Practical points:

- Offer at least three months of supervised intensive PFMT as first-line therapy for female SUI or mixed UI before surgery (EAU 2026, Strong), unless the patient declines conservative treatment.<sup>[[50]](#ref50)</sup>
- Supervised visits can verify and teach a correct contraction, especially when a patient cannot isolate the muscles; superiority over home PFMT for incontinence quality of life remains very uncertain.<sup>[[3]](#ref3)[[5]](#ref5)[[13]](#ref13)</sup>
- Group-based PFMT can be noninferior to individual PFMT in older women when professionally delivered, improving access without turning PFPT into unsupervised advice.<sup>[[14]](#ref14)</sup>
- Resistance devices have uncertain added value for incontinence quality of life when added to PFMT in women (three trials, 227 women; very-low-certainty evidence).<sup>[[3]](#ref3)</sup>

## Urgency Urinary Incontinence and OAB

For adults with idiopathic OAB, with or without UUI, AUA/SUFU 2024 recommends bladder training for all patients (Strong Recommendation; Grade A) and allows selected noninvasive therapies, including clinician-delivered PFMT (Clinical Principle).<sup>[[52]](#ref52)</sup> PFPT can teach urge suppression and pelvic-floor contractions that may inhibit detrusor activity.<sup>[[10]](#ref10)[[52]](#ref52)</sup> Patients learn to stop, sit or stand still, relax accessory muscles, perform rapid pelvic-floor contractions if appropriate, breathe, and delay voiding until the urgency wave passes.

PFPT is especially useful when urgency symptoms coexist with pelvic-floor overactivity, fear-driven guarding, constipation, or stress leakage. Evidence is more heterogeneous than for female SUI, but systematic reviews support symptom reduction in frequency and urgency incontinence for selected patients.<sup>[[10]](#ref10)[[15]](#ref15)</sup>

## Pelvic Organ Prolapse

PFPT is first-line for symptomatic stage I-III prolapse when the patient is not seeking immediate surgery. In the POPPY randomized trial, individualized one-to-one PFMT reduced prolapse symptoms at 12 months (POP-SS reduction 3.77 versus 2.09; adjusted difference 1.52, 95% CI 0.46–2.59); 295 of 447 randomized women (66%) completed 12-month questionnaires.<sup>[[17]](#ref17)</sup> The International Consultation chapter also reports improved support by one POP-Q stage in some trials.<sup>[[16]](#ref16)[[17]](#ref17)</sup>

PFMT can relieve symptoms in selected women with stage I–III prolapse. For advanced, ulcerated, obstructive, or highly symptomatic prolapse, assess whether PFMT, a pessary, or surgery best addresses the clinical findings and patient goals.<sup>[[49]](#ref49)</sup> It can improve symptom control, optimize bowel mechanics and support pessary use. Perioperative PFMT has not consistently improved prolapse surgical outcomes; EAU 2026 strongly recommends against offering preoperative PFMT specifically to improve LUTS when pessary treatment or prolapse surgery is indicated.<sup>[[18]](#ref18)[[51]](#ref51)</sup>

## Post-Prostatectomy Incontinence

Pelvic-floor muscle exercise or training is offered immediately after radical prostatectomy (AUA/GURS/SUFU 2024, Moderate Recommendation; Grade B).<sup>[[45]](#ref45)</sup> Evidence for other prostate outlet surgeries is sparse: a 2023 Cochrane review included only one TURP trial and judged the overall value of conservative interventions uncertain.<sup>[[46]](#ref46)</sup> The goal is early, correct recruitment of the striated sphincter and pelvic floor without abdominal bracing or gluteal substitution.

Evidence is heterogeneous, but meta-analyses support supervised, structured, higher-volume PFMT in the first 3-6 months after prostatectomy, while unsupervised exercise performs less consistently.<sup>[[19]](#ref19)[[20]](#ref20)</sup> A post-prostatectomy network meta-analysis ranked electrical stimulation plus biofeedback highest for continence recovery and their combination with PFMT highest for pad weight and ICIQ-UI-SF scores; continence outcomes preferentially used six-month follow-up.<sup>[[21]](#ref21)</sup>

In a 182-man cohort after radical prostatectomy, baseline pad-test leakage was associated with greater absolute pad-weight improvement by six months.<sup>[[24]](#ref24)</sup> A later 182-man analysis from the same center associated higher initial leakage and delayed rehabilitation with worse continence at three months among men incontinent at baseline.<sup>[[23]](#ref23)</sup>

## Orthotopic Neobladder Rehabilitation

Pelvic-floor rehabilitation after orthotopic neobladder differs from post-prostatectomy retraining because voiding relies on abdominal straining with pelvic-floor relaxation rather than sphincter-focused strengthening alone. Johnson and Kirages propose a staged neobladder rehabilitation schedule: preoperative pelvic-floor awareness and coordination training 4–6 weeks before surgery; after catheter removal, at about 3 weeks postoperatively, awareness and sphincter-coordination work paired with timed voiding at intervals of at least 1.5 hours and nighttime voiding alarms every 2 hours; a neuromuscular-reeducation phase over the following 4–6 weeks; and an 8–12-week progression to upright and functional training as healing and performance allow.<sup>[[41]](#ref41)</sup> Thakare et al. describe checking postvoid residuals as voiding intervals increase to confirm neobladder emptying.<sup>[[56]](#ref56)</sup> This is a chapter-author schedule built on exercise physiology and tissue-healing principles, not a neobladder-specific comparative trial; its cited supporting evidence for preoperative training and biofeedback is drawn mainly from radical-prostatectomy populations, and the authors describe its extension to neobladder patients as a hypothesis requiring further study.<sup>[[41]](#ref41)</sup> See [Urinary Diversion Principles](/docs/surgical-techniques/04c-urinary-diversion/urinary-diversion-principles) and the [Modified Studer Pouch](/docs/surgical-techniques/04c-urinary-diversion/modified-studer-pouch) for the continence mechanics and scheduled-emptying framework this program supports.

## IC/BPS and Chronic Pelvic Pain

For men with pelvic-floor or abdominopelvic myalgia, AUA 2025 permits individualized internal and external manual therapy (Statement 29; Conditional Recommendation, Grade C).<sup>[[43]](#ref43)</sup> EMG biofeedback may be used for increased resting tone (Statement 30; Expert Opinion).<sup>[[43]](#ref43)</sup> Pelvic-floor physical therapy may also be offered for CSCP (Statement 36; Expert Opinion).<sup>[[44]](#ref44)</sup>

For IC/BPS with pelvic-floor tenderness, the guideline treatment is manual physical therapy, and strengthening is avoided. Appropriate techniques include trigger-point release, connective-tissue mobilization, scar release, and lengthening of painful muscle contractures.<sup>[[4]](#ref4)</sup>

In the multicenter randomized trial by FitzGerald et al. (n = 81), 59% with myofascial physical therapy and 26% with global therapeutic massage reported moderate or marked overall improvement at 12 weeks on the Global Response Assessment in women with IC/PBS and pelvic-floor tenderness (p = 0.0012).<sup>[[25]](#ref25)</sup> ACOG chronic pelvic pain guidance similarly emphasizes pelvic floor assessment and physical therapy for neuromuscular pain contributors.<sup>[[8]](#ref8)</sup> A 2025 review pooled seven RCTs (476 women with chronic pelvic pain without a defined underlying disease) and found lower pain immediately after multimodal physical therapy than inert or nonconservative care (SMD −1.69, 95% CI −2.54 to −0.85; I² = 92%).<sup>[[57]](#ref57)</sup> EAU 2026 weakly recommends myofascial treatment as first-line therapy for pelvic floor dysfunction in chronic pelvic pain and strongly recommends biofeedback as an adjunct to muscle exercises for anal pain from an overactive pelvic floor.<sup>[[40]](#ref40)</sup> In an RCT of 84 men with primary prostate pain syndrome that EAU cites, biofeedback with pelvic floor relaxation and conventional therapy (alpha blocker, anti-inflammatory drug and sitz baths) both improved symptoms at 3 months, but only the biofeedback group kept the gain 3 months after treatment ended. EAU also cites one trial of biofeedback superior to electrogalvanic stimulation and levator massage for chronic anal pain syndrome in patients with tenderness on traction (87% vs 45% and 22% adequate relief at 1 month).<sup>[[40]](#ref40)</sup>

### Do not miss the phenotype

- Pelvic-floor tenderness, trigger points, dyspareunia, bladder pain flares after intercourse, and urinary frequency with guarded pelvic floor should push toward down-training and manual therapy.
- Weakness, stress leakage, prolapse symptoms, and poor voluntary contraction should push toward strengthening and coordination.
- Many patients have mixed tone and weakness; therapy may sequence relaxation first, then strengthening later.

## Vulvodynia, Vaginismus, and Dyspareunia

PFPT is a core treatment for genito-pelvic pain / penetration disorders when pelvic-floor overactivity, introital guarding, or myofascial pain is present. ACOG supports PFPT for dyspareunia related to pelvic floor dysfunction, and persistent vulvar pain guidance recommends assessment for pelvic floor dysfunction with biofeedback and physical therapy as treatment options.<sup>[[9]](#ref9)[[26]](#ref26)</sup>

In provoked vestibulodynia, a multicenter randomized trial found multimodal physical therapy superior to topical lidocaine for reducing intercourse pain.<sup>[[27]](#ref27)</sup> Typical programs combine education, down-training, manual therapy, graded dilation, sexual pain counseling, and later coordination work.

### Gender-Affirming Care

Intake for a transgender or gender-diverse patient follows the same phenotyping framework used elsewhere on this page, with additions specific to gender-affirming history and anatomy. A practical intake and examination framework has been described: ask the patient's preferred name and pronouns; review prior medical, genital-surgical, and permanent-hair-removal history; and elicit urinary, bowel, sexual, and pain symptoms and their triggers. The accompanying examination adds external lumbopelvic and pelvic-floor assessment and, when appropriate to the patient and anatomy, internal pelvic-floor assessment vaginally or rectally. This is a chapter-author framework, not a validated transgender-specific protocol, and internal examination is offered rather than mandated.<sup>[[42]](#ref42)</sup> In the FLOWER trial, 41 transgender women undergoing vaginoplasty were randomized and 37 were assessed at 12 weeks; no between-group difference was detected in dilation ease or pain, vaginal length, or pelvic-floor symptoms with scheduled PFPT at postoperative weeks 3 and 6 compared with usual care.<sup>[[58]](#ref58)</sup> See [Penile Inversion Vaginoplasty](/docs/surgical-techniques/04k-gender-affirming-surgery/penile-inversion-vaginoplasty) and [Neovaginal Stenosis Management](/docs/surgical-techniques/04k-gender-affirming-surgery/neovaginal-stenosis-management) for dilation-specific PFPT guidance.

## Fecal Incontinence and Defecatory Disorders

For fecal incontinence, ACOG includes pelvic-floor exercises with or without biofeedback and notes that optimal protocols are uncertain; after inadequate response to conservative measures, ACG recommends anorectal biofeedback (strong; moderate-quality evidence) and ASCRS says it may be considered (conditional; low-quality evidence).<sup>[[7]](#ref7)[[36]](#ref36)[[37]](#ref37)</sup> Biofeedback can be especially important when the problem is sensory awareness, squeeze timing, or coordination rather than pure weakness.

For dyssynergic defecation, ACG 2021 strongly recommends instrumented anorectal biofeedback (moderate-quality evidence) to train pelvic-floor relaxation during defecation with coordinated abdominal propulsion.<sup>[[36]](#ref36)</sup> Generic PFMT is not the target therapy. The 2026 Pelvic Floor Consortium consensus recommends PFPT before rectocele repair when obstructed defecation and posterior vaginal wall prolapse coexist with high tone or dyssynergia. For external rectal prolapse (procidentia), it recommends timely surgery first, with preoperative PFPT only if surgery is not delayed and postoperative PFPT for persistent symptoms.<sup>[[39]](#ref39)</sup>
 The 2026 Pelvic Floor Consortium consensus recommends prompt referral to a bowel-trained therapist for obstructed defecation once other pathology is excluded (statement 1), calls biofeedback a best practice, and finds insufficient evidence to favor one biofeedback technique over another (statement 7). It states that 4 to 6 sessions are generally needed before symptoms change (statement 5). These are expert consensus statements.<sup>[[39]](#ref39)</sup>

## Pregnancy and Postpartum

PFPT is commonly used during pregnancy and postpartum for urinary incontinence, fecal incontinence, pelvic girdle or pelvic floor pain, scar sensitivity, and return to activity. Individualized instruction can confirm a correct contraction and tailor exercises, but whether therapist feedback or device biofeedback improves postpartum urinary or anal incontinence more than PFMT without feedback remains uncertain.<sup>[[28]](#ref28)[[54]](#ref54)</sup>

A Cochrane review of PFMT for preventing and treating urinary and fecal incontinence in antenatal and postnatal women found that antenatal PFMT in continent women reduced the risk of urinary incontinence in late pregnancy (62% less; RR 0.38, 95% CI 0.20-0.72; 6 trials, 624 women) and in the mid-postnatal period, more than 3 to 6 months after birth (29% less; RR 0.71, 95% CI 0.54-0.95; 5 trials, 673 women).<sup>[[28]](#ref28)</sup> The 2020 Cochrane review judged PFMT for incontinence already established at delivery uncertain. A 2025 postpartum meta-analysis found lower UI odds at treatment end with PFMT than with control (7 RCTs, n = 1,930; OR 0.63, 95% CI 0.41–0.97; moderate certainty; I² = 72%), but did not isolate cure of established UI.<sup>[[28]](#ref28)[[55]](#ref55)</sup>

Postpartum therapy should be tailored to delivery injury, lactational hypoestrogenism, perineal scars, anal sphincter injury, levator injury, and patient goals for sex, continence, exercise, and future pregnancy.

## Evidence Snapshot

| Condition | Evidence position | Practical recommendation |
|---|---|---|
| **Female SUI** | High-quality evidence for participant-perceived cure with PFMT vs. no treatment / inactive control<sup>[[11]](#ref11)[[12]](#ref12)</sup> | First-line; supervised program preferred |
| **Urgency / mixed UI** | Moderate, heterogeneous evidence<sup>[[10]](#ref10)[[15]](#ref15)</sup> | Combine PFMT, urge suppression, bladder training, and relaxation when tone is high |
| **Pelvic organ prolapse** | High-level conservative-treatment evidence for stages I-III<sup>[[16]](#ref16)[[17]](#ref17)</sup> | Offer before surgery or with pessary; perioperative benefit less certain |
| **Post-prostatectomy UI** | Supervised PFMT may improve early recovery after radical prostatectomy; effects vary by outcome and comparator<sup>[[19]](#ref19)[[20]](#ref20)</sup> | Start early; confirm correct contraction; add biofeedback or stimulation selectively |
| **IC/BPS with pelvic-floor tenderness** | AUA Standard, Evidence Strength Grade A, for manual therapy<sup>[[4]](#ref4)</sup> | Manual therapy; avoid Kegels |
| **Vestibulodynia / dyspareunia** | RCT and guideline support<sup>[[9]](#ref9)[[27]](#ref27)</sup> | Multimodal down-training, manual therapy, dilation, and education |
| **Fecal incontinence** | ASCRS 2023 recommends dietary and medical care first (Strong; Low-certainty evidence) and considers biofeedback after inadequate response (Conditional; Low-certainty evidence)<sup>[[37]](#ref37)</sup> | Optimize stool consistency first; consider pelvic-floor exercises with biofeedback for persistent FI when sensation or coordination is impaired<sup>[[7]](#ref7)</sup> |

## Referral and Safety

Refer early when the patient cannot isolate pelvic floor muscles, has pain with attempted contraction, has pelvic-floor tenderness, has failed self-directed exercises, has mixed storage-emptying symptoms, or needs postpartum / postoperative rehabilitation.

Before internal pelvic-floor examination or treatment, assess active pelvic infection, unexplained bleeding, open pelvic wounds, pregnancy, recent delivery or pelvic surgery/radiation, postoperative restrictions, severe pain flares, consent and ability to participate. A 2012 Brigham and Women’s institutional protocol calls for the obstetrician’s written consent before internal examination during pregnancy and physician approval within 6–8 weeks of delivery, pelvic surgery, or radiation; it also lists electrical stimulation during pregnancy as a precaution. Evaluate red flags and adapt or defer the relevant modality.<sup>[[39]](#ref39)[[48]](#ref48)</sup> For defecatory-disorder referrals, before an internal examination, explain what it entails, obtain consent, and make clear that the patient may stop the examination or session at any time.<sup>[[39]](#ref39)</sup>

Adverse-event reporting varies by modality. PFMT reviews report few or no serious adverse events<sup>[[11]](#ref11)[[16]](#ref16)</sup>, while biofeedback trials have reported vaginal or rectal device discomfort and vaginal discharge; pelvic-floor pain led to withdrawal in a pregnancy PFMT trial.<sup>[[6]](#ref6)[[28]](#ref28)</sup>

## Operative and Clinic Pearls

- Document PFPT as a specific referral, not "do Kegels."
- Ask the therapist to phenotype tone, strength, relaxation, coordination, and pain.
- For SUI and prolapse, teach timed pre-contraction before stress events.
- For pelvic pain with pelvic-floor tenderness or high tone, begin with relaxation and manual therapy; add strengthening only when the phenotype warrants it.<sup>[[4]](#ref4)[[9]](#ref9)[[40]](#ref40)</sup>
- For post-prostatectomy UI, confirm a correct pelvic-floor contraction and use supervised retraining when needed; direct trials comparing supervision with written instructions have mixed results.<sup>[[53]](#ref53)</sup>
- For defecatory dysfunction, use anorectal biofeedback when dyssynergia is the target.
