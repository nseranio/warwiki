# Continence Pessary

**Continence (incontinence) pessaries** are intravaginal devices designed to manage stress urinary incontinence (SUI) and, selectively, mixed incontinence by mechanically supporting the bladder neck and adding urethral resistance. AUA/SUFU 2023 states that clinicians may offer a continence pessary or vaginal insert to patients with SUI or stress-predominant mixed incontinence (Expert Opinion).<sup>[[13]](#ref13)</sup> Pessaries suit patients who prefer or require nonsurgical management, and they can serve as a bridge to surgery, during pregnancy or postpartum, or for situational SUI.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[2]](#ref2)[[5]](#ref5)</sup>

For the broader SUI ladder, see [Female Stress Incontinence Database](/docs/surgical-techniques/04f-incontinence-procedures/female-sui/female-stress-incontinence-database). For physical therapy, see [Pelvic Floor PT](/docs/surgical-techniques/04f-incontinence-procedures/procedures/pelvic-floor-pt). For office-based bulking, see [Urethral Bulking Agents](/docs/surgical-techniques/04f-incontinence-procedures/procedures/urethral-bulking-agents).

---

## Mechanism

Continence pessaries differ from prolapse pessaries in that they include a **knob or protrusion** positioned directly under the urethra. The knob compresses the urethra against the pubic symphysis, increasing urethral resistance and limiting leakage during increases in intra-abdominal pressure (cough, sneeze, exercise).<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[6]](#ref6)</sup> Standard prolapse pessaries (ring, Gellhorn) support the vaginal vault but do not specifically target urethral compression.

---

## Indications

- **Stress urinary incontinence.** Primary indication, including situational SUI (exercise-related).<sup>[[4]](#ref4)</sup>
- **Mixed urinary incontinence.** Symptoms may improve; evidence is more limited.<sup>[[5]](#ref5)</sup>
- Patients who prefer to avoid or delay surgery, have surgical contraindications, or wish to try conservative management first.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[5]](#ref5)</sup>
- **Bridge therapy** during pregnancy / postpartum or while awaiting surgery.

---

## Device Types

- **Provider-fitted continence pessaries.** Ring with knob, dish with knob, and similar configurations; require professional fitting.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[6]](#ref6)</sup>
- **Over-the-counter / commercial:**
  - **[Poise Impressa](/docs/surgical-techniques/04f-incontinence-procedures/procedures/poise-impressa).** Single-use, tampon-like, disposable; OTC; self-fitted from a sizing kit (sizes 1–3); 8 h wear time.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup>
  - **Uresta.** Reusable medical-grade plastic device (lasts up to 12 mo); self-fitted at home from a starter kit, without an in-person exam (manufacturer information).<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup>
- **Emerging.** 3D-printed custom pessaries and hormone-releasing pessaries.<sup>[[7]](#ref7)</sup>

---

## Fitting and Follow-Up

Fitting is empiric. Trial and error identifies the correct size and shape, and a ring pessary is generally a good first choice for prolapse.<sup>[[8]](#ref8)</sup> NICE advises counseling that more than one fitting may be needed.<sup>[[14]](#ref14)</sup> The fitting-success and follow-up data below come largely from prolapse-pessary studies and are extrapolated to continence pessaries.

Fitting checkpoints:

- A finger should sweep between the pessary and vaginal walls.<sup>[[8]](#ref8)</sup>
- Patient stands, walks, performs Valsalva, and voids successfully with the device in place before discharge.<sup>[[8]](#ref8)</sup>
- In prolapse-pessary series, fitting success ranged from 41% to 96.6% depending on population.<sup>[[9]](#ref9)</sup>
- Predictors of unsuccessful fitting in those series were prior prolapse surgery, prior hysterectomy, short vaginal length, wide genital hiatus and posterior compartment involvement.<sup>[[9]](#ref9)</sup>

Follow-up:

- For prolapse pessaries, NICE advises removal at least once every 6 months and a pessary-clinic visit every 6 months for women at risk of complications.<sup>[[14]](#ref14)</sup> A cohort of 140 older women with prolapse was managed safely with a mean visit interval of 3.3 months.<sup>[[10]](#ref10)</sup>
- Patients who self-manage (remove and reinsert) require less frequent follow-up.
- Consider vaginal estrogen in patients with vaginal atrophy; NICE suggests considering it before pessary treatment.<sup>[[14]](#ref14)</sup>

## Efficacy

| Source | Finding |
|---|---|
| Richter 2010 ATLAS multicenter RCT (446 women; pessary vs behavioral therapy vs combined)<sup>[[3]](#ref3)</sup> | At 3 mo, 33% of the pessary group vs 49% of the behavioral group reported no bothersome incontinence symptoms (P = 0.006), and 46% of the pessary group (69/149) had at least a 75% reduction in incontinence episodes. Group differences did not persist to 12 mo, when satisfaction in the pessary group was 50% (63% at 3 mo). Combination therapy was not superior to behavioral therapy alone |
| Lipp 2014 Cochrane<sup>[[11]](#ref11)</sup> | Eight trials (787 women) of mechanical devices; evidence was inconclusive for device vs no treatment, and the review concluded that the place of mechanical devices remains in question |
| Todhunter-Brown 2022 Cochrane overview<sup>[[2]](#ref2)</sup> | Moderate or high certainty that a continence pessary plus PFMT was more beneficial than a pessary alone for cure or improvement |

ACOG PB 155 lists pessaries among nonsurgical options for stress and mixed incontinence.<sup>[[5]](#ref5)</sup>

---

## Complications

Complications are generally minor. Most data come from prolapse-pessary studies.<sup>[[6]](#ref6)</sup> A Cochrane review of prolapse pessaries found that adverse events (increased vaginal discharge, increased urinary incontinence, or erosion or irritation of the vaginal walls) may be more frequent than with PFMT, based on one study of 97 women (low-certainty evidence).<sup>[[12]](#ref12)</sup>

- **Vaginal discharge or odor.** Most common.<sup>[[8]](#ref8)</sup>
- **Vaginal erosion or irritation.** In the 140-woman prolapse cohort, 11 (7.9%) needed temporary removal for 2-4 weeks because of significant discharge or superficial erosion.<sup>[[10]](#ref10)</sup> Management is temporary removal and topical estrogen.<sup>[[8]](#ref8)</sup>
- **New stress incontinence after pessary insertion.** In the same cohort, 12 women (8.6%) developed stress incontinence after insertion of a prolapse pessary.<sup>[[10]](#ref10)</sup>
- **Discomfort during intercourse.** NICE advises discussing the effect of pessary type on intercourse.<sup>[[14]](#ref14)</sup>
- **Serious complications** are rare and usually occur in older women whose pessary care has been neglected: vesicovaginal or rectovaginal fistula, fecal impaction, hydronephrosis, urosepsis and pessary impaction.<sup>[[8]](#ref8)[[9]](#ref9)</sup>

If erosion develops, remove the pessary, apply vaginal estrogen until healing, then refit with a smaller or different shape. Non-healing erosions warrant biopsy.<sup>[[8]](#ref8)</sup>

## Place in the SUI Treatment Algorithm

AUA/SUFU 2023 directs clinicians to counsel patients about observation, PFMT with or without biofeedback, other nonsurgical options such as a continence pessary, and surgery, and its text states that patients should be offered all viable options before a decision (Clinical Principle).<sup>[[13]](#ref13)</sup> Review articles describe a stepwise sequence:<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[5]](#ref5)</sup>

1. **First-line.** Pelvic floor muscle training (PFMT) with or without behavioral or lifestyle modification (weight loss BMI ≥ 25, fluid or caffeine).
2. **Second-line.** Continence pessary (or urethral bulking) when conservative measures fail or for situational use.
3. **Third-line.** Surgical intervention (midurethral sling, autologous fascial PVS, Burch colposuspension, or office-based bulking) when conservative options are insufficient.

Surgery, particularly the midurethral sling, remains the most effective definitive option for SUI.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup>

---

## Summary

The continence pessary is a reversible nonsurgical option for female SUI with mainly minor complications. In the ATLAS trial, 46% of pessary users had at least a 75% reduction in incontinence episodes at 3 months, behavioral therapy gave more women no bothersome symptoms at 3 months, and the difference was gone by 12 months. Pessary plus PFMT was more beneficial than a pessary alone, but the combination was not superior to behavioral therapy alone. The role is greatest as a bridge to surgery or for patients who prefer or require non-surgical management.<sup>[[2]](#ref2)[[3]](#ref3)</sup>
