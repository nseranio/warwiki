# Continence Pessary

**Continence (incontinence) pessaries** are intravaginal devices specifically designed to manage **stress urinary incontinence (SUI)** — and selectively mixed incontinence — by mechanically supporting the bladder neck and adding urethral resistance. They sit between behavioral therapy and surgery in the SUI treatment algorithm, are appropriate for patients who prefer or require nonsurgical management, and can serve as a bridge to surgery, during pregnancy / postpartum, or for situational SUI.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[2]](#ref2)[[5]](#ref5)</sup>

For the broader SUI ladder, see [Female Stress Incontinence Database](/docs/surgical-techniques/04f-incontinence-procedures/female-sui/female-stress-incontinence-database). For physical therapy, see [Pelvic Floor PT](/docs/surgical-techniques/04f-incontinence-procedures/procedures/pelvic-floor-pt). For office-based bulking, see [Urethral Bulking Agents](/docs/surgical-techniques/04f-incontinence-procedures/procedures/urethral-bulking-agents).

---

## Mechanism

Continence pessaries differ from prolapse pessaries in that they include a **knob or protrusion** positioned directly under the urethra. The knob compresses the urethra against the pubic symphysis, increasing urethral resistance and preventing involuntary leakage during increases in intra-abdominal pressure (cough, sneeze, exercise).<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[3]](#ref3)</sup> Standard prolapse pessaries (ring, Gellhorn) support the vaginal vault but do not specifically target urethral compression.

---

## Indications

- **Stress urinary incontinence** — primary indication, including situational SUI (exercise-related).<sup>[[4]](#ref4)</sup>
- **Mixed urinary incontinence** — may improve symptoms; evidence more limited.<sup>[[5]](#ref5)</sup>
- Patients who prefer to avoid or delay surgery, have surgical contraindications, or wish to try conservative management first.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[5]](#ref5)</sup>
- **Bridge therapy** during pregnancy / postpartum or while awaiting surgery.

---

## Device Types

- **Provider-fitted continence pessaries** — ring with knob, dish with knob, and similar configurations; require professional fitting.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[6]](#ref6)</sup>
- **Over-the-counter / commercial:**
  - **[Poise Impressa](/docs/surgical-techniques/04f-incontinence-procedures/procedures/poise-impressa)** — single-use, tampon-like, disposable; OTC; self-fitted from a sizing kit (sizes 1–3); 8 h wear time.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup>
  - **Uresta** — reusable medical-grade plastic device (lasts up to 12 mo); telehealth-fitted without in-person exam.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup>
- **Emerging** — 3D-printed custom pessaries and hormone-releasing pessaries.<sup>[[7]](#ref7)</sup>

---

## Fitting and Follow-Up

Fitting is empiric — trial-and-error to identify the correct size and shape; a **ring pessary is generally a good first choice**.<sup>[[8]](#ref8)</sup>

Fitting checkpoints:

- A finger should sweep between the pessary and vaginal walls.<sup>[[8]](#ref8)</sup>
- Patient stands, walks, performs Valsalva, and voids successfully with the device in place before discharge.<sup>[[8]](#ref8)</sup>
- Reported fitting success **41–96.6%** depending on population.<sup>[[9]](#ref9)</sup>
- Predictors of unsuccessful fitting — prior pelvic surgery, prior hysterectomy, short vaginal length, wide genital hiatus, posterior compartment prolapse.<sup>[[9]](#ref9)</sup>

Follow-up:

- Manufacturers recommend every 4–6 wk; evidence supports safe management with visits every **3 mo in the first year, then every 6 mo**.<sup>[[8]](#ref8)</sup>
- Patients who self-manage (remove and reinsert) require less frequent follow-up.
- Consider **vaginal estrogen** in patients with vaginal atrophy.<sup>[[8]](#ref8)[[10]](#ref10)</sup>

---

## Efficacy

| Source | Finding |
|---|---|
| Richter 2010 multicenter RCT<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[4]](#ref4)</sup> | **33%** of pessary-only users had no bothersome incontinence at 3 mo vs 49% PFMT (p = 0.006); **no significant difference at 12 mo** (~ 50% satisfaction in both arms) |
| Lipp 2014 Cochrane<sup>[[11]](#ref11)</sup> | **46%** met ≥ 75% reduction in weekly incontinence episodes at 3 mo |
| Todhunter-Brown 2022 Cochrane overview<sup>[[2]](#ref2)</sup> | High-certainty: **PFMT &gt; pessary alone** on some measures; **pessary + PFMT &gt; pessary alone**; combination **not clearly superior** to PFMT alone |
| Long-term continuation<sup>[[6]](#ref6)</sup> | ~ 50% of successfully fitted women continue at 1–2 yr |

**ACOG (PB 155)** rates the evidence as **Level B** — pessaries may improve SUI / mixed-incontinence symptoms, but objective effectiveness data are limited.<sup>[[5]](#ref5)</sup>

---

## Complications

Generally minimal and manageable:<sup>[[6]](#ref6)</sup>

- **Vaginal discharge / odor** — most common.<sup>[[8]](#ref8)</sup>
- **Vaginal erosion / irritation** — ~ 10–29% of long-term users; managed with temporary removal + topical estrogen.<sup>[[8]](#ref8)[[12]](#ref12)</sup>
- **Paradoxical worsening of incontinence** — ~ 8.6%.<sup>[[10]](#ref10)</sup>
- **Discomfort during intercourse**.<sup>[[12]](#ref12)</sup>
- **Rare serious** (almost always neglected pessary in elderly): vesicovaginal / rectovaginal fistula, fecal impaction, hydronephrosis, urosepsis, pessary impaction.<sup>[[8]](#ref8)[[9]](#ref9)</sup>

If erosion develops — remove the pessary, apply vaginal estrogen until healing, then refit with a smaller or different shape. **Non-healing erosions warrant biopsy.**<sup>[[8]](#ref8)</sup>

---

## Place in the SUI Treatment Algorithm

Per ACOG and contemporary review:<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[5]](#ref5)</sup>

1. **First-line** — pelvic floor muscle training (PFMT) ± behavioral / lifestyle modification (weight loss BMI ≥ 25, fluid / caffeine).
2. **Second-line** — **continence pessary** (or urethral bulking) when conservative measures fail or for situational use.
3. **Third-line** — surgical intervention (midurethral sling, autologous fascial PVS, Burch colposuspension, or office-based bulking) when conservative options are insufficient.

Surgery — particularly the midurethral sling — remains the most effective definitive option for SUI.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup>

---

## Summary

The continence pessary is a low-risk, reversible, **second-line** option in the female SUI ladder, with ~ 33–46% short-term symptom-control benefit, ~ 50% long-term continuation, and primarily nuisance-level complications. **PFMT outperforms pessary alone**, and **pessary + PFMT &gt; pessary alone**, but the combination is not clearly superior to PFMT alone. The role is greatest as a **bridge to surgery** or for patients who prefer or require non-surgical management.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[2]](#ref2)[[4]](#ref4)[[5]](#ref5)</sup>
