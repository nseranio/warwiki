# Implantable Tibial Nerve Stimulation (iTNM)

Implantable tibial nerve stimulation (iTNM) targets the posterior tibial nerve through a lower-leg implant rather than repeated [office PTNS](/docs/surgical-techniques/04f-incontinence-procedures/procedures/percutaneous-tibial-nerve-stimulation). The at-home maintenance model differs by device: eCoin stimulates automatically, Revi requires an external wearable, and Medtronic Altaviva is rechargeable. These devices avoid routine in-office PTNS induction and maintenance visits but require implantation, system-specific precautions and follow-up.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[18]](#ref18)[[19]](#ref19)[[20]](#ref20)</sup>

This page is the canonical implantable tibial-nerve stimulation target for the OAB database. It is intentionally separate from PTNS because the procedure, durability, maintenance burden, and device counseling differ substantially. Mechanism-of-action and efficacy fundamentals shared with PTNS are not duplicated here. See the [PTNS page](/docs/surgical-techniques/04f-incontinence-procedures/procedures/percutaneous-tibial-nerve-stimulation) for the underlying neuromodulation rationale and the SUmiT pivotal sham-controlled foundation.

---

## Rationale for Implantable Devices

The primary limitations of PTNS (the burden of weekly in-office visits, short-lived effects requiring indefinite maintenance, and limited insurance coverage) have driven the development of implantable alternatives. Real-world data show that over 40% of PTNS maintenance patients discontinue therapy due to logistic reasons and physical strain rather than treatment failure.<sup>[[2]](#ref2)</sup> Implantable devices address these barriers by providing continuous or scheduled at-home stimulation through a permanent implant, with the added potential for dynamic, patient-centered programming.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup>

## Implantable Tibial Nerve Stimulation Systems

### eCoin® (Valencia Technologies)

The eCoin is a nickel-sized, coin-shaped, fully implanted, primary battery-powered device placed in the lower leg above the fascia over the tibial nerve. It delivers automated, pre-programmed, low-duty-cycle stimulation without requiring any external wearable for daily therapy.<sup>[[3]](#ref3)[[4]](#ref4)</sup>

- **Implantation:** Performed under local anesthesia in approximately 20 minutes; placed suprafascially over the tibial nerve in the lower leg and activated 4 weeks post-implantation.<sup>[[5]](#ref5)[[6]](#ref6)</sup>
- **Power source:** Internal primary (non-rechargeable) battery
- **Stimulation:** Automatic, intermittent low-duty cycle; no patient interaction required for daily therapy delivery<sup>[[4]](#ref4)</sup>
- **FDA status:** PMA-approved for UUI after intolerance of or inadequate response to more conservative treatments, or after a successful PTNS trial; the precise label matters even though the AUA guideline permits individualized selection.<sup>[[18]](#ref18)</sup>

### Revi® (BlueWind Medical, formerly RENOVA™)

The Revi is a leadless, miniature, battery-free implant placed subfascially near the tibial nerve. It is powered wirelessly by an external, battery-operated lower-leg wearable (worn in a leg band over the implant site) that the patient controls, allowing individually tailored stimulation parameters.<sup>[[8]](#ref8)[[9]](#ref9)</sup>

- **Implantation:** Subfascial placement near the tibial nerve; median operative time approximately 34 minutes in early studies<sup>[[10]](#ref10)</sup>
- **Power source:** Battery-free implant; powered externally by a wearable leg-band device
- **Stimulation:** Patient-controlled via the external wearable, enabling personalized therapy sessions at home<sup>[[9]](#ref9)[[11]](#ref11)</sup>
- **FDA status:** FDA-cleared Revi labeling is for urgency incontinence alone or with urinary urgency, without an explicit prior-conservative-treatment failure condition; clinician assessment and model contraindications still apply.<sup>[[19]](#ref19)[[20]](#ref20)</sup>

**Revi implantation (manufacturer technique).** The US surgical technique guide describes the following steps. A BlueWind representative runs a system self-test on the clinician programmer before the implant pouch is opened.<sup>[[24]](#ref24)</sup>

1. Infiltrate local anesthetic along the marked incision line above the medial malleolus, avoiding injection beneath the fascia; general or regional anesthesia may be added if needed. Prepare the skin with chlorhexidine and drape the foot tightly.
2. Make a 5 cm incision and dissect bluntly through fat to the crural fascia. Cutting-mode electrocautery is not used at any stage, and no electrocautery is used once the crural fascia is exposed.
3. Divide the crural fascia with a guarded technique and identify the posterior tibial neurovascular bundle. If a second fascial layer or muscle obscures it, count two tendons posterior to the tibia (tibialis posterior and flexor digitorum longus), or extend the incision distally, where the bundle is more superficial.
4. Hold the implant by its silicone membrane with plastic or anatomical forceps, because sharp objects, including tissue forceps, can damage its Parylene coating. Place it over the neurovascular bundle with the larger electrode distal and the smooth silicone surface superficial, parallel to the skin and no deeper than 3 cm, or energy transfer may fail.
5. Test stimulation through the wearable in a sterile sleeve. Paresthesia or a motor response (great-toe flexion or fanning of the toes) at a low or moderate amplitude (below 5 mA) is optimal; testing at the treatment frequency is recommended, but a low frequency (4 Hz) makes the motor response easier to see and is recommended under general or regional anesthesia. Reposition the implant closer to the nerve if the response is unsatisfactory.
6. Anchor the implant to the fascia with nonabsorbable suture (such as 3-0 polypropylene or polyester) and retest. Leave the fascial incision open. Close the subcutaneous layer with absorbable braided suture and the skin with interrupted nylon or an intradermal absorbable monofilament, retest, and apply a compressive dressing.

The guide calls for antibiotics and analgesics for the first 24 hours, leg elevation for the first 3 days, suture removal at 10 to 14 days and the first treatment setup after the wound has healed, at least 2 weeks after surgery; OASIS activated the device 4 weeks (±2 weeks) after implantation. The guide recommends against explantation without a specific medical need or the treating clinician's judgment that it is necessary.<sup>[[24]](#ref24)</sup>

### Altaviva™ (Medtronic, formerly investigational ITNM)

Altaviva is a rechargeable, implanted tibial stimulator. Its TITAN 2 trial appears below. The FDA approved Altaviva in September 2025 for UUI in patients who failed or could not tolerate more conservative treatments; it should not be described only as investigational or assigned Revi's broader label.<sup>[[21]](#ref21)</sup>

### Protect PNS (Uro Medical Corp.) and Others

A percutaneous implantable pulse generator (pIPG) with integrated quadripolar electrodes has been studied in a pilot trial. It is implanted in the office under local anesthesia using a retrograde approach and activated via an external wireless transmitter. Early data show feasibility and safety, though larger studies are needed.<sup>[[12]](#ref12)</sup>

The investigational Intibia system was described in a first-in-human feasibility study of 10 patients (7 OAB-wet and 3 OAB-dry). After 2 weeks of daily stimulation followed by 13 weeks of weekly stimulation, 8 of 10 had at least 50% improvement in one or more OAB components, not necessarily UUI; no serious adverse events were reported.<sup>[[13]](#ref13)</sup>

## Key Pivotal Trials

### OASIS Study (Revi)

The pivotal OASIS trial enrolled 151 women with UUI. The primary endpoint, ≥50% reduction in UUI episodes at 6 months, was met by 76.4% (95% CI 68.7% to 82.6%) in the intention-to-treat analysis; a post hoc 12-month ITT analysis gave 78.4%.<sup>[[24]](#ref24)</sup> The two-year report gives 78% at 6 months among participants with diaries, and 79% of the 90 participants with 36-month diaries were responders.<sup>[[8]](#ref8)[[9]](#ref9)</sup> Quality-of-life improvements were clinically significant across all OAB-q domains. At 3 years, 83 of 87 survey respondents (95%) reported therapeutic satisfaction, and 74 of 90 participants (82%) reported feeling "much better" or "very much better".<sup>[[8]](#ref8)</sup> No device- or procedure-related serious adverse events occurred through 36 months.<sup>[[8]](#ref8)[[9]](#ref9)[[11]](#ref11)</sup>

### eCoin Pivotal Trial

The FDA pivotal study included 133 implanted or attempted-implant participants in the ITT population, with 68% achieving ≥50% reduction in UUI episodes after 48 weeks of stimulation.<sup>[[18]](#ref18)</sup> A smaller observed extension cohort reported 78% responders at 96 weeks and 22% dry; it should not be read as an improvement in the same complete denominator or as a controlled comparison.<sup>[[7]](#ref7)</sup> Medication-use and extension safety details require the full original report.<sup>[[7]](#ref7)[[18]](#ref18)</sup>

### TITAN 2 (Medtronic ITNM)

This pivotal IDE study enrolled 126 subjects implanted with the Medtronic leadless, rechargeable ITNM system. At 6 months, 59% achieved ≥50% improvement in UUI episodes/day (primary endpoint met, p &lt; 0.001 versus performance goal). Twenty percent experienced adverse device effects through 12 months; one serious ADE resolved without sequelae.<sup>[[14]](#ref14)</sup>

## Comparison with Sacral Neuromodulation (SNM)

A systematic review of separate iTNM and [SNM](/docs/surgical-techniques/04f-incontinence-procedures/procedures/sacral-neuromodulation) studies (20 studies, 1,766 patients) reported pooled UUI responder proportions of 71.3% and 71.8%, respectively. These indirect single-arm proportions do not establish equivalent efficacy or comparable adverse-event risk: selection, trial phase, follow-up and endpoint definitions differ. The studied tibial implants generally did not use an SNM-style temporary test phase.<sup>[[15]](#ref15)</sup>

## Safety Profile

Safety and MRI conditions are device-specific; do not substitute PTNS or one implant's labeling for another:

- **eCoin:** The original FDA physician labeling contraindicates implantation in specified poor lower-leg surgical candidates (including open wound, local vascular disease/edema and local infection) and patients unable to operate the magnet controller if unwanted stimulation occurs. The implant is MR conditional only under its labeled conditions, including keeping it at least 20 cm outside the MRI bore at 1.5/3 T; the controller magnet is MR unsafe.<sup>[[18]](#ref18)</sup> Diathermy is prohibited. The patient manual also lists prior surgery in the implant area and lower-leg venous or arterial disease as contraindications, gives stimulation as 30-minute sessions every 3 days for 18 weeks and every 4 days afterward, and states battery life of 3 to 5 years at the nominal 6 mA setting.<sup>[[23]](#ref23)</sup> Confirm any later PMA supplement or label revision.<sup>[[18]](#ref18)[[23]](#ref23)</sup>
- **Revi:** The manufacturer lists inability to operate the system, men with obstructive BPH or other lower urinary tract obstruction, metallic implants within about 20 cm (8 in) of the implant site, nerve damage affecting treatment, lower-leg wound-healing risks (open wounds, edema, venous or arterial disease) and pregnancy among contraindications. The implant is MR Conditional at 1.5 T or 3.0 T (scanning at lower field strengths is also permitted) with a maximum spatial gradient of 30 T/m and maximum slew rate of 200 T/m/s, plus SAR and scan-duration limits that depend on the body region scanned. All external components and accessories are MR Unsafe and stay out of the scanner room. Shortwave, microwave and ultrasound diathermy are prohibited anywhere on or near the body because energy transfer can cause severe injury or implant damage. Full current model labeling governs screening and scanning.<sup>[[20]](#ref20)</sup>
- **Altaviva:** FDA's original PMA label limits the indication to UUI after failure or intolerance of conservative therapies; FDA's contraindications are skin lesions or compromised skin integrity, current or recent venous insufficiency or venous stasis ulcers in the lower leg, anatomic defects or prior surgery at the implant site that preclude use, and inability to operate the system or receive help operating it. The leadless rechargeable neurostimulator sits in a deep-fascia pocket superior to the medial malleolus and anterior to the Achilles tendon. Non-clinical testing supports MRI at 1.5 T and 3 T only under labeled conditions. The FDA patient guide says patients should not have shortwave or microwave diathermy and warns that recharging within 5 cm of another below-knee metal implant in the same leg may cause tissue injury requiring surgery; review the current implant and MRI manuals before treatment.<sup>[[21]](#ref21)</sup>

Observed study harms (different denominators and ascertainment):

- **eCoin:** Most adverse events relate to wound healing and infection at the implant site. In the pivotal trial, 16% experienced device-related events through 52 weeks, mostly minor.<sup>[[3]](#ref3)[[4]](#ref4)</sup> The patient manual counts 23 of 133 (17%) with device-related events.<sup>[[23]](#ref23)</sup> The FDA labeling reports device- or procedure-related events in 27 of 133 implanted patients (20%) through 52 weeks; 25 had nonserious and 4 had serious related events, with 2 participants counted in both groups.<sup>[[18]](#ref18)</sup> Three of the 4 serious related events were infections and 1 was contact dermatitis, all resulting in explantation. No serious or unanticipated AEs were reported in the 2-year extension.<sup>[[7]](#ref7)[[18]](#ref18)</sup>
- **Revi:** No device- or procedure-related serious adverse events through 3 years of follow-up in the OASIS study.<sup>[[8]](#ref8)</sup> The earlier 36-patient feasibility study reported implant site pain (14%), suspected infection (22%) and procedural wound complications (8%); one system was removed for inflammation.<sup>[[16]](#ref16)</sup>
- **Medtronic ITNM:** 20% adverse device effects through 12 months; one serious ADE that resolved.<sup>[[14]](#ref14)</sup>

## Advantages Over PTNS

- **Avoids repeated in-office PTNS treatment visits.** At-home therapy delivery<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup>
- **Maintenance burden.** Home-based stimulation avoids office PTNS maintenance visits, a barrier reported in a retrospective PTNS cohort; that cohort did not compare adherence with implants.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[2]](#ref2)</sup>
- **Longer observed follow-up.** Selected eCoin and Revi cohorts report responses through 2–3 years; lack of a randomized PTNS comparator prevents concluding that implants eliminate wearing-off or are more durable<sup>[[7]](#ref7)[[8]](#ref8)</sup>
- **Patient-controlled therapy** (Revi) allows individualized stimulation parameters<sup>[[9]](#ref9)</sup>
- **No SNM-style temporary test phase in the studied implant pathways.** Confirm the individual device's indication and preimplant evaluation; eCoin labeling also allows eligibility after a successful PTNS trial<sup>[[15]](#ref15)[[18]](#ref18)</sup>

## Guideline Positioning

The AUA/SUFU 2024 guideline lists implantable tibial nerve stimulation among the minimally invasive OAB therapies (Table 3), but its named recommendation for patients with inadequate response to or intolerance of drugs or behavioral therapy covers sacral neuromodulation, percutaneous tibial nerve stimulation and intradetrusor onabotulinumtoxinA rather than implantable devices, because the panel found no direct comparison or placebo-controlled data for the implantable systems (its review cites single-arm studies of 137 patients for eCoin, with 68% achieving a ≥50% UUI reduction at 48 weeks, and 36 patients for Revi).<sup>[[17]](#ref17)</sup> AUA/SUFU Statement 24 permits minimally invasive therapy without prior behavioral, noninvasive or drug trials through shared decision-making (Expert Opinion); its named treatment offer in Statement 25 is a Moderate Recommendation (Grade A).<sup>[[17]](#ref17)</sup> The EAU 2026 female LUTS guideline recommends offering percutaneous or transcutaneous posterior tibial nerve stimulation as an option for OAB/UUI (Strong) but makes no statement on implanted tibial systems.<sup>[[22]](#ref22)</sup>

## Limitations and Considerations

- The pivotal eCoin, Revi and Altaviva efficacy studies cited above were single-arm and open-label.<sup>[[4]](#ref4)[[9]](#ref9)[[14]](#ref14)</sup> Sham-controlled PTNS trials support tibial stimulation as therapy.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup>
- **No head-to-head randomized trials** comparing iTNM devices to each other, to SNM, or to onabotulinumtoxinA<sup>[[15]](#ref15)</sup>
- Long-term data beyond 3 years remain limited
- The eCoin uses a non-rechargeable primary battery, raising questions about device longevity and potential need for replacement
- MRI compatibility varies by device and should be confirmed before implantation
- Cost-effectiveness data are still emerging
