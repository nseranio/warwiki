# Barrier Creams and Incontinence-Associated Dermatitis Prevention

**Barrier products** are topical skin protectants applied to perineal and perigenital skin to help prevent or manage **incontinence-associated dermatitis (IAD)**: irritant contact dermatitis associated with prolonged exposure to urine, feces or both. The AUA/SUFU 2024 OAB Guideline includes barrier creams among incontinence-management strategies to discuss with patients with urgency urinary incontinence (Expert Opinion). The 2025 Cochrane review below addresses prevention on intact skin, not treatment of established IAD.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[2]](#ref2)[[3]](#ref3)[[4]](#ref4)</sup> 

For absorbent-product selection and changes, see [Absorbent Products & the Pad Test](/docs/surgical-techniques/04f-incontinence-procedures/procedures/absorbent-products). A [condom catheter](/docs/surgical-techniques/04f-incontinence-procedures/procedures/condom-catheters) may reduce urine contact in a selected man with adequate emptying, but does not address fecal exposure or guarantee dry skin.

---

## Definition and Concept

Barrier creams belong to the broader class of leave-on products: formulations of lipophilic ingredients (petrolatum, wax) and hydrophilic ingredients (water, glycerin) applied after cleansing. The protective effect comes from a hydrophobic film that reduces direct contact between urine or feces and the epidermis, reduces transepidermal water loss, and minimizes friction against absorbent products. Comparative evidence does not show that any single ingredient is more effective than another.<sup>[[4]](#ref4)[[5]](#ref5)</sup>

---

## Categories of Barrier Products

| Category | Key ingredients | Mechanism | Examples |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Traditional creams / ointments** | Zinc oxide, petrolatum, lanolin | Physical occlusive barrier; zinc oxide adds mild astringent / antimicrobial action | Sudocrem (15.25% zinc oxide), standard zinc oxide cream (32%), Desitin, A+D ointment<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup> |
| **Dimethicone (silicone) products** | Dimethicone 1–3%, silicone polymers | Transparent water-insoluble protective film; breathable | Comfort Shield perineal washcloth (3% dimethicone); dimethicone silicone oil<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup> |
| **Film-forming polymers** | Acrylate terpolymers; silicone-based sprays | Thin transparent film resistant to wash-off; does not interfere with pad adhesion | Cavilon No Sting Barrier Film; 3M skin protective film spray<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[2]](#ref2)</sup> |
| **Cyanoacrylate-based protectants** | Product-specific polymer/cyanoacrylate formulation | Durable transparent film; some products are indicated for superficial damaged skin; follow each product's instructions | Cavilon Advanced Skin Protectant (2-octyl cyanoacrylate plus acrylic tetrapolymer and solvent)<sup>[[6]](#ref6)[[11]](#ref11)</sup> |
| **Combination / multi-step bundles** | Cleanser + moisturizer + protectant | Cleansing and barrier in one workflow | Comfort Shield (cleanser + moisturizer + dimethicone); Cavilon cleanser + barrier-cream bundles<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup> |

## Mechanism — How Barrier Creams Protect

IAD develops through a cascade:<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[2]](#ref2)</sup>

1. Prolonged urine contact raises skin pH (normally approximately 5.5), activating fecal lipases and proteases that degrade the stratum corneum. 
2. Overhydration (maceration) further weakens the barrier.
3. Friction from absorbent products compounds damage.

Barrier products counter this by:

- A hydrophobic layer reduces direct urine and fecal contact with the epidermis.
- Reduced transepidermal water loss limits maceration.
- Less friction develops between skin and absorbent product.
- Some formulations (for example zinc oxide) add a mild antimicrobial or astringent action.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[5]](#ref5)</sup>

## Clinical Evidence

The 2025 Cochrane review (Graham et al.) evaluated 15 prevention trials (1,020 participants; 13 small, high overall risk of bias, too heterogeneous to pool). All comparisons were very low certainty, and the authors could not draw meaningful conclusions about which interventions prevent IAD. Its treatment review is separate.<sup>[[4]](#ref4)</sup>

### Leave-on vs leave-on (selected comparisons from Cochrane 2025)<sup>[[4]](#ref4)</sup>

| Comparison | n | IAD rate | Effect |
|---|---|---|---|
| Petroleum jelly vs zinc oxide powder | 20 | 60% vs 40% | RR 1.50 (95% CI 0.60–3.74), NS |
| Sudocrem vs standard zinc oxide cream | 27 | 18.75% vs 18.18% | RR 1.03 (95% CI 0.20–5.19), NS |
| Dimethicone silicone oil vs acrylate terpolymer film spray | 56 | 17.9% vs 42.85% | RR 0.42 (95% CI 0.17–1.03), trend favoring dimethicone |
| 3M skin protective film vs IV3000 semipermeable film | 74 | 20.6% vs 47.5% | RR 0.43 (95% CI 0.21–0.90); single-trial, very-low-certainty result, not general product superiority |

### Bundle vs single product

- Cavilon Advanced plus routine cleansing versus routine cleansing alone: 2/15 (13.3%) versus 3/16 (18.8%) developed IAD in the Coyer ICU pilot; RR 0.71 (95% CI 0.14–3.68), very low certainty. This is a prevention comparison and does not test treatment.<sup>[[4]](#ref4)</sup>
- A structured cleansing plus topical-product protocol in Gaspar 2016 yielded 0/90 versus 15/90 incident IAD under conventional care (RR 0.03, 95% CI 0.00–0.53); very low certainty and not a product-specific zinc-oxide effect.<sup>[[4]](#ref4)</sup> 
- Cambel/Brava colostomy powder plus 3M film-forming product versus warm-water cleansing plus zinc oxide: 7/32 (21.9%) versus 26/31 (83.9%); RR 0.26 (95% CI 0.13–0.51), very low certainty in one trial. This is not a Stomahesive-product result.<sup>[[4]](#ref4)</sup> 

### Umbrella review

The 2023 nursing-practice umbrella review (12 systematic reviews) found that reviews of leave-on products indicated effectiveness of barrier films and lipophilic products for preventing and treating IAD, without establishing that any product is superior. The authors noted that most systematic reviews in the field are at high risk of bias.<sup>[[7]](#ref7)</sup>

## Cyanoacrylate-Based Protectants — Emerging Category

Cyanoacrylate-based protectants form a durable, transparent barrier. The manufacturer IFU for Cavilon Advanced permits use on intact or superficially damaged skin and excludes full-thickness wounds. It cautions that other creams can impair the film and that the still-wet product is highly flammable (allow at least 90 seconds before heat or ignition devices). The IFU specifies reapplication one to three times weekly rather than after each cleansing; local product IFUs govern.<sup>[[11]](#ref11)</sup> Comparative data are limited:<sup>[[5]](#ref5)[[6]](#ref6)</sup>

- A Medline-funded comparison favored its solvent-free 100% cyanoacrylate over solvent-containing Cavilon Advanced in an ex-vivo porcine-skin moisture/abrasion model; this is not a comparative clinical IAD outcome. 
- In healthy volunteers, both products maintained barrier function over the eight-day observation period without a clear clinical difference in wear duration. Do not extrapolate this to an eight-day reapplication schedule in people with IAD. 
- On superficial denudation, select a product specifically indicated for damaged skin, and assess infection or deeper injury separately.<sup>[[11]](#ref11)</sup>

---

## Wash-Off Resistance

A head-to-head study of five silicone-containing barrier creams in 36 healthy volunteers found that Medi Derma-S, Cavilon Barrier Cream and LBF Barrier Cream gave significantly better moisture-barrier protection and wash-off resistance than Medihoney, Remedy Barrier Cream and untreated skin (p &lt; 0.001).<sup>[[8]](#ref8)</sup>

## Guideline Position

| Source | Statement |
|---|---|
| **AUA/SUFU 2024 OAB** (Statement 10) | Discuss incontinence management strategies including **barrier creams** with all UUI patients (Expert Opinion); no head-to-head RCT data<sup>[[3]](#ref3)</sup> |
| **Wund-D.A.CH best-practice consensus** | Continence management, efficient absorbent aids with good retention, **consistent skin protection with barrier products**, and adequate skin care are the effective strategies for IAD prevention<sup>[[9]](#ref9)</sup> |
| **General expert consensus** | Three pillars of IAD prevention / treatment: (1) gentle cleansing; (2) hydrating topical agents; (3) barrier products. Recommendations remain largely expert-opinion-based<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup> |

## Structured Skin-Care Protocol

A practical regimen integrating evidence and consensus:<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[2]](#ref2)[[4]](#ref4)</sup>

1. **Cleanse.** Gently remove urine or stool after incontinence episodes; consider a low-irritant, pH-compatible cleanser. One small trial favored a foam cleanser over soap and water (RR 0.35, 65 participants) and another found no clear difference, so certainty is very low. Avoid harsh soap, friction and vigorous scrubbing.
2. **Moisturize.** Apply a hydrating leave-on agent to maintain stratum-corneum hydration, particularly in elderly patients with xerotic skin.
3. **Protect.** Apply a suitable barrier product (cream, ointment or film). Reapplication and compatibility depend on the formulation; follow the actual product IFU. For example, Cavilon Advanced does not require reapplication between routine cleansing episodes and its IFU specifies one to three applications per week.<sup>[[11]](#ref11)</sup>
4. **Change absorbent products regularly.** High-absorbency products with superabsorbent polymers reduce skin moisture exposure.

## Treating Established IAD

| Severity | Management |
|---|---|
| **Mild (erythema only)** | Continue barrier product; consider switching to a more occlusive formulation<sup>[[2]](#ref2)</sup> |
| **Moderate (erosion / denudation)** | Protect from further irritant exposure; a cyanoacrylate-based protectant indicated for damaged skin can be used, following its IFU<sup>[[10]](#ref10)[[11]](#ref11)</sup> |
| **Severe or atypical** | Assess for pressure injury, allergy and infection; arrange wound/dermatology assessment where needed. A review describes topical steroids for severe cases; the Cochrane prevention trials did not test them.<sup>[[4]](#ref4)[[10]](#ref10)</sup> |
| **Secondary infection** | Assess and treat a diagnosed fungal or bacterial infection as appropriate; a film barrier may reduce absorption of topical antimicrobials, so check the IFU and sequencing.<sup>[[2]](#ref2)[[11]](#ref11)</sup> |
| **Refractory** | Reassess continence care, product fit, skin diagnosis and exposure; consider an external collection device in a suitable patient. Do not place an indwelling urinary catheter solely to manage incontinence, and do not assume any device eliminates skin exposure.<sup>[[2]](#ref2)[[12]](#ref12)</sup> |

---

## Limitations of the Evidence

Across all comparisons the evidence is very low certainty, with small samples, heterogeneous interventions and inconsistent endpoints. The 2025 Cochrane review could not draw meaningful conclusions about the effectiveness of the tested interventions. Single small trials favored a foam cleanser over soap and water and a structured cleansing-plus-leave-on protocol over cleansing alone, but each result is very low certainty.<sup>[[4]](#ref4)</sup>

---

## Key Principles

- Barrier products can help protect skin as part of structured prevention and care for IAD; comparative treatment evidence must not be inferred from prevention trials.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[2]](#ref2)[[4]](#ref4)</sup> 
- The AUA/SUFU 2024 OAB guideline states that clinicians should discuss incontinence management strategies, including barrier creams, with all patients who have urgency urinary incontinence (Expert Opinion).<sup>[[3]](#ref3)</sup>
- The evidence base does not show that any product is superior.<sup>[[4]](#ref4)[[7]](#ref7)</sup>
- The Cavilon Advanced IFU allows superficial damaged-skin use, carries flammability and topical-drug compatibility precautions, and specifies one to three reapplications per week. Other formulations have their own IFUs.<sup>[[11]](#ref11)</sup>
- Wash-off resistance differed between brands in healthy volunteers: Medi Derma-S, Cavilon Barrier Cream and LBF Barrier Cream outperformed Medihoney and Remedy.<sup>[[8]](#ref8)</sup>
- A structured regimen (gentle cleansing, suitable leave-on protection, timely absorbent-product changes) is practical, but comparative effects remain very uncertain.<sup>[[4]](#ref4)</sup>
- Harsh soap and friction are avoided.<sup>[[4]](#ref4)</sup>
- For refractory IAD, reassess the cause and exposure; consider appropriate external collection without using an indwelling catheter for incontinence alone.<sup>[[2]](#ref2)[[12]](#ref12)</sup>
