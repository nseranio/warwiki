# Penile Clamp (External Penile Compression Device)

A penile clamp is an **external compression device** applied to the penile shaft to reduce urinary leakage in selected men with stress urinary incontinence, most commonly **post-prostatectomy incontinence (PPI)**. It may be an activity-specific adjunct, a bridge while awaiting recovery or surgery, or a chosen containment option when surgery is unsuitable or declined. [Male sling](/docs/surgical-techniques/04f-incontinence-procedures/procedures/male-urethral-slings) and [artificial urinary sphincter](/docs/surgical-techniques/04f-incontinence-procedures/procedures/artificial-urinary-sphincter) are separate options after assessment and are not mandatory replacements for every user.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[2]](#ref2)[[7]](#ref7)</sup>

> **Do not wear continuously or while asleep.** Follow the particular device instructions for fit, release, repositioning and inspection; prolonged compression risks pain, impaired perfusion and skin injury. A Cunningham patient instruction sheet specifies removal every two hours, repositioning away from the same shaft site, and no overnight wear.<sup>[[8]](#ref8)</sup>

---

## Mechanism

Compression aims to reduce leakage while minimizing penile pressure and impaired blood flow. Finite-element modeling found higher modeled tissue stresses with some cuff and knurled geometries than with softer contoured geometries; this does not establish a validated 10-kPa clinical injury threshold, the safest commercial brand, or long-term safety.<sup>[[3]](#ref3)</sup> A short physiologic wear study found perfusion and inflammatory changes during a 50-minute test, with recovery after removal; longer manufacturer-recommended wear intervals were not tested in that study.<sup>[[9]](#ref9)</sup>

## Available Devices

| Device | Design | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| **Cunningham clamp** | Hinged frame with foam padding; direct dorsal-ventral compression | In Moore 2004's 12-man crossover, lowest mean 4-h pad-test loss and most favorable rating, but also reduced distal systolic blood-flow velocity.<sup>[[8]](#ref8)[[10]](#ref10)</sup> |
| **U-Tex Male Adjustable Tension Band** | Adjustable band-type | Studied in Moore 2004's small crossover.<sup>[[10]](#ref10)</sup> |
| **C3 penile compression device** | Compression strap design | Studied in Moore 2004's small crossover.<sup>[[10]](#ref10)</sup> |
| **Dribblestop** | Circular soft-compression ring | New Zealand study showed significant IIQ-7 reduction (67.3 → 26.8) in selected men.<sup>[[4]](#ref4)</sup> |
| **CLAMPMED** | Adjustable Japanese device | MORE RCT — significant pad-use reduction vs no clamp.<sup>[[5]](#ref5)</sup> |
| **Wiesner Incontinence Clamp** | Commercial compression design | Most frequently reviewed positively in a selected Amazon-review sample; this does not establish comparative safety.<sup>[[6]](#ref6)</sup> |
| **Pacey Cuff** | Cuff-type | Most frequently reviewed negatively in the same sample; a computational design study does not validate brand-specific injury ranking.<sup>[[3]](#ref3)[[6]](#ref6)</sup> |

A separate **Brodney clamp** is occasionally used by reconstructive urologists not for SUI but as a [retrograde urethrography](/docs/evaluation/imaging/rug-vcug) adjunct when a Foley balloon cannot be safely seated at the fossa navicularis; this is a different application of the same compression principle.

## Efficacy

Several studied devices reduced leakage under trial conditions; a result from three devices in 12 men cannot be generalized to all commercial clamps.<sup>[[10]](#ref10)</sup> Headline data:

- **Moore 2004 crossover (n = 12).** Cunningham mean 4-h pad-test loss 17.1 g versus 32.3 g with C3, 53.3 g with U-Tex and 122.8 g baseline; 10/12 rated Cunningham positively. No device eliminated leakage at comfortable pressure, and Cunningham reduced distal systolic blood-flow velocity.<sup>[[10]](#ref10)</sup>
- **MORE RCT (Gotoh 2023; 40 enrolled, 37 analyzed).** CLAMPMED use reduced daily pad counts relative to no clamp (−0.83 versus −0.16 pads/day, p = 0.0071) over four weeks, while King's Health Questionnaire scores did not significantly change. Participants used it in daytime with 15-minute breaks every 2–3 hours; this protocol is not proof of a safe wear interval for other devices.<sup>[[5]](#ref5)</sup>
- **Macaulay 2015 4-arm device trial (n = 56).** Clamps were rated most secure, least likely to leak, and most discreet of the four containment options tested (pads, sheath catheters, body-worn urinals, clamps).<sup>[[7]](#ref7)</sup>
- **Dribblestop 2015 report.** IIQ-7 score fell from 67.3 to 26.8 in a selected group; uncontrolled results do not establish comparative efficacy or long-term safety.<sup>[[4]](#ref4)</sup>

## Best Use Cases

Some men prefer a clamp for short activities, such as swimming or exercise, when a pad or drainage bag is inconvenient; suitability, comfort and timing are individual.<sup>[[11]](#ref11)</sup> The Macaulay 4-arm trial found that many men mixed containment products rather than using one for every situation; its selected trial population does not prescribe a universal pattern.<sup>[[7]](#ref7)</sup>

---

## Safety and Adverse Effects

- **Pain and discomfort.** A major limitation; the 56-man containment trial rated clamps more painful than several alternatives.<sup>[[7]](#ref7)</sup>
- **Vascular effects.** The Cunningham clamp reduced distal systolic velocity in the 12-man trial, and short physiologic testing found transient circulatory impedance with several designs.<sup>[[9]](#ref9)[[10]](#ref10)</sup>
- **Tissue injury.** Pressure and skin breakdown are concerns; modeled stress and brief physiologic tests are not a validated clinical injury threshold or evidence that one commercial brand is safe for prolonged wear.<sup>[[3]](#ref3)[[9]](#ref9)</sup>
- **Stop and assess** pain, swelling, discoloration, discharge, skin irritation or loss of sensation; the Cunningham instruction sheet explicitly lists these signs for contacting a clinician.<sup>[[8]](#ref8)</sup>

**Patient selection and fitting.** An International Consultation on Incontinence continence-products chapter recommends trained assessment and follow-up, with particular attention to men who:<sup>[[11]](#ref11)</sup>

- cognitively intact and aware of bladder filling,
- have adequate genital sensation to detect pain or irritation (sensory loss needs individualized assessment),
- have intact penile skin at contact sites,
- have sufficient manual dexterity to apply, release, and reapply the device,
- can judge when to release it (when too tight, when bladder full).

For the Cunningham device, the available patient instruction sheet advises removing every two hours to void, moving the position on reapplication, avoiding sleep, and checking skin and blood flow; it also warns of latex content. Confirm the supplied device's own instructions rather than applying a single interval to every model.<sup>[[8]](#ref8)</sup>

## Patient Satisfaction

A qualitative analysis of 719 selected Amazon customer reviews found 425 positive and 294 negative overall sentiments. Incontinence control was the most frequent praise (n = 334), while poor design or material was the most frequent complaint (n = 166). Wiesner was most frequently reviewed positively and Pacey most frequently negatively within this nonrepresentative marketplace sample. These observations do not measure clinical injury, compare matched users or establish a safer brand.<sup>[[6]](#ref6)</sup>

## Containment-Device Comparison

Both the penile clamp and [condom catheter](/docs/surgical-techniques/04f-incontinence-procedures/procedures/condom-catheters) offer nonsurgical **containment**, while [male sling](/docs/surgical-techniques/04f-incontinence-procedures/procedures/male-urethral-slings) or [AUS](/docs/surgical-techniques/04f-incontinence-procedures/procedures/artificial-urinary-sphincter) may be considered separately when suitable and desired. Device choices depend on leakage, emptying, fit, preferences and safety:

| Feature | Penile clamp | [Condom catheter](/docs/surgical-techniques/04f-incontinence-procedures/procedures/condom-catheters) |
|---|---|---|
| Mechanism | Occludes urethra via compression | Passively collects voided urine |
| Potential use | Short activities (swim, exercise) in selected men | Daytime or overnight collection in selected men, with appropriate fit |
| Leak protection | Can markedly reduce leakage, but may not eliminate it | Collects voided urine; leakage or dislodgment can occur |
| Comfort | Pressure and pain limit some users; release per device instructions | Variable fit and skin tolerance; no urethral compression |
| Discretion | Most discreet (no bag) | Requires leg bag |
| Skin / vascular risk | Pressure injury and impaired perfusion; examine and reposition | Adhesive/skin injury or constriction with poor sizing |
| Infection | No drainage system, but not risk-free | Bacteriuria/UTI risks vary by setting and duration; no universal monthly rate |
| Patient selection | Sensation, intact contact skin, cognition, dexterity, fit | Appropriate fit and effective emptying without retention/obstruction; interpret PVR in context |
| Patient preference | Varies with activity, comfort and individual priorities | Varies; see the [condom-catheter page](/docs/surgical-techniques/04f-incontinence-procedures/procedures/condom-catheters) for trial context |

Combination use was common in the Macaulay trial, but the specific mix of pads, sheath and clamp should be individualized.<sup>[[7]](#ref7)</sup>

## Where the Penile Clamp Fits in the PPI Pathway

Within the [male SUI / PPI treatment pathway](/docs/surgical-techniques/04f-incontinence-procedures/male-sui/male-stress-incontinence-database), a clamp is an optional containment tool alongside pads and external collection. [PFMT](/docs/surgical-techniques/04f-incontinence-procedures/procedures/pelvic-floor-pt) and procedural treatment address different goals. Some men use a clamp while awaiting recovery or surgery, whereas others choose intermittent use longer term after weighing fit, comfort, tissue risk and alternatives; no device replaces individualized evaluation.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[7]](#ref7)[[11]](#ref11)</sup>

---

## Limitations of the Evidence

The penile-clamp evidence base is small: the Moore device comparison enrolled 12 men, the Macaulay four-option containment trial enrolled 56, and the MORE clamp-versus-no-clamp trial enrolled 37. Consumer reviews are not clinical safety data. Long-term comparative safety, ideal release schedules across models and injury thresholds remain uncertain.<sup>[[5]](#ref5)[[6]](#ref6)[[7]](#ref7)[[9]](#ref9)[[10]](#ref10)</sup>
