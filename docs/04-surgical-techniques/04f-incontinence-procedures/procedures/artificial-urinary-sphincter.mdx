# Artificial Urinary Sphincter (AUS)

The **artificial urinary sphincter (AUS)** is the gold standard surgical treatment for moderate-to-severe male stress urinary incontinence (SUI), particularly following prostate surgery.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[2]](#ref2)[[3]](#ref3)</sup> The **AMS 800™** is the predominant device, in clinical use since 1972 with more than 250,000 implanted worldwide.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[5]](#ref5)[[9]](#ref9)</sup> It provides durable continence restoration through a completely implantable, fluid-filled hydraulic system that mimics voluntary sphincter function.

---

## Device Components

The AMS 800 consists of three silicone components connected by kink-resistant tubing forming a closed hydraulic circuit:

| Component | Location | Function |
|---|---|---|
| **Occlusive Cuff** | Bulbous urethra (men) or bladder neck | Applies circumferential compression to maintain continence; 13 sizes (3.5–11.0 cm) |
| **Pressure-Regulating Balloon (PRB)** | Prevesical (space of Retzius) | Maintains constant system pressure; most common range **61–70 cmH₂O** |
| **Control Pump** | Scrotum (men) / labia majora (women) | Patient-operated valve mechanism; contains a **deactivation button** to keep cuff open |

Many components are coated with **InhibiZone™** — silicone impregnated with rifampin and minocycline hydrochloride — to reduce bacterial colonization at implantation.<sup>[[16]](#ref16)</sup>

---

## System Operation

The AUS cycles between continence and voiding by fluid transfer between the cuff and balloon:

1. **At rest (continent):** Cuff is inflated, applying urethral compression sufficient to prevent leakage.
2. **To void:** Patient squeezes and releases the scrotal pump several times, transferring fluid from the cuff → PRB, deflating the cuff and opening the urethra.
3. **Auto-refill:** After approximately **1–11 minutes** (depending on component size), the PRB passively returns fluid to the cuff, restoring continence without patient action.<sup>[[6]](#ref6)</sup>

## Indications

Primary indications for AUS implantation:<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[2]](#ref2)[[8]](#ref8)</sup>

- **Moderate-to-severe SUI** (typically &gt;2 pads/day, or 24-hour pad weight &gt;400 g) after prostate surgery — accounts for &gt;90% of male implants
- Failed conservative management (pelvic floor physiotherapy, behavioral modification)
- Intrinsic sphincter deficiency from radical prostatectomy, cryotherapy, HIFU, TURP, or radiation
- Neurogenic bladder with SUI in select patients with adequate dexterity<sup>[[7]](#ref7)[[21]](#ref21)</sup>
- Women with severe SUI refractory to other treatments (limited data)<sup>[[21]](#ref21)</sup>

**Absolute contraindications:** Active UTI or urethral erosion, inadequate manual dexterity to operate the pump, uncontrolled detrusor overactivity incompatible with safe storage pressures.

## Preoperative Assessment

| Evaluation | Purpose |
|---|---|
| **Cystoscopy** | Exclude urethral stricture, bladder neck contracture, bladder pathology, cancer recurrence |
| **Urodynamics** | Assess storage parameters; essential in neurogenic patients or complex histories |
| **24-hour pad weight** | Quantify severity; &gt;400 g favors AUS over sling |
| **Manual dexterity assessment** | Patient must reliably operate scrotal pump |
| **PSA** | Exclude cancer recurrence prior to implant |
| **Infection screening** | Clear any UTI before proceeding |

## Surgical Technique

### Approaches

**Perineal approach** (traditional, most common)
- Midline perineal incision for cuff dissection around the bulbous urethra
- Separate small lower abdominal / inguinal incision for PRB placement
- Optimal visualization; favored for primary implants

**Transverse scrotal / penoscrotal approach**<sup>[[10]](#ref10)</sup>
- Single-incision access to the proximal bulbar urethra
- Shorter operative time (mean ~28 min); comparable outcomes in experienced hands
- Some series report slightly lower dry rates vs perineal approach

**Bladder neck placement**<sup>[[21]](#ref21)</sup>
- Used primarily in women and children, or men with bulbar urethral compromise
- Requires suprapubic incision; higher erosion risk

**Transalbugineal approach** (emerging)<sup>[[11]](#ref11)</sup>
- Cuff passed through the tunica albuginea of the corpora cavernosa
- May reduce erosion risk while preserving erectile function in select patients

### Key Principles

- Cuff placed around **bulbous urethra** in the majority of men; size typically 3.5–6.0 cm
- PRB placed in the **space of Retzius** (prevesical space)
- Meticulous hemostasis and atraumatic tissue handling to minimize urethral injury
- Perioperative antibiotic prophylaxis; InhibiZone coating provides additional protection
- Device left **deactivated** at closure; activated 6 weeks postoperatively once tissues have healed<sup>[[8]](#ref8)</sup>

## Outcomes

### Continence

| Endpoint | Rate | Source |
|---|---|---|
| Social continence (0–1 pad/day) | 60–83% | <sup>[[13]](#ref13)[[14]](#ref14)[[15]](#ref15)[[19]](#ref19)</sup> |
| Total dryness (0 pads/day) | 51–60% at 12 months | <sup>[[12]](#ref12)[[15]](#ref15)</sup> |
| &gt;50% pad weight reduction | ~94% at 12 months (AUSCO trial) | <sup>[[12]](#ref12)</sup> |
| Patient satisfaction | &gt;80%; 92–99% would recommend or repeat the procedure | <sup>[[13]](#ref13)[[19]](#ref19)</sup> |

The prospective multicenter **AUSCO trial** (Kaufman et al.<sup>[[12]](#ref12)</sup>, *J Urol* 2025) demonstrated 94% of patients achieved &gt;50% pad weight reduction and 60% reported zero pad use at 12 months, with significant quality-of-life improvements across all validated instruments.

**Institutional volume effect:** Higher-volume centers demonstrate better continence and lower revision rates — patient referral to experienced implanting surgeons is recommended.<sup>[[14]](#ref14)</sup>

---

## Complications

### Early
- Urinary retention: ~5.8%
- Hematoma / seroma
- Wound infection

### Late (requiring reoperation)

| Complication | Rate | Source |
|---|---|---|
| Infection | 0.5–10.6% | <sup>[[16]](#ref16)</sup> |
| Urethral erosion | 2.9–12% | <sup>[[4]](#ref4)[[16]](#ref16)</sup> |
| Mechanical failure (fluid leak, pump failure) | Most common long-term complication | <sup>[[16]](#ref16)[[17]](#ref17)</sup> |
| Urethral atrophy (recurrent incontinence with functional device) | 1.6–11.4% | <sup>[[16]](#ref16)[[17]](#ref17)</sup> |

**Infection and erosion** often occur together and mandate **complete device explantation**.<sup>[[16]](#ref16)</sup> Risk is substantially higher in radiated patients and those with neurogenic bladder.<sup>[[20]](#ref20)[[21]](#ref21)</sup> A minimum 3-month waiting period before reimplantation is generally recommended; the salvage wash-out technique (immediate reimplantation with antibiotic irrigation) can be considered in select non-eroded cases.

---

## Revision and Long-Term Durability

Revision burden is a critical counseling point — approximately **50% of patients require reoperation within 10 years**:<sup>[[17]](#ref17)[[18]](#ref18)</sup>

| Timepoint | Revision-free survival |
|---|---|
| 1 year | ~94% |
| 2 years | 71–88% |
| 5 years | 57–62% |
| 10 years | ~40% |

**Median time to first revision:** 6.6 years (Lenfant et al.<sup>[[18]](#ref18)</sup>, *J Urol* 2025).

### Causes of revision<sup>[[17]](#ref17)[[23]](#ref23)</sup>

| Failure mode | Share of revisions | Detail |
|---|---|---|
| **Non-mechanical failure (NOMECA)** | ~57% | Persistent/recurrent SUI with a functioning device — traditionally attributed to urethral atrophy, though the exact pathophysiology is debated |
| **Mechanical failure** | ~28% | Cuff (46% of mechanical failures) > abdominal reservoir (23%) > tubing (22%) > pump (10%) |
| **Infection / erosion** | ~11–15% | Urethral erosion mandates explantation |

### Risk factors for earlier revision<sup>[[17]](#ref17)[[20]](#ref20)[[22]](#ref22)</sup>

| Risk factor | Impact |
|---|---|
| **Pelvic radiation** | Earlier complications, higher erosion rates; 5-yr revision-free survival drops from 83% (no risk factors) → 73% (radiation alone) → 46% (radiation + urethroplasty)<sup>[[17]](#ref17)</sup> |
| **Larger initial cuff size** | Only independent predictor of overall revision (HR 1.04, p = 0.01) and NOMECA revision (HR 1.05, p = 0.004)<sup>[[22]](#ref22)</sup> |
| **Prior erosion history** | ~4× higher future erosion risk (RR 4.02, p = 0.02)<sup>[[36]](#ref36)</sup> |
| **High comorbidity burden** | Increased risk of removal/revision<sup>[[24]](#ref24)</sup> |
| **Diabetes / poor ASA status** | Earlier failure |
| **Low institutional volume** | Higher revision rates |

## Revision and Salvage Techniques

### Preoperative Evaluation

Before any revision, a systematic workup is essential:<sup>[[25]](#ref25)</sup>

- Physical examination and interview for signs of **infection** (scrotal erythema, purulence, pain)
- **Cystoscopy** to assess for urethral erosion
- Device function assessment (pump cycling, fluid status)
- If infection or erosion is present → **all components must be removed first**, with delayed reimplantation several months later
- If no infection or erosion → simultaneous device removal and reimplantation can be performed

### 1. Cuff Downsizing

Replacing the existing cuff with a smaller one to improve urethral coaptation in the setting of urethral atrophy. The introduction of the **3.5 cm cuff** in 2010 significantly reduced the need for revision in patients initially receiving a 4.0 cm cuff (revision rate dropped from 22.2% to 4.7%, p &lt; 0.001).<sup>[[26]](#ref26)</sup> However, downsizing is associated with a **higher rate of subsequent mechanical failure** compared to other revision techniques (p = 0.01).<sup>[[27]](#ref27)</sup>

### 2. Cuff Repositioning (Relocation)

Moving the cuff to a more proximal or distal site on the bulbar urethra. Appropriate when the original cuff site shows unhealthy tissue. Repositioning with the same cuff size is associated with a **higher rate of persistent incontinence** compared to other techniques (p = 0.02).<sup>[[27]](#ref27)</sup> Paradoxically, a multicenter NOMECA study found better functional outcomes with repositioning **when combined with complete device change**.<sup>[[22]](#ref22)</sup>

### 3. Tandem Cuff Placement

Adding a second cuff to increase the coaptive length of the urethra. This technique is associated with the **lowest rate of recurrent incontinence** among revision strategies (p = 0.02).<sup>[[27]](#ref27)</sup> In a series of 18 patients, pad use decreased from a mean of 4.3 to 1.6 per day (p &lt; 0.001).<sup>[[28]](#ref28)</sup> Tandem cuffs can be placed in standard or transcorporal fashion.<sup>[[29]](#ref29)</sup>

### 4. Transcorporal Cuff Placement

The cuff is placed through incisions in the **corporal tunica albuginea**, incorporating corporal tissue into the cuff circumference.<sup>[[30]](#ref30)</sup>

- Adds bulk to the urethra, allowing better cuff sizing at distal sites where the urethra is small
- Protects the urethra from dissection injury and reduces erosion risk by buttressing the vulnerable dorsal surface
- Achieves continence (0–1 pad) in approximately **84% of patients** at 17 months follow-up
- Particularly useful after prior erosion, urethral atrophy, or when strong periurethral adhesions preclude safe standard dissection<sup>[[25]](#ref25)</sup>

A multicenter analysis found that transcorporal placement **may not significantly decrease repeat complications after prior erosion**, particularly in irradiated patients (50% complication rate in irradiated vs. 17% in non-irradiated, p = 0.006).<sup>[[31]](#ref31)</sup> There is also a potential risk of **erectile function deterioration** in potent men.<sup>[[30]](#ref30)</sup>

### 5. Pressure-Regulating Balloon (PRB) Modification

Exchanging the PRB for a higher-pressure balloon (e.g., 71–80 cm H₂O instead of 61–70 cm H₂O) or adding fluid to the system. This is the least commonly used strategy and has limited evidence. The **6th International Consultation on Incontinence** lists changing the pressure balloon and increasing system fluid as options for recurrent SUI due to urethral atrophy.<sup>[[32]](#ref32)</sup>

### 6. Urethral Wrapping / Tissue Interposition

Using **small intestinal submucosa (SIS)** or other biomaterials as a wrap around the urethra before cuff placement to add bulk and serve as a buffer. In a series of 8 patients with multiple prior AUS failures and erosions, 38% achieved complete dryness, though patients with prior radiation accounted for 80% of failures.<sup>[[33]](#ref33)</sup>

### 7. Complete vs. Partial Device Replacement

A key decision at revision is whether to replace only the malfunctioning component or the entire device. Evidence increasingly favors **complete device replacement**:

- Complete change associated with significantly higher 3-month continence (83.3% vs. 63.3%, OR 2.7, p = 0.03) and better 5-year reoperation-free survival (82.2% vs. 69.6%, p = 0.03)<sup>[[22]](#ref22)</sup>
- Trend toward improved 3-year device survival with entire device replacement vs. single component (76% vs. 60%, p = 0.11)<sup>[[23]](#ref23)</sup>
- When a long interval has passed since initial implantation, complete replacement is recommended due to device aging and deterioration<sup>[[25]](#ref25)</sup>

## Salvage After Erosion or Infection

Management of AUS erosion requires a staged approach:<sup>[[24]](#ref24)[[25]](#ref25)</sup>

**Step 1 — Explantation of all device components.** Performing **in situ urethroplasty at the time of explantation** significantly reduces subsequent stricture formation (38% vs. 85%, p = 0.047) and facilitates future reimplantation (54% vs. 15% underwent secondary AUS, p = 0.04). Notably, urethral reapproximation **without** formal urethroplasty was associated with **increased stricture risk** (OR 4.2, p &lt; 0.05).<sup>[[24]](#ref24)[[34]](#ref34)</sup>

**Step 2 — Delayed reimplantation,** typically at a median of **9 months** after explantation.<sup>[[35]](#ref35)</sup>

### Outcomes of Salvage Reimplantation

| Outcome | Result |
|---|---|
| 5-year device survival after salvage | **68%** (vs. 76% for primary, p = 0.38)<sup>[[35]](#ref35)</sup> |
| Future erosion risk vs. virgin cases | **4×** higher (14.3% vs. 3.6%, p = 0.02)<sup>[[36]](#ref36)</sup> |
| Repeat erosion / re-explantation in salvage cohort | ~40% in one large series<sup>[[24]](#ref24)</sup> |
| Functioning salvage AUS at last follow-up | **36%** of erosion patients<sup>[[24]](#ref24)</sup> |
| Restoration of baseline continence at 2nd/3rd revision | **89%**<sup>[[37]](#ref37)</sup> |

---

## Salvage Options for Patients Not Candidates for Reimplantation

For patients with severely compromised urethral tissue who cannot undergo further AUS placement:

- **Permanent urethral ligation (PUL)** with suprapubic tube drainage achieved continence in 90% of patients (mean age 75), with 55% experiencing complications (mostly Clavien-Dindo Grade II–III). Over 30 months of follow-up, most patients reported improved quality of life.<sup>[[38]](#ref38)</sup>
- **Bladder neck closure** with catheterizable stoma or supravesical diversion remains an option for the most refractory cases.

---

## Sequential Salvage Paradigm

A proposed sequential approach to maximize the duration of AUS-based incontinence therapy:<sup>[[30]](#ref30)[[39]](#ref39)</sup>

1. **Primary standard bulbar cuff** → if failure occurs →
2. **Distal double cuff or cuff downsizing/relocation** → if failure occurs →
3. **Transcorporal cuff placement** → if failure occurs →
4. **Permanent urethral ligation or urinary diversion**

A prospective comparison of distal double cuff (DC) vs. transcorporal cuff (TC) in salvage settings showed comparable continence (88%/94% social continence for DC vs. 72%/100% for TC, p = NS) and similar explantation-free survival.<sup>[[39]](#ref39)</sup>

---

## Key Principles of Revision and Salvage

- Outcomes of secondary AUS implantation are **comparable to primary implantation** (5-year durability ~80–88%); salvage of a good outcome is achievable even after multiple revisions<sup>[[37]](#ref37)</sup>
- **Complete device replacement** appears superior to partial replacement for both mechanical and non-mechanical failures<sup>[[22]](#ref22)[[23]](#ref23)</sup>
- **Tandem cuff placement** yields the best continence outcomes among revision techniques<sup>[[27]](#ref27)</sup>
- **Transcorporal cuff placement** is a valuable option when urethral tissue is compromised, though it does not eliminate erosion risk in irradiated patients<sup>[[30]](#ref30)[[31]](#ref31)</sup>
- **In situ urethroplasty at explantation** for erosion significantly reduces stricture formation and improves the likelihood of successful reimplantation<sup>[[34]](#ref34)</sup>
- Patients undergoing second or third AUS implantation can achieve revision-free survival comparable to their primary device; multiple risk factors (radiation + prior revision + large cuff) substantially reduce durability<sup>[[20]](#ref20)</sup>

## Special Populations

**Post-radiation:** Earlier device failure but still meaningfully effective; patients should be counseled on reduced durability and higher erosion risk.<sup>[[20]](#ref20)</sup>

**Neurogenic bladder:** AUS can be offered to selected patients with adequate upper extremity function; bladder storage parameters must be optimized (often with antimuscarinic therapy or Botox) before implantation to avoid unsafe storage pressures with a competent outlet.<sup>[[7]](#ref7)[[21]](#ref21)</sup>

**Concurrent penile prosthesis:** Simultaneous or staged implantation can be safely performed in experienced hands; careful attention to scrotal pump positioning avoids device-on-device interference.<sup>[[21]](#ref21)</sup>

**Women:** AUS via bladder neck cuff is an option for severe SUI refractory to all other interventions; data are more limited than in men.<sup>[[3]](#ref3)[[21]](#ref21)</sup>

---

## Postoperative Management

- **Deactivation period:** Device remains deactivated for **4–8 weeks** (typically 6 weeks) to allow tissue healing around the cuff before first activation<sup>[[8]](#ref8)</sup>
- **Activation visit:** Clinician confirms wound healing and trains patient on pump cycling technique
- **Patient ID card:** Patients carry a permanent card identifying the implant; emergency staff must be informed — **no urethral catheter or instrument should be passed without first deactivating the device**
- **MRI compatibility:** Varies by component generation; device identification and manufacturer consultation required before any MRI

| Point | Detail |

| Social continence probability | ~80% (0–1 pad/day) |
| Complete dryness probability | ~50–60% |

| Device lifespan | Lifelong implant; manual operation required daily |
| Medical precaution | Must deactivate before any urethral instrumentation |
