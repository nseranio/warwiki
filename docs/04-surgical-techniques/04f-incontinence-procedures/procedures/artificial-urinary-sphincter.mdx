# Artificial Urinary Sphincter (Procedure)

The **artificial urinary sphincter (AUS)** is the gold standard surgical treatment for moderate-to-severe male stress urinary incontinence (SUI), particularly following prostate surgery.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[2]](#ref2)[[3]](#ref3)</sup> The AMS 800™ is the predominant device, descended from Scott's 1972 design, with more than 250,000 implanted worldwide.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[5]](#ref5)[[9]](#ref9)</sup> It provides durable continence restoration through a completely implantable, fluid-filled hydraulic system that mimics voluntary sphincter function.

---

## Device Components

The AMS 800 consists of three silicone components connected by kink-resistant tubing forming a closed hydraulic circuit:

![AMS 800 components and fluid cycle: Male pelvis, simplified device schematic](/img/diagrams/aus-components.svg)

*A urethral cuff, scrotal pump and pressure-regulating balloon form a closed fluid circuit. Pumping opens the cuff; gradual fluid return closes it. Implant location, pressure selection and handling are device- and patient-specific. (Original WARWIKI schematic; see the figure source record and review limits.)*

| Component | Location | Function |
|---|---|---|
| **Occlusive Cuff** | Bulbous urethra (men) or bladder neck | Applies circumferential compression to maintain continence; 13 sizes (3.5–11.0 cm)<sup>[[6]](#ref6)[[74]](#ref74)</sup> |
| **Pressure-Regulating Balloon (PRB)** | Prevesical (space of Retzius) | Maintains constant system pressure; most common range **61–70 cmH₂O** |
| **Control Pump** | Scrotum (men) / labia majora (women) | Patient-operated valve mechanism; contains a **deactivation button** to keep cuff open |

The cuff and pump are available with InhibiZone™, a rifampin/minocycline hydrochloride surface treatment intended to reduce colonization; the PRB is not treated. It does not replace perioperative antibiotic prophylaxis. The manufacturer's comparative evidence is a voluntary implant registry of infection-related surgical revisions, with incomplete reporting and no capture of infections treated without component surgery; it does not establish a randomized reduction in all infections. For label contraindications and device details, see the [AUS device page](/docs/foundations/tools/biomaterials/prosthetics/artificial-urinary-sphincter).<sup>[[74]](#ref74)</sup>

## System Operation

The AUS cycles between continence and voiding by fluid transfer between the cuff and balloon. Patients are trained to operate it at the activation visit and must demonstrate competent cycling before leaving on an active device.<sup>[[57]](#ref57)</sup>

### Voiding cycle

1. **At rest (continent):** the cuff is fluid-filled, applying circumferential urethral compression sufficient to prevent leakage.
2. **To void:** the patient locates the scrotal pump and squeezes and releases the bulb several times, transferring fluid from the cuff to the PRB. The cuff deflates and the urethra opens.
3. **Auto-refill:** the PRB passively returns fluid to the cuff over the next few minutes (the IFU describes this as a few minutes; slow or weak streams may need the patient to deflate the cuff again to finish voiding), restoring continence with no further patient action.<sup>[[74]](#ref74)</sup>

### Deactivation and reactivation

The control pump has two palpable parts: a **soft lower bulb** the patient squeezes to pump, and a **firmer upper block** housing the refill resistor and the **deactivation button**.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[57]](#ref57)</sup> After the cuff has been emptied, pressing the button as described below prevents refilling. Pressing it while the cuff is inflated can instead trap fluid in the cuff.<sup>[[74]](#ref74)</sup>

- **To deactivate (AMS 800 male IFU):** squeeze and release the pump bulb until the cuff empties and the bulb remains flat. Allow the bulb to partially refill, approximately 30–60 seconds, then press the deactivation button while a slight indentation remains in the pump bulb. The partly filled bulb retains this indentation; the button itself remains palpable in either state. Confirm deactivation before instrumentation.<sup>[[74]](#ref74)</sup>
- **To reactivate:** the IFU instructs pressing the deactivation button a few times to loosen the valve poppet, then giving the pump bulb a quick, forceful squeeze. The pump fills first, followed by the cuff over a few minutes. If activation is difficult, use the manufacturer's troubleshooting sequence rather than repeated blind force. Initial activation and training should be supervised.<sup>[[74]](#ref74)</sup>

**Deflated is not the same as deactivated.** A cuff that has only been pumped down will auto-refill within a few minutes; it stays open *only* while the deactivation button is engaged. This is the crux of safe catheterization (below); emptying the cuff without deactivating lets it re-pressurize around an indwelling catheter.<sup>[[57]](#ref57)[[72]](#ref72)</sup>

At the close of surgery the system is filled, cycled to purge air, and left locked open with the cuff empty, not merely deflated, for the healing period.<sup>[[57]](#ref57)[[71]](#ref71)</sup> Deactivation is then used deliberately in several settings: the postoperative period until activation (see [Postoperative Management](#postoperative-management)); before any urethral catheterization or instrumentation; electively overnight in selected patients to decompress the urethra and potentially limit atrophy; and in the obtunded or hospitalized patient who cannot self-cycle.<sup>[[57]](#ref57)[[71]](#ref71)</sup>

**At activation,** the clinician first confirms the pump sits in the dependent scrotum (teaching the patient to gently "milk" it down if it has ridden up), then activates the device using its instructions and watches the patient cycle it competently. Counsel patients not to forcibly squeeze the pump while it is deactivated, as a hard squeeze can inadvertently reactivate it.<sup>[[57]](#ref57)</sup>

### Catheterization and urethral instrumentation in an AUS patient

A cuff left inflated around a catheter causes pressure necrosis and erosion, yet only about a third of surveyed urologists (34%) felt able to instrument an AUS patient independently in an emergency before brief training, making it a high-yield safety point.<sup>[[72]](#ref72)</sup> Whenever urethral access is needed:<sup>[[57]](#ref57)[[72]](#ref72)</sup>

- **Deactivate the device first** (cuff open) before passing any catheter, cystoscope, or sound; never instrument an active AUS.
- Use the smallest catheter that will do the job (≤14 Fr) for the shortest possible time.
- If indwelling drainage is expected to exceed 48 hours, place a suprapubic catheter instead (image-guided, to avoid the device); by 7 days of urethral catheterization a suprapubic tube is mandatory to limit erosion risk.
- In the obtunded patient who only needs output monitoring, deactivate and use a condom catheter or pad weights rather than an indwelling urethral catheter.
- Urology must be consulted whenever an AUS patient needs catheterization, and patients should wear a MedicAlert bracelet / carry a device card so emergency staff deactivate before instrumenting.

## Indications

Primary indications for AUS implantation:<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[2]](#ref2)[[8]](#ref8)</sup>

- **Bothersome SUI of any severity after prostate treatment.** The AUA/GURS/SUFU guideline strongly recommends discussing AUS for mild to severe SUI;<sup>[[77]](#ref77)</sup> most implants are for moderate-to-severe leakage (typically &gt;2 pads/day, or 24-hour pad weight &gt;400 g) and account for &gt;90% of male implants
- Failed conservative management (pelvic floor physiotherapy, behavioral modification); the AUA/GURS/SUFU guideline says surgery should be offered at 12 months for bothersome SUI that persists despite conservative therapy, and may be offered from 6 months when leakage is not improving (men with severe SUI seldom improve after 6 months)<sup>[[77]](#ref77)</sup>
- Intrinsic sphincter deficiency from radical prostatectomy, cryotherapy, HIFU, TURP, or radiation
- Neurogenic bladder with SUI in select patients with adequate dexterity<sup>[[7]](#ref7)[[21]](#ref21)</sup>
- Women with severe SUI refractory to other treatments (limited data)<sup>[[21]](#ref21)</sup>

**Absolute contraindications:** Active UTI or urethral erosion, inadequate manual dexterity or cognition to operate the pump, uncontrolled detrusor overactivity incompatible with safe storage pressures.

## Preoperative Assessment

| Evaluation | Purpose |
|---|---|
| **Cystoscopy** | Exclude urethral stricture, bladder neck contracture, bladder pathology, cancer recurrence |
| **Urodynamics** | Selective: use when diagnosis or counselling is uncertain; highest yield in severe radiation cystitis or neurogenic dysfunction. Detrusor overactivity and small capacity have not been shown to worsen AUS outcomes, but poor compliance with high storage pressure needs treatment or surveillance<sup>[[77]](#ref77)</sup> |
| **24-hour pad weight** | Quantify severity; severe leakage favors AUS over sling (sling studies generally defined severe as &gt;500 g/day or &gt;5 pads/day)<sup>[[77]](#ref77)</sup> |
| **Manual dexterity assessment** | Patient must reliably operate scrotal pump |
| **PSA** | Exclude cancer recurrence prior to implant |
| **Infection screening** | Clear any UTI before proceeding |

## Surgical Technique

### Approaches

The AUA/GURS/SUFU guideline preferentially recommends a single-cuff perineal approach (moderate recommendation). In non-randomized comparisons, transverse scrotal placement gave lower dry rates and more short-term explantation (19% versus 9%), and tandem cuffs gave equivalent continence with more explants (17% versus 4% at 48 months); both are reserved for specific situations.<sup>[[77]](#ref77)</sup>

**Perineal approach** (traditional, most common)
- Midline perineal incision for cuff dissection around the bulbous urethra
- Separate small lower abdominal / inguinal incision for PRB placement
- Optimal visualization; favored for primary implants

**Transverse scrotal / penoscrotal approach**<sup>[[10]](#ref10)</sup>
- Single-incision access to the proximal bulbar urethra
- Shorter operative time (mean approximately 28 min in a 156-man series); comparable outcomes in experienced hands; the AMS patient-information-form database (n = 27,096) associated the penoscrotal route with higher explantation and revision rates<sup>[[45]](#ref45)</sup>
- A 2025 meta-analysis of six observational studies (595 men) found similar dry rates, social continence and complications; the penoscrotal route saved about 33 minutes, and the perineal route was associated with larger cuffs<sup>[[76]](#ref76)</sup>

**Bladder neck placement**<sup>[[21]](#ref21)</sup>
- Used primarily in women and children, or men with bulbar urethral compromise
- Requires suprapubic incision; higher erosion risk

**Transalbugineal approach** (emerging)<sup>[[11]](#ref11)</sup>
- Cuff passed through the tunica albuginea of the corpora cavernosa
- Proposed to buttress the urethra in high-risk tissue while preserving erectile function in select patients; the AUA 2024 guideline notes that recent reports have not shown it protects against later erosion<sup>[[77]](#ref77)</sup>

### Key Principles

- Cuff placed around bulbous urethra in the majority of men; size typically 3.5–6.0 cm (the operating room manual notes 4.0 or 4.5 cm is typically needed at the bulbous urethra)<sup>[[74]](#ref74)[[79]](#ref79)</sup>
- PRB placed in the space of Retzius (prevesical space)
- Meticulous hemostasis and atraumatic tissue handling to minimize urethral injury
- Perioperative antibiotic prophylaxis according to the local implant protocol; InhibiZone does not replace it. Do not soak treated components; the IFU allows a brief sterile rinse or dip immediately before implantation.<sup>[[74]](#ref74)</sup>
- Device left deactivated at closure; activated about 4–6 weeks postoperatively once tissues have healed (manufacturer IFU)<sup>[[74]](#ref74)</sup>

## Outcomes

### Continence

| Endpoint | Rate | Source |
|---|---|---|
| Social continence (0–1 pad/day) | 60–83% | <sup>[[13]](#ref13)[[14]](#ref14)[[15]](#ref15)[[19]](#ref19)</sup> |
| Total dryness (0 pads/day) | 51–60% at 12 months | <sup>[[12]](#ref12)[[15]](#ref15)</sup> |
| ≥50% pad weight reduction | 91/97 evaluable participants (94%) at 12 months (AUSCO trial) | <sup>[[12]](#ref12)</sup> |
| Patient satisfaction | &gt;80%; 92–99% would recommend or repeat the procedure | <sup>[[13]](#ref13)[[19]](#ref19)</sup> |

The prospective, single-arm AUSCO trial implanted 115 men at 17 sites. At 12 months, 91/97 evaluable participants (94%) achieved ≥50% pad-weight reduction, while 61/101 (60%) reported zero pad use. These are different outcomes with different denominators; 94% is not the dry rate. Nine of 115 participants (7.8%) underwent revision, with zero revisions attributed to device infection. This does not mean zero postoperative infection: the Results report three wound infections, all resolving without erosion or explantation. Follow-up was one year after activation, and the single-arm study does not establish comparative long-term safety. The study appeared online in October 2025 and in the February 2026 journal issue.<sup>[[12]](#ref12)</sup>

**Institutional volume effect:** Higher-volume centers demonstrate better continence and lower revision rates; patient referral to experienced implanting surgeons is recommended.<sup>[[14]](#ref14)</sup>

## Complications

### Early
- Urinary retention: reported in roughly 8–32% of implants, higher with transcorporal cuff placement<sup>[[16]](#ref16)</sup>
- Hematoma / seroma
- Wound infection

### Late (requiring reoperation)

| Complication | Rate | Source |
|---|---|---|
| Infection | 1–33% across series | <sup>[[16]](#ref16)</sup> |
| Urethral erosion | 3–28% across series (about 8% overall) | <sup>[[16]](#ref16)</sup> |
| Mechanical failure (fluid leak, pump failure) | Most common long-term complication | <sup>[[16]](#ref16)[[17]](#ref17)</sup> |
| Recurrent incontinence with a functioning device ("urethral atrophy") | Not reliably quantified; whether atrophy is a distinct cause is debated (see below) | <sup>[[16]](#ref16)[[51]](#ref51)</sup> |

Infection and erosion often occur together and mandate complete device explantation.<sup>[[16]](#ref16)</sup> Cross-sectional imaging can help characterize component position and complications when the presentation is unclear.<sup>[[4]](#ref4)</sup> Risk is substantially higher in radiated patients and those with neurogenic bladder.<sup>[[20]](#ref20)[[21]](#ref21)</sup> Explant all components and wait at least 3 months (3–6 months in the guideline) before reimplantation; wash-out with immediate replacement has not been shown reliable, even without purulence.<sup>[[77]](#ref77)</sup> Long-term series in the guideline report infection in under 1–5% and erosion in 1–10%, and device failure of about 24% at 5 years and 50% at 10 years.<sup>[[77]](#ref77)</sup>

## Revision and Long-Term Durability

Revision burden is a necessary counseling point, with approximately 50% of patients requiring reoperation within 10 years:<sup>[[17]](#ref17)[[18]](#ref18)</sup>

| Timepoint | Revision-free survival |
|---|---|
| 1 year | ~94% |
| 2 years | 71–88% |
| 5 years | 57–62% |
| 10 years | ~40% |

**Median time to first revision:** 6.6 years (Lenfant et al.<sup>[[18]](#ref18)</sup>, *J Urol* 2025).

### Causes of revision

Bentellis et al. retrospectively studied 1,020 nonneurological men and excluded revisions/explantations for infection or erosion. Among 214 included revisions, 121 (56.5%) were nonmechanical, 59 (27.6%) mechanical and 34 (15.9%) other causes. Nonmechanical failure included persistent/recurrent SUI with an apparently functioning device; it was not a diagnosis of tissue atrophy in every patient. These percentages cannot be combined with an infection/erosion percentage to describe all AUS reoperations.<sup>[[17]](#ref17)</sup>

In a separate mechanical-failure cohort, Linder et al. identified 125 malfunctions among 1,082 primary placements. The failed component was the cuff in 46.1%, reservoir in 22.6%, tubing in 21.7% and pump in 9.6%. These are shares of mechanical failures, not risks for all implanted patients.<sup>[[23]](#ref23)</sup>

### Risk factors for earlier revision<sup>[[17]](#ref17)[[20]](#ref20)[[22]](#ref22)</sup>

| Risk factor | Impact |
|---|---|
| **Pelvic radiation** | Earlier complications, higher erosion rates; 5-yr revision-free survival drops from 83% (no risk factors) → 73% (radiation alone) → 46% (radiation + urethroplasty)<sup>[[17]](#ref17)</sup> |
| **Larger initial cuff size** | Only independent predictor of overall revision (HR 1.04, p = 0.01) and NOMECA revision (HR 1.05, p = 0.004) in the retrospective Bentellis cohort, which excluded infection/erosion revisions; this association does not establish a cuff-sizing rule.<sup>[[17]](#ref17)</sup> |
| **Prior erosion history** | ~4× higher future erosion risk (RR 4.02, p = 0.02)<sup>[[36]](#ref36)</sup> |
| **High comorbidity burden** | Increased risk of removal/revision<sup>[[24]](#ref24)</sup> |
| **Diabetes / poor ASA status** | Earlier failure |
| **Low institutional volume** | Higher revision rates in some series;<sup>[[14]](#ref14)</sup> surgeon volume was not an independent predictor in the AMS patient-information-form database<sup>[[45]](#ref45)</sup> |

## Revision and Salvage Techniques

### Preoperative Evaluation

Before any revision, a systematic workup is needed:<sup>[[25]](#ref25)</sup>

- Physical examination and interview for signs of infection (scrotal erythema, purulence, pain)
- **Cystoscopy** to assess for urethral erosion
- Device function assessment (pump cycling, fluid status)
- If infection or erosion is present, all components must be removed first, with delayed reimplantation several months later
- If no infection or erosion, simultaneous device removal and reimplantation can be performed

### Capsular Constriction, Balloon Aging and the Atrophy Question

A constricted or "hourglass" appearance beneath an explanted cuff can reflect periurethral capsule formation. In Bugeja et al.'s uncontrolled exploration series, 19 of 50 patients had no identified cause beyond low leak-point/closure pressures. In six patients whose restrictive sheath was excised, urethral circumference increased immediately; tested explanted PRBs showed pressure loss. Replacement with a same-size cuff and same-pressure PRB succeeded in 12 of 14 patients from that 19-patient subgroup. The six capsule-release observations and 14 replacement outcomes are different denominators.<sup>[[51]](#ref51)</sup>

Terlecki and Wilson's narrative expert review discusses capsule release, remeasurement and device replacement as an alternative to reflexive downsizing. These observations support evaluating both capsular constriction and PRB aging; they do not establish that all recurrent incontinence results from these mechanisms or that tissue atrophy never occurs.<sup>[[52]](#ref52)[[53]](#ref53)</sup>

**Operative application:** consider careful capsulotomy/capsulectomy in selected revisions when a constrictive capsule is present and tissue can be protected. Reassess urethral circumference after release when performed. Choose cuff size, cuff location and component replacement from the actual tissue and device findings; neither routine capsule excision nor automatic same-size replacement has proven superiority for every nonmechanical failure.<sup>[[51]](#ref51)[[52]](#ref52)</sup>

### 1. Cuff Downsizing

Replacing the existing cuff with a smaller one to improve urethral coaptation in the setting of presumed urethral atrophy, which is the most common NOMECA revision (approximately 69% of NOMECA revisions in the largest multicenter series).<sup>[[22]](#ref22)</sup>

- **Continence outcomes (Krughoff 2023):** 97% subjective improvement, 93% decreased pad use, mean −2.2 ± 1.45 pads/day (p < 0.001).<sup>[[40]](#ref40)</sup>
- **Durability:** at median 1.8-yr follow-up, 70.6% of downsized cuffs remained in place; 11.8% removed for erosion, 5.9% replaced for mechanical failure, 5.9% for herniated PRB.<sup>[[40]](#ref40)</sup>
- **Pad reduction (Saffarian 2003):** in the original downsizing series for sub-cuff atrophy, pad use fell from 3.9 to 0.5 per day with 80% satisfaction.<sup>[[41]](#ref41)</sup>
- **Tradeoff:** downsizing carries a higher rate of subsequent mechanical failure versus other revision techniques (p = 0.01), hypothesized to reflect increased cuff-to-urethra pressure with a tighter cuff.<sup>[[27]](#ref27)</sup>
- **Impact of the 3.5 cm cuff:** introduction in 2010 cut revision rates in patients initially receiving 4.0 cm cuffs from 22.2% to 4.7% (p < 0.001) — supporting accurate primary sizing as a prevention strategy.<sup>[[26]](#ref26)</sup>

Capsule release and same-size replacement are alternatives in selected patients; the limited evidence does not establish that they should replace downsizing in most cases.<sup>[[51]](#ref51)[[52]](#ref52)</sup>

### 2. Cuff Repositioning (Relocation)

Moving the cuff to a more proximal or distal site on the bulbar urethra. Appropriate when the original site shows unhealthy tissue.

- **Better 3-month complete continence than cuff downsizing** in the multicenter NOMECA study (p = 0.04).<sup>[[22]](#ref22)</sup>
- **Proximal repositioning (Couillard / Stone, 1995):** 5/6 (83%) significantly improved at > 1 yr; the single failure had poor detrusor compliance on preoperative UDS in addition to sphincter weakness.<sup>[[42]](#ref42)</sup>
- **Tradeoff:** repositioning with the same cuff size alone is associated with a higher rate of persistent incontinence (p = 0.02); the benefit appears to depend on appropriate re-measurement / sizing at the new site.<sup>[[27]](#ref27)</sup>

### 3. Tandem Cuff Placement

Adding a second cuff to increase the coaptive length of the urethra.

- **Lowest rate of recurrent incontinence** among revision techniques (Eswara multicenter; p = 0.02).<sup>[[27]](#ref27)</sup>
- **DiMarco / Elliott** series of 18 patients: pad use 4.3 ± 0.35 to 1.6 ± 0.42 per day (p < 0.001); reoperations for cuff leakage (1) and cuff erosion (2, one with 3 prior revisions including a high-pressure balloon).<sup>[[28]](#ref28)</sup>
- **Single versus double cuff at primary placement (O'Connor 2008):** no significant difference in continence, dry rate, or QoL (IIQ-7 14.8 to 4.1 single-cuff versus 16.3 to 6.4 double-cuff, p = 0.34), but double-cuff patients underwent 12 additional surgeries for complications versus 7 for single-cuff.<sup>[[43]](#ref43)</sup>
- **Cadaver model (Manka / Wright):** tandem cuff did not significantly improve retrograde leak point pressure versus a single cuff (73.5 versus 77.75 versus 79.25 cm H₂O for distal, proximal, and tandem, p = 0.44); the perceived benefit of tandem cuffs may reflect the more proximal placement of one cuff rather than the dual-cuff mechanism itself.<sup>[[44]](#ref44)</sup>
- **AMS PIF database (n = 27,096):** tandem cuff (8.2% of cases) was associated with higher explantation on multivariate analysis, likely reflecting selection bias toward higher-risk patients.<sup>[[45]](#ref45)</sup>
- **Ahyai 2016** prospectively compared 57 low-risk single-cuff versus 123 high-risk double-cuff: no significant difference in pad usage or objective continence, but double-cuff patients reported superior subjective / social continence and less persistent UI (p ≤ 0.02), at a cost of 5.7× higher late explantation risk (p = 0.02) and shorter explantation-free survival (p = 0.003), again confounded by underlying risk.<sup>[[46]](#ref46)</sup>

Tandem cuffs can be placed in standard or transcorporal fashion.<sup>[[29]](#ref29)</sup>

### 4. Transcorporal (TC) Cuff Placement

The cuff is placed through incisions in the corporal tunica albuginea, incorporating corporal tissue into the cuff circumference.<sup>[[30]](#ref30)</sup>

- Adds bulk to the urethra; allows better cuff sizing at distal sites where the urethra is small.
- Protects the urethra from dissection injury and buttresses the vulnerable dorsal surface.
- Particularly useful after prior erosion, urethral atrophy, or when strong periurethral adhesions preclude safe standard dissection.<sup>[[25]](#ref25)</sup>
- Counsel re: potential erectile-function deterioration in potent men.<sup>[[30]](#ref30)</sup>

**Systematic review and meta-analysis (Domínguez Gutiérrez 2026, 20 studies; comparative studies pooled):** TC versus standard showed:<sup>[[47]](#ref47)</sup>

| Outcome | TC vs standard |
| --- | --- |
| Revision rate | **Higher** (OR 2.99, 95% CI 1.16–7.75) |
| Explantation rate | No significant difference (OR 1.41, 0.27–7.24) |
| **Infection** | **Lower** (OR 0.33, 0.12–0.95) |
| **Erosion** | **Lower** (OR 0.35, 0.15–0.81) |
| Continence / QoL | Comparable |

Series:

- **Guralnick / Webster (n = 31, mean follow-up 17 mo):** 84% achieved 0–1 pads/day; no erosion or infection in transcorporally placed cuffs.<sup>[[30]](#ref30)</sup>
- **Wiedemann 2013 (n = 23, median 20 mo):** 8/17 (47%) perfectly dry, 5/17 (29%) social continence at < 1 pad, improvement in QoL.<sup>[[48]](#ref48)</sup>
- **Ortiz "erosion heat map" 2020:** erosion in 18.3% of TC versus 6.1% of standard cases (p < 0.05) in the heat-mapped cohort — anatomic distribution of erosion is similar between approaches.<sup>[[49]](#ref49)</sup>
- **Mock 2015 (n = 37 TC):** overall explantation 18.9%; ≥ 2 urethral risk factors were associated with 1.65× higher erosion probability (95% CI 1.3–2.2); 35-mo erosion-free survival 100% with 0–1 risk factors versus 64% with ≥ 2 (p < 0.001); all 4 erosion cases had prior radiation.<sup>[[50]](#ref50)</sup>
- **Moser 2018 (TC after prior erosion, n = 34):** recurrent complication 32.4%, recurrent erosion 26.4%. No significant difference versus non-TC group (p = 0.438), but irradiated TC patients had significantly higher repeat-complication rate (p = 0.006); TC does not eliminate erosion risk in irradiated tissue.<sup>[[31]](#ref31)</sup>

### 5. Tandem Transcorporal Cuff

Combines the proximal protection of a tandem second cuff with the dorsal-tissue augmentation of TC placement. **Magera / Elliott series (n = 18, median follow-up 26 mo):** median pad use from 5.0 to 2.0 (p < 0.001); 69% used ≤2 pads/day, and two devices were explanted (erosion, infection), with androgen-deprivation therapy in 4 of the 5 patients with little or no improvement; reserved for the most compromised urethras after multiple prior revisions.<sup>[[29]](#ref29)</sup>

### 6. Distal Double Cuff vs Transcorporal — Comparative Outcomes

**Maurer 2019** prospectively compared 58 distal-double-cuff (DC) versus 13 TC patients (all high-risk salvage cases, median 24 mo follow-up):<sup>[[39]](#ref39)</sup>

| | DC | TC | p |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Objective continence | 88% | 72% | 0.37 |
| Social continence | 94% | 100% | 1.0 |
| Explantation-free survival | — | — | 0.399 |

No significant differences in infection, erosion, mechanical failure, or explantation. The authors proposed a sequential salvage strategy: DC first, then TC if DC fails, maximizing total duration of AUS-based therapy across the patient's lifetime.

### 7. Pressure-Regulating Balloon (PRB) Modification

Exchanging the PRB for a higher-pressure balloon (e.g., 71–80 cm H₂O instead of 61–70 cm H₂O) or adding fluid to the system. Listed by the 6th International Consultation on Incontinence as an option for recurrent SUI due to atrophy.<sup>[[32]](#ref32)</sup>

- **PRB herniation** through the external inguinal ring is a correctable cause of AUS malfunction in approximately 3.2% of patients, accessible at revision and often missed.<sup>[[54]](#ref54)</sup>
- **Loh-Doyle 2020** identified predictors of device-related complications after exchange to a 71–80 cmH₂O balloon (n = 55): 38% required operative repair or removal at a median of 26 months (erosion 7%). Higher BMI predicted mechanical failure, while hypertension and lower BMI predicted erosion; radiotherapy was not a predictor in this cohort.<sup>[[55]](#ref55)</sup>

Higher-pressure balloons require careful assessment of tissue quality and erosion risk. Capsule release, component exchange and complete replacement are individualized options; an expert revision review does not establish one preferred strategy for all patients.<sup>[[52]](#ref52)[[55]](#ref55)</sup>

**"Drain and retain" the old PRB.** When a revision needs a new balloon but the old PRB is densely adherent in the space of Retzius, the existing balloon can be emptied and intentionally left in situ rather than dissected out, sparing a hazardous retropubic dissection near the bladder, iliac vessels, and bowel; a fresh PRB is placed (typically high submuscular or contralateral). Aspirate all fluid, place the tubing on traction, and transect it proximally to defunctionalize the balloon. Cefalu et al. reported AUS and IPP balloons/reservoirs drained-and-retained with infection rates matching virgin implants (1.8% versus 1.5%, p = 0.88);<sup>[[69]](#ref69)</sup> a 7-center IPP series (Pereira et al., 2026) found no complications attributable to retained reservoirs at approximately 12.6 months.<sup>[[70]](#ref70)</sup> Contraindicated when explanting for infection (nidus risk), and a retained intraperitoneal balloon can present years later as small bowel obstruction; confirm an extraperitoneal position. The full evidence is collated on the [penile prosthesis revision-scenarios page](/docs/surgical-techniques/04j-sexual-dysfunction/penile-implants/revision-scenarios#drain-and-retain).

### 8. Urethral Wrapping / Tissue Interposition

Using small intestinal submucosa (SIS) or other biomaterials as a wrap around the urethra before cuff placement to add bulk and serve as a buffer. **Trost / Elliott series (n = 8 with multiple prior AUS failures / erosions):** 38% completely dry at median 12.4 mo; 37.5% required explantation (2 erosion, 1 infection); prior radiation accounted for 80% of failures; temporary postoperative retention in all. Reserved for the most complex salvage cases; it carries a substantial complication burden in this small series.<sup>[[33]](#ref33)</sup>

### 9. Complete vs. Partial Device Replacement

A decision at revision is whether to replace the malfunctioning component or the entire device. Available comparisons are observational and affected by selection and device age:

- In the multicenter NOMECA cohort, complete change was associated with higher 3-month complete continence (83.3% versus 63.3%, adjusted OR 2.7, 95% CI 1.1–7.1) and longer reported reoperation/explantation-free survival. Selection and other confounding limit causal interpretation.<sup>[[22]](#ref22)</sup>
- In Linder's mechanical-failure cohort, three-year device survival was 76% after entire-device replacement versus 60% after single-component replacement, but the difference was not statistically significant (p = 0.11); no validated time cutoff favoring one strategy was identified.<sup>[[23]](#ref23)</sup>
- When a long interval has passed since initial implantation, complete replacement is recommended due to device aging and deterioration<sup>[[25]](#ref25)</sup>

## Salvage After Erosion or Infection

Management of AUS erosion requires a staged approach:<sup>[[24]](#ref24)[[25]](#ref25)</sup>

**Step 1. Explantation of all device components.** Performing in situ urethroplasty at the time of explantation significantly reduces subsequent stricture formation (38% versus 85%, p = 0.047) and facilitates future reimplantation (54% versus 15% underwent secondary AUS, p = 0.04). Urethral reapproximation without formal urethroplasty was associated with increased stricture risk (OR 4.2, p &lt; 0.05).<sup>[[24]](#ref24)[[34]](#ref34)</sup>

**Step 2. Delayed reimplantation,** typically at a median of 9 months after explantation.<sup>[[35]](#ref35)</sup>

:::info[July 2026 evidence: patency and wound healing are separate outcomes]
Park et al. retrospectively studied 16 men undergoing immediate repair during explantation for erosion. At median 14 months, one developed stricture (15/16 stricture-free), but 7/16 had superficial wound dehiscence requiring secondary closure. Seven underwent another AUS implant; two subsequently required explantation for recurrent infection. This small, uncontrolled series supports considering immediate repair in selected tissue while counseling about substantial wound morbidity. It does not establish that immediate repair is preferable for every erosion.<sup>[[73]](#ref73)</sup>
:::

### Outcomes of Salvage Reimplantation

| Outcome | Result |
|---|---|
| 5-year device survival after salvage | **68%** (vs. 76% for primary, p = 0.38)<sup>[[35]](#ref35)</sup> |
| Future erosion risk vs. virgin cases | **4×** higher (14.3% vs. 3.6%, p = 0.02)<sup>[[36]](#ref36)</sup> |
| Repeat erosion / re-explantation in salvage cohort | ~40% in one large series<sup>[[24]](#ref24)</sup> |
| Functioning salvage AUS at last follow-up | **36%** of erosion patients<sup>[[24]](#ref24)</sup> |
| Restoration of baseline continence at 2nd/3rd revision | **89%**<sup>[[37]](#ref37)</sup> |

## Salvage Options for Patients Not Candidates for Reimplantation

For patients with severely compromised urethral tissue who cannot undergo further AUS placement:

- **[Permanent urethral ligation (PUL)](/docs/surgical-techniques/04a-urethral-reconstruction/permanent-urethral-ligation)** with suprapubic tube drainage achieved continence in 90% of patients (mean age 75), with 55% experiencing complications (mostly Clavien-Dindo Grade II–III). Over 30 months of follow-up, most patients reported improved quality of life.<sup>[[38]](#ref38)</sup>
- **Bladder neck closure** with catheterizable stoma or supravesical diversion remains an option for the most refractory cases.

## Sequential Salvage Paradigm

A proposed sequential approach to maximize the duration of AUS-based incontinence therapy:<sup>[[30]](#ref30)[[39]](#ref39)</sup>

1. **Primary standard bulbar cuff**. If failure occurs:
2. **Distal double cuff or cuff downsizing/relocation**. If failure occurs:
3. **Transcorporal cuff placement**. If failure occurs:
4. **[Permanent urethral ligation](/docs/surgical-techniques/04a-urethral-reconstruction/permanent-urethral-ligation) or urinary diversion**

A prospective comparison of distal double cuff (DC) versus transcorporal cuff (TC) in salvage settings showed comparable continence (88%/94% social continence for DC versus 72%/100% for TC, p = NS) and similar explantation-free survival.<sup>[[39]](#ref39)</sup>

---

## Key Principles of Revision and Salvage

- Outcomes of secondary AUS implantation are comparable to primary implantation (5-year durability approximately 80–88%); salvage of a good outcome is achievable even after multiple revisions<sup>[[37]](#ref37)</sup>
- Evaluate capsular constriction, tissue quality and device function in recurrent SUI; capsule release and same-size replacement are selected options supported by small series and expert discussion.<sup>[[51]](#ref51)[[52]](#ref52)</sup>
- When capsule release is performed, remeasure afterward and select the cuff from operative findings.<sup>[[52]](#ref52)</sup>
- **Complete versus partial replacement** remains an individualized decision; observational associations do not prove universal superiority.<sup>[[22]](#ref22)[[23]](#ref23)</sup>
- **Tandem cuff placement** yields favorable continence outcomes among revision techniques, but cadaver-model and database data suggest some of the benefit reflects more-proximal placement and selection bias rather than the dual-cuff mechanism itself<sup>[[27]](#ref27)[[44]](#ref44)[[45]](#ref45)</sup>
- **Transcorporal cuff placement** has lower infection (OR 0.33) and erosion (OR 0.35) but higher revision rate (OR 2.99); does not eliminate erosion risk in irradiated patients<sup>[[30]](#ref30)[[31]](#ref31)[[47]](#ref47)</sup>
- **Sequential salvage:** distal double cuff first, then transcorporal if DC fails; maximizes total duration of AUS-based therapy<sup>[[39]](#ref39)</sup>
- **In situ urethroplasty at explantation** for erosion significantly reduces stricture formation and improves the likelihood of successful reimplantation<sup>[[34]](#ref34)</sup>
- Patients undergoing second or third AUS implantation can achieve revision-free survival comparable to their primary device; multiple risk factors (radiation, prior revision and large cuff) substantially reduce durability<sup>[[20]](#ref20)</sup>

## Special Populations

**Post-radiation:** Earlier device failure but still meaningfully effective; patients should be counseled on reduced durability and higher erosion risk.<sup>[[20]](#ref20)</sup>

**Neurogenic bladder:** AUS can be offered to selected patients with adequate upper extremity function; bladder storage parameters must be optimized (often with antimuscarinic therapy or Botox) before implantation to avoid unsafe storage pressures with a competent outlet.<sup>[[7]](#ref7)[[21]](#ref21)</sup>

**Concurrent penile prosthesis:** Simultaneous or staged implantation may be offered (conditional recommendation); combined surgery lengthens the operation, and a statewide database study found more reoperation than prosthesis alone, so counsel accordingly. Careful attention to scrotal pump positioning avoids device-on-device interference.<sup>[[21]](#ref21)[[77]](#ref77)</sup>

**After failed male sling:** AUS is recommended for persistent or recurrent SUI after a sling; outcomes of AUS after sling are similar to primary AUS.<sup>[[77]](#ref77)</sup>

**Prior urethral reconstruction, stricture surgery or stenting:** AUS may be offered with counseling that complication rates are higher (prior urethroplasty raised erosion risk in cohorts cited by the guideline); treat symptomatic bladder neck contracture or anastomotic stenosis before implanting.<sup>[[77]](#ref77)</sup>

**Women:** See the dedicated [Female AUS (Bladder Neck Placement)](#female-aus-bladder-neck-placement) section below.

## Postoperative Management

- **Deactivation period:** Device remains deactivated for 4–6 weeks (manufacturer IFU) to allow tissue healing around the cuff before first activation<sup>[[8]](#ref8)[[74]](#ref74)</sup>
- **Activation visit:** Clinician confirms wound healing and trains patient on pump cycling technique
- **Patient ID card:** Patients carry a permanent card identifying the implant; emergency staff must be informed; no urethral catheter or instrument should be passed without first deactivating the device
- **MRI compatibility:** The current AMS 800 IFU lists the system (InhibiZone-treated and untreated) as MR Conditional at 1.5 T or 3.0 T in a horizontal-bore cylindrical scanner, with a maximum spatial field gradient of 40 T/m, normal operating mode, and whole-body SAR of 2 W/kg for up to 60 minutes of continuous RF; expect an image artifact extending about 36 mm from the device. The IFU does not require deactivating the cuff for MRI. Identify the implanted components and follow the labeling for older devices or other implants before scanning<sup>[[74]](#ref74)</sup>

## Female AUS (Bladder Neck Placement)

The AMS 800 in women is placed around the bladder neck, not the bulbar urethra. It is a high-acuity salvage operation for severely compromised outlet function, distinct enough in indication, anatomy, technique, sizing, and outcome profile that it is best treated as its own operation rather than a sex-modified version of the male procedure.

### Indications

The 2023 AUA/SUFU guideline (Statement 22) allows an obstructing autologous pubovaginal sling (PVS) or bladder neck closure with urinary drainage for women with severe outlet dysfunction or persistent SUI after failed surgery. Its discussion adds that in more severe cases an AUS, catheterizable bladder neck closure or diversion may be needed, and that the AUS is a viable consideration in the non-index patient after thorough counseling. NICE NG123 offers an AUS only when previous surgery has failed.<sup>[[56]](#ref56)[[78]](#ref78)</sup>

- **Failed prior anti-incontinence surgery.** The 2015 ICS Consensus, the 2023 AUA/SUFU guideline discussion and NICE NG123 position AUS as a salvage option in women with persistent SUI after failed surgery; the ICS consensus favors it particularly when residual urethral mobility is absent. Expert consensus recommends referral after a maximum of two prior surgical procedures; erosion and revision risk rises with each additional intervention.<sup>[[56]](#ref56)[[57]](#ref57)[[58]](#ref58)</sup>
- **Severe intrinsic sphincter deficiency (ISD).** Low urethral closure pressure (or low VLPP), loss of urethral mobility, and a negative Marshall / Bonney test. This is the core pathophysiologic indication.<sup>[[59]](#ref59)</sup>
- **Neurogenic SUI.** Women with neurogenic bladder and ISD (spina bifida, multiple sclerosis) are a defining population for female bladder-neck AUS.<sup>[[60]](#ref60)</sup>

**Contraindication:** prior pelvic radiotherapy substantially raises infection and erosion risk and is treated as a contraindication by expert consensus.<sup>[[57]](#ref57)</sup>

### Surgical Approaches

Three approaches have been described. The vaginal route is abandoned due to unacceptable infection and erosion rates; consensus favors a retropubic approach without opening the vagina (Grade B).<sup>[[57]](#ref57)</sup>

#### 1. Open Retropubic (Traditional)

- Low midline or Pfannenstiel incision; entry into the retropubic (Retzius) space.
- **Circumferential bladder-neck dissection,** separating the posterior bladder neck from the anterior vaginal wall, the most technically demanding step, as there is no natural plane between urethra and vagina.<sup>[[59]](#ref59)</sup>
- Cuff placed around the bladder neck between periurethral fascia and vagina.
- PRB placed in the retropubic space or between abdominal musculature and transversalis fascia.<sup>[[57]](#ref57)</sup>
- Control pump positioned in the labia majora.<sup>[[60]](#ref60)</sup>

#### 2. Laparoscopic

- Transperitoneal access to the Retzius space; cuff placed around the bladder neck between periurethral fascia and vagina as in the open approach.<sup>[[61]](#ref61)[[62]](#ref62)</sup>
- Largest single-center series (n = 74, mean follow-up approximately 45 months): 78% complete continence, 19% improvement, mean operative time approximately 120 min.<sup>[[61]](#ref61)</sup>

#### 3. Robot-Assisted

Two variants:

- **Anterior approach.** Transperitoneal access to the Retzius space; an assistant's vaginal finger guides dissection of the vesicovaginal space.<sup>[[63]](#ref63)[[64]](#ref64)</sup>
- **Posterior approach.** Dissection begins posterior to the bladder neck, potentially avoiding blind anterior dissection and reducing vaginal/bladder injury risk. Some centers add intraoperative cystoscopic monitoring to confirm the correct plane.<sup>[[65]](#ref65)[[66]](#ref66)</sup>

The largest open-vs-robotic comparison (Dubois 2025, multicenter, n = 135) found robotic implantation associated with:<sup>[[67]](#ref67)</sup>

| Outcome | Robotic | Open |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Intraoperative complications | 12.7% | 27.4% |
| Postoperative complications | 15.5% | 46.8% |
| Full continence | 83.3% | 62.3% |
| Explantation | 1.4% | 27.4% |

### Device Specifications in Women

| Parameter | Female bladder-neck value | Notes |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Cuff size** | Median **7.0 cm** (typical range 6.5–8 cm); 5.5–9 cm in neurogenic series | Substantially larger than the male bulbar cuff (3.5–6 cm). The same 13-size cuff family (3.5–11 cm) is used for bladder-neck placement in men, women and children, and the surgeon sizes the cuff with the cuff sizer<sup>[[60]](#ref60)[[68]](#ref68)[[79]](#ref79)</sup> |
| **PRB** | **61–70 cmH₂O** in most patients; 71–80 cmH₂O at surgeon discretion | Bladder neck tolerates higher pressure than bulbar urethra<sup>[[57]](#ref57)[[68]](#ref68)</sup> |

| **Pump location** | Labia majora (right or left) | <sup>[[60]](#ref60)</sup> |
| **Activation** | 4–6 weeks postoperatively | <sup>[[65]](#ref65)[[68]](#ref68)</sup> |

### Outcomes

A meta-analysis of 964 women (15 studies) reported a mean complete continence rate of 80% (95% CI 72–87) at mean 22-month follow-up.<sup>[[75]](#ref75)</sup> A systematic review of 17 case series reported complete continence rates of 61–100% across open, laparoscopic, and robotic approaches.<sup>[[56]](#ref56)[[59]](#ref59)</sup>

**Complications:**

| Complication | Rate (range) | Mean | Source |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Mechanical failure | 0–47% | ~13% | <sup>[[75]](#ref75)</sup> |
| Vaginal / urethral erosion | 0–27% | ~9% | <sup>[[75]](#ref75)</sup> |
| Infection | 0–46% | ~7% | <sup>[[75]](#ref75)</sup> |
| Revision required | 0–44% | ~15% | <sup>[[75]](#ref75)</sup> |
| Explantation required | 0–44% | ~13% | <sup>[[75]](#ref75)</sup> |

**Long-term durability** in neurological women: explantation-free survival >74% at 20 years, though only about half remained free of revision at 10 years (75% at 5 years) in this 26-woman cohort.<sup>[[60]](#ref60)</sup>

**Surgical volume and experience** influence outcomes; the two largest open series report the most favorable results.<sup>[[59]](#ref59)</sup> The robotic approach may offer visualization and deep-pelvic-access advantages, potentially reducing morbidity even during the learning curve.<sup>[[63]](#ref63)[[67]](#ref67)</sup>
