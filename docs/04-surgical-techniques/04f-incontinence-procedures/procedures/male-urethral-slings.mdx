# Male Urethral Slings

**Male urethral slings** are minimally invasive devices used primarily for **post-prostatectomy stress urinary incontinence (PPI)**, with overall success rates (cure + improvement) of **62–92%** depending on sling design and incontinence severity.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[2]](#ref2)</sup> Slings are a reasonable primary surgical option for **mild-to-moderate** male SUI; the [artificial urinary sphincter (AUS)](/docs/surgical-techniques/04f-incontinence-procedures/procedures/artificial-urinary-sphincter) remains the gold standard for **moderate-to-severe** incontinence.<sup>[[3]](#ref3)</sup>

---

## Epidemiology and context

Post-prostatectomy incontinence affects **5–20%** of men long-term after radical prostatectomy and is the most common indication for male sling surgery. Other causes include SUI after TURP, HoLEP, or pelvic radiation. Conservative management — pelvic floor muscle training and behavioral therapy — should be attempted for **6–12 months** before surgical intervention.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[3]](#ref3)</sup>

---

## Classification of male slings

Male slings are broadly classified by **mechanism of action** and **route of insertion**.<sup>[[2]](#ref2)[[4]](#ref4)[[5]](#ref5)</sup>

**A. Fixed (non-adjustable) slings**

1. Retrourethral transobturator sling (AdVance / AdVance XP)
2. Quadratic sling (Virtue)
3. Bone-anchored sling (InVance) — largely historical
4. Four-arm transobturator-prepubic slings (Surgimesh M-Sling)

**B. Adjustable slings**

1. ATOMS (Adjustable Transobturator Male System)
2. Argus / Argus T — adjustable retropubic / transobturator
3. REMEEX — re-adjustable suburethral sling with suprapubic mechanical regulator

---

## Mechanisms of action

Understanding the mechanism is critical for patient selection and counseling.

| Sling | Primary mechanism | Secondary mechanism |
|---|---|---|
| **AdVance / AdVance XP** | Repositioning of the lax membranous urethra proximally | Lengthening of the functional urethral length |
| **Virtue (quadratic)** | Ventral urethral elevation (transobturator arms) | Urethral compression against the genitourinary diaphragm (prepubic arms) |
| **Bone-anchored (InVance)** | Direct bulbar urethral compression | — |
| **ATOMS** | Adjustable bulbar urethral compression via inflatable cushion | — |
| **Argus** | Adjustable urethral compression via silicone foam pad | — |

A dynamic-MRI study demonstrated that the transobturator sling works by **lengthening the vesicourethral anastomosis-to-bulbar-urethra distance**, restoring functional urethral length toward continent post-prostatectomy controls. At rest, functional urethral length increased from 1.27 cm pre-sling to 1.53 cm post-sling, approaching the 1.92 cm seen in continent controls (P = .09).<sup>[[6]](#ref6)</sup>

## AdVance / AdVance XP — retrourethral transobturator sling

The most extensively studied male sling. First described by Rehder &amp; Gozzi 2007. The AdVance XP (2010) is the second-generation device with **revised mesh anchoring features and a redesigned needle** to improve fixation and reduce migration.<sup>[[9]](#ref9)[[10]](#ref10)[[11]](#ref11)</sup> Originally American Medical Systems; now Boston Scientific.

**AdVance vs AdVance XP head-to-head:** Hüsch 2018 found **no significant difference in functional outcomes** (ICIQ-SF, PGI, I-QoL, pad usage) between the two generations, though the XP had a **significantly higher rate of postoperative urinary retention (p = 0.042)** — likely reflecting the tighter anchoring profile.<sup>[[38]](#ref38)</sup>

### Technique

- Lithotomy position; perineal incision over the bulbar urethra
- Polypropylene tape passed retrourethrally through the transobturator route (inside-out or outside-in)
- The sling **repositions** the lax posterior urethra and membranous-sphincter complex proximally
- **Non-compressive** — does not obstruct voiding
- Operative time ~30–45 min; typically outpatient<sup>[[9]](#ref9)[[12]](#ref12)</sup>

### Patient selection — the "repositioning test"

- A positive repositioning test (improved coaptation of the membranous urethra with upward perineal pressure during cystoscopy) predicts success
- Requires **residual sphincter function** — patients with intrinsic sphincter deficiency or absent coaptive zone are poor candidates<sup>[[13]](#ref13)[[14]](#ref14)</sup>
- Preoperative 24-hour pad weight &lt;200–400 g and ≤2–3 pads/day favor success<sup>[[13]](#ref13)</sup>

### Outcomes

| Study | N | Follow-up | Cure | Cure + improved | Key finding |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Bauer 2009<sup>[[9]](#ref9)</sup> | 124 | 12 mo | 51.4% | 77.1% | Prospective; QoL significantly improved |
| Cornu 2009<sup>[[12]](#ref12)</sup> | 102 | 13 mo (median) | 62.7% | 80.4% | Prior radiation predicted higher failure (P = .039) |
| Rehder 2012<sup>[[17]](#ref17)</sup> | 156 | 3 yr | — | 76.8% | Stable efficacy at 3 yr; no worsening over time |
| Bauer 2017 (XP)<sup>[[11]](#ref11)</sup> | 115 | 36 mo | 66.0% | 89.4% | Prospective multicenter; pad weight 272 → 21.8 g; **no erosions or explantations** |
| Ye 2018 (XP)<sup>[[40]](#ref40)</sup> | 113 | 24 mo | 22.6% (no leak / no pad) | 80.6% (≤1 PPD) | Strict definition; reintervention 10%; Clavien IIIb 2.7% |
| Grabbert 2019 (XP)<sup>[[39]](#ref39)</sup> | — | 48 mo | 71.7% | 86.7% | Selected cohort; 13.3% failure |
| Collado 2019<sup>[[13]](#ref13)</sup> | 94 | 49 mo (median) | 77% | — | Preoperative pad weight predicts outcome |
| Chua 2019<sup>[[15]](#ref15)</sup> | 215 | 56 mo (mean) | 44.7% | 69.8% | Largest long-term series; severity and urgency predict failure |

**Key points**

- **Cure rates decline over time** — from ~65–77% at 1 yr to ~45–62% at 4–5 yr, though most patients remain improved<sup>[[15]](#ref15)[[16]](#ref16)[[18]](#ref18)</sup>
- Preoperative SUI severity is the **strongest predictor** of long-term success — Collado Serra 2013 reported a **0.4% decrease in cure rate per 1 g increase** in preoperative 24-h pad weight<sup>[[13]](#ref13)[[15]](#ref15)[[16]](#ref16)</sup>
- **No erosions or explantations** in most large series — a major advantage over compressive slings<sup>[[11]](#ref11)[[13]](#ref13)</sup>

### Safety profile detail

Bauer 2010 (n = 156) catalogued an overall complication rate of **23.9%** with the AdVance, with the dominant entries being **transient acute urinary retention 12.9–21.3%** (self-limiting, resolves within 6 wk without intervention) and **sling explantation just 0.9%**.<sup>[[42]](#ref42)</sup> MacAskill 2021 specifically addressed the chronic-pain question: ~23% of men experience early postoperative pain, but the **majority resolve within 4 weeks and only 2.3% remain with chronic pain (&gt; 3 mo)** — all of those resolved within 1 year.<sup>[[41]](#ref41)</sup> Erectile function (IIEF-5) and voiding parameters (IPSS, Qmax, PVR) are **not significantly affected**.<sup>[[2]](#ref2)[[9]](#ref9)[[11]](#ref11)</sup>

## Virtue quadratic sling

A unique **four-arm polypropylene mesh** design (Coloplast) combining transobturator and prepubic fixation, providing both urethral relocation and compression — the only commercially available male sling that incorporates both mechanisms in a single device.<sup>[[7]](#ref7)[[19]](#ref19)[[46]](#ref46)</sup>

### Mechanism — incremental RLPP buildup

Comiter 2012 measured retrograde leak point pressure (RLPP) after each technical step and demonstrated incremental contributions: baseline **33.4 → 43.3 (transobturator tensioning) → 55.8 (prepubic tensioning) → 68.8 cm H₂O (final fixation)** — each step significantly higher than the preceding one.<sup>[[7]](#ref7)</sup>

### Technique

- Perineal incision; mesh placed beneath the bulbar urethra
- Two **transobturator arms** through the obturator foramen → urethral elevation
- Two **prepubic arms** anterior to the pubic bone → urethral compression
- Intraoperative RLPP measurement to confirm adequate urethral resistance
- **Fixation is critical** — the original unfixed design produced 41.9% success at 12 mo vs **70.9% subjective / 79.2% objective success with fixation**, with 88.3% pad-weight reduction; fixation is now standard.<sup>[[19]](#ref19)</sup>

### Outcomes

| Study | N | Follow-up | Cure | Improvement | Note |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Comiter 2014 (fixation)<sup>[[19]](#ref19)</sup> | — | 12 mo | — | 70.9% subjective / 79.2% objective | Fixation technique critical; 88.3% pad-weight reduction; efficacy across all severity strata |
| Ferro 2017<sup>[[21]](#ref21)</sup> | 29 | 36 mo | — | Pad weight 128.6 → 2.5 g; pads/day 2 → 0; ICIQ-SF 14.3 → 0.9 | Italian prospective, predominantly mild SUI; all 17 complications Clavien I; PGI-I median 1 |
| Roumeguère 2022<sup>[[20]](#ref20)</sup> | 117 | 36 mo | **19%** | 51% objective / 34% subjective | European multicenter; ICIQ-UI-SF 15 → 9; BMI, PVR, nocturia, ICIQ predict outcome; 5.1% Clavien IIIb |
| **McCall 2016 (Mayo)**<sup>[[45]](#ref45)</sup> | 31 | **55 mo (median)** | — | **68% failure**; pads 3 → 2 only | **22% explanted; 20% required AUS; 7% chronic pain; radiation predicted failure (p = 0.02). Authors abandoned the device.** |
| Hogewoning 2017<sup>[[47]](#ref47)</sup> | 8 | 12 mo | 4 / 8 continent | 2 / 8 improved | Small post-TURP series; very limited evidence for that indication |

### A note on the long-term controversy

The Virtue evidence base is **sharply divided**. Short-to-medium-term European data (Ferro / Comiter / Roumeguère) show meaningful improvement and acceptable safety; the **Mayo Clinic long-term experience (McCall 2016)** reported a 68% failure rate at 55-month median follow-up with 22% explantation and 20% subsequent AUS requirement, leading the authors to **abandon the device**.<sup>[[45]](#ref45)</sup> The reasons for this divergence are unclear — center experience, fixation technique, patient mix, and follow-up duration likely all contribute. The wide variability in reported outcomes (19% strict cure to 79% objective success) makes firm recommendations difficult, and **prior radiation should be considered a relative contraindication** based on the Mayo data and the broader male-sling literature.<sup>[[34]](#ref34)[[45]](#ref45)</sup> Unlike the AdVance, baseline severity has not consistently predicted outcome — fixation-cohort efficacy was similar across mild, moderate, and severe SUI.<sup>[[19]](#ref19)[[20]](#ref20)</sup>

No urethral erosion has been reported across Virtue-specific studies; explantation rates range from 0% (multinational fixation trial) to 22% (Mayo long-term).<sup>[[19]](#ref19)[[21]](#ref21)[[20]](#ref20)[[45]](#ref45)</sup>

## Bone-anchored sling (InVance) — largely historical

The first widely used male sling — silicone-coated polyester or polypropylene mesh secured to the inferior pubic rami with **titanium bone screws**.<sup>[[2]](#ref2)[[22]](#ref22)[[23]](#ref23)</sup>

- Success rates **40–88%** (highly variable across series)<sup>[[2]](#ref2)</sup>
- Cure rates **39.5–87%** depending on severity and sling material<sup>[[22]](#ref22)[[23]](#ref23)</sup>
- **Mesh infection 2–12%** — the major limitation, usually requiring explantation<sup>[[2]](#ref2)</sup>
- Silicone mesh outperformed porcine dermal collagen (87% vs. 56% cure)<sup>[[22]](#ref22)</sup>
- Severe incontinence, prior radiation, or prior AUS substantially worsened outcomes<sup>[[23]](#ref23)</sup>
- **Largely abandoned** due to high infection rates and superior alternatives<sup>[[2]](#ref2)</sup>

## ATOMS — Adjustable Transobturator Male System

A self-anchoring transobturator device (A.M.I., Feldkirch, Austria; first introduced 2008) with an **adjustable silicone cushion** filled via a subcutaneous port, allowing postoperative titration of urethral compression. Unlike the AdVance, which depends on **urethral repositioning and residual sphincter function**, the ATOMS provides **adjustable ventral compression of the urethra against the urogenital diaphragm** — making it suitable across a broader severity range.<sup>[[8]](#ref8)[[24]](#ref24)[[25]](#ref25)[[26]](#ref26)[[27]](#ref27)[[46]](#ref46)</sup>

### Device generations — port design matters

The ATOMS has evolved through three port designs that materially affect outcome and complication profiles:<sup>[[49]](#ref49)[[50]](#ref50)</sup>

| Generation | Years | Port | Key issue |
|---|---|---|---|
| **IP** — Inguinal Port | 2009–2013 | Titanium, inguinal | Highest port-complication and explant rate; **41% of removals from local titanium intolerance** |
| **SP** — Scrotal Port | 2013–2015 | Manually-connectable titanium, scrotal | Shorter OR vs IP; titanium intolerance persists |
| **SSP** — Silicone-covered Scrotal Port | 2014–present | Pre-connected, fully silicone-covered, scrotal | **Current standard**; significantly fewer port complications (IP vs SSP p = 0.019; SP vs SSP p = 0.045) and better ICIQ-SF scores; meta-confirmed device-generation effect on dryness rate |

The current SSP is the only generation that should be implanted today.

### Technique

- Perineal incision; mesh with integrated silicone cushion placed beneath the bulbar urethra (proximal-bulbar positioning per Queissert 2023 optimization).<sup>[[63]](#ref63)</sup>
- Transobturator arms (outside-in) self-anchor to the inferior pubic ramus.
- Subcutaneous **silicone-covered scrotal port** (SSP) for percutaneous saline adjustment.
- Mean OR time **~47 min** (range 29–112).<sup>[[8]](#ref8)</sup>
- Postoperative saline adjustments: **mean 2.4–3.8** to optimize continence.<sup>[[8]](#ref8)[[24]](#ref24)</sup>

### Outcomes

| Study | N | Follow-up | Continence | Dry rate | Note |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Seweryn 2012<sup>[[8]](#ref8)</sup> | 38 | 16.9 mo | 84.2% | 60.5% | First prospective study |
| Hoda 2013<sup>[[27]](#ref27)</sup> | 99 | 17.8 mo | 92% | 63% | European multicenter; 34.3% had prior failed surgery |
| Friedl 2017 (long-term EMC)<sup>[[50]](#ref50)</sup> | 287 | 31 mo (median) | **90%** overall success | 64% | Pad use 4 → 1; pad test 400 → 18 mL (both p &lt; 0.001) |
| Angulo 2018 (Iberian)<sup>[[51]](#ref51)</sup> | 215 | 24.3 mo | — | **80.5% dry after adjustment** | 96.2% mild / 75.3% mod-severe; 85.1% satisfaction |
| Doiron 2019<sup>[[24]](#ref24)</sup> | 160 | 9 mo (median) | 80.0% | — | Canadian multicenter; radiation → 62.5% vs 87.9% |
| **Esquinas 2019 SR/meta**<sup>[[48]](#ref48)</sup> | **1,393 (20 studies)** | 20.9 mo (mean) | — | **67% pooled dry / 90% improved** | Pad count −4.14/d; 24-h pad test −443 mL |
| Redmond 2021<sup>[[26]](#ref26)</sup> | 289 | 19.6 mo | 73.3% | — | Largest series of 3rd-gen SSP only; radiation HR 2.3, diabetes HR 2.2 predict failure |

| **Giammò 2023** (Italian long-term)<sup>[[53]](#ref53)</sup> | 99 | **62.9 mo (median)** | 74.7% | — | **Device survival 97/93/91/90/87.9% at 12/24/36/48/60 mo** |
| Bajaj 2024<sup>[[25]](#ref25)</sup> | 140 | 11 mo (median) | 82.9% | 76.4% | Effective across all severity levels |

### Comparative evidence

- **vs ProACT** — Angulo 2019 PLoS meta (ATOMS superior across all endpoints: dry 68% vs 55%, p = 0.01; satisfaction 87% vs 56%, p = 0.002; explant 5% vs 24%; working device at 1 / 2 / 3 yr 92/85/81% vs 76/61/58%).<sup>[[54]](#ref54)</sup>
- **vs AUS — Geretto 2023 propensity-matched** (n = 49 each, 43-mo mean) — AUS dryness 44.9% vs ATOMS 22.5% (p = 0.03), but equivalent self-reported improvement (81% vs 71%); AUS had more high-grade complications, reinterventions, and explantations; **device survival at 60 mo favored ATOMS (82% vs 67%, p = 0.03)**.<sup>[[59]](#ref59)</sup>
- **vs AUS — Esquinas 2021 prospective** (102 ATOMS / 27 AUS) — equivalent total dryness (76.5% vs 66.7%) and social continence despite AUS patients having more severe baseline disease; surgical revision higher with AUS (22.2% vs 6.9%, p = 0.029); device durability favored ATOMS.<sup>[[60]](#ref60)</sup>

### Salvage roles

- **ATOMS after failed AdVance / AdVance XP** — Queissert 2021 multicenter (n = 88, 42.5-mo mean): **76.1% social continence / 56.8% pad-free**; mean leakage 422 → 38 g/d (p &lt; 0.001) — the strongest evidence for any second-line device after a failed fixed sling.<sup>[[61]](#ref61)</sup>
- **After failed initial ATOMS** — Angulo 2021 international multicenter (n = 902): 8.65% revision rate at mean 4.1 yr; of 58 reimplants (31 repeat ATOMS / 27 AUS), **repeat ATOMS outperformed AUS** on pad test (p = 0.016), pad count (p = 0.029), satisfaction (p = 0.04), and PGI-I (p = 0.025), with equivalent complications.<sup>[[62]](#ref62)</sup>

### Predictors of failure

- **Prior radiation** — most consistent negative predictor across studies. Doiron 2019 — 62.5% irradiated vs 87.9% non (p = 0.002), satisfaction 69.8% vs 93.2% (p = 0.001), explant HR 2.7 (p = 0.02). **Angulo 2023 dedicated multicenter of 223 irradiated patients** — 42.6% dry / 11.7% explant / 84% satisfied. Kidess 2025 — radiation timing matters; lower complete continence (21% vs 51%, p = 0.02) but comparable long-term continence.<sup>[[24]](#ref24)[[55]](#ref55)[[56]](#ref56)</sup>
- **Diabetes** — independent predictor (HR 2.2, p = 0.007).<sup>[[26]](#ref26)</sup>
- **Higher baseline severity** — independently predictive (HR 1.1 per pad).<sup>[[26]](#ref26)[[52]](#ref52)</sup>
- **Prior urethral / bladder-neck stricture** — Ullate 2022 multicenter — dry rate **38% vs 83% (p &lt; 0.05)**.<sup>[[57]](#ref57)</sup>
- **Positive preop urine culture, poor Charlson comorbidity index, prior devices** — Friedl 2016 multicenter risk-factor analysis.<sup>[[58]](#ref58)</sup>
- **Age and obesity were NOT significant predictors** in most studies.<sup>[[26]](#ref26)[[58]](#ref58)</sup>

### Advantages and complications (summary)

- **Postoperative adjustability** in clinic via SSP — optimize continence without reoperation.<sup>[[8]](#ref8)[[24]](#ref24)</sup>
- **Effective across all severity strata** (96.2% mild → 75.3% mod-severe per Iberian).<sup>[[51]](#ref51)</sup>
- **Very low erosion** (essentially unreported).<sup>[[8]](#ref8)[[27]](#ref27)[[48]](#ref48)</sup>
- **Major complications (Clavien III+):** ~3.0% pooled / 4.4% Canadian.<sup>[[24]](#ref24)[[48]](#ref48)</sup>
- **Explantation:** 3.25–11.6% depending on follow-up and generation; reasons in long-term Iberian — inefficacy 44%, port erosion 40%, pain + inefficacy 12%, infection 4%.<sup>[[52]](#ref52)</sup>
- **Transient perineal / scrotal pain** common early (68.7% in one series), resolves within 3–4 weeks; **no chronic pain signal**.<sup>[[8]](#ref8)[[50]](#ref50)</sup>
- **Not currently FDA-approved in the US**; widely used in Europe, Canada, Australia, New Zealand.<sup>[[10]](#ref10)[[24]](#ref24)</sup>

## Argus — adjustable suburethral compression sling

The **Argus sling** (Promedon, Córdoba, Argentina; first described Romano 2006) is an adjustable, **compressive** suburethral sling system that exists in two variants: the **Argus Classic (retropubic)** and the **Argus T (transobturator)**. Its defining feature is **mechanical postoperative adjustability via radio-opaque silicone washers** — re-tensioning requires a minor surgical procedure (unlike the ATOMS, which adjusts via percutaneous port injection).<sup>[[64]](#ref64)[[65]](#ref65)</sup>

### Device design

- Radio-opaque **silicone foam cushion pad** (42 × 26 × 9 mm) for soft bulbar compression.
- Pad attached to two silicone columns formed by multiple conical elements.
- Columns pass to the abdominal wall (Classic) or through the obturator foramina (Argus T).
- **Radio-opaque washers** at the exit points regulate and maintain tension; fluoroscopy can verify position during follow-up.<sup>[[64]](#ref64)</sup>

### Argus Classic vs Argus T

The Argus T was introduced after the Classic to avoid retropubic-passage complications (bladder perforation, vascular injury). The largest comparative analysis (Loertzer 2020, n = 106, 44-mo median) found a **trend favoring the Classic**:<sup>[[67]](#ref67)</sup>

- Dry rates 33.3% (Classic) vs 11.8% (T), p = 0.114
- 24-h pad test postop 71 vs 160 g, p = 0.066
- Explantation 14.0% vs 23.3%, p = 0.371
- **Inguinal pain significantly higher with Argus T** (p = 0.033)
- Kaplan-Meier device survival favored Classic (NS)

### Outcomes

| Study | Variant | N | Follow-up | Dry / success | Note |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Romano 2006 (Phase III)<sup>[[64]](#ref64)</sup> | Classic | 48 | 7.5 mo | **73% dry / 10% improved** | ICIQ-SF 19.2 → 4.0 |
| Hübner 2011<sup>[[65]](#ref65)</sup> | Classic | 101 | 2.1 yr | **79.2% dry** (pad 0–1 g) | I-QoL 28.8 → 63.2 (p &lt; 0.001) |
| Bochove-Overgaauw 2011<sup>[[66]](#ref66)</sup> | Classic | 100 | 27 mo | 72% overall (**92% mild / 67% mod / 67% severe**) | All-severity efficacy |
| Bauer 2015<sup>[[68]](#ref68)</sup> | T | 42 | 28.8 mo | **61.9% cure** | Effective even after radiotherapy |
| Siracusano 2017 (multicenter)<sup>[[69]](#ref69)</sup> | T | **182** | 22 mo | **86.2%** (95% mild / 78% mod / 70% severe) | 61.2% success in irradiated; adjustment in 42.9% |
| Casteleijn 2021 (long-term)<sup>[[70]](#ref70)</sup> | T | 78 | **3.2 yr (up to 6.1)** | 63.6% immediate dry → **53.3% dry at 5 yr** | 79.6% &gt; 50% improvement at 5 yr; reimplant only 2.6% |
| Loertzer 2020<sup>[[67]](#ref67)</sup> | Classic vs T | 106 | 44 mo | 33.3% vs 11.8% (strict zero-pad) | Classic favored |

### Complications — the central caveat

Argus carries a **notably higher complication burden than non-adjustable male slings**, and outcomes vary dramatically by center experience.

| Complication | Reported range |
|---|---|
| Overall complication rate | 14.3% (multicenter T) → 55–83% (single-center series) |
| Explantation | 14.0–35% (Classic) / 2.6–23.3% (T) |
| Urethral erosion | **3–10%** (Classic) — unique among modern male slings |
| Acute urinary retention | 15–35% (Classic) |
| Significant perineal pain | 27% (Dalpiaz) / inguinal pain higher with T |
| Intraoperative urethral perforation | ~6% in some series |
| Bowel perforation | 2/18 in one early Argus Classic series<sup>[[29]](#ref29)</sup> |

The most concerning data come from **Dalpiaz 2011** (n = 29 Argus Classic, 35-mo median): **83% complication rate**, 35% AUR, 35% sling removal (urethral erosion / infection / dislocation / retention / pain), 27% significant perineal pain requiring continuous analgesia, ureteral reimplantation in one patient for ureteral erosion from a dislocated sling, 17% dry, **72% dissatisfied** — leading the authors to conclude that "significant changes are warranted in the sling system and in the implantation technique."<sup>[[71]](#ref71)</sup> Hübner 2011 also reported a 15.8% explantation rate, with **6 of 16 explantations occurring within the first 22 cases** — a strong learning-curve signal.<sup>[[65]](#ref65)</sup>

### Predictors of failure

- **Severity** — strong gradient (mild 92–95%, moderate 67–78%, severe 67–70%).<sup>[[66]](#ref66)[[69]](#ref69)</sup>
- **Prior pelvic radiotherapy** — success drops to ~61% irradiated.<sup>[[69]](#ref69)</sup>
- **Preoperative urinary loss ≥ 250 g** — significantly worse outcomes.<sup>[[70]](#ref70)</sup>
- **Center volume / learning curve** — Loertzer 2020 required &gt; 150 prior implantations for inclusion; Hübner 2011 explant signal concentrated in early cases.<sup>[[65]](#ref65)[[67]](#ref67)</sup>

### Salvage after Argus failure

In the Hübner 101-pt series, 13 of 16 explanted patients received successful subsequent treatment — **7 AUS and 5 sling reimplantation**.<sup>[[65]](#ref65)</sup>

### Current positioning

The Argus occupies a **diminishing role** in contemporary male SUI care. Its theoretical advantage (adjustability across all severity strata) is offset by the **highest complication burden of any modern male sling** — particularly the unique urethral-erosion signal, high explantation rates, and significant perineal pain. The [ATOMS](#atoms--adjustable-transobturator-male-system) has largely **superseded the Argus** in many European centers because it offers easier office-based adjustability (percutaneous port vs surgical washer repositioning), lower complication rates, and more consistent center-to-center outcomes. The Argus remains in use primarily in Europe and South America, with the strongest case in moderate-to-severe SUI when [AUS](/docs/surgical-techniques/04f-incontinence-procedures/procedures/artificial-urinary-sphincter) is unavailable or contraindicated.

## REMEEX — Male Readjustable Sling (MRS) with suprapubic varitensor

The **Remeex / MRS** (Neomedic; first described Sousa-Escandón 2004) is the earliest adjustable male sling and the only one with **truly non-invasive percutaneous re-tensioning** — the suprapubic mechanical regulator (**varitensor**) is activated by an **external screwdriver-like manipulator** under local anesthesia, with no incision or port access required.<sup>[[72]](#ref72)[[73]](#ref73)</sup>

### Device design

- Monofilament polypropylene mesh sling beneath the bulbar urethra.
- Two monofilament traction threads pass **retropubically** (analogous to a retropubic female TVT trajectory) to the suprapubic region.
- **Varitensor** — permanent subcutaneous regulator over the rectus fascia ~ 2 cm above the pubis. Tension is increased or decreased percutaneously with the external manipulator at any time post-op.<sup>[[73]](#ref73)</sup>
- The retropubic passage carries the same **bladder-perforation risk** seen with retropubic female MUS — and rare bowel injury.<sup>[[73]](#ref73)[[29]](#ref29)</sup>

The current generation is **Remeex System II**.

### Outcomes

| Study | N | Follow-up | Outcome |
|---|---|---|---|

### REMEEX vs ATOMS — head-to-head meta

The **Angulo 2021 SR/meta** is the definitive comparison:<sup>[[74]](#ref74)</sup>

| Outcome | ATOMS | REMEEX | p |
|---|---|---|---|
| Dryness | **69.3%** | 53.4% | 0.008 |
| Improvement | **90.8%** | 80.2% | 0.007 |
| Complication | 18.9% | 35.8% | 0.096 (NS) |
| Explantation | **5.5%** | 13.9% | **0.027** |

Differences persisted when the analysis was restricted to current-generation devices only (ATOMS SSP vs Remeex System II).

### Complications

- **Intraoperative bladder perforation 9.8%** (Sousa-Escandón 2007 EMC) — analogous to retropubic female MUS; usually managed conservatively.<sup>[[73]](#ref73)</sup>
- **Bowel injury** — 11.1% in Chiu 2022 (2/18) during retropubic needle passage; rare but serious.<sup>[[29]](#ref29)</sup>
- Urethral erosion ~ 2%; varitensor infection ~ 4% requiring removal; perineal hematoma 5.9%.<sup>[[73]](#ref73)</sup>
- Pooled explantation 13.9% — meaningfully higher than ATOMS (5.5%, p = 0.027).<sup>[[74]](#ref74)</sup>

### Predictors of failure

- **Prior pelvic radiotherapy** — Kim 2016 OR **8.4** for failure, among the highest reported for any male sling.<sup>[[75]](#ref75)</sup>
- **Higher baseline severity** — OR 1.41 per pad (Kim 2016).<sup>[[75]](#ref75)</sup>
- **Severity-related complete-dry gradient** — non-severe 57.9% vs severe 23.1% (Altan 2017, p = 0.029) despite comparable overall success.<sup>[[77]](#ref77)</sup>

### Salvage

When REMEEX fails, **AUS is the most common salvage** — 12 / 18 surgical failures in Kim 2016 received subsequent AUS.<sup>[[75]](#ref75)</sup>

### Current positioning

Remeex occupies a **niche role** today. Its conceptual advantage — the only truly non-invasive percutaneous re-tensioning mechanism — is offset by retropubic-passage risks (bladder / bowel injury) absent from transobturator devices, lower dryness rates, and higher explantation than ATOMS. **Largely superseded by ATOMS** in centers with access to both. **Not FDA-approved in the US**; remains in use primarily in Europe and parts of Asia.<sup>[[74]](#ref74)</sup>

## Male sling vs. AUS — the MASTER trial

The **MASTER trial** is the only RCT comparing male sling (transobturator) to AUS — 380 men across 27 UK centers.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[30]](#ref30)</sup>

- **Primary outcome (continence at 12 mo):** male sling **noninferior** to AUS (87.0% vs. 84.2% still incontinent by strict definition; risk difference 3.6%, 95% CI −11.6 to 4.6; non-inferiority P = .003)
- **ICIQ-UI SF:** both groups improved; AUS slightly greater improvement (mean difference 1.4, P = .02)
- **Serious adverse events:** fewer with sling (6 vs. 13)
- **All secondary outcomes** that showed statistically significant differences favored AUS
- **Cost-effectiveness:** sling more cost-effective (99% probability at £30,000 / QALY threshold), though slightly lower QALY gain<sup>[[30]](#ref30)</sup>
- **Conclusion:** both procedures improve symptoms and satisfaction, but overall secondary analyses favor AUS<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup>

A propensity-score-matched study confirmed that for **moderate SUI** (3–5 pads/day), AUS significantly outperformed the fixed transobturator sling: 94.3% vs. 68.6% continence at 12 mo (P &lt;.001).<sup>[[31]](#ref31)</sup>

A single-surgeon series demonstrated that for **mild SUI**, sling success was 78%; for **moderate SUI**, AUS achieved 80% vs. only 63% for sling (P = .02).<sup>[[32]](#ref32)</sup> Grabbert 2019 large multicenter retrospective comparison of adjustable slings vs AUS reported subjective complete-dry rates of **57.3% vs 22.0%** in favor of AUS (p &lt; 0.05).<sup>[[43]](#ref43)</sup>

### Current consensus (6th ICI; AUA / SUFU)<sup>[[3]](#ref3)</sup>

- **Mild SUI (≤2 pads/day):** male sling is an acceptable first-line surgical option
- **Moderate SUI (3–5 pads/day):** AUS preferred; sling may be offered with counseling about lower efficacy
- **Severe SUI (&gt;5 pads/day):** AUS recommended; slings have poor outcomes in this group

## Predictors of failure

| Risk factor | Effect on outcome | Strength of evidence |
|---|---|---|
| **Prior pelvic radiation** | OR 0.67–0.68 for success/cure; ~3× higher explantation and infection | Meta-analysis (strongest predictor)<sup>[[34]](#ref34)</sup> |
| **Severe preoperative SUI (≥5 pads/day)** | Significantly lower cure and success rates | Multiple prospective studies<sup>[[15]](#ref15)[[16]](#ref16)</sup> |
| **Detrusor overactivity** | Independent predictor of poor mid-term outcome | Prospective / urodynamic<sup>[[35]](#ref35)</sup> |
| **Obesity (BMI &gt;30)** | OR 7.9 for failure (AdVance); predictive in Virtue | Multiple series<sup>[[14]](#ref14)[[20]](#ref20)</sup> |
| **Prior bladder neck stenosis** | OR 2.6 for failure | Prospective<sup>[[14]](#ref14)</sup> |
| **Prior urethral stricture surgery** | Higher failure (p = 0.013) | Prospective<sup>[[44]](#ref44)</sup> |
| **Weak residual sphincter / negative repositioning test** | OR 29.0 for failure | Prospective<sup>[[14]](#ref14)</sup> |
| **Diabetes** | HR 2.2 for failure (ATOMS) | Multicenter<sup>[[26]](#ref26)</sup> |

## Sling after failed sling, and sling before AUS

A failed male sling does **not preclude** subsequent AUS implantation.<sup>[[3]](#ref3)</sup> ATOMS can be used after prior failed incontinence surgery (16–19% of patients in large series).<sup>[[24]](#ref24)[[26]](#ref26)</sup> Simultaneous male sling + penile prosthesis implantation is feasible and safe — social continence 72–100% and prosthesis satisfaction 84–100% in pooled series.<sup>[[37]](#ref37)</sup>

## Surgical technique — AdVance XP, step by step

1. Patient in lithotomy; Foley catheter in place
2. **Perineal incision** 3–4 cm over the bulbar urethra, midway between scrotum and anus
3. Dissection through bulbospongiosus to expose the **bulbar urethra**
4. Mobilize urethra from the central tendon posteriorly
5. **Helical trocar needles** passed from the perineal incision through the obturator membrane to small groin incisions (inside-out)
6. Polypropylene mesh tape attached to the needles and drawn through the transobturator path
7. Mesh positioned **retrourethrally** behind the bulbar urethra, creating a hammock
8. Tension applied to **reposition** the membranous urethra proximally — goal is relocation, not compression
9. Mesh secured with **non-absorbable sutures** to bulbospongiosus — critical for long-term fixation; use of absorbable sutures with ≤4 stitches is an independent risk factor for failure (OR 8.4)<sup>[[14]](#ref14)</sup>
10. Cystoscopy to confirm no urethral injury and adequate coaptation
11. Close perineal and groin incisions; Foley removed next day
12. Operative time **30–45 min**; typically same-day discharge<sup>[[9]](#ref9)[[12]](#ref12)</sup>

## Key takeaways

1. Male slings are best suited for **mild-to-moderate SUI** (≤2–3 pads/day); for moderate-to-severe SUI, the [AUS](/docs/surgical-techniques/04f-incontinence-procedures/procedures/artificial-urinary-sphincter) provides superior outcomes.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[3]](#ref3)[[31]](#ref31)[[32]](#ref32)</sup>
2. The **AdVance XP** is the most studied fixed sling with the best safety profile (no erosions, no explantations in most series), but cure rates decline over time (~45–62% at 5 yr).<sup>[[11]](#ref11)[[15]](#ref15)[[18]](#ref18)</sup>
3. **ATOMS** offers postoperative adjustability and can be effective even in severe SUI and after prior failed surgery, though port-related complications occur in ~5%.<sup>[[24]](#ref24)[[25]](#ref25)[[26]](#ref26)</sup>
4. **Radiation history** is the strongest predictor of sling failure across all types — irradiated patients have ~33% lower odds of success and ~3× higher odds of explantation.<sup>[[34]](#ref34)</sup>
5. The **MASTER trial** showed noninferiority of sling vs. AUS at 12 mo, but all secondary outcomes favored AUS. The sling is more cost-effective but provides slightly less QALY gain.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[30]](#ref30)</sup>
6. **Residual sphincter function** is essential for transobturator sling success — a positive repositioning test and adequate coaptive zone on cystoscopy are prerequisites.<sup>[[13]](#ref13)[[14]](#ref14)</sup>
7. Male slings do **not preclude** subsequent AUS placement, making them a reasonable first-line option in appropriate candidates.<sup>[[3]](#ref3)</sup>
