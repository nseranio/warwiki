# Retropubic Mid-Urethral Sling

The retropubic mid-urethral sling (RMUS, TVT) is the classic synthetic sling for female stress urinary incontinence (SUI). A type I macroporous monofilament polypropylene tape is placed under the midurethra and passed through the retropubic space to two small suprapubic exit sites.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[2]](#ref2)[[3]](#ref3)</sup>

This page focuses on the retropubic route. For shared counseling across all midurethral sling types, see [Female Slings & Suspensions](/docs/surgical-techniques/04f-incontinence-procedures/procedures/female-slings-suspensions). For anatomy, see [The Retropubic Space](/docs/foundations/anatomy-physiology/pelvis-support/retropubic-anatomy) and [Female Urethra](/docs/foundations/anatomy-physiology/urinary-tract/female-urethra). For the introduction of TVT, see the [History of Prolapse Surgery & Urogynecology](/docs/roots/history/prolapse-urogynecology#tension-free-vaginal-tape-tvt--ulmsten-1996).

## Indications

Retropubic MUS is most useful for an index patient with demonstrable SUI who wants the most established synthetic sling route and accepts the retropubic risk profile.<sup>[[3]](#ref3)[[4]](#ref4)</sup> NICE NG123 includes a retropubic mesh sling among the choices for women whose non-surgical management has failed (alongside colposuspension and an autologous rectus fascial sling), and asks surgeons to counsel that the sling is a permanent implant that may not be completely removable and to use type 1 macroporous polypropylene.<sup>[[10]](#ref10)</sup>

| Scenario | Why RMUS fits |
|---|---|
| **Hypermobility-predominant SUI** | Restores a midurethral backstop at the functional support zone |
| **Intrinsic sphincter deficiency concern** | May be favored over transobturator tape in selected ISD: in a 164-woman RCT with urodynamic ISD, repeat surgery for SUI by 3 years was needed in 1.4% (1/72) after retropubic tape versus 20% (15/75) after transobturator tape (RR 15, 95% CI 2-113). A fixed immobile urethra still warrants discussion of autologous pubovaginal sling rather than assuming RMUS is best<sup>[[4]](#ref4)[[9]](#ref9)</sup> |
| **Need for strongest long-term evidence base** | TOMUS, Cochrane reviews, and large cohorts anchor most long-term counseling<sup>[[2]](#ref2)[[3]](#ref3)[[5]](#ref5)</sup> |
| **Avoidance of obturator-groin pain risk** | Does not traverse the obturator compartment |

Relative reasons to choose another option include hostile retropubic anatomy, major prior retropubic surgery, high concern for bladder perforation or retropubic bleeding, inability to tolerate transient retention, or patient preference for a non-mesh operation. AUA/SUFU says clinicians should not use synthetic MUS when SUI surgery accompanies urethral diverticulectomy, urethrovaginal fistula repair, or urethral mesh excision (Clinical Principle), and should strongly consider avoiding mesh in patients at risk for poor wound healing after radiation therapy, with significant scarring, or with poor tissue quality (Expert Opinion).<sup>[[4]](#ref4)</sup>

## Mechanism

The RMUS is a tension-free support operation, not a constricting operation. During stress events, the urethra compresses against the tape. Over the first postoperative weeks, tissue ingrowth through the polypropylene pores fixes the tape in place.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[6]](#ref6)</sup>

The retropubic route creates a more vertical U-shaped vector than the transobturator route. This is a proposed explanation for some route-specific outcomes. Retropubic passage carries higher bladder-injury and voiding-dysfunction risk.<sup>[[2]](#ref2)[[3]](#ref3)</sup>

Tape compression at the midurethra can improve stress leakage without necessarily correcting proximal urethral hypermobility.<sup>[[11]](#ref11)</sup> In a prospective cohort of 191 women followed for up to 36 months, postoperative bladder-neck hypermobility on introital ultrasound was associated with a lower risk of therapeutic failure, and women with therapeutic failure or secondary cure had significantly lower postoperative bladder-neck mobility than women with primary cure.<sup>[[12]](#ref12)</sup>

## Technique

### Bottom-to-top TVT

NICE NG123 advises limiting top-down retropubic mesh sling insertion to a clinical trial; the EAU 2026 evidence summary associates top-to-bottom passage with more postoperative voiding dysfunction (level 1b).<sup>[[8]](#ref8)[[10]](#ref10)</sup>

1. Place the patient in dorsal lithotomy and insert a Foley catheter.
2. Mark two suprapubic exit sites just above the pubic symphysis, lateral to midline.
3. Make a small midline vaginal incision over the midurethra, about 1 cm proximal to the external meatus.
4. Develop bilateral paraurethral tunnels toward the inferior pubic ramus and endopelvic fascia.
5. For the GYNECARE TVT EXACT system, drain the bladder and use the rigid catheter guide to displace the bladder, bladder neck, and urethra contralaterally before each trocar pass. Pass the trocar through the retropubic space close to the posterior pubic bone toward the IFU-specified suprapubic exit site.<sup>[[18]](#ref18)</sup>
6. Bring the trocar out through the suprapubic skin incision and repeat contralaterally.
7. Perform cystoscopy after trocar passage. One chapter author's approach fills the bladder with at least 300 mL and inspects the entire surface with 30- and 70-degree lenses, with particular attention to the dome, then inspects the urethra as the scope is withdrawn; if perforation is seen, the needle is withdrawn, correctly repassed, and a catheter is left for at least 5 to 7 days. The catheter interval is one chapter author's recommendation, to be reconciled with injury-specific management.<sup>[[13]](#ref13)</sup>
8. Adjust the tape with a spacer between tape and urethra so the sling lies flat and tension-free. A #13 Hegar dilator has been described as one example spacer; it is not a required instrument, and no comparative outcome data establish that size or spacer use as protection against retention.<sup>[[14]](#ref14)</sup>
9. Remove the plastic sheaths, trim excess tape at the skin, and close vaginal and suprapubic incisions. Assess postoperative voiding and post-void residual; provide temporary bladder drainage if emptying is inadequate.<sup>[[8]](#ref8)</sup>

### Technical rules

- Hug the posterior pubic bone during trocar passage.
- Avoid lateral deviation, which increases vascular and visceral risk.
- AUA/SUFU advises intraoperative cystoscopy after retropubic sling passage to check for bladder or urethral injury; bladder perforation is a recognized complication of retropubic passage.<sup>[[4]](#ref4)</sup>
- Do not cinch the tape. If postoperative voiding dysfunction occurs, evaluate for bladder outlet obstruction and detrusor underactivity rather than assuming excessive tape tension.<sup>[[8]](#ref8)</sup>

## Outcomes

Outcome percentages depend on the definition and follow-up time. In TOMUS, the 12-month objective composite was 80.8% with RMUS versus 77.7% with TMUS and met the prespecified equivalence criterion.<sup>[[3]](#ref3)</sup> The subjective composite was 62.2% versus 55.8%: not statistically different, but not proven equivalent by the trial's prespecified margin.<sup>[[3]](#ref3)</sup> Across Cochrane trials, retropubic subjective cure ranged from 71–97% short term and 51–88% beyond 5 years; the wide ranges reflect varied definitions and studies, with less certain long-term route comparisons.<sup>[[2]](#ref2)[[3]](#ref3)</sup>

At a mean 10.9 years, 1,022/1,535 (66.6%) retropubic and 479/837 (57.2%) transobturator respondents in a Swedish register cohort reported no SUI. Repeat incontinence surgery was reported by 85/1,505 (5.6%) versus 74/814 (9.1%). Of 4,894 identified women, 2,421 entered the analysis; responders differed from nonresponders.<sup>[[20]](#ref20)</sup>

For long-term durability, a prospective cohort of 90 women treated with TVT at three Nordic centers was followed for 17 years. Of the potentially assessable women, 58/74 (78%) were evaluated by clinic visit (46) or telephone interview (12); among clinic attendees, 42/46 (91.3%) had a negative cough stress test, while 48/55 respondents (87.2%) reported being cured or significantly improved. Among the 46 women examined in clinic, one minimal, asymptomatic tape exposure was found and no other tape effects were detected. The cohort was small and incompletely followed, which limits generalization.<sup>[[17]](#ref17)</sup>

| Outcome | RMUS counseling point |
|---|---|
| **Efficacy** | TOMUS demonstrated 12-month objective equivalence, but not prespecified subjective equivalence; a broader network review ranked RMUS above TMUS near 12 months, while longer-term comparative certainty is lower. EAU 2026 rates retropubic and transobturator patient-reported outcomes as equivalent at 1 year (level 1a) and retropubic cure as higher in the longer term (level 1b), and strongly recommends telling women that long-term outcomes are superior with the retropubic route<sup>[[2]](#ref2)[[3]](#ref3)[[7]](#ref7)[[8]](#ref8)</sup> |
| **Bladder perforation** | 5.0% (15/298) in TOMUS and about 4.5% in the Cochrane pooled comparison, much higher than TMUS; trocar injuries were recognized and repassed in TOMUS. EAU 2026 lists higher bladder perforation and voiding dysfunction with the retropubic route (level 1a) and strongly recommends cystoscopy after synthetic MUS insertion<sup>[[2]](#ref2)[[3]](#ref3)[[8]](#ref8)</sup> |
| **Voiding dysfunction** | More common than TMUS; usually transient, but persistent obstruction may require sling loosening or simple sling incision.<sup>[[10]](#ref10)</sup> See [urethrolysis](/docs/surgical-techniques/04f-incontinence-procedures/procedures/urethrolysis) for further operative management. In TOMUS, voiding dysfunction requiring surgery, catheter use, or both occurred in 8/298 (2.7%) RMUS patients versus 0/299 TMUS patients during the first 12 months<sup>[[2]](#ref2)[[3]](#ref3)</sup> |
| **Groin / thigh pain** | Less common than TMUS because the obturator compartment is avoided<sup>[[2]](#ref2)[[3]](#ref3)</sup> |
| **Mesh exposure** | Similar to other full-length MUS routes in pooled comparative reviews<sup>[[2]](#ref2)</sup> |
| **Mesh removal** | In an English national cohort of 95,057 women, the estimated 9-year removal risk was 3.6% after retropubic versus 2.7% after transobturator insertion; removal is not the same endpoint as exposure or repeat SUI surgery.<sup>[[5]](#ref5)</sup> |

Massive retropubic hematoma may have life-threatening consequences. In a retrospective series, 7 massive hematomas occurred among 2,091 retropubic MUS procedures (0.33%); six became symptomatic within 24 hours, mean hemoglobin fell 5.7 g/dL, and six required surgical drainage.<sup>[[19]](#ref19)</sup>

Bowel injury during retropubic passage is rare, reported in the range of 0-0.04% of the literature reviewed in one chapter summary, and the injuries in that review followed retropubic rather than transobturator passage; prior abdominal or pelvic surgery with bowel adhesions is a recurring risk context. Presentation is typically within hours to days, with abdominal pain, nausea or vomiting, decreased urine output, or peritoneal signs, but case reports also describe delayed or atypical presentations, including diarrhea and right-sided pain from ascending-colon involvement, bowel obstruction, and an incidentally discovered injury without symptoms. Abdominal/pelvic CT is considered when the course is unclear. Management described in that review is operative exploration, sling removal, and bowel repair or resection as findings dictate; a normal early course does not exclude a later-presenting injury.<sup>[[15]](#ref15)</sup>

AUA/SUFU recommends early postoperative contact to assess significant voiding problems, pain, or unexpected events, with examination if these occur (Expert Opinion).<sup>[[4]](#ref4)</sup> NICE NG123 recommends a follow-up appointment within 6 months after SUI surgery; after RMUS, the visit should include a vaginal examination for mesh exposure or extrusion.<sup>[[10]](#ref10)</sup>

## Operative Pearls

- Confirm SUI objectively before surgery. In mixed incontinence, EAU weakly recommends treating the most bothersome symptom first; urgency after SUI surgery is unpredictable, and other components may need separate treatment.<sup>[[8]](#ref8)</sup>
- Put the tape at the midurethra, not the bladder neck.
- Use cystoscopy deliberately: bladder, urethra, trigone, and trocar path all matter.
- If the trocar perforates bladder, remove it, repass it correctly, and re-cystoscope.
- If urethral injury occurs during vaginal dissection or trocar passage, abandon synthetic sling placement, close the urethral defect primarily, and leave an indwelling Foley catheter for healing, consistent with guidance against placing synthetic sling material in the presence of a urethrotomy.<sup>[[4]](#ref4)[[16]](#ref16)</sup>
- A flat loose tape is better than a perfect-looking tight tape.
