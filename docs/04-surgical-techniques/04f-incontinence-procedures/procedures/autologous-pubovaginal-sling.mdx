# Autologous Fascial Pubovaginal Sling (PVS)

An **autologous fascial sling** uses the patient's own rectus sheath or fascia lata to support the urethra. It is an established surgical option for female stress urinary incontinence (SUI), including primary and selected recurrent cases. It avoids a synthetic sling mesh but adds a harvest wound and can cause obstruction, pain, infection or recurrent incontinence. Traditional bladder-neck PVS and modified midurethral fascial slings differ in position and fixation; their outcomes and operative details should be identified separately.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[2]](#ref2)</sup>

For other options, see the [female SUI database](/docs/surgical-techniques/04f-incontinence-procedures/female-sui/female-stress-incontinence-database), [synthetic slings](/docs/surgical-techniques/04f-incontinence-procedures/procedures/female-slings-suspensions) and [Burch colposuspension](/docs/surgical-techniques/04f-incontinence-procedures/procedures/burch-colposuspension). The FDA's 2019 action concerned transvaginal mesh for prolapse and was not a ban on every SUI sling or abdominal sacrocolpopexy mesh.<sup>[[3]](#ref3)</sup>

## Selection and Counseling

| Situation | Decision points |
|---|---|
| **Primary SUI or stress-predominant mixed incontinence** | Autologous fascia PVS is one option alongside synthetic MUS, Burch and bulking. Discuss expected stress-leakage benefit, existing urgency, recovery, harvest morbidity and the possibility of catheterization. |
| **Fixed or poorly mobile urethra** | AUA/SUFU includes PVS, retropubic MUS, bulking and adjustable retropubic slings as options. Its discussion favors PVS for a non-mobile urethra. Urethral mobility and intrinsic sphincter deficiency are related assessments, not interchangeable diagnoses. |
| **Recurrent SUI after prior surgery** | Reassess the mechanism: recurrent stress leakage, obstruction, urgency, mesh complications and impaired emptying need different treatment. A salvage PVS is an option; prior surgery does not guarantee success or mandate immediate sling placement. |
| **Concomitant urethral diverticulectomy, urethrovaginal fistula repair or urethral mesh excision** | AUA/SUFU advises against a synthetic MUS during these repairs. If concurrent continence surgery is appropriate, autologous fascia is preferred over other biological sling substitutes. This is different from a remote, healed history of urethral surgery. |
| **Radiated, scarred or otherwise poorly healing tissue** | Consider avoiding synthetic mesh. Autologous harvest and recipient-site healing still carry risks; timing and reconstruction should be individualized. |

These distinctions follow the AUA/SUFU guideline's index-patient and special-case recommendations; they do not establish one operation as best for every patient.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup> NICE NG123 offers an autologous rectus fascial sling, or colposuspension, as the first surgical choices after failed non-surgical management (with a retropubic mesh sling included in that choice), and advises against porcine dermis slings.<sup>[[22]](#ref22)</sup>

Before surgery, document demonstrable stress leakage, symptom burden, pelvic examination, urinalysis and residual urine. Additional evaluation is appropriate for diagnostic uncertainty, substantial voiding dysfunction, recurrent surgery or other non-index features. Discuss what will happen if the patient cannot empty: temporary drainage, intermittent catheterization and possible sling revision.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup>

## Graft Choice and Harvest

The [rectus-fascia page](/docs/foundations/tools/biomaterials/autologous-tissue/rectus-fascia) covers abdominal anatomy, harvest planning and the cadaveric nerve-mapping study. The [fascia-lata page](/docs/foundations/tools/biomaterials/autologous-tissue/fascia-lata) covers thigh harvest and donor-site follow-up. A free graft has no retained vascular pedicle and should not be confused with a vascularized muscle or myofascial flap.

| Donor site | Practical considerations |
|---|---|
| **Anterior rectus sheath** | Convenient when the abdominal donor site is suitable. Inspect prior incisions, hernia repair, mesh and fascial quality. Typical grafts are about 8–12 × 2 cm (10 cm in the Miller technique) through a Pfannenstiel or low transverse incision of roughly 6–8 cm; dimensions and stay-suture material depend on the sling construction. To protect the ilioinguinal and iliohypogastric nerves, a cadaveric mapping study placed the safest harvest 5.4 cm above the pubic symphysis, allowing a graft up to 9.4 cm long (4.7 cm each side of midline); the safe length shortens nearer the symphysis.<sup>[[21]](#ref21)</sup> Do not extend the incision beyond the lateral borders of the rectus. Preserve viable edges and close the donor defect; closure reduces a defect but does not guarantee freedom from hernia or pain. |
| **Fascia lata** | Useful when the abdominal fascia is unsuitable or another donor site is preferred. Direct exposure and fascial-stripper techniques (a short longitudinal lateral-thigh incision with a stripper advanced along the iliotibial band) are distinct approaches; the stripper technique has been reported with minimal donor morbidity.<sup>[[9]](#ref9)</sup> Account for thigh anatomy, mobility, prior surgery and the required graft width/length; counsel about hematoma, sensory change, contour changes, pain and herniation. |

Do not infer equivalent continence or a universally safer donor site from tissue strength or small series. Hong's registry included 41,533 sling operations but only 160 rectus-fascia and 81 fascia-lata cases. Autologous fascia had higher adjusted odds of 30-day composite adverse events than synthetic mesh (aOR 3.63), and fascia lata higher than rectus (aOR 2.11); the fascia lata difference was not significant once UTI was excluded (aOR 1.93, 95% CI 0.81–4.63). The data lacked key information about previous continence surgery, retention and long-term outcomes. It is not a randomized donor-site comparison.<sup>[[4]](#ref4)</sup>

In Buckley's 201-woman pelvic-floor harvest cohort, 64 women reported a thigh issue at six weeks. One postoperative thigh hematoma required transfusion and drainage, and two women underwent donor-site hernia repair. Reported absence of functional deficit in that cohort is not an assurance for every patient. Graft sizes differed between sling and prolapse operations.<sup>[[5]](#ref5)</sup>

## Traditional Bladder-Neck PVS

McGuire and Lytton (1978) reported a pubovaginal autogenous fascial sling in 52 patients with stress incontinence and poor urethral sphincter function (urethral pressure below 10 cm H₂O), 42 of whom had undergone a previous incontinence operation. The result was satisfactory with good urinary control in 50 and a failure in 2. Uninhibited detrusor dysfunction present in 29 before surgery ceased in 20, and postoperative urethral pressure studies showed that the sling raised urethral pressure without obstructing voiding.<sup>[[32]](#ref32)</sup>

The aim is support at the proximal urethra or bladder neck with an individualized balance between continence and emptying. A published rectus-fascia technique uses a roughly 10-cm graft and nonabsorbable stay sutures; other constructions use different dimensions and fixation. The following stages describe the operative principles, not a universal suture or spacer prescription.<sup>[[6]](#ref6)[[1]](#ref1)</sup>

1. **Plan and harvest the graft.** Select the donor site, keep the graft moist in saline-soaked gauze, place nonabsorbable stay sutures (for example 0 polypropylene) at each end as handles for retropubic passage and fixation, and close the donor defect with running absorbable suture.
2. **Expose the proximal urethra.** An inverted-U anterior vaginal incision is one described approach. Develop controlled paraurethral planes and identify the intended entry into the retropubic space; prior surgery or scarring can alter these planes. The bladder-neck/proximal-urethral junction has been identified by gently tugging on the Foley balloon while palpating vaginally; some surgeons begin the vaginal exposure before graft harvest in cases where difficult vaginal access might force the case to be abandoned, so the harvest is not committed unnecessarily.<sup>[[25]](#ref25)</sup>
3. **Pass the suspension sutures.** Use the chosen carrier (Stamey needle, Pereyra ligature carrier or long clamp) from the vaginal incision through the retropubic space to the abdominal incision, and pull the graft on its stay sutures. Match needle direction, vaginal dissection and abdominal guidance to the specific technique and the patient's anatomy. A described tactile method keeps a long curved clamp against the back of the pubis while a finger in the ipsilateral vaginal incision guides the tip through the endopelvic fascia beside the urethra; if the clamp is seen in the bladder on cystoscopy, it is withdrawn, repositioned and cystoscopy is repeated, and passage alone (without a full-thickness injury) does not require formal repair.<sup>[[24]](#ref24)</sup> When the retropubic space is scarred by prior surgery or radiation and blind passage is impractical, an alternative is to open the suprapubic fascia and dissect bilaterally under direct vision down to the endopelvic fascia before passing the carrier, or, working from the vaginal side, to pass the carrier upward along the plane just posterior to the symphysis (or along a fibrous band bridging a pubic diastasis) until it exits the suprapubic incision; forcing a pathway through distorted post-traumatic or post-surgical anatomy is discouraged, and bimanual guidance with cystoscopy is used to confirm the carrier's position. These are described alternative routes for a hostile retropubic space, not evidence that open or vaginal-approach passage is safer than the standard technique.<sup>[[25]](#ref25)[[26]](#ref26)</sup>
4. **Check lower-tract integrity.** Perform intraoperative cystourethroscopy to identify bladder or urethral injury and intraluminal material, and confirm ureteral efflux. If a passage is revised, reassess integrity. Tailor repair and drainage to any injury's location and extent. A bladder or urethral injury from deep dissection has been managed with two-layer closure; for a transvaginal urethral injury, closing the urethra with the sling itself providing an additional layer has been described, with a Martius interposition flap considered if the defect is large or tissue quality is poor. Direct obturator-nerve injury during retropubic passage, or nerve compression from a retroperitoneal hematoma, can present as medial-thigh sensory symptoms and weak hip adduction; sling release within the first 24 postoperative hours has been suggested for significant neuropraxia without a major hematoma, with hematoma evacuation and physical therapy used as otherwise indicated. These are source-specific practices, not validated timing rules or proof that one repair method is superior.<sup>[[24]](#ref24)</sup>
5. **Position and fix the sling.** Support the intended urethral level without twisting the graft. Tie the stay sutures over the rectus fascia without tension, leaving the sling loose for the intended support and emptying strategy. No particular instrument under the sling, Q-tip maneuver or visual cystoscopic appearance guarantees absence of postoperative obstruction. Described confirmation methods include tying the sutures loosely across the midline while a right-angle clamp is held between the sling and posterior urethra, or directly viewing the proximal urethra with a rigid cystoscope while gently pulling on the free sling ends to confirm the graft lies under the bladder neck; one illustrated technique instead uses a catheter in the urethra with scissors between the sling and urethra and two assistant fingers over the rectus sheath while the sutures are tied. Each confirms sling position, not an adequate tension or freedom from later obstruction.<sup>[[23]](#ref23)[[27]](#ref27)</sup>
6. **Close and plan drainage.** Confirm hemostasis and document the graft source, dimensions, fixation, cystoscopic findings, any injury and the postoperative voiding or drainage plan.<sup>[[6]](#ref6)[[1]](#ref1)</sup>

## Modified Midurethral Fascial Slings

**“Sling on a string” describes a family of modifications, not one interchangeable recipe.** Asfour's illustrated modified Aldridge technique uses a 6-cm skin incision to harvest approximately 1 × 8 cm of rectus sheath, with doubled 0 PDS suspension sutures and a midpoint marker. The graft is placed at the midurethra, with controlled bottom-to-top passage, cystoscopy and tension adjustment. The incision length should not be mistaken for the graft length.<sup>[[7]](#ref7)</sup>

Osman's retrospective series included 106 women, with mean follow-up of nine months. Subjective cure was 87.8% in primary SUI and 72.0% after previous surgery; intermittent catheterization beyond two weeks occurred in 2.0% and 10.5%, respectively. These are selected, short-term subgroup observations, not a randomized demonstration that this modification eliminates retention or is superior to traditional bladder-neck PVS. Shaw's separate 19-woman midurethral series included four women with voiding dysfunction and three sling loosenings.<sup>[[8]](#ref8)[[9]](#ref9)</sup>

**Adjustment evidence:** a 2025 randomized trial compared 3-cm and 6-cm suspension-knot heights above the aponeurosis for a retropubic rectus-fascial sling. Among 52 analyzed women, retention at two weeks occurred in 9/27 versus 5/25; the difference was uncertain. With 12-week follow-up and limited precision, the trial does not establish an optimal universal gap or prove the methods equivalent. It did not test a clamp placed beneath the urethra as a universal safeguard.<sup>[[10]](#ref10)</sup> An earlier prospective cohort of 51 women found a strong association between a shorter lax sling height above the rectus fascia and prolonged retention: a height of at least 40 mm had 100% sensitivity and 51% specificity for avoiding retentive complications, and a looser sling up to 60 mm was not associated with persistent SUI. Overall, 19.6% (10/51) developed postoperative retention; half resolved with temporary self-catheterization and half needed ongoing catheterization or sling division. This single-center observational cohort predates the 2025 trial and does not by itself establish a universal target height.<sup>[[31]](#ref31)</sup>

Transobturator and laparoscopic fascial modifications have separate, smaller evidence bases. They should not inherit the long-term results of a different retropubic operation merely because all use autologous fascia.<sup>[[2]](#ref2)</sup>

## Evidence for Continence and Durability

| Evidence | Finding that helps counseling | Interpretation |
|---|---|---|
| **SISTEr RCT, 655 women** | At 24 months, rectus-fascia PVS achieved **66% versus 49% SUI-specific success** compared with Burch; strict overall success was **47% versus 38%**. | Participants had urethral hypermobility; outcome assessment was available for 520 women. These are cumulative success estimates. Sling patients had more UTI, voiding difficulty and postoperative urgency incontinence. This is a Burch comparison, not a synthetic-MUS comparison.<sup>[[11]](#ref11)</sup> |
| **E-SISTEr extension, 482 enrolled** | Five-year cumulative continence estimates were **30.8% sling versus 24.1% Burch**; most respondents remained satisfied. | Observational follow-up preferentially enrolled women with persistent incontinence. The cumulative endpoint differs from both cross-sectional dryness and the original trial's definition.<sup>[[12]](#ref12)</sup> |
| **2020 Cochrane synthesis, 34 trials/3,244 women** | Traditional slings offered better medium-term continence than open colposuspension, with more voiding morbidity. Against MUS, 1–5-year continence was **67% versus 74%**, OR **0.67 (95% CI 0.44–1.02)**. | Fully incorporated search ended February 2017. The six-trial medium-term MUS analysis included 458 women and heterogeneous traditional sling materials. This is not proof of equal efficacy or an autologous-only pooled cure rate.<sup>[[2]](#ref2)</sup> |
| **2024 and 2025 randomized-study syntheses** | No clear cure difference between autologous and synthetic sling groups; autologous operations took longer. | The 2025 review included three studies of primary surgery; studies, methods, outcome definitions and follow-up overlap. Nonsignificance is not equivalence.<sup>[[13]](#ref13)[[14]](#ref14)</sup> |
| **Khan randomized-cohort follow-up, 162/201 respondents** | At median ten years, **dry or improved** was 75.4% after fascia versus 73% after TVT; **completely dry** was 50.8% versus 31.7%. | Selected long-term responders and a single trial. No further operation for persistent SUI in the fascia arm does not mean no complication or intervention of any kind.<sup>[[15]](#ref15)</sup> |
| **Nair standard-versus-short fascial-sling follow-up, 107/165 original participants** | At median 17 years, symptom/QoL comparisons did not clearly differ; reported satisfaction was 67.2%. | This compares two fascial techniques, not fascia versus mesh or rectus versus thigh tissue. Satisfaction is not a complete-dry rate.<sup>[[16]](#ref16)</sup> |

**Observational comparisons require cautious counseling.** Offiah's 2025 single-center survey analyzed 395 respondents, including only 34/90 invited fascia-sling patients. Reported SUI absence was similar for fascia and TVT, but procedure choice was not randomized, follow-up and technique varied, and mesh exposure was not assessed. These limitations prevent a reliable overall safety ranking or a recommendation to prefer fascia for every patient.<sup>[[17]](#ref17)</sup>

## Emptying, Urgency and Follow-up

- **Voiding trial and drainage:** assess postoperative emptying and arrange a specific catheterization plan if the patient cannot empty safely. Persistent difficulty warrants review for obstruction and other causes, with discussion of the continence consequences of sling loosening, incision or revision.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup> Older series describe intermittent self-catheterization stretching an autologous sling and relieving obstruction for up to three months after the index operation; if obstructive voiding persists beyond that, sling incision has been reported with reasonable outcomes. This is a historical, study-specific option rather than a universal instruction to defer reassessment or intervention for three months.<sup>[[29]](#ref29)[[30]](#ref30)</sup>
- **Urgency and mixed incontinence:** stress leakage may improve while urgency persists or develops. Bothersome new symptoms, infection and impaired emptying are evaluated rather than assuming every symptom is idiopathic OAB. A urodynamic detrusor-overactivity endpoint is not interchangeable with patient-reported urgency.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[2]](#ref2)</sup>
- **Donor and recipient wounds:** monitor pain, hematoma, infection, dehiscence and hernia; examine persistent vaginal symptoms or concern for urinary-tract injury. Autologous tissue does not eliminate graft-related or suture-related or wound complications.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[4]](#ref4)[[5]](#ref5)</sup>
- **Long-term assessment:** distinguish SUI recurrence, all-incontinence burden, satisfaction and retreatment. A patient can remain satisfied without being completely dry; a low reoperation rate does not prove durable complete continence.<sup>[[12]](#ref12)[[16]](#ref16)</sup>

Preoperative residual urine and flow help identify patients needing more evaluation and catheterization counseling. A small historical series does not make PVR >100 mL or Qmax ≤20 mL/s a universally validated predictive cutoff, and low flow alone does not diagnose obstruction.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup>

## Salvage and the Severely Compromised Outlet

Autologous PVS can be offered for selected persistent or recurrent SUI after a synthetic sling or mesh complication, with realistic expectations:

| Series | Observed outcome | Limitation |
|---|---|---|
| **Milose, 66 women after at least one failed MUS** | 46/66 reported SUI cure at mean 14.5 months; only 25/66 had neither stress nor urgency incontinence. | Retrospective; most had mixed incontinence. SUI-specific benefit is not cure of all leakage.<sup>[[18]](#ref18)</sup> |
| **Chen, 18 women after at least two failed MUS** | 16/18 were cured or improved at median 80 months. | All also had partial sling excision and urethrolysis; small selected cohort, not an isolated-sling efficacy estimate.<sup>[[19]](#ref19)</sup> |
| **McCoy, 46 women after mesh removal** | 42/46 objective and 35/46 subjective success at mean 16 months; **22% required subsequent interventions**. | Staged/concomitant groups were not randomized. No detected difference does not prove that timing strategies are equivalent.<sup>[[20]](#ref20)</sup> |

For severe outlet dysfunction or recurrent or persistent SUI, AUA/SUFU permits consideration of an obstructing autologous PVS or bladder-neck closure with drainage after counseling. These require an explicit plan for bladder storage safety, upper-tract protection and catheterization; they are not routine next steps in a fixed escalation sequence. The guideline discussion advises against tensioning a synthetic sling to create this obstructing effect.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup>

For a very wide-open proximal urethra with complete loss of proximal sphincter function, crossing the sling ends in front of the urethra in the retropubic space (rather than the conventional posterior-vector construction) has been described to increase sling contact and coaptation, with the crossed vector reported to achieve closure at lower pressures than a posterior-vector sling; the degree of compression needed may make normal voiding or intermittent catheterization difficult, and autologous fascia has been favored over synthetic mesh when ongoing catheterization is anticipated. This is author-described technique and opinion for a rare severe-outlet phenotype, without a comparative cohort or measured pressure difference, not an established sling configuration or a routine SUI recommendation.<sup>[[26]](#ref26)[[28]](#ref28)</sup>

See [bladder-neck closure](/docs/surgical-techniques/04ab-bladder-neck-reconstruction/outlet-continence/bladder-neck-closure) and [bladder-neck reconstruction](/docs/surgical-techniques/04ab-bladder-neck-reconstruction) for the distinct outlet-closure and diversion decisions.
