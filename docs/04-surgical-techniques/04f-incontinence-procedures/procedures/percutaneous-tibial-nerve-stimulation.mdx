# Percutaneous Tibial Nerve Stimulation

Percutaneous tibial nerve stimulation (PTNS) is a minimally invasive neuromodulation therapy primarily used for overactive bladder (OAB) and urgency urinary incontinence, with more uncertain evidence for fecal incontinence and chronic pelvic pain. AUA/SUFU 2024 and EAU recommend tibial stimulation as an OAB option; AUA/SUFU does not require prior behavioral or drug treatment before selecting a minimally invasive therapy through shared decision-making.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[2]](#ref2)[[3]](#ref3)[[22]](#ref22)</sup>

[Implantable tibial stimulation](/docs/surgical-techniques/04f-incontinence-procedures/procedures/implantable-tibial-nerve-stimulation) and [Sacral Neuromodulation](/docs/surgical-techniques/04f-incontinence-procedures/procedures/sacral-neuromodulation) have separate procedure and device pages because implantation and maintenance differ. The AUA/SUFU 2024 guideline groups PTNS, implanted tibial systems, SNM and [Intradetrusor OnabotulinumtoxinA](/docs/surgical-techniques/04f-incontinence-procedures/procedures/intradetrusor-botox) as minimally invasive choices rather than a mandatory third-line ladder.<sup>[[2]](#ref2)</sup>

---

## Mechanism of Action

PTNS delivers retrograde electrical stimulation to the sacral nerve plexus (S2–S4) via the posterior tibial nerve, a mixed sensory-motor nerve that shares common nerve roots with the pelvic floor and bladder.<sup>[[4]](#ref4)[[22]](#ref22)</sup> The exact mechanism remains incompletely understood, but research suggests stimulation modulates central and peripheral neural pathways involved in bladder control. Animal studies indicate that recruitment of unmyelinated C-fibers mediates the bladder-inhibitory effects, and co-activation of the adjacent saphenous nerve may also contribute.<sup>[[5]](#ref5)[[6]](#ref6)[[7]](#ref7)</sup>

---

## Technique and Protocol

In the commonly described office protocol, a fine needle is inserted proximal and posterior to the medial malleolus near the tibial nerve, with a surface electrode near the ipsilateral heel. A 34-gauge needle, approximately 5 cm proximal insertion point and approximately 60° approach describe one technique, not every system's IFU; the applicable manufacturer manual governs placement and electrical setup.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[23]](#ref23)</sup>

**Commonly studied induction parameters** (confirm the specific device manual):

- **Frequency:** 20 Hz
- **Pulse width:** 200 μs (the FDA 510(k) summary for the NURO system lists 200 μs, 20 Hz and an adjustable current from 0 to 9 mA, equivalent to the Urgent PC predicate)<sup>[[25]](#ref25)</sup>
- **Current:** Titrated within the selected system's limits to a comfortable response such as toe movement or plantar sensation; the 0.5–9 mA range is the SUmiT protocol (34-gauge needle, 20 Hz, 30 minutes weekly for 12 weeks, as summarized by AUA/SUFU) rather than a universal prescription<sup>[[2]](#ref2)</sup>
- **Session duration:** 30 minutes
- **Treatment course:** Once weekly for 12 weeks (induction phase), followed by periodic maintenance sessions<sup>[[8]](#ref8)[[9]](#ref9)[[10]](#ref10)</sup>

The effect is relatively short-lived, and ongoing maintenance treatments (typically monthly or as needed) are required to sustain benefit.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[9]](#ref9)</sup>

---

## Clinical Indications

- **Overactive bladder (OAB):** the primary and best-supported indication, including urgency, frequency, nocturia, and urgency urinary incontinence
- **Neurogenic detrusor overactivity:** evidence in multiple sclerosis and Parkinson's disease<sup>[[9]](#ref9)</sup>
- **Fecal incontinence:** studied but with mixed results (see below)
- **Chronic pelvic pain or interstitial cystitis:** limited evidence with lower efficacy compared to OAB<sup>[[11]](#ref11)[[12]](#ref12)</sup>

---

## Efficacy for Overactive Bladder

The SUmiT trial (n=220), a multicenter double-blind sham-controlled RCT, demonstrated that 54.5% of PTNS patients reported moderate or marked improvement on the 13-week global response assessment versus 20.9% with sham (p &lt; 0.001), with better diary frequency, nocturia, urgency and urgency incontinence outcomes.<sup>[[13]](#ref13)</sup>

A meta-analysis of 28 studies (n=2,461) reported a pooled success rate of 68% (95% CI 59–78%), with significant reductions in voiding frequency (−2.48/day), nocturia (−1.57/day), urgency episodes (−2.20/day), and incontinence episodes (−1.37/day).<sup>[[14]](#ref14)</sup>

### Comparison with Other Therapies

| Comparison | Key findings | Evidence |
|---|---|---|
| **PTNS vs. Sham** | Superior for all OAB parameters; 54.5% vs. 20.9% improvement (SUmiT) | <sup>[[13]](#ref13)</sup> |
| **PTNS vs. Antimuscarinics** | Comparable for overall symptom score, frequency and urgency; the EAU panel's review found PTNS more effective for reducing UUI episodes (level 1a). PTNS has fewer adverse events and lower discontinuation rates; adding an antimuscarinic to PTNS gave no further benefit | <sup>[[2]](#ref2)[[3]](#ref3)[[15]](#ref15)[[22]](#ref22)</sup> |
| **PTNS vs. Sacral Neuromodulation** | In a network meta-analysis, SNM produced the greatest reductions in incontinence episodes and voiding frequency; long-term comparisons are lacking | <sup>[[16]](#ref16)</sup> |
| **PTNS vs. OnabotulinumtoxinA** | Both effective; botulinum toxin associated with higher UTI and urinary retention rates. Direct trials (TROOP, Sherif) are small or underpowered with low-certainty evidence; success was similar in TROOP (50% vs 44%), and botulinum toxin was more durable at 9 months in the smaller trial | <sup>[[2]](#ref2)[[16]](#ref16)</sup> |
| **PTNS vs. Pelvic Floor Muscle Training** | Pooled PTNS/TTNS reduced daily voiding frequency more than sham, placebo, no treatment or conservative management | <sup>[[21]](#ref21)</sup> |

The current EAU female LUTS guideline recommends offering posterior tibial nerve stimulation as an option for OAB/UUI symptom improvement; it does not call all such therapy a required first-line intervention alongside drugs (Strong recommendation). EAU also grades maintenance P-PTNS as effective for up to three years (level 2a) and transcutaneous PTNS as not inferior to percutaneous PTNS for urgency, frequency and quality of life (level 1a), noting that the optimal schedule has not been established.<sup>[[22]](#ref22)</sup> AUA/SUFU 2024 recommends offering tibial stimulation, SNM and/or botulinum toxin after inadequate response or intolerance and separately permits choosing minimally invasive therapy without required prior conservative trials.<sup>[[2]](#ref2)</sup>

---

## Efficacy for Fecal Incontinence

Evidence for PTNS in fecal incontinence is mixed. A prospective cohort study (n=150) showed improved FI scores in the 115 patients analyzed at a median 26 months (range 12–42) with maintenance sessions; the effect waned between sessions and the study was uncontrolled.<sup>[[17]](#ref17)</sup> However, the CONFIDeNT trial (n=227), a large double-blind RCT, found no significant difference between PTNS and sham (38% versus 31% achieved ≥50% reduction in FI episodes, p=0.396).<sup>[[8]](#ref8)</sup> Similarly, the NOTABLE trial (n=166 women) found that PTNS did not differ from sham stimulation for FI severity.<sup>[[18]](#ref18)</sup> A smaller RCT did show a modest advantage for PTNS over sham in reducing median FI episodes.<sup>[[19]](#ref19)</sup> The evidence does not strongly support PTNS for fecal incontinence based on sham-controlled data.<sup>[[20]](#ref20)</sup>

---

## Safety and Adverse Effects

PTNS typically has a favorable short-term safety profile; needle-site pain, minor inflammation or bleeding are possible.<sup>[[13]](#ref13)[[23]](#ref23)</sup> Serious device-related events were not reported in the cited major OAB trial, but this does not establish zero risk across all systems or populations. Office PTNS leaves no implanted metal, unlike implantable tibial systems; an MRI assessment still needs to account for any other implanted hardware.<sup>[[13]](#ref13)[[23]](#ref23)</sup>

---

## Contraindications

Screen against the chosen system's actual labeling; study exclusions are not universal contraindications. Medtronic NURO labeling, for example, excludes pacemakers or implantable defibrillators, a tendency to excessive bleeding, nerve damage affecting the tibial nerve or pelvic-floor function, and pregnancy or planned pregnancy. It also says not to use the device where treatment-site skin is compromised, and advises caution in patients with cardiac conditions. Pelvic radiation history or lower-tract malignancy alone are not listed as universal NURO contraindications in the manufacturer's short safety statement; evaluate the underlying condition and the full technical manual separately. These NURO rules must not be copied onto every PTNS or TTNS system.<sup>[[23]](#ref23)</sup>

---

## Limitations and Practical Considerations

- **Short-lived effect** requiring ongoing maintenance sessions, which can be burdensome<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[9]](#ref9)</sup>
- **Patient compliance** is limited by the need for weekly in-office visits during induction<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup>
- **Coverage and visit limits** vary by payer and policy; verify the patient's current benefits rather than assuming a universal lifetime cap
- These limitations have driven the development of implantable tibial nerve stimulation devices, at least two of which have received FDA marketing authorization, offering at-home stimulation with the same mechanism of action<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup>
- A poor PTNS response does not necessarily predict SNM failure; the targets and stimulation patterns differ

---

## Transcutaneous Alternative (TTNS)

Transcutaneous tibial nerve stimulation (TTNS) uses surface electrodes rather than a needle and can be performed at home. Against sham or conservative comparators, meta-analysis shows TTNS and PTNS each reduce voiding frequency by similar amounts (about 3 voids/day), though the evidence is more limited and the same analysis found no significant improvement in incontinence episodes.<sup>[[21]](#ref21)</sup> The AUA/SUFU guideline classes TTNS as a non-invasive therapy, and its systematic review found PTNS and TTNS similar for frequency, nocturia, urgency and incontinence episodes after inadequate response to other treatment.<sup>[[2]](#ref2)</sup> TTNS is particularly useful for neurological patients for whom repeated office visits are difficult.<sup>[[9]](#ref9)</sup> EAU Neuro-Urology 2026 judges tibial nerve stimulation and TENS possibly effective and safe in neurogenic LUTD but needing better RCTs; home percutaneous and transcutaneous tibial stimulation improved bladder-diary and patient-reported outcomes in multiple sclerosis but not detrusor-sphincter dyssynergia, and the effect in Parkinson disease remains unclear.<sup>[[24]](#ref24)</sup>
