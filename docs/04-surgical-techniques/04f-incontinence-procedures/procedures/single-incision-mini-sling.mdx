# Single-Incision Mini-Sling

Single-incision mini-slings (SIMS, SIS) are short polypropylene slings placed through one vaginal incision without suprapubic or groin exit incisions. The sling anchors laterally, usually into the obturator internus muscle or obturator membrane, to support the midurethra while avoiding full retropubic or transobturator trocar passage.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[2]](#ref2)[[3]](#ref3)</sup>

This page focuses on SIMS. For shared midurethral sling counseling, see [Female Slings & Suspensions](/docs/surgical-techniques/04f-incontinence-procedures/procedures/female-slings-suspensions).

---

## Indications

SIMS is most attractive when the patient values less dissection, no skin exit incisions, shorter operative time, and lower early postoperative pain, while accepting less mature long-term durability data than standard full-length MUS.<sup>[[2]](#ref2)[[3]](#ref3)[[4]](#ref4)</sup>

**Jurisdiction matters:** AUA/SUFU includes a single-incision sling among options for selected index patients, while NICE NG123 limits single-incision short mesh slings to clinical trials. Device-specific evidence and local guidance must shape this choice.<sup>[[2]](#ref2)[[6]](#ref6)</sup>

| Scenario | Why SIMS fits |
|---|---|
| **Uncomplicated index SUI** | The SIMS trial supports noninferior patient-reported success through 3 years after randomization for the adjustable anchored devices studied, in selected patients<sup>[[3]](#ref3)[[5]](#ref5)</sup> |
| **Need to reduce early pain / recovery burden** | No retropubic or full obturator exit passage |
| **Local or limited anesthesia strategy** | Smaller dissection can support lower-intensity anesthesia pathways in selected patients |
| **High concern for organ injury** | Avoids retropubic trocar passage; a pooled organ-injury signal favors SIMS, but events are uncommon and the estimate depends on the comparator and included devices<sup>[[4]](#ref4)[[5]](#ref5)</sup> |

Relative reasons to choose standard MUS instead include severe SUI, high concern for durability, complex recurrent SUI, prior failed sling, marked ISD, or need for the longest available follow-up evidence.

---

## Mechanism

SIMS uses the same midurethral support concept as RMUS and TMUS but with a shorter implant and device-specific lateral anchors. The operation depends on:

- accurate midurethral placement,
- symmetric lateral anchoring,
- avoidance of urethral compression,
- tissue ingrowth into macroporous polypropylene.

Because each SIMS device has a specific anchor and tensioning system, follow its current package instructions. The broad sequence below is illustrated by the Ajust/Altis methods in the SIMS trial; it is not a substitute for a model-specific IFU.<sup>[[5]](#ref5)</sup>

---

## Technique

1. Make a single midurethral vaginal incision.
2. Develop short bilateral paraurethral tunnels toward the obturator internus / obturator membrane.
3. Deploy the sling anchors laterally according to the device-specific system.
4. Confirm symmetric tape position under the midurethra.
5. Adjust the sling so it supports without compressing the urethra.
6. Perform cystoscopy to check the bladder and urethra for intraoperative injury.
7. Close the vaginal incision.<sup>[[5]](#ref5)[[7]](#ref7)</sup>

### Technical rules

- Small operation does not mean casual operation; anchor position and symmetry matter.
- Avoid aggressive tensioning to compensate for a short implant.
- Confirm that the tape lies flat and centered before closure.
- Counsel that dyspareunia, exposure, and recurrent SUI remain possible despite the smaller implant.

---

## Evidence Position

The UK SIMS trial randomized 600 women across 21 hospitals; 596 remained after four post-randomization exclusions. It compared adjustable anchored Ajust/Altis devices with a mix of retropubic and transobturator standard MUS. Patient-reported success was 79% (212/268) versus 76% (189/250) at 15 months and 72% (177/246) versus 67% (157/235) at 3 years after randomization, meeting the prespecified 10% noninferiority margin at both times. The trial excluded prior incontinence surgery, substantial anterior/apical prolapse and concomitant surgery, so these results do not establish performance for every mini-sling or complex patient.<sup>[[3]](#ref3)[[5]](#ref5)</sup>

The 2023 Cochrane review found similar 12-month subjective cure or improvement versus standard sling routes, while emphasizing device heterogeneity and uncertainty about long-term adverse events and durability. A later 2025 meta-analysis abstract reports 24 trials (3,687 women; mean follow-up 13.4 months), no statistically significant patient-reported cure difference, and lower day-1 pain and organ-injury signals. A non-significant difference in a pooled comparison is not, by itself, proof of noninferiority or long-term safety.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[4]](#ref4)</sup>

### Counseling cautions

| Issue | SIMS counseling point |
|---|---|
| **Long-term data** | The selected-device SIMS trial reaches 3 years after randomization; most pooled trials have shorter follow-up, and later durability remains uncertain |
| **Dyspareunia** | 11.7% (17/145) vs. 4.8% (7/145) at 3 years among respondents to direct sexual-function questions; baseline dyspareunia was also higher in the SIMS arm<sup>[[5]](#ref5)</sup> |
| **Mesh exposure** | 3.3% (9/276) vs. 1.9% (5/261) over 3 years in the SIMS trial; the difference was not statistically significant<sup>[[5]](#ref5)</sup> |
| **Further surgery for SUI** | 2.5% (7/276) vs. 1.1% (3/261) over 3 years in that trial; descriptive rates, not a device-wide prediction<sup>[[5]](#ref5)</sup> |
| **Device heterogeneity** | SIMS evidence should not be generalized blindly across all historical mini-sling products |

---

## Operative Pearls

- SIMS is best framed as a less invasive MUS option, not a universally better sling.
- Use it in patients whose severity and goals match the evidence base.
- Keep the follow-up conversation honest: this trial found less early pain and a shorter hospital stay, but not a statistically clear difference in return to normal activities within 28 days. Long-term comparative data remain less mature than for RMUS and TMUS.<sup>[[5]](#ref5)</sup>
- Persistent dyspareunia after SIMS should prompt evaluation for exposure, focal anchor tenderness, tape tension, and pelvic floor myalgia.
