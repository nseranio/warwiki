# Single-Incision Mini-Sling

Single-incision mini-slings (SIMS, SIS) are short polypropylene slings placed through one vaginal incision without suprapubic or groin exit incisions. The sling anchors laterally, usually into the obturator internus muscle or obturator membrane, to support the midurethra while avoiding full retropubic or transobturator trocar passage.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[2]](#ref2)[[3]](#ref3)</sup>

This page focuses on SIMS. For shared midurethral sling counseling, see [Female Slings & Suspensions](/docs/surgical-techniques/04f-incontinence-procedures/procedures/female-slings-suspensions).

---

## Indications

SIMS is most attractive when the patient values less dissection, no skin exit incisions, shorter operative time, and lower early postoperative pain, while accepting less mature long-term durability data than standard full-length MUS.<sup>[[2]](#ref2)[[3]](#ref3)[[4]](#ref4)</sup>

**Jurisdiction matters:** AUA/SUFU 2023 says clinicians may offer a single-incision sling to index patients selecting midurethral sling surgery (Conditional Recommendation; Grade B evidence for single-incision slings), while NICE NG123 limits single-incision short mesh slings to clinical trials. Device-specific evidence and local guidance must shape this choice.<sup>[[2]](#ref2)[[6]](#ref6)</sup>

| Scenario | Why SIMS fits |
|---|---|
| **Uncomplicated index SUI** | The SIMS trial supports noninferior patient-reported success through 3 years after randomization for the adjustable anchored devices studied, in selected patients<sup>[[3]](#ref3)[[5]](#ref5)</sup> |
| **Need to reduce early pain / recovery burden** | No retropubic or full obturator exit passage |
| **Local or limited anesthesia strategy** | Smaller dissection can support lower-intensity anesthesia pathways in selected patients |
| **High concern for organ injury** | Avoids retropubic trocar passage; a pooled organ-injury signal favors SIMS, but events are uncommon and the estimate depends on the comparator and included devices<sup>[[4]](#ref4)[[5]](#ref5)</sup> |

Relative reasons to choose standard MUS instead include severe SUI, high concern for durability, complex recurrent SUI, prior failed sling, marked ISD, or need for the longest available follow-up evidence. For Altis specifically, the manufacturer's April 2024 brief statement lists pregnancy or desire for future pregnancy, potential for further growth (for example, adolescents), known active urinary tract or operative-field infection, anticoagulant therapy, abnormal urethra (for example, fistula or diverticulum), conditions that could compromise the implant or its placement, and documented hypersensitivity or allergic reaction to polypropylene or polyurethane as contraindications. Check the selected device's current IFU.<sup>[[13]](#ref13)</sup>

## Mechanism

SIMS uses the same midurethral support concept as RMUS and TMUS but with a shorter implant and device-specific lateral anchors. The operation depends on:

- accurate midurethral placement,
- symmetric lateral anchoring,
- avoidance of urethral compression,
- tissue ingrowth into macroporous polypropylene.

Because each SIMS device has a specific anchor and tensioning system, follow its current package instructions. The broad sequence below is illustrated by the Ajust/Altis methods in the SIMS trial; it is not a substitute for a model-specific IFU.<sup>[[5]](#ref5)</sup>

---

## Technique

1. Make a single midurethral vaginal incision.
2. Develop short bilateral paraurethral tunnels toward the obturator internus / obturator membrane. Older SIMS literature described vaginal-wall perforation during trocar passage in rates as high as 2%, without a device-specific estimate; a described countermeasure for one anchored device guides the delivery trocar with the surgeon's index finger placed through the vaginal incision, directing the trocar tip toward the obturator internus once it is felt near the vaginal fornix.<sup>[[8]](#ref8)[[9]](#ref9)</sup>
3. Deploy the sling anchors laterally according to the device-specific system. One chapter author's sequence for an anchored device opens the endopelvic fascia for a direct route, aims the trocar toward the superior-medial obturator foramen, advances it beyond the inferior pubic ramus, and rotates it so the anchor enters obturator internus perpendicular to the muscle surface; purchase is tested by pulling on the deployed anchor, repositioning deeper at 90 degrees if it does not hold. These are one author's technique details for the device shown, not a validated angle for every SIS product.<sup>[[10]](#ref10)</sup> Coloplast cautions that a deployed Altis anchor is not designed to be retracted or advanced further after placement; follow the selected device's current IFU.<sup>[[13]](#ref13)</sup>
4. Confirm symmetric tape position under the midurethra.
5. Adjust the sling so it supports without compressing the urethra. The shorter implanted mesh length places more tension on a mini-sling at insertion than on a full-length MUS; one description frames a correctly placed implant as closely apposed to the urethra without laxity or redundant tape, which should be distinguished from tensioning the sling more tightly to compensate for its short length.<sup>[[9]](#ref9)</sup>
6. Perform cystoscopy to check the bladder and urethra for intraoperative injury. If urethral injury is identified, do not place a synthetic mesh sling (AUA/SUFU 2023 Clinical Principle).<sup>[[2]](#ref2)</sup> If bladder perforation is found, remove the SIMS rather than attempting a secondary insertion during the same operation; this is a chapter-author recommendation without a supporting cohort denominator and should not be generalized to other sling routes.<sup>[[9]](#ref9)</sup>
7. Close the vaginal incision.<sup>[[5]](#ref5)</sup>

### Technical rules

- Small operation does not mean casual operation; anchor position and symmetry matter.
- Avoid aggressive tensioning to compensate for a short implant.
- Confirm that the tape lies flat and centered before closure.
- Counsel that dyspareunia, exposure, and recurrent SUI remain possible despite the smaller implant.

## Evidence Position

The UK SIMS trial randomized 600 women across 21 hospitals; 596 remained after four post-randomization exclusions. It compared adjustable anchored Ajust/Altis devices with a mix of retropubic and transobturator standard MUS. Patient-reported success was 79% (212/268) versus 76% (189/250) at 15 months and 72% (177/246) versus 67% (157/235) at 3 years after randomization, meeting the prespecified 10% noninferiority margin at both times.<sup>[[5]](#ref5)</sup> The trial excluded prior incontinence surgery, substantial anterior or apical prolapse and concomitant surgery, so these results do not establish performance for every mini-sling or complex patient.<sup>[[3]](#ref3)[[5]](#ref5)</sup> A separate 2024 randomized trial enrolled women with stage II or greater prolapse and objectively confirmed SUI undergoing reconstructive or obliterative native-tissue vaginal repair; 126 were randomized to Altis and 129 to a retropubic sling. At 12 months, the composite of bothersome SUI, SUI retreatment, or treatment for retention occurred in 29 (25%) versus 24 (20%) evaluable women and met the prespecified 12-percentage-point noninferiority margin.<sup>[[12]](#ref12)</sup> This result does not establish noninferiority for SUI cure alone or longer-term outcomes.<sup>[[12]](#ref12)</sup>

The 2023 Cochrane review found little or no difference in 12-month subjective cure or improvement versus retropubic slings (RR 0.99, 95% CI 0.91–1.07; 2 trials, 297 women; low-certainty evidence) or transobturator slings (RR 1.00, 95% CI 0.97–1.03; 17 trials, 2,359 women; high-certainty evidence), while device heterogeneity and uncertainty about long-term adverse events and durability limit interpretation.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup> A later 2025 meta-analysis abstract reports 24 trials (3,687 women; mean follow-up 13.4 months), no statistically significant patient-reported cure difference, and lower day-1 pain and organ-injury signals.<sup>[[4]](#ref4)</sup> A non-significant difference in a pooled comparison is not, by itself, proof of noninferiority or long-term safety.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[4]](#ref4)</sup>

EAU 2026 (limited update March 2026) rates Ajust and Altis noninferior to conventional MUS at 15 and 36 months (level 1b) but reports higher mesh exposure, repeat SUI surgery and dyspareunia at 3 years (level 1b). Its network meta-analysis (ESTER, low-quality evidence) found retropubic and transobturator MUS more effective than single-incision slings at 12 months (OR 0.50 for TVT, 0.68 for TOT), and it notes that most single-incision data come from the withdrawn TVT Secur device. Two strong recommendations apply: tell women that short-term efficacy of Ajust and Altis appears equivalent to conventional MUS, and that long-term efficacy of any single-incision sling remains uncertain.<sup>[[7]](#ref7)</sup>

For intrinsic sphincter deficiency, the Mini RISD multicenter trial randomized 112 women with urodynamic stress incontinence and ISD to a single-incision sling (Solyx; n = 54) or a retropubic sling (n = 58) between 2016 and 2022; 108 (96%) completed 12 months. Objective cure (negative cough stress test) and subjective cure did not differ at 12 months, and 74% versus 69% reported being very much or much better (relative risk 0.91, 95% CI 0.62 to 1.3). Five women in the single-incision arm had repeat continence surgery, compared with 2 repeat retropubic slings for recurrent SUI in the retropubic arm. The trial was not designed to show long-term equivalence.<sup>[[11]](#ref11)</sup>

### Counseling cautions

| Issue | SIMS counseling point |
|---|---|
| **Long-term data** | The selected-device SIMS trial reaches 3 years after randomization; most pooled trials have shorter follow-up, and later durability remains uncertain |
| **Dyspareunia** | 11.7% (17/145) vs. 4.8% (7/145) at 3 years among respondents to direct sexual-function questions; baseline dyspareunia was also higher in the SIMS arm<sup>[[5]](#ref5)</sup> |
| **Mesh exposure** | 3.3% (9/276) vs. 1.9% (5/261) over 3 years in the SIMS trial; the difference was not statistically significant<sup>[[5]](#ref5)</sup> |
| **Further surgery for SUI** | 2.5% (7/276) vs. 1.1% (3/261) over 3 years in that trial; descriptive rates, not a device-wide prediction<sup>[[5]](#ref5)</sup> |
| **Device heterogeneity** | SIMS evidence should not be generalized blindly across all historical mini-sling products |

---

## Operative Pearls

- SIMS is best framed as a less invasive MUS option, not a universally better sling.
- Use it in patients whose severity and goals match the evidence base.
- Keep the follow-up conversation honest: this trial found less early pain and a shorter hospital stay, but not a statistically clear difference in return to normal activities within 28 days. Long-term comparative data remain less mature than for RMUS and TMUS.<sup>[[5]](#ref5)</sup>
- Persistent dyspareunia after SIMS should prompt evaluation for exposure, focal anchor tenderness, tape tension, and pelvic floor myalgia.
