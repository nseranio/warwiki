# Pelvic Floor Electrical Stimulation (PFES)

**Pelvic floor electrical stimulation (PFES)** delivers current via non-implanted electrodes to recruit pelvic-floor musculature or modulate bladder reflexes, depending on electrode placement and protocol. Tibial-nerve stimulation and extracorporeal magnetic stimulation are related but distinct modalities, discussed below for comparison. For female stress UI, ES is probably more effective than no active or sham treatment; the evidence is insufficient to establish equivalence or superiority to PFMT.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[2]](#ref2)[[4]](#ref4)</sup>

For PFMT, the first-line conservative therapy for SUI or UUI, see [Pelvic Floor PT](/docs/surgical-techniques/04f-incontinence-procedures/procedures/pelvic-floor-pt). For office-based tibial-nerve neuromodulation, see [Percutaneous Tibial Nerve Stimulation](/docs/surgical-techniques/04f-incontinence-procedures/procedures/percutaneous-tibial-nerve-stimulation). For implanted neuromodulation, see [Sacral Neuromodulation](/docs/surgical-techniques/04f-incontinence-procedures/procedures/sacral-neuromodulation). For incontinence behavioral therapy, see [Behavioral Therapy for Urinary Incontinence](/docs/surgical-techniques/04f-incontinence-procedures/procedures/behavioral-therapy-incontinence).

## Mechanism

PFES operates through two distinct neurophysiologic pathways:<sup>[[2]](#ref2)[[3]](#ref3)[[4]](#ref4)</sup>

**Stress UI (neurostimulation).** Stimulation of the motor-efferent fibers of the pudendal nerve elicits direct contraction of the pelvic floor muscles and striated periurethral musculature, supporting urethral closure. Proposed mechanisms: increased muscle bulk, improved active and resting urethral closure, passive exercise in patients who cannot voluntarily contract, and improved tissue vascularity.<sup>[[2]](#ref2)[[1]](#ref1)</sup>

**Urgency UI or OAB (neuromodulation).** Stimulation of afferent fibers of the pudendal nerve travels to the sacral cord and reflexively inhibits parasympathetic activity at the sacral micturition center, reducing detrusor overactivity and urgency. The dominant mechanism is remodeling of neuronal reflex loops, particularly the detrusor inhibition reflex.<sup>[[5]](#ref5)[[3]](#ref3)[[4]](#ref4)</sup>

**Biofeedback function.** PFES also serves as a biofeedback adjunct; by stimulating awareness, it helps patients with poor proprioception learn where and how to contract or relax the pelvic floor.<sup>[[3]](#ref3)[[1]](#ref1)</sup>

## Modalities (Non-Implanted)

The options below differ in anatomy, invasiveness and mechanism; they are not interchangeable PFES protocols. Yu's 30-RCT network meta-analysis compared six electrical classes (transcutaneous tibial, percutaneous tibial, trans-sacral, intravaginal, pelvic-floor and electroacupuncture) in women with UI. It did not study magnetic-chair stimulation as an electrical class.<sup>[[6]](#ref6)</sup>

| Modality | Electrode placement | Mechanism | Typical schedule |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Intravaginal ES (IVES)** | Vaginal probe | Direct pudendal stimulation | Home or clinic; 15–20 min, 2×/d or 2–3×/wk |
| **Intra-anal / rectal ES** | Anal plug | Direct pudendal stimulation | Clinic; for men or when vaginal route is contraindicated |
| **Surface / transcutaneous ES** | Suprapubic + perineal surface electrodes | Indirect pudendal stimulation | Clinic; 20 min, 2×/wk |
| **PTNS** (percutaneous tibial) | Needle 5 cm cephalad to medial malleolus | Retrograde sacral plexus neuromodulation | Office; 30 min, weekly × 12 wk |
| **TTNS** (transcutaneous tibial) | Surface electrodes near medial malleolus | Same as PTNS, non-invasive | Home; 20–30 min, daily or 3×/wk |
| **Extracorporeal magnetic stimulation (ExMS)** | Magnetic chair (no electrodes) | Magnetic-field-induced pelvic-floor contraction | Clinic; 20 min, 2×/wk × 8 wk |

Schedules are examples from heterogeneous studies and are not instructions for every device; follow the specific device IFU and clinical protocol. PTNS is an office-based minimally invasive OAB therapy, whereas TTNS and intravaginal ES are non-invasive options under the 2024 AUA/SUFU framework.<sup>[[29]](#ref29)</sup>

## Stimulation Parameters

| Parameter | Stress UI | Urgency UI / OAB | Mixed UI |
|---|---|---|---|
| Frequency | 20–50 Hz | 5–20 Hz (typically 10–12 Hz) | 20 Hz (compromise) |
| Pulse width | 200–1000 μs | 200–300 μs | 300 μs |
| Current intensity | Titrate to comfortable effective response within device limits | Titrate to comfortable effective response within device limits | Individualize within device limits |
| Duty cycle | 4–5 s on / 5–8 s off | 5 s on / 5 s off | 1:1 ratio |
| Session duration | 15–20 min | 20–30 min | 15–20 min |
| Frequency of sessions | Daily–every other day (home), 2–3×/wk (clinic) | 2×/d or 2–3×/wk | Every other day |
| Course | 8–12 wk | 8–12 wk | 8 wk |

These are reported protocol ranges, not universal settings or proof of a frequency-specific mechanism; electrode position, waveform, intensity and patient response also matter. Confirm parameters against the relevant device IFU and clinician protocol.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[3]](#ref3)[[4]](#ref4)</sup>

**PTNS example:** 20 Hz, 200 μs, a fine needle near the posterior tibial nerve proximal to the medial malleolus, and 30-min weekly sessions for 12 weeks; sensory or toe responses help confirm placement. Needle gauge, placement, programming and maintenance vary by system; consult its IFU rather than using this summary as an implantation or stimulation instruction.<sup>[[9]](#ref9)[[29]](#ref29)</sup>

## Efficacy by Indication

### Female stress urinary incontinence

**Cochrane 2017 (Stewart, 56 trials, n = 3,781):** ES is probably better than no active or sham treatment for SUI, but certainty differs by comparison and outcome. Evidence for subjective cure or improvement versus PFMT and for adding ES to PFMT is low quality; it cannot establish equivalence, superiority or a reliable additive benefit. Quality-of-life benefit versus no treatment has moderate-certainty evidence.<sup>[[2]](#ref2)</sup>

**Lunardi 2025 SR / meta** (7 RCTs, 411 women): ES is not superior to supervised or unsupervised PFMT for leakage reduction or QoL (very-low certainty).<sup>[[10]](#ref10)</sup>

**Goode 2003 *JAMA* RCT** (n = 200): behavioral training alone, behavioral plus PFES, and a self-help booklet. Both clinic-based programs reduced incontinence episodes by 69–72% (versus 53% with the booklet); adding PFES did not increase effectiveness of comprehensive behavioral therapy.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup>

**Han 2022 meta** (9 RCTs versus sham or no treatment, n = 982): ES significantly improves short-term QoL (p = 0.003) and reduces leakage (p &lt; 0.05).<sup>[[11]](#ref11)</sup>

### Urgency UI / OAB

**Cochrane 2016** (Stewart): ES is probably better than no treatment for OAB; insufficient evidence versus anticholinergics or other active treatments.<sup>[[4]](#ref4)</sup>

**Leonardo 2022 meta** (low-frequency pelvic ES versus PFMT or bladder training alone): MD QoL +7.41 (p = 0.008); MD incontinence episodes −1.33 (p = 0.02); RR cure or improvement 1.46 (p = 0.003).<sup>[[12]](#ref12)</sup>

### PTNS for OAB

**Xia 2021 meta** (8 RCTs, n = 420): PTNS as effective as antimuscarinics for frequency, nocturia, urge incontinence, and voided volume, with significantly fewer AEs (p = 0.0001) and lower discontinuation (p = 0.003).<sup>[[13]](#ref13)</sup>

**Yu 2024 network meta** (30 RCTs): percutaneous tibial stimulation ranked the most effective non-implanted ES modality for symptom severity (SMD −1.86); intravaginal stimulation second (SMD −0.97).<sup>[[6]](#ref6)</sup>

PTNS is a minimally invasive OAB option in the 2024 AUA/SUFU guideline; it may be chosen through shared decision-making without mandatory prior trials of behavioral, non-invasive or drug therapy. Implantable tibial systems are a different device category.<sup>[[14]](#ref14)[[29]](#ref29)</sup>

### Post-radical-prostatectomy incontinence

ES shows the most consistent benefit in the early postoperative period (1–3 mo):

- **Tang 2025 meta** (10 RCTs): short-term ES (≤ 3 mo) significantly improved ICIQ-SF (MD −3.50, p &lt; 0.05).<sup>[[15]](#ref15)</sup>
- **Zhao 2025 network meta**: ES plus biofeedback ranked highest for continence recovery (SUCRA 89.9); ES plus biofeedback plus PFMT most effective for pad weight and ICIQ-UI SF.<sup>[[16]](#ref16)</sup>
- **Mariotti 2009**: early combined PFES plus biofeedback (starting 7 d post-catheter removal), 96.7% continence at 6 mo versus 66.7% with no treatment.<sup>[[17]](#ref17)</sup>
- **Goode 2011 *JAMA*** (men with persistent post-prostatectomy incontinence &gt; 1 yr): behavioral therapy reduced incontinence, but adding biofeedback plus PFES did not enhance outcomes.<sup>[[18]](#ref18)</sup>

### Fecal incontinence

- **Cochrane** (Norton 2012; 21 trials, n = 1,525): biofeedback plus ES may be more effective than biofeedback alone or ES alone; participants 40% more likely to achieve full continence with biofeedback plus ES.<sup>[[20]](#ref20)</sup>
- **Mundet 2021 RCT** (n = 150, FI women): all four arms (Kegel, biofeedback plus Kegel, ES plus Kegel, neuromodulation plus Kegel) similarly improved severity and QoL; physiologic effects are treatment-specific: ES plus Kegel uniquely increased resting and squeeze pressure, endurance, and shortened anal sensory-evoked-potential latency.<sup>[[21]](#ref21)</sup>
- **ACOG PB 210 (2019)** and **ACG 2021**: biofeedback or PFMT first-line; ES as adjunct; sacral nerve stimulation first-line surgical for refractory FI.<sup>[[19]](#ref19)[[22]](#ref22)</sup>

## Extracorporeal Magnetic Stimulation

Completely non-invasive; the patient sits fully clothed on a magnetic chair and a pulsed magnetic field induces pelvic-floor contraction. No probes or electrodes.<sup>[[8]](#ref8)[[23]](#ref23)</sup>

- **Yang 2025 meta** (14 RCTs): significant ICIQ-SF improvement (SMD −0.73) and QoL (SMD −0.43); no substantial OAB or chronic-pelvic-pain effects.<sup>[[8]](#ref8)</sup>
- **Lim 2017 sham-controlled RCT** (n = 120): pulsed MS for SUI, 75% response (active) versus 21.7% (sham) at 2 mo (p &lt; 0.05).<sup>[[24]](#ref24)</sup>
- **Lukanović 2025 sham-controlled RCT for UUI** (n = 70): ICIQ-UI SF −4.05 versus −1.19 (p &lt; 0.05).<sup>[[25]](#ref25)</sup>
- **Li 2026 SUI meta** (17 RCTs, n = 1,389): MS improves incontinence, QoL, 1-h pad test, and MUCP, but does not significantly improve PFM strength.<sup>[[26]](#ref26)</sup>
- **AUA / SUFU 2023**: MS appears safe and may be effective for SUI; data limited by protocol heterogeneity.<sup>[[7]](#ref7)</sup>

## Home and Wearable Devices

- **Traditional home IVES.** Battery-powered with vaginal probe; programmed by clinician with patient-adjustable intensity; 15 min every other day or 20 min twice daily.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup>
- **Innovo neuromuscular electrical stimulation wearable garment (NMESWG).** FDA-cleared external garment delivering ES via surface electrodes, no vaginal probe. Pad-weight reductions reported; small samples.<sup>[[27]](#ref27)</sup>
- **Leva intravaginal sensor-based system (IVSBS).** Accelerometer-based vaginal sensor with mobile-app feedback (technology-enhanced biofeedback rather than ES); superior outcomes in symptom severity and adherence versus NMESWG.<sup>[[27]](#ref27)</sup>

## Contraindications and Adverse Effects

**Device- and route-specific screening:** A trial's exclusion criteria are not universal contraindications. Consult the actual device IFU and the treating specialist before use in a patient with a pacemaker or defibrillator or other electronic implant, during pregnancy, or with metal in a magnetic device's field. Evaluate unexplained bleeding or an active local infection before an internal probe; defer internal stimulation until wounds and postoperative restrictions permit it. Prolapse, neurological disease, denervation and recent GU surgery call for individual assessment, not an automatic six-month prohibition. Needle PTNS, surface ES, vaginal or rectal probes and magnetic chairs have different precautions. Full representative device IFUs have not been reconciled here.<sup>[[2]](#ref2)[[4]](#ref4)[[30]](#ref30)</sup>

**Adverse effects:** generally mild. Cochrane meta-analyses found no significant difference in AEs between ES and sham or no treatment (RR 1.24, 95% CI 0.84–1.83).<sup>[[2]](#ref2)[[4]](#ref4)</sup> Reported AEs:

- Vaginal discomfort, pain, or tingling (most common)
- Skin irritation (surface electrodes)
- UTI
- Mild bleeding / tenderness
- Approximately 9% experience discomfort with ES for FI<sup>[[19]](#ref19)</sup>

Serious events were not reported in Yu's included RCTs, but small, heterogeneous trials and incomplete adverse-event reporting cannot establish safety for every device or patient.<sup>[[6]](#ref6)</sup>

## Guideline Positions

| Organization | Position |
|---|---|
| **AUA / SUFU 2023**<sup>[[7]](#ref7)</sup> | ES and magnetic stimulation are emerging therapies; data inconsistent and of poor quality; ES better than sham but unclear if equivalent to PFMT |
| **Cochrane Overview 2022**<sup>[[5]](#ref5)</sup> | ES probably better than no treatment for SUI; insufficient evidence vs PFMT; adding ES to PFMT may not add benefit |
| **AUA / SUFU OAB 2024**<sup>[[29]](#ref29)</sup> | PTNS is a minimally invasive option; shared decision-making may bypass required trials of behavioral, non-invasive or drug treatment; offer tibial stimulation, SNM and/or botulinum toxin after inadequate response or intolerance |
| **NICE NG123 (female UI)**<sup>[[30]](#ref30)</sup> | Do not routinely use ES for OAB or add ES to PFMT; consider ES and/or biofeedback when a woman cannot actively contract the pelvic floor. Do not offer TTNS for OAB under this guideline |
| **ACOG PB 210 (2019)**<sup>[[19]](#ref19)</sup> | ES is an adjunct to biofeedback / PFMT for FI; biofeedback + ES may be more effective than either alone |
| **ACG 2021**<sup>[[22]](#ref22)</sup> | Sacral nerve stimulation for FI refractory to conservative therapy; PTNS evidence insufficient for FI |
| **EAU (Male UI)** | PFMT + ES significantly increases short-term continence recovery after prostatectomy |

## Synthesis — Where PFES Fits

1. **PFMT remains first-line conservative therapy** for SUI and UUI. PFES is not clearly superior to supervised PFMT for any indication.<sup>[[2]](#ref2)[[10]](#ref10)</sup>
2. **PFES is most useful** for patients who cannot voluntarily contract the pelvic floor (extreme weakness, poor proprioception); passive exercise and biofeedback role.<sup>[[3]](#ref3)[[1]](#ref1)</sup>
3. **Adding PFES to PFMT** is most consistently helpful in the early post-prostatectomy period (first 3 mo); long-term outcomes equalize.<sup>[[15]](#ref15)[[16]](#ref16)[[28]](#ref28)</sup>
4. **PTNS** ranked highest for symptom severity and QoL in Yu's network of women with UI, with moderate-to-low confidence and heterogeneous populations; this does not prove universal superiority for OAB. A smaller pooled OAB comparison found similar short-term symptom outcomes to antimuscarinics with fewer reported adverse effects.<sup>[[6]](#ref6)[[13]](#ref13)[[29]](#ref29)</sup>
5. **Magnetic stimulation** is a non-invasive alternative with growing sham-controlled evidence, particularly for SUI.<sup>[[8]](#ref8)[[26]](#ref26)[[24]](#ref24)</sup>
6. For fecal incontinence, ES is best used as an adjunct to biofeedback rather than monotherapy.<sup>[[19]](#ref19)[[20]](#ref20)</sup>

---

## Summary

Non-implanted pelvic-floor ES can be a selected adjunct to PFMT and behavioral therapy. Tibial-nerve stimulation is a related OAB neuromodulation modality; magnetic stimulation is distinct and uses no electrodes. For female SUI, ES probably improves outcomes versus no active treatment but is not established as equivalent or superior to PFMT. Consider ES or biofeedback to aid a woman who cannot actively contract the pelvic floor; do not routinely add ES to PFMT when a good contraction is possible. PTNS can be offered for OAB through shared decision-making without a mandatory step-therapy sequence; national guidance differs on TTNS. Post-prostatectomy and fecal-incontinence adjunctive findings need interpretation by population, timing and comparator.<sup>[[2]](#ref2)[[6]](#ref6)[[13]](#ref13)[[20]](#ref20)[[29]](#ref29)[[30]](#ref30)</sup>
