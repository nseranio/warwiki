# Urethral Bulking Agents

**Urethral bulking** improves mucosal coaptation by injecting material into the urethral wall. It offers a less invasive option for women with stress urinary incontinence (SUI) who prioritize a shorter procedure or wish to avoid more invasive surgery. AUA/SUFU 2023 considers bulking agents viable treatments for SUI, notes that retreatment is common, and advises counseling women on the expected need for repeat injections. Improvement is more common than durable complete dryness.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup>

This page addresses selection and procedural decisions. Product composition, labeling and device details belong in the [Bulkamid](/docs/foundations/tools/biomaterials/bulking-agents/bulkamid), [Macroplastique](/docs/foundations/tools/biomaterials/bulking-agents/macroplastique) and other device pages. See [Female SUI](/docs/clinical-conditions/03a-storage-incontinence/sui-female) for the evaluation and treatment pathway.

## Patient selection

| Situation | Decision |
|---|---|
| Patient prefers a less invasive procedure or declines mesh | Discuss bulking alongside nonmesh surgery and nonsurgical options. The lower procedural burden comes with a lower chance of dryness |
| Anesthetic risk, frailty or anticoagulation complexity | Office treatment under local anesthesia may be feasible, but retention and infection still require reliable access to follow-up |
| Recurrent SUI after a sling | Establish the cause of leakage and assess for obstruction or mesh complications before adding outlet resistance |
| Fixed, poorly mobile urethra | AUA/SUFU states that clinicians may offer pubovaginal slings, retropubic midurethral slings, bulking agents or adjustable retropubic slings (Expert Opinion). The panel prefers autologous pubovaginal sling in this setting because of suboptimal bulking outcomes. ISD alone does not establish a preference for bulking; the 2024 AUGS clinical practice statement also notes that ISD does not predict bulking outcomes.<sup>[[27]](#ref27)</sup> |
| Mixed symptoms or incomplete emptying | Separate stress leakage from urgency and voiding dysfunction before choosing an outlet procedure |

Bulking is a poor match for a patient who expects a sling-like dry rate, has severe leakage, untreated obstruction or a high PVR, has active UTI, or has a urethral diverticulum, erosion or fistula that needs anatomic management first. AUA/SUFU finds inadequate data to recommend one injectable agent over another.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup>

For Bulkamid specifically, the US indication is SUI due to ISD in adult women with SUI or stress-predominant mixed incontinence. The current manufacturer summary contraindicates acute UTI and warns against use before correction of urethral or bladder-neck strictures.<sup>[[2]](#ref2)</sup> The IFU lists populations in which safety and effectiveness have not been established: urethral hypermobility with a straining angle above 30 degrees, predominant urge incontinence, detrusor overactivity, polyuria of 3 L/24 h or more, unevaluated hematuria, prolapse above stage II, BMI above 35 kg/m² and neurogenic bladder. These are gaps in evidence and not absolute contraindications. The IFU also states that only women with well-controlled diabetes should be considered; safety and effectiveness are unestablished with fragile urethral mucosa or active genital herpes, and it advises caution with immunosuppressive therapy while autoimmune-disease safety is unestablished.<sup>[[2]](#ref2)[[3]](#ref3)</sup>

Review prior bulking products and injection sites. Bulkamid labeling advises against injecting a site previously treated with another agent, or placing another agent into a Bulkamid-treated site. It also warns not to use Bulkamid in men.<sup>[[2]](#ref2)</sup>

## Procedural workflow

Use the current instructions and training for the exact product. Needle, access system, injection depth, location and volume differ between agents, so one clock-face recipe does not fit every product. Bulking can be done as an office procedure under local anesthesia with no down time.<sup>[[18]](#ref18)</sup> Injection may be transurethral or periurethral. In the Cochrane review, the two methods gave similar outcomes, with a higher but not statistically significant rate of early complications after periurethral injection.<sup>[[5]](#ref5)</sup>

1. Confirm the stress-leakage target, baseline emptying and relevant prior procedures. Test urine and treat infection before injection.
2. Review anticoagulants and bleeding risk with the prescribing team; a short office procedure does not eliminate hematoma risk. Choose an anesthesia plan and antimicrobial prophylaxis according to the product and local protocol.
3. Inspect the urethra and bladder and confirm tissue suitability before injection.
4. For endoscopically administered systems, place deposits under direct vision in the product-specified plane, aiming for coaptation while preserving perfusion. Follow the exact instructions for other delivery systems.
5. With Bulkamid, stop if superficial mucosal capillaries fade. Avoid intravascular injection, damaged tissue and overcorrection that can obstruct the outlet.
6. Empty the bladder with a soft catheter when needed, without disrupting the deposits. An indwelling urethral catheter is avoided after bulking because it may disrupt the injected material; if retention persists, intermittent catheterization (one description uses an 8-12 Fr catheter) or, if needed, temporary suprapubic drainage has been advised instead.<sup>[[20]](#ref20)</sup>
7. Assess voiding and residual urine before discharge. Give a clear retention-management and urgent-contact plan.<sup>[[3]](#ref3)[[2]](#ref2)</sup>

The January 2020 FDA-hosted Bulkamid IFU (manufacturer instructions) describes the following sequence. It differs from the current manufacturer summary, which references a later revision, so the IFU supplied with the device governs.<sup>[[3]](#ref3)[[2]](#ref2)</sup>

- **Anesthesia.** Urethral anesthetic gel, or local anesthetic injected at 3 and 9 o'clock 5 to 10 minutes before injection, with antibiotic prophylaxis according to local protocol.
- **Site 1.** The rotatable sheath is positioned with the needle at the first site (for example 6 o'clock), about 2 cm from the bladder neck, pressed parallel to the urethral wall so the needle enters submucosa to its 1 cm mark. Angling the system risks a deposit that is too deep or too superficial.
- **Deposit.** Hydrogel is injected to the midline of the lumen while superficial vessels stay visible, under water inflow that dilates the urethra.
- **Further sites.** Two more sites (for example 2 and 10 o'clock, or 3 and 9 o'clock) are treated in the same plane by rotating only the rotatable sheath. Puncture holes are limited to reduce extravasation.
- **Volume.** The typical volume is 1.5 to 2.0 mL, with a maximum of 2 mL per session. A top-up is possible at least 4 weeks later, and safety and effectiveness are demonstrated for up to three sessions.
- **Finish.** The bladder is emptied with a 10 to 12 Fr catheter. The patient is discharged if the residual after the first void is 100 mL or less. Intermittent catheterization at home is the fallback for voiding difficulty.

These instructions apply to the cited IFU version and do not set a universal dose or schedule for other agents.

## Available agents

AUA/SUFU 2023 notes longer-term persistence of effect for calcium hydroxyapatite, polydimethylsiloxane and polyacrylamide hydrogel (PAHG) at 73.2, 83 and 96 months, and that erosion was not reported with PAHG in multiple studies. A 2021 systematic review supports Bulkamid and Macroplastique as the best-supported agents. No direct comparisons of agents have been performed.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[9]](#ref9)</sup> The EAU 2026 guideline recommends against autologous fat and hyaluronic acid as urethral bulking agents because of higher adverse-event risk (Strong).<sup>[[26]](#ref26)</sup>

| Agent | Composition | Short-term efficacy | Longer-term data | Main safety signal |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| [**Bulkamid**](/docs/foundations/tools/biomaterials/bulking-agents/bulkamid) | Polyacrylamide hydrogel (PAHG) | 30-90% cure or improvement across series | 42-70%; 7-year data | No erosion or migration in the 2021 review<sup>[[9]](#ref9)</sup> |
| [**Macroplastique**](/docs/foundations/tools/biomaterials/bulking-agents/macroplastique) | Polydimethylsiloxane | 40-85% | 21-80%; 24-month multicenter durability study of 12-month responders<sup>[[17]](#ref17)</sup> | Erosion reported; largest historical evidence base<sup>[[9]](#ref9)[[16]](#ref16)</sup> |
| [**Coaptite**](/docs/foundations/tools/biomaterials/bulking-agents/coaptite) | Calcium hydroxyapatite | Variable | 60-75% in selected series | Higher transient retention (mean 34.2%) and erosion reported<sup>[[9]](#ref9)</sup> |
| [**Durasphere**](/docs/foundations/tools/biomaterials/bulking-agents/durasphere) | Original: pyrolytic carbon-coated zirconium; current EXP: pyrolytic carbon-coated graphite<sup>[[28]](#ref28)</sup> | Variable | Limited | Lymph-node migration reported with the historical formulation<sup>[[9]](#ref9)</sup>; urinary urgency of any attribution in 44/178 (24.7%) in the original trial<sup>[[28]](#ref28)</sup> |
| [**Urolastic**](/docs/foundations/tools/biomaterials/bulking-agents/urolastic) | In situ polymerizing polydimethylsiloxane elastomer | Variable | Limited | A 65-woman series reported 17 combined exposures or erosions and 14 partial or complete implant removals (17/65, 26.2%; 14/65, 21.5%); three women lacked complication follow-up, so a later review recalculated exposure or erosion as 17/62 (27.4%).<sup>[[9]](#ref9)[[21]](#ref21)[[29]](#ref29)[[30]](#ref30)</sup> |
| [**Contigen**](/docs/foundations/tools/biomaterials/bulking-agents/historical-agents) | Cross-linked bovine collagen | Historical comparator | No longer commercially available | Comparator in the pivotal Bulkamid trial<sup>[[11]](#ref11)</sup> |

## Efficacy and the most useful comparison

### Pivotal randomized PAHG versus collagen trial

In the North American trial of 345 women (229 hydrogel, 116 collagen), 47.2% of PAHG recipients and 50.0% of Contigen recipients reported zero stress incontinence episodes at 12 months, and 77.1% versus 70.0% considered themselves cured or improved. Bulkamid was not inferior to Contigen, and major adverse events were rare.<sup>[[11]](#ref11)</sup>

### Five-year randomized PAHG versus TVT trial

The Helsinki noninferiority trial randomized 223 women; 212 received the assigned treatment and 195 attended five-year follow-up. High satisfaction (score at least 80/100) occurred in 74/99 PAHG recipients (74.7%) and 89/96 TVT recipients (92.7%). The difference was 18.0 percentage points (95% CI 7.7–28.0); PAHG did not meet the prespecified 20-point noninferiority margin.<sup>[[4]](#ref4)</sup>

Perioperative or postoperative complications before crossover were reported in 22/99 (22.2%) after PAHG and 42/96 (43.8%) after TVT. These counts include all complications and not severe complications or reoperation alone. The trial shows a tradeoff between higher satisfaction with TVT and fewer recorded complications with PAHG. Satisfaction is not objective dryness.<sup>[[4]](#ref4)</sup>

### Other evidence

The 2017 Cochrane review included 14 trials and 2,004 women, but its reported search date was November 2010. It found limited and heterogeneous evidence, generally greater improvement with surgery, and no reliable ranking of agents or injection locations. In two trials comparing injection with surgery, objective cure was significantly better with surgery. Saline injection produced improvement similar to bulking agent in one trial. It should be read alongside later trials.<sup>[[5]](#ref5)</sup>

A six-study meta-analysis (710 patients overall) pooled five studies for subjective symptom improvement at 12 months and favored surgery (bulking-versus-surgery RR 0.70, 95% CI 0.53–0.92).<sup>[[6]](#ref6)</sup> Its Clavien-Dindo grade 3 complication estimate from three of six studies was imprecise (RR 1.30, 95% CI 0.30–5.66), leaving comparative safety unresolved.<sup>[[6]](#ref6)</sup> A modeling analysis estimated 1.86 QALYs for midurethral sling and 1.82 QALYs for PAHG; the annualized difference was 0.02 QALY/year, below the stated minimally important utility difference of 0.03 per year, with PAHG cost-effective when more than 58% of procedures are done in the office.<sup>[[13]](#ref13)</sup>

An older, smaller randomized trial directly compared pubovaginal sling with transurethral Macroplastique in 45 women with SUI and ISD (22 sling, 23 Macroplastique). At the 6- and 12-month assessments, symptomatic and satisfaction success were similar, but objective success favored sling (81% versus 9%, p &lt; 0.001); Macroplastique had lower morbidity (shorter operating time, less blood loss, shorter catheterization and hospital stay) but was more expensive. At mean 62-month telephone follow-up, 60% of each arm responded, with reported continence success of 69% versus 21% and satisfaction of 69% versus 29% (p = 0.057 for satisfaction). This older ISD-selected Macroplastique trial reports outcomes for 45 women, not a current general cure estimate or a PAHG comparison.<sup>[[22]](#ref22)</sup>

Longer-term observational data support persistence of benefit for some women. Brosche's single-center series included 388 seven-year completers from 1,200 treated patients; among 261 receiving PAHG as their primary procedure, 175 (67.1%) reported cure or improvement and 51 (19.5%) underwent another continence procedure.<sup>[[7]](#ref7)</sup> This is not a seven-year cure rate in an unselected cohort. Among the 388 patients who completed seven-year follow-up, the study’s postoperative complication table records transient prolonged emptying in 48 and UTI in 11.<sup>[[7]](#ref7)</sup> A French two-center retrospective series of 320 women (median follow-up 24 months) reported 63.7% cured and 21.9% improved at last follow-up.<sup>[[12]](#ref12)</sup>

## Recurrent SUI after a sling

Bulking may suit a patient who has persistent stress leakage and wishes to avoid further dissection. An 11-study review reported a pooled 75% cure-or-improvement estimate with substantial heterogeneity, and pooled failure and reoperation rates of 32% and 25%. Subgroup estimates were 84% for Macroplastique and 80% for Bulkamid. These are not randomized head-to-head data and do not rank the products.<sup>[[8]](#ref8)</sup>

A retrospective cohort directly comparing the two retreatment options after a failed midurethral sling found a lower failure rate with a repeat sling than with urethral bulking (11.2% of 98 repeat slings versus 38.8% of 67 bulking procedures, p = 0.004), with no difference in perioperative complications between the groups. Bulking still spares the patient a repeat dissection, so this is a counseling trade-off between a lower failure rate and a less invasive procedure, not a reason to avoid bulking.<sup>[[19]](#ref19)</sup>

Persistent urgency, obstruction, urethral erosion, fistula or other structural complications require their own treatment. A later sling or other reconstruction may remain possible. The injected permanent material must be documented for future surgery, and the effect of prior injection on later operations is not established.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[3]](#ref3)</sup>

## Safety and follow-up

Counseling covers dysuria, hematuria, infection, pain, transient retention, persistent obstruction, new urgency and treatment failure. Agent-specific problems include erosion, extrusion, granuloma and migration. The 2021 systematic review found no PAHG erosion or migration, but current labeling includes them among potential adverse events.<sup>[[9]](#ref9)[[2]](#ref2)</sup>

| Complication | Reported range |
|---|---|
| **UTI** | 4-10.6% across agents (2021 review); postoperative UTI in 11 of 388 seven-year Bulkamid completers; 7/320 (2.2%) in the French Bulkamid series<sup>[[9]](#ref9)[[7]](#ref7)[[12]](#ref12)</sup> |
| **Retention** | 10/320 (3.1%) acute retention in the French Bulkamid series; postoperative transient prolonged emptying in 48 of 388 seven-year Bulkamid completers; mean 34.2% temporary acute retention with Coaptite<sup>[[12]](#ref12)[[7]](#ref7)[[9]](#ref9)</sup> |
| **Transient hematuria** | 7.2% in the French Bulkamid series<sup>[[12]](#ref12)</sup> |
| **Urinary urgency** | Reported across agents; 44/178 (24.7%) of Durasphere recipients in the original trial reported urgency of any attribution, not necessarily de novo<sup>[[9]](#ref9)[[28]](#ref28)</sup> |
| **Erosion or extrusion** | Reported with Macroplastique, Coaptite and Urolastic<sup>[[9]](#ref9)</sup> |
| **Migration** | Lymph node migration reported with Durasphere<sup>[[9]](#ref9)</sup> |

After discharge, inability to void, worsening pain, fever or substantial bleeding warrants prompt assessment. Reassess persistent leakage before repeating treatment: the target may be residual SUI, urgency, incomplete emptying or another problem. Record the material, lot, volume, location and date so later clinicians can interpret cystoscopy or imaging correctly.<sup>[[3]](#ref3)</sup> In a retrospective radiology review of 528 patients who had undergone periurethral bulking, 79 (15%) had subsequent abdominal or pelvic imaging with abnormal periurethral findings, and in 72 of 111 such studies (65%) the bulking agent was not correctly identified; the most common misdiagnoses were bladder or periurethral calcification, a urethral diverticulum with stone, an indeterminate pelvic density, and a mass suspicious for malignancy.<sup>[[23]](#ref23)</sup> This reflects imaging misinterpretation rather than a clinical complication rate, and is a further reason to document the injected material clearly for future clinicians.<sup>[[23]](#ref23)</sup>

Persistent retention after injection can reflect over-bulking or outlet obstruction, but other causes, including evolving detrusor weakness in an older patient, should also be considered. A delayed periurethral pseudocyst or abscess can present as a palpable anterior vaginal-wall mass with irritative or obstructive urinary symptoms, sometimes with negative cultures despite reported infection; imaging can help distinguish retained bulking material or a fluid collection from another pelvic mass. Case reports and small series describe transurethral aspiration or unroofing for an obstructing deposit, though aspiration alone may recur, and removing or decompressing the material can restore stress leakage. For a larger or loculated collection not reachable transurethrally, complete transvaginal drainage with preservation of periurethral fascia and watertight closure has been described; a transurethral approach is generally favored when feasible because of a theoretical urethrovaginal-fistula risk with transvaginal excision, and fistula or diverticulum formation has also been reported after spontaneous or procedural drainage. These case reports and small series describe rescue approaches without a standard protocol or product-risk comparison.<sup>[[24]](#ref24)</sup>

Vaginal estrogen may be appropriate for a separate GSM indication; it has not been shown to be required preparation for bulking. [Vaginal/topical estrogen](/docs/foundations/pharmacology/hormonal-therapies/vaginal-topical-estrogen) and [duloxetine](/docs/foundations/pharmacology/neuropathic-pelvic-pain/snris) have separate indications and jurisdiction-specific considerations.

## Men and investigational injections

AUA/GURS/SUFU 2024 recommends that clinicians counsel men with incontinence after prostate treatment that efficacy is low and cure is rare with bulking agents (Strong Recommendation, Grade B).<sup>[[10]](#ref10)</sup> A systematic review of 8 studies (mostly Macroplastique; one randomized study against AUS) reported dry rates from 0% to 83%, with short follow-up and small samples.<sup>[[14]](#ref14)</sup> A narrative review of 25 studies reported success from 13% to 100% with deterioration over time.<sup>[[15]](#ref15)</sup> Female PAHG results do not extrapolate to men, and Bulkamid labeling warns against male use.<sup>[[2]](#ref2)</sup>

[Male slings](/docs/surgical-techniques/04f-incontinence-procedures/procedures/male-urethral-slings) and [AUS](/docs/surgical-techniques/04f-incontinence-procedures/procedures/artificial-urinary-sphincter) are separate surgical pathways. Regenerative cell injections, including autologous adipose-derived and muscle-derived cells, aim to regenerate sphincter function and not merely bulk the urethra. Studies are few, small and heterogeneous, with success rates that appear comparable to conventional bulking. AUA/SUFU recommends against offering stem cell therapy outside investigative protocols (Expert Opinion).<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[16]](#ref16)</sup> A multicenter, double-blind, placebo-controlled trial of autologous muscle-derived cells for urinary sphincter repair (AMDC-USR) in women with stress or stress-predominant mixed incontinence planned a sample of 246 women, randomized 2:1 to cells or placebo; enrollment halted after 150 women were biopsied (61% of the plan), of whom 143 received cells or placebo, because of an unexpectedly high placebo response on the prespecified composite primary outcome, which prevented assessment of the intended treatment effect.<sup>[[25]](#ref25)</sup> A post hoc analysis using a more stringent response threshold suggested a possible treatment effect in a subgroup. This trial is inconclusive on its primary endpoint; a regenerative-medicine designation is not regulatory approval, and the investigational-only boundary above still applies.<sup>[[25]](#ref25)</sup>

## Counseling points

- Bulking is minimally invasive and can be done in the office under local anesthesia.
- It is less effective than midurethral or fascial sling surgery.
- Repeat injection is common.
- The 2021 systematic review reported no PAHG erosion or migration, although current labeling lists both as potential adverse events.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[9]](#ref9)[[2]](#ref2)</sup>
