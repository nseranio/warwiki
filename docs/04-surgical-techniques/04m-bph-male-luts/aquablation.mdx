# Aquablation — AquaBeam Robotic Waterjet for BPH

**Aquablation** (AquaBeam Robotic System; Procept BioRobotics) is described by its developers as the first surgical BPH therapy to combine real-time transrectal ultrasound guidance with robotically executed, heat-free, high-velocity saline waterjet ablation of the prostatic adenoma.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[2]](#ref2)[[3]](#ref3)</sup> It is a tissue-removing option with randomized evidence against TURP in 30–80 mL glands and separate prospective evidence in larger glands. Selection turns on ejaculation preservation, bleeding risk, gland size and access to the robotic system. For other BPH options, see [BPH & Male LUTS — chooser](/docs/surgical-techniques/bph-male-luts).

---

## Mechanism of action

The AquaBeam delivers a high-velocity saline jet (500–8,000 PSI) through a robotically controlled handpiece. The procedure has two phases<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup>:

1. **Cutting mode.** The surgeon maps the treatment contour on real-time TRUS, defining angle, length, depth and contour. The console then executes the ablation automatically with the high-pressure waterjet.
2. **Hemostasis after ablation.** The surgeon assesses the resection bed and controls bleeding. EAU 2026 describes a Foley balloon catheter on light traction, diathermy or low-power laser as needed, and the AUA guideline describes a limited TUR for hemostasis and focal bladder neck cautery as standard.<sup>[[25]](#ref25)[[20]](#ref20)</sup> Heat-free tissue resection therefore does not make the whole operation cautery-free.

The waterjet preferentially ablates adenomatous tissue while sparing collagenous structures such as the surgical capsule and blood vessels. The ablation itself generates no thermal energy.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[2]](#ref2)[[4]](#ref4)[[25]](#ref25)</sup>

The surgeon can define a "veru-protection zone" in the treatment plan that spares the verumontanum and ejaculatory ducts. This is the proposed mechanism for the lower rate of ejaculatory dysfunction compared with TURP.<sup>[[4]](#ref4)[[5]](#ref5)</sup> Ejaculation is preserved in a portion of men, not all: among sexually active men without anejaculation at baseline in WATER, anejaculation occurred in 10% after Aquablation.<sup>[[3]](#ref3)</sup>

---

## Procedure

The procedure is performed under general or regional anesthesia in the OR<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[6]](#ref6)</sup>:

1. A custom 22 Fr rigid cystoscope is introduced under direct vision.
2. The AquaBeam handpiece is advanced and a 15 mL balloon is inflated against the bladder neck.
3. TRUS is performed. The surgeon maps the prostate contour and resection zone on the conformal planning unit (CPU).
4. The robotic system executes the ablation automatically per plan.
5. Mean total OR time was 33 min in WATER and 37 min in WATER II; mean Aquablation resection time was 4 and 8 min, respectively. In WATER, TURP resection took 27 min (p &lt; 0.0001 versus the 4-min Aquablation resection time).<sup>[[3]](#ref3)[[6]](#ref6)</sup>
6. A three-way Foley is placed for hemostasis. Early catheter removal (before day 2) is commonly achievable.<sup>[[25]](#ref25)</sup>
7. Mean length of stay was 1.4 days in WATER and 1.6 days in WATER II. In an earlier 15-patient pilot, most men were discharged on postoperative day 1.<sup>[[6]](#ref6)[[7]](#ref7)[[25]](#ref25)</sup>

### Single vs multi-pass

In a post hoc analysis of WATER and WATER II, in which the number of passes was not randomized, two or more passes were associated with an IPSS about 4 points lower and a Qmax about 5 mL/s higher at 24–36 months than a single pass. The multi-pass group had larger prostates, and the difference in ejaculatory dysfunction was not statistically significant.<sup>[[8]](#ref8)</sup>

## Patient selection

WATER and WATER II enrolled different populations<sup>[[3]](#ref3)[[9]](#ref9)</sup>:

- Age 45–80 years (WATER).
- Moderate-to-severe LUTS. WATER excluded active urinary infection, prior urethral stricture or bladder-neck contracture, PVR &gt; 300 mL, and current or recent catheterization; these trial exclusions limit direct application of its outcomes but are not universal contraindications.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup>
- Prostate volume 30–80 mL in WATER and 80–150 mL in WATER II. Use in glands outside these ranges is reported in real-world series but has no guideline recommendation.<sup>[[3]](#ref3)[[9]](#ref9)[[11]](#ref11)</sup>
- Obstructing median lobes can be treated, whereas iTIND excludes them. An exploratory WATER subgroup analysis found larger IPSS changes after Aquablation than after TURP in men with enlarged or severely obstructing middle lobes.<sup>[[12]](#ref12)</sup> The AUA 2026 guideline notes that subanalyses found no IPSS difference by presence of a median lobe.<sup>[[20]](#ref20)</sup>
- General or regional anesthesia is required, unlike office-based MISTs.
- **EAU 2026:** offer Aquablation for moderate-to-severe LUTS/BPO and a 30–80 mL prostate as an alternative to TURP, particularly for men who want to preserve ejaculation (strong recommendation). The EAU summary of evidence rates feasibility above 80 mL as level 2a and calls for additional randomized trials, and WATER II is a single-arm study.<sup>[[9]](#ref9)[[25]](#ref25)</sup>
- **AUA 2026:** clinicians should offer robotic waterjet treatment for prostates of 30–80 cc (Moderate Recommendation; Evidence Level B) and may offer it for 80–150 cc (Expert Opinion). The panel advises considering the larger range only in appropriate patients and with sufficient procedural experience, and gives no guidance above 150 cc because evidence is limited.<sup>[[20]](#ref20)</sup>

## Clinical efficacy

### WATER: pivotal RCT vs TURP (30–80 mL)

WATER randomized 184 men (117 Aquablation, 67 TURP); 181 entered the treated safety cohort.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[3]](#ref3)</sup>

- Noninferiority was met for IPSS reduction at 6 months.
- The primary safety endpoint (Clavien-Dindo persistent grade 1 or grade 2 or higher complications) occurred in 26% versus 42% (p = 0.0149). A 2019 Cochrane review of the same trial rated the evidence for major adverse events as very low certainty.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup>
- At 12 months, IPSS fell by 15.1 points in both groups. Surgical retreatment was 2.6% after Aquablation and 1.5% after TURP (not significant).<sup>[[13]](#ref13)</sup>
- IPSS and Qmax improvements were maintained at 5 years. Surgical retreatment over 5 years was 5.1% after Aquablation and 1.5% after TURP.<sup>[[25]](#ref25)</sup>

### WATER II: large prostates (80–150 mL)

WATER II was a single-arm prospective study of 101 men<sup>[[9]](#ref9)</sup>:

| Timepoint | IPSS | Qmax (mL/s) | Freedom from retreatment |
|---|---|---|---|
| Baseline | 23.2 for the 101-patient 12-mo cohort<sup>[[6]](#ref6)</sup>; 22.6 for the 62 five-year completers<sup>[[9]](#ref9)</sup> | 8.6 in the 5-yr completer analysis<sup>[[9]](#ref9)</sup> | — |
| 12 mo | 6.2 (−17.0) | 21.1 (+12.5) | 100%<sup>[[6]](#ref6)</sup> |
| 36 mo | 6.5 (−16.3) | 18.5 (+9.8) | 97%<sup>[[33]](#ref33)</sup> |
| 5 yr (60 visit completers) | 6.8 (−15.9) | 17.1 (+9.2) | 96.3% Kaplan–Meier<sup>[[9]](#ref9)</sup> |

A prespecified subgroup analysis using a 100 mL cutoff showed no difference in efficacy outcomes through 5 years.

### WATER vs WATER II 5-year comparison

A cross-trial comparison found similar 5-year outcomes in 30–80 mL and 80–150 mL glands<sup>[[14]](#ref14)</sup>:

| Outcome | WATER (30–80 mL) | WATER II (80–150 mL) | p |
|---|---|---|---|
| IPSS at 5 yr | 7.0 | 6.8 | 0.85 |
| Qmax at 5 yr | 17.3 | 17.1 mL/s | 0.93 |
| BPH-medication-free | 99% | 94% | 0.05 |
| Freedom from surgical retreatment | 95% | 97% | 0.51 |

### WATER III: vs laser enucleation (80–180 mL)

WATER III was an investigator-initiated, nonblinded, partially randomized noninferiority trial with Bayesian analysis. It enrolled 202 men; 186 were treated (98 Aquablation, 88 laser enucleation), and 3-month data were available for 170 (66 randomized, 104 nonrandomized)<sup>[[15]](#ref15)</sup>:

- **IPSS improvement at 3 months:** −12.9 (Aquablation) versus −13.1 (laser enucleation); noninferiority probability &gt; 0.999.
- **Clavien-Dindo ≥ 2 or persistent grade 1 complications:** 40.8% vs 56.8% (estimated difference −9.4%; 95% credible interval −31.8% to 12.9%).<sup>[[15]](#ref15)</sup>
- **Retrograde ejaculation:** 14.8% vs 77.1% (p &lt; 0.001).<sup>[[15]](#ref15)</sup>
- **Persistent stress incontinence:** none after Aquablation vs 9.3% after laser enucleation (p &lt; 0.05).<sup>[[15]](#ref15)</sup>

### Pooled 5-study analysis

A pooled analysis of 528 men from WATER, WATER II, OPEN WATER, FRANCAIS WATER and JAPAN PMS reported a mean prostate volume of 71.7 mL (range 20–242 mL). IPSS fell from 21.5 to 6.6 at 12 months, Qmax rose from 8.1 to 20.5 mL/s, and PVR fell from 105.9 to 53.1 mL.<sup>[[16]](#ref16)</sup>

### Single-center real-world experience (4 years)

A prospective single-center observational study of 330 men reported<sup>[[17]](#ref17)</sup>:

- Mean prostate volume 110.3 mL (range 38–330 mL).
- IPSS 23.8 to 6.9 and Qmax 6.4 to 17.4 mL/s at 4 years.
- Prostate volume reduction of 41.3% at 1 year.<sup>[[17]](#ref17)</sup>
- Antegrade ejaculation preserved in 249 of 250 men (99.6%) with data.<sup>[[17]](#ref17)</sup>
- Transfusion in 3.3%.<sup>[[17]](#ref17)</sup> A second procedure was needed in 3.9%, including TURP of residual anterior tissue in 3.0%.<sup>[[17]](#ref17)</sup>

## Sexual function preservation

Ejaculatory preservation is the main functional difference from TURP and enucleation<sup>[[5]](#ref5)[[18]](#ref18)[[19]](#ref19)</sup>:

- **Bettencourt 2026 systematic review** (15 studies, 1,533 patients): antegrade ejaculation was preserved in 72–99.6%, and erectile function remained stable across the reviewed studies.<sup>[[18]](#ref18)</sup>
- **WATER:** at 6 months, anejaculation in sexually active men without anejaculation at baseline was 10% with Aquablation versus 36% with TURP (p = 0.0003); later follow-up found better ejaculatory function scores after Aquablation through 5 years.<sup>[[3]](#ref3)[[18]](#ref18)</sup>
- **WATER II:** antegrade ejaculation was maintained in 81% of sexually active men, versus 90% in the smaller glands of WATER (EAU).<sup>[[6]](#ref6)[[25]](#ref25)</sup>
- **WATER III:** retrograde ejaculation 14.8% versus 77.1% with laser enucleation (p &lt; 0.001).<sup>[[15]](#ref15)</sup>
- **Perez 2026:** ejaculatory function was preserved in 82.8% of 318 prospectively followed Aquablation patients versus 36% of TURP and 18% of HoLEP patients. The TURP and HoLEP comparators (n = 83 each) came from a retrospective database, and the groups differed in prostate size.<sup>[[19]](#ref19)</sup>

The AUA 2026 guideline directs clinicians to counsel patients about erectile, ejaculatory and sexual function after procedural intervention (Clinical Principle).<sup>[[20]](#ref20)</sup> The veru-protection zone is the proposed mechanism.<sup>[[4]](#ref4)[[21]](#ref21)</sup>

## Safety profile and bleeding

In WATER, the primary safety endpoint at 3 months occurred in 26% of Aquablation and 42% of TURP patients (p = 0.0149).<sup>[[3]](#ref3)</sup> In WATER's 12-month report, no procedure-related adverse events were recorded from months 6 to 12.<sup>[[13]](#ref13)</sup> De novo incontinence was 2% at 12 months in WATER II.<sup>[[6]](#ref6)</sup>

Bleeding is the principal safety concern. In a nonrandomized multicenter comparison (75 per arm), postoperative hemoglobin decline was 2.6 g/dL after Aquablation versus 0.4 g/dL after HoLEP, while one patient in each arm required transfusion; follow-up was 6 months.<sup>[[27]](#ref27)</sup>

- **WATER:** one transfusion after Aquablation and none after TURP (EAU 2026). The AUA 2026 guideline notes that early studies showed a greater hemoglobin decrease with waterjet treatment than with TURP.<sup>[[25]](#ref25)[[20]](#ref20)</sup>
- **WATER II (large prostates):** the 12-month report recorded postoperative transfusion in 10 of 101 men (10%) and takeback fulguration in 5 (5%).<sup>[[6]](#ref6)</sup> No formalized bladder neck cautery was performed in that study. Standard traction with a limited TUR was associated with less hemoglobin decrease and transfusion than heavier traction or standard traction alone, especially in medium and large prostates, and focal bladder neck cautery is now considered a standard part of the procedure.<sup>[[20]](#ref20)</sup>
- **Post-market data (Bitar 2026):** a manufacturer database combined with FDA MAUDE reports covering 70,270 procedures gave a combined rate of transfusion or return to the OR for hemostatic fulguration of 0.2%.<sup>[[11]](#ref11)</sup> Passive reporting can miss events, so this figure is not comparable with the prospectively ascertained WATER II rates and should not be quoted as an individual patient's risk.<sup>[[11]](#ref11)[[26]](#ref26)</sup>
- A prospective comparative study reported that complication rates declined significantly after the first 30 Aquablation cases.<sup>[[19]](#ref19)</sup>

No Aquablation-specific VTE-prophylaxis recommendation exists; apply the general urologic-surgery risk framework on the [VTE Prophylaxis](/docs/foundations/pharmacology/perioperative-eras/vte-prophylaxis) page.

## Learning curve

In a retrospective analysis of 175 consecutive patients treated by three surgeons, the trifecta (operative time &lt; 60 min, hemoglobin reduction ≤ 2 g/dL and no Clavien-Dindo grade ≥ 2 complication within 90 days) was achieved in more than 50% of cases after 4 cases and 70% after 50. The pentafecta, which adds IPSS reduction and ejaculation preservation, exceeded 50% after 38 cases.<sup>[[21]](#ref21)</sup> Hemoglobin drop and ejaculation preservation improved with experience, whereas grade ≥ 2 complications and 3-month IPSS reduction did not depend on it.<sup>[[21]](#ref21)</sup> The 2026 EAU endourology perspective describes a shorter learning curve than HoLEP or AEEP, which it estimates at 50–200 cases.<sup>[[10]](#ref10)</sup>

## Retreatment and durability

| Source | Population | Surgical retreatment |
|---|---|---|
| WATER (30–80 mL) | 5 yr | 5.1% surgical retreatment<sup>[[25]](#ref25)</sup> |
| WATER II (80–150 mL) | 5 yr | 3/101 (3.0%) underwent BPH surgery; 96.3% Kaplan–Meier freedom<sup>[[9]](#ref9)</sup> |
| Single-center, n = 330 | Up to 4 yr | 10/330 (3.0%) underwent TURP for residual anterior tissue (BPH retreatment); separately, 13/330 (3.9%) had any second procedure, including 2 for bleeding and 1 for bladder-neck disruption<sup>[[17]](#ref17)</sup> |
| Shin 2024 BJU Int network meta-analysis<sup>[[22]](#ref22)</sup> | 12-mo reintervention ranking | Ranked 2nd, behind TURP |
| Akgul 2025 systematic review<sup>[[23]](#ref23)</sup> | 5 yr | 4.4–6% |

For comparison, the Akgul review reported surgical reintervention of 13.6% for UroLift and 11.1% for Rezūm at 5 years, 11.1% for iTIND at 4 years, 2.5% for Optilume BPH at 2 years, and 21% for PAE at 2 years.<sup>[[23]](#ref23)</sup> It noted that industry sponsorship was high-risk in 65% of the included studies and that follow-up varied.<sup>[[23]](#ref23)</sup> The 2026 EAU endourology perspective describes AEEP as the reference standard for size-independent treatment, with long-term retreatment of about 1–2%.<sup>[[10]](#ref10)</sup>

## Aquablation vs AEEP (HoLEP) — key trade-offs

The 2026 EAU endourology perspective is a narrative review by authors with industry ties, and it summarizes<sup>[[10]](#ref10)</sup>:

| Parameter | Aquablation | AEEP / HoLEP |
|---|---|---|
| Prostate size range | 30–150+ mL | Any size |
| Learning curve | ~ 30–50 cases | ~ 50–200 cases |
| 5-yr surgical retreatment | 3–8% | 1–2%<sup>[[10]](#ref10)</sup> |
| Ejaculatory outcomes | 72–99.6% antegrade ejaculation after Aquablation across reviewed studies<sup>[[18]](#ref18)</sup> | 18% preservation in Perez HoLEP cohort<sup>[[19]](#ref19)</sup>; 77.1% retrograde ejaculation after laser enucleation at 3 mo in WATER III<sup>[[15]](#ref15)</sup> |
| Stress incontinence | 0% persistent at 3 mo in WATER III<sup>[[15]](#ref15)</sup> | 9.3% persistent at 3 mo in WATER III; longer-term comparative risk undetermined<sup>[[15]](#ref15)</sup> |
| Anesthesia | General / regional | General / regional |
| Tissue for pathology | Yes | Yes |

In the EAU network meta-analysis of 23 studies, HoLEP gave greater Qmax (+3.24 mL/s) and PVR (−23 mL) improvement, IPSS and quality of life were similar, and HoLEP carried a higher incontinence risk (RR 4.48; 95% CI 1.02–19.65).<sup>[[25]](#ref25)</sup>

## Emerging applications

**Localized prostate cancer with concurrent LUTS.** A small prospective study in men on active surveillance (PIRADS ≥ 3 lesions) reported improved IPSS, stable or improved sexual function and a transient rise in circulating tumor cells that lasted less than 2 days. No grade group progression was seen at 6 months. The authors concluded that Aquablation may be a safe option in this setting; the evidence is limited to this small study.<sup>[[24]](#ref24)</sup>

---

## Where Aquablation fits

Aquablation is a resective procedure with a lower rate of ejaculatory dysfunction than TURP or enucleation.

- WATER showed IPSS improvement noninferior to TURP in 30–80 mL glands, and WATER II showed similar 5-year outcomes in 80–150 mL glands in a single-arm design.
- Obstructing median lobes are treatable without device modification.
- Surgical retreatment at 5 years is about 3–8% after Aquablation versus about 1–2% after AEEP in the EAU Endourology narrative review.<sup>[[10]](#ref10)</sup>
- The learning curve appears shorter than that of enucleation.

Compared with HoLEP, the network meta-analysis found smaller Qmax and PVR improvements after Aquablation.<sup>[[25]](#ref25)</sup> Separate long-term cohorts report about 3–8% surgical retreatment after Aquablation and 1–2% after AEEP, but WATER III has only 3-month comparative follow-up, so relative long-term durability remains untested.<sup>[[10]](#ref10)[[15]](#ref15)</sup> Aquablation also requires the AquaBeam robotic system. Limitations versus office-based MISTs (UroLift, Rezūm, iTIND, Optilume BPH) are the need for general or regional anesthesia and an OR setting. Bleeding remains a consent and perioperative-planning issue, and post-market reporting should be read with the limitations described above.<sup>[[11]](#ref11)[[26]](#ref26)</sup>
