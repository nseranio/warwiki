# TUIP — Transurethral Incision of the Prostate

**Transurethral Incision of the Prostate (TUIP)** is a short procedure for bladder outlet obstruction in men with small prostates (≤ 30 g), described by the authors of a randomized trial as underutilized.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[3]](#ref3)[[4]](#ref4)</sup> Randomized trials showed symptom relief equivalent to TURP, with lower perioperative morbidity, shorter operative time and less retrograde ejaculation.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[11]](#ref11)</sup> The AUA 2026 guideline states that clinicians may offer TUIP for prostates &lt; 30 cc without a median lobe (Clinical Principle).<sup>[[19]](#ref19)</sup> TUIP or bladder neck incision is also used for primary bladder neck obstruction (PBNO), especially in younger men.<sup>[[5]](#ref5)[[6]](#ref6)</sup> For positioning against other BPH options, see [BPH & Male LUTS — chooser](/docs/surgical-techniques/bph-male-luts).

---

## Mechanism

Unlike [TURP](/docs/surgical-techniques/bph-male-luts/turp), which removes tissue, TUIP works by incising the bladder neck and prostatic urethra without removing prostatic tissue. As the incisions are deepened through the prostatic capsule, the bladder neck and prostatic urethra spring open, relieving the circumferential compression that causes obstruction.<sup>[[2]](#ref2)[[7]](#ref7)</sup> The tissue splays apart, widening the channel, much as a tight ring does when it is cut. No tissue is resected and no specimen is available for histological analysis.<sup>[[8]](#ref8)</sup> 

---

## Procedure

TUIP is performed under regional or general anesthesia. Operative time is typically 10–20 min.<sup>[[2]](#ref2)[[3]](#ref3)[[9]](#ref9)</sup> 

1. Standard resectoscope (or cystoscope with Collins knife or Collings electrode) into the prostatic urethra. 
2. Identify interureteric ridge (proximal limit), verumontanum (distal limit), and ureteral orifices. 
3. Incision technique:
   - Single incision at the 6 o'clock position from just distal to the interureteric ridge to the verumontanum, deepened through prostatic tissue until the surgical capsule or periprostatic fat is reached.<sup>[[7]](#ref7)[[9]](#ref9)</sup> 
   - Double incision at the 5 and 7 o'clock positions with the same proximal-to-distal trajectory; more commonly described in the literature.<sup>[[2]](#ref2)</sup> 
4. Deepen with electrocautery (monopolar or bipolar) or holmium:YAG laser until the bladder neck visibly springs open.
5. Hemostasis with coagulation.
6. Foley catheter placed; often removed within 24 hours, or omitted entirely with laser TUIP. 

### Energy sources

- **Monopolar electrocautery** (Collins knife): traditional approach. 
- **Bipolar electrocautery** with saline irrigation avoids the TUR-syndrome risk of monopolar current.
- **Holmium:YAG laser.** In a prospective series of 100 men with glands &lt; 30 g (Cornford 1998), 97 voided without postoperative catheterization. IPSS fell from 19.2 to 3.7 and Qmax rose from 9.8 to 19.2 mL/s at 6 weeks, and both remained improved at 2 yr. All 77 men who were potent preoperatively remained potent, and 8 (10.4%) developed retrograde ejaculation.<sup>[[10]](#ref10)</sup>

## Patient selection

TUIP suits a specific subset of men with BPH<sup>[[2]](#ref2)[[8]](#ref8)[[4]](#ref4)</sup>:

- **Prostate size ≤ 30 g.** This is the main selection criterion. Trials set upper limits of about 20 to 30 g, and the AUA 2026 guideline uses &lt; 30 cc.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[19]](#ref19)</sup> The 2001 meta-analysis found no clear size cutoff above which results fall off.<sup>[[11]](#ref11)</sup>
- Moderate-to-severe LUTS with urodynamic evidence of bladder outlet obstruction.
- **No significant median lobe.** TUIP does not address median-lobe obstruction. 
- **Primary bladder-neck obstruction (PBNO).** Incision is an established treatment, particularly in younger men (mean age approximately 40 yr) in whom alpha-blockers gave inadequate relief.<sup>[[5]](#ref5)[[6]](#ref6)</sup> 
- **Ejaculatory-preservation priority.** Retrograde ejaculation is less frequent after TUIP than after TURP.<sup>[[3]](#ref3)[[11]](#ref11)</sup> 
- Can be performed as an outpatient procedure.<sup>[[8]](#ref8)</sup>

## Clinical efficacy

### Yang 2001 and Lourenco 2010 systematic reviews — TUIP vs TURP

Yang 2001 pooled 9 RCTs. Lourenco 2010 pooled 10 RCTs (795 participants) of moderate to poor quality.<sup>[[11]](#ref11)[[1]](#ref1)</sup>

- Symptom improvement was equivalent at 12 mo.<sup>[[11]](#ref11)[[1]](#ref1)</sup>
- Qmax improvement was greater with TURP (+112% vs +76% with TUIP in the pooled data cited by Oesterling).<sup>[[2]](#ref2)</sup> 
- TUIP had shorter operative time and shorter length of stay.<sup>[[11]](#ref11)[[1]](#ref1)</sup> 
- Reoperation at 12 mo was equivalent in Yang 2001. Lourenco 2010 found a higher reoperation rate after TUIP, and Yang 2001 noted little evidence beyond 12 mo.<sup>[[11]](#ref11)[[1]](#ref1)</sup> 

### Riehmann 1995 RCT — 120 randomized, 112 evaluated at a mean of 34 mo<sup>[[3]](#ref3)</sup>

- No significant difference in irritative / obstructive / total symptom scores at any visit.
- Qmax higher (not statistically) with TURP.
- Retrograde ejaculation developed in 15 of 22 (68%) sexually active men after TURP and 8 of 23 (35%) after TUIP (p = 0.020). 
- Retreatment 16% (TURP) versus 23% (TUIP), not significant (p = 0.908). 
- The authors concluded that in small prostates (estimated resectable weight &lt; 20 g) TURP and TUIP were generally equally effective, and called TUIP "an underutilized procedure".

### Christensen 1990 RCT — 93 patients enrolled to date, single 6 o'clock incision<sup>[[9]](#ref9)</sup>

- 80–90% of patients in each arm reported improvement at 3 mo. Prostates were estimated at ≤ 20 g.
- No significant difference in symptom scores or Qmax.
- OR time, blood loss, catheter duration, LOS all significantly favored TUIP. 
- Retrograde ejaculation 37% (TURP) versus 13% (TUIP), not statistically significant in this sample. 

### Sirls 1993 long-term — 41 patients, mean 53 mo<sup>[[12]](#ref12)</sup>

- Significant decrease in total / obstructive / irritative scores (p &lt; 0.05).
- Only 11% new retrograde ejaculation.

### Comparison figures attributed to Wei 2025 *JAMA* review<sup>[[4]](#ref4)</sup>

| Parameter | **TUIP** | TURP |
|---|---|---|
| Prostate size | ≤ 30 g | ≤ 80 g |
| IPSS Δ | −12.7 at 48 mo | −15.1 at 12 mo / −13.2 at 60 mo |
| Qmax Δ | +8.2 mL/s at 48 mo | +10.6 / +6.3 |
| 5-yr retreatment | 13% | 7.7% |
| Incontinence (0–3 mo) | 0–2% | 0–5% |
| Erectile dysfunction | 8% | 14% |
| Retrograde ejaculation | 22% | 60–75% |

## Retreatment and durability — TUIP's principal limitation

| Source | Population | Follow-up | Retreatment |
|---|---|---|---|
| He 2023 SR | 130,106 patients across all BPH procedures<sup>[[13]](#ref13)</sup> | 5 yr | TUIP 13.4% vs TURP 7.7%, HoLEP 6.6%, Aquablation 4.1% |
| Lourenco 2010 SR | 10 RCTs, 795 patients<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup> | up to several years | Higher reoperation rate after TUIP than TURP |
| Page 2025 NHS England retrospective study | 155,874 patients<sup>[[14]](#ref14)</sup> | up to 10 yr | Less invasive treatments, which included TUIP, were associated with more reintervention than TURP but lower odds of in-hospital complications |
| Katz 1990 single-incision series | 66 pts, mean 2.24 yr<sup>[[7]](#ref7)</sup> | medium | Subsequent TURP 7.6% |

## Safety profile

TUIP carries lower rates of transfusion and TUR syndrome than TURP. Urinary retention, UTI, stricture and incontinence did not differ significantly between the two, although clinically important differences could not be excluded.<sup>[[2]](#ref2)[[11]](#ref11)[[1]](#ref1)</sup> 

| Complication | **TUIP** | TURP |
|---|---|---|
| Transfusion | 1% | 6% |
| TUR syndrome | ~ 0% (rare) | 1–2% |
| Impotence / ED | 2–8% | 5–14% |
| Retrograde ejaculation | 11–35% | 37–75% |
| Incontinence | 0–2% | 0–6% |
| UTI | similar | similar |
| Urethral stricture | similar | similar |
| Urinary retention | similar | similar |
| Mortality | extremely rare | 0.1% |

## Sexual function — TUIP's defining advantage

- Across four early RCTs, retrograde ejaculation occurred in 15% after TUIP and 66% after TURP.<sup>[[2]](#ref2)</sup> The AUA 2026 guideline cites 18.2% vs 65.4%.<sup>[[19]](#ref19)</sup> 
- Riehmann RCT: 35% versus 68% (p = 0.020).<sup>[[3]](#ref3)</sup> 
- In a single-incision series (Katz 1990), antegrade ejaculation was preserved in 83.3% of men who had it preoperatively.<sup>[[7]](#ref7)</sup> 
- After holmium-laser TUIP (Cornford 1998), retrograde ejaculation developed in 10.4% of preoperatively potent men.<sup>[[10]](#ref10)</sup> 
- At a mean of 53 mo (Sirls 1993), 11% of men reported new retrograde ejaculation.<sup>[[12]](#ref12)</sup> 
- Pooled impotence: 2% TUIP versus 5% TURP.<sup>[[2]](#ref2)</sup> 

**Proposed mechanism of preservation.** TUIP makes one or two discrete incisions through the bladder neck. TURP resects the bladder neck circumferentially. The incisions are thought to leave more of the internal sphincter's circular muscle fibers intact and so maintain the antegrade ejaculatory pathway.<sup>[[15]](#ref15)</sup> 

### Ejaculation-Sparing Bladder Neck Incision (ESBNI)

Sinha 2026 was a retrospective cohort with prospective outcome assessment of men ≤ 40 years with videourodynamically confirmed PBNO. A single 6 o'clock incision with the distal extent limited to approximately 10 mm proximal to the verumontanum preserved antegrade ejaculation in 100% of patients with available data (51/53) at a median follow-up of 13 mo, and 90% had normal ejaculatory function. Median postoperative Qmax was 17 mL/s, and 18 of 19 catheter-dependent patients became catheter-free. Three patients required redo surgery.<sup>[[16]](#ref16)</sup>

## TUIP for primary bladder-neck obstruction (PBNO)

PBNO affects younger men (mean age approximately 40 yr) who present with LUTS, decreased flow and elevated voiding pressures localized to the bladder neck on video-urodynamics. Alpha-blockers are first-line. The AUA 2026 guideline states that clinicians may offer TUIP, transurethral resection of the bladder neck or a temporary implantable prostate device in suspected PBNO (Expert Opinion), and notes that long-term data are limited.<sup>[[19]](#ref19)</sup> 

| Series | n | Key finding |
|---|---|---|
| Trockman 1996<sup>[[5]](#ref5)</sup> | 36 (mean age 41); 18 underwent incision | Retrospective review. In the 18 men who underwent incision, symptom scores, Qmax, PVR and voiding pressures improved significantly, with a mean 87% overall symptom improvement. Only 30% of men who started alpha-blockers continued them long term, usually because of inadequate relief. |
| Kaplan 1994<sup>[[6]](#ref6)</sup> | 34 (age 26–51) treated as chronic nonbacterial prostatitis; 31 had bladder-neck obstruction on urodynamics | Incision at 5 o'clock from the bladder neck to the verumontanum; marked improvement in 30 of 31 and Qmax 9.2 to 16.4 mL/s at 3 mo. All 31 reported postoperative antegrade ejaculation. |
| Kochakarn 2003<sup>[[17]](#ref17)</sup> | 35 (mean age 40) | Unilateral incision: 55% IPSS reduction and 95% Qmax improvement at 1 yr. Sperm count fell 63–69% to 18–21 million/cc, which the authors considered probably sufficient for fertility. |

## Holmium-laser TUIP vs GreenLight PVP for small prostates

A retrospective review of a prospectively maintained database compared 47 holmium TUIP and 144 GreenLight PVP procedures in men with prostates &lt; 40 mL.<sup>[[18]](#ref18)</sup>

- Holmium TUIP had a shorter operative time (30 vs 46 min) and a longer hospital stay (0.8 vs 0.3 d) and catheter duration (1.3 vs 0.4 d). 
- IPSS, QoL, PVR and Qmax improvements were comparable at 1 and 5 yr. 
- Reoperation was 6.4% after TUIP and 10.4% after PVP (not significant). 
- Mean estimated cost per procedure was lower for holmium TUIP ($509 CAD vs $1,766 CAD, p = 0.002).

## Where TUIP fits in 2026

| Strength | Limitation |
|---|---|
| Symptom relief equivalent to TURP at 12 mo in small prostates | Limited to prostates ≤ 30 g |
| Lower retrograde ejaculation (11–35% vs 37–75% after TURP) | Higher long-term retreatment rate (13.4% at 5 yr vs 7.7% after TURP) |
| Short operative time (10–20 min) | No tissue for histological analysis |
| Outpatient feasibility | Does not address median-lobe obstruction |
| Lower perioperative morbidity (transfusion 1%, TUR syndrome ~ 0%) | Smaller Qmax improvement than TURP |
| Option for PBNO (AUA 2026, Expert Opinion) | Used less often than TURP |
| Low cost, especially with holmium laser (one Canadian series) | Limited long-term data |

Randomized trials, most of moderate to poor quality, found TUIP and TURP equivalent for symptom relief in small prostates over the first year, and the authors of one trial described TUIP as underutilized.<sup>[[3]](#ref3)[[11]](#ref11)[[1]](#ref1)</sup> It suits men with small prostates (≤ 30 g), PBNO, or a priority on ejaculatory preservation. It competes for the ejaculation-sparing role with [UroLift](/docs/surgical-techniques/bph-male-luts/urolift-pul), [Rezūm](/docs/surgical-techniques/bph-male-luts/rezum-water-vapor-therapy) and [iTIND](/docs/surgical-techniques/bph-male-luts/itind). TUIP requires anesthesia and carries a higher retreatment rate than TURP.
