# GreenLight PVP — Photoselective Vaporization of the Prostate

**GreenLight Photoselective Vaporization of the Prostate (PVP)** is an FDA-cleared 532 nm laser-based BPH therapy that vaporizes obstructive prostatic tissue with simultaneous hemostatic coagulation. Randomized evidence supports outcomes equivalent to TURP for prostates up to about 80 mL, with less bleeding.<sup>[[12]](#ref12)[[13]](#ref13)[[14]](#ref14)[[16]](#ref16)</sup> The AUA 2026 guideline states that clinicians should offer PVP as an option for LUTS/BPH (Strong Recommendation; Evidence Level B), and it lists PVP among the options a clinician may offer to patients at high risk of bleeding or requiring anticoagulant or antiplatelet therapy (Expert Opinion).<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup> For other BPH options, see [BPH & Male LUTS — chooser](/docs/surgical-techniques/bph-male-luts).

---

## Mechanism

The GreenLight laser emits a 532 nm wavelength (green light) that is selectively absorbed by oxyhemoglobin but not by water. The result is rapid heating and vaporization of the highly vascular prostatic adenoma while sparing surrounding water-rich structures.<sup>[[2]](#ref2)[[3]](#ref3)</sup> Tissue is converted to vapor and evacuated through the irrigant, creating an immediate cavity similar to TURP. The laser also coagulates blood vessels at the resection surface, and this is the proposed explanation for the lower bleeding rates compared with electrocautery-based TURP.<sup>[[3]](#ref3)[[4]](#ref4)</sup>

## Device evolution

| Generation | Power | Crystal | Fiber | Advance |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1st (PV / KTP) | 60–80 W | Potassium-titanyl-phosphate | Side-firing | Original platform; smaller prostates |
| 2nd (HPS) | 120 W | Lithium triborate (LBO) | Side-firing | Higher power, faster vaporization, larger prostates |
| 3rd (XPS) | 180 W | LBO | MoXy fiber (liquid-cooled, per the manufacturer) | Faster vaporization; often a single fiber per case |

The AUA 2026 panel agreed that the 80 W laser is less effective than the 120 W and 180 W lasers and that the higher-power laser is best when there is a choice.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup>

A meta-analysis of 8 studies comparing 180 W XPS with 120 W HPS found that XPS had shorter operative time (MD 12.7 min, p = 0.0008), shorter lasing time (MD 10.1 min, p = 0.03) and shorter catheterization (MD 0.43 d, p = 0.007). Overall complications were lower with XPS (OR 1.52 for HPS, p = 0.005). IPSS and Qmax did not differ, quality of life differed slightly (MD 0.43, p = 0.02), and PSA reduction was greater with XPS (MD −0.77, p = 0.003).<sup>[[5]](#ref5)</sup> A prospective nonrandomized single-center comparison (120 XPS and 80 HPS cases) found that XPS required fewer fibers (1.0 versus 1.5) and fewer saline bags (4.1 versus 7.0).<sup>[[6]](#ref6)</sup>

## Procedure

The procedure is performed under general, spinal or local anesthesia. Median total OR time was 62 min (IQR 46–85) in an expert-center database and 90 min in men with prostates larger than 100 mL.<sup>[[7]](#ref7)[[8]](#ref8)</sup>

1. Introduce a standard cystoscope (22–26 Fr) into the prostatic urethra.
2. Advance the side-firing fiber through the working channel.
3. Vaporize layer by layer with a "painting" sweep at near-contact distance.
4. Work from the bladder neck distally to the verumontanum, treating the lateral lobes and the median lobe as needed.
5. In the Global GreenLight Group cohort, median lasing time was 34 min (IQR 23–48) and median energy 250 kJ (IQR 168–368).<sup>[[7]](#ref7)</sup> A single fiber sufficed in 92.6% of procedures.<sup>[[7]](#ref7)</sup>
6. The catheter was removed on postoperative day 1 in 60.1%, with a median catheterization of 2 days.<sup>[[7]](#ref7)</sup>
7. Median length of stay was about 24 hours, and same-day discharge is described.<sup>[[7]](#ref7)[[8]](#ref8)</sup>

## Patient selection

- Moderate-to-severe LUTS.
- Prostate volume up to about 80 mL is the range with randomized evidence. Use in larger glands is feasible, but retreatment rates rise, particularly after 3 years.<sup>[[8]](#ref8)[[24]](#ref24)</sup>
- Patients on antithrombotic therapy have been treated in retrospective series, including those on warfarin, aspirin, clopidogrel and combinations.<sup>[[9]](#ref9)[[10]](#ref10)[[11]](#ref11)[[24]](#ref24)</sup>
- Obstructing median lobes can be treated.
- Patients of high ASA class who may not tolerate TURP have been included in these series.<sup>[[10]](#ref10)[[11]](#ref11)</sup>
- PVR &lt; 250 mL.

## Clinical efficacy: equivalence to TURP (GOLIATH)

GOLIATH is the main RCT supporting PVP. It randomized 281 men to 180 W XPS PVP or TURP at 29 centers in 9 European countries. Patients and physicians were not blinded.<sup>[[12]](#ref12)[[13]](#ref13)[[14]](#ref14)</sup>

- XPS was noninferior to TURP for IPSS, Qmax and the complication-free proportion at 6 months, and noninferiority was maintained at 12 and 24 months.
- Prostate volume, PSA and PVR reductions did not differ significantly between arms at 12 months.
- The 24-month complication-free rate was 83.6% (XPS) versus 78.9% (TURP).
- Time to stable health, catheterization time and length of stay were shorter with XPS.
- Early reintervention within 30 days was three times higher after TURP (p = 0.025), and overall reintervention rates were not significantly different.<sup>[[12]](#ref12)</sup>

### Systematic reviews and meta-analyses

A meta-analysis of randomized trials and prospective studies (22 publications, 2,665 patients) found no significant difference in IPSS, Qmax, QoL, PVR or IIEF at 3, 24, 36 or 60 months. PVP was associated with less blood loss, transfusion, clot retention, TUR syndrome, capsular perforation, catheterization time and hospitalization, and with a higher reintervention rate and longer procedure time (all p &lt; 0.05).<sup>[[15]](#ref15)</sup> A meta-analysis of 13 randomized trials (1,757 patients; some authors employed by the manufacturer) found less transfusion (RR 0.26; 95% CI 0.12–0.58) and clot retention (RR 0.12; 95% CI 0.05–0.32), and less sexual dysfunction (RR 0.66; 95% CI 0.45–0.98, p = 0.04). Procedure time was about 10 min longer with GreenLight, and reoperation did not differ significantly (RR 1.17; 95% CI 0.82–1.66).<sup>[[16]](#ref16)</sup>

### Global GreenLight Group: large international experience

The Global GreenLight Group is a retrospective series of 3,627 men treated with the XPS 180 W system by eight expert surgeons in seven centers<sup>[[7]](#ref7)</sup>:

- Median prostate volume 64 mL (IQR 47–90).
- 30-day all-cause mortality 0.3%.
- Median catheterization 2 days.
- IPSS, QoL, Qmax and PVR improved significantly, with improvement sustained through 60 months in men with long follow-up.
- Median PSA reduction 43.9% at 3 months and 46.4% at 60 months.

---

## Long-term outcomes (5–10 yr)

- **120 W HPS, 5 yr (retrospective, n = 159):** success 82.1%, 80.8% and 76.1% at 1, 3 and 5 years; reoperation 3.1% repeat PVP and 1.2% for bladder neck contracture or stricture.<sup>[[17]](#ref17)</sup>
- **80 W KTP versus TURP, 5-yr RCT:** 238 men were randomized and 105 had 5-year data. IPSS, PVR and Qmax improvements were similar, and retreatment was 14.3% versus 11.9% (p = 0.9).<sup>[[18]](#ref18)</sup>
- **10-yr follow-up (retrospective, n = 1,154; 80 W then 120 W; median follow-up 24 months, maximum 125):** IPSS, QoL and PSA improved significantly at 10 years, whereas Qmax and PVR did not. Retreatment-free survival was 93.9% at 5 years and 79.0% at 10 years.<sup>[[19]](#ref19)</sup>
- **80 W KTP, 5 yr (n = 246 of 321 with complete follow-up):** AUA symptom score improved 79%, Qmax 172%, and retreatment was 8.9%.<sup>[[20]](#ref20)</sup>

## Retreatment and durability

| Setting | Volume | Follow-up | Retreatment |
|---|---|---|---|
| Global GreenLight Group (XPS, expert centers) | 64 mL median | variable | 1.5%<sup>[[7]](#ref7)</sup> |
| 120 W HPS | not stated | 5 yr | 3.1% repeat PVP<sup>[[17]](#ref17)</sup> |
| 80 W KTP | 55 mL mean | 5 yr | 8.9%<sup>[[20]](#ref20)</sup> |
| 80 W KTP vs TURP RCT | mixed | 5 yr | 14.3% vs 11.9%<sup>[[18]](#ref18)</sup> |
| XPS, very large (&gt; 100 mL) | 121 mL median | 3 yr | 9.3%<sup>[[8]](#ref8)</sup> |
| PVP in matched comparison with GreenLEP (&gt; 80 g) | 107 g mean | 2 yr | 10.1%<sup>[[25]](#ref25)</sup> |
| Single-center, 3 laser models (includes stricture and bladder neck contracture) | mixed | 61 mo median | 13.8% overall<sup>[[22]](#ref22)</sup> |
| Retrospective 10-yr series | mixed | 10 yr | 21% (retreatment-free 79%)<sup>[[19]](#ref19)</sup> |

Reported retreatment is 1.5% to 9% in most XPS and HPS series and higher with the 80 W laser or longer follow-up.<sup>[[7]](#ref7)[[17]](#ref17)[[18]](#ref18)[[19]](#ref19)[[20]](#ref20)</sup> In glands larger than 100 mL it reached 5.4% at 2 years and 9.3% at 3 years, and the authors noted rising retreatment after 3 years.<sup>[[8]](#ref8)</sup> In a randomized comparison in 80–150 mL glands, 3-year redo surgery for recurrent adenoma was 6.7% after GreenLight vapo-enucleation and none after HoLEP.<sup>[[21]](#ref21)</sup> Reported predictors of retreatment include larger gland volume, lower energy density (2.4 versus 3.4 kJ/mL in retreated men), longer lasing time and less experienced surgeons.<sup>[[8]](#ref8)[[22]](#ref22)[[23]](#ref23)</sup>

## Safety profile

PVP has a better perioperative safety profile than TURP, particularly for bleeding<sup>[[15]](#ref15)[[16]](#ref16)</sup>:

- TUR syndrome is not a concern because saline irrigation is used.
- Transfusion is less frequent (RR 0.26 versus TURP) and clot retention is 88% lower (RR 0.12).<sup>[[16]](#ref16)</sup>
- Dysuria is the most common adverse event and is more frequent than after TURP; urgency, UTI and hematuria also occur.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[15]](#ref15)</sup>
- Bladder neck contracture was 3.4% to 7.7% and urethral stricture 2.1% to 5.6% across single-center series, and the late complication rate was described as comparable to TURP.<sup>[[22]](#ref22)[[23]](#ref23)[[24]](#ref24)</sup>
- 30-day all-cause mortality was 0.3% in the Global GreenLight Group.<sup>[[7]](#ref7)</sup>
- Clavien-Dindo grade III–V complications did not differ significantly between PVP and TURP.<sup>[[16]](#ref16)</sup>

### Antithrombotic therapy

All of the following are retrospective series:

- Lee 2016 (n = 384; aspirin 38%, clopidogrel 9%, warfarin 15%, three-drug combinations 1.7%): patients on and off anticoagulation did not differ significantly in UTI, retention, stricture or reoperation over up to 2 years.<sup>[[9]](#ref9)</sup>
- Meskawi 2019 (n = 422, of whom 148 were on aspirin, other antiplatelet agents or anticoagulants): antithrombotic status did not predict serious bleeding events on multivariable analysis. Minor hematuria, readmissions, length of stay and catheterization time were higher in these patients.<sup>[[10]](#ref10)</sup>
- Chung 2011 (n = 162 on systemic anticoagulation, including combinations): mean hematocrit decrease was 1.94%, delayed bleeding occurred in 4% and transfusion in 2%.<sup>[[11]](#ref11)</sup>
- Ruszat 2008 (n = 500, 80 W; 45% on ongoing oral anticoagulation): no severe intraoperative complications were observed.<sup>[[24]](#ref24)</sup>
- A review by authors with industry ties concluded that PVP was not associated with serious bleeding events requiring transfusion or medical treatment in patients on anticoagulant or antiplatelet therapy.<sup>[[3]](#ref3)</sup>

## Sexual function

PVP sexual-function data are more heterogeneous than those for UroLift, Aquablation and iTIND.

- A 13-RCT meta-analysis reported less sexual dysfunction than with TURP (RR 0.66; 95% CI 0.45–0.98).<sup>[[16]](#ref16)</sup>
- A 22-study meta-analysis found no significant IIEF difference versus TURP at 3, 24, 36 or 60 months.<sup>[[15]](#ref15)</sup>
- A systematic review noted a trend toward decline in erectile function in men with sustained preoperative erection, with heterogeneous data.<sup>[[4]](#ref4)</sup>
- Retrograde ejaculation is the most common sexual side effect. In a matched prospective comparison of glands larger than 80 g, antegrade ejaculation was preserved in 26.9% after standard PVP and 1.2% after GreenLEP at 12 months.<sup>[[25]](#ref25)</sup>

### Ejaculatory hood-sparing PVP

A modified technique preserves the paracollicular and supracollicular tissue proximal to the verumontanum.

- **Abolazm 2020** (double-blinded RCT, 25 hood-sparing and 24 standard patients): antegrade ejaculation was preserved in 85% versus 31.6% (p = 0.001), with comparable urodynamic relief of obstruction at 6 months. The authors describe the technique as feasible for small to moderate glands in well-informed, sexually interested patients.<sup>[[26]](#ref26)</sup>
- **Brant 2020** (25 patients, 3-month follow-up): 80% antegrade-ejaculation preservation and improved total MSHQ scores.<sup>[[27]](#ref27)</sup>

## GreenLEP: laser enucleation extension

GreenLEP combines vaporization with en bloc enucleation using the 180 W XPS laser and has been applied to prostates larger than 80 mL.

- Wang 2025 systematic review and meta-analysis of 13 studies: GreenLEP had better energy efficiency and better Qmax, PVR and PSA at final follow-up than standard PVP or anatomical PVP, and the lowest reoperation rate (OR 27.30; 95% CI 8.52–87.51), with a wide confidence interval.<sup>[[28]](#ref28)</sup>
- Misrai 2016, retrospective comparison of 60 patients per group with glands larger than 80 mL: GreenLEP had a shorter OR time (60 versus 82 min, p &lt; 0.0001) and a greater Qmax improvement (+78% versus +64%). Incontinence at 2 months was higher after GreenLEP (25% versus 3.4%) and was similar at 6 months.<sup>[[29]](#ref29)</sup>
- Randomized trial in 80–150 mL glands comparing GreenLEP, HoLEP and bipolar TURIS (Elshal 2020): GreenLEP had a longer operative time than HoLEP and TURIS and more 3-year redo surgery than HoLEP (6.7% versus 0%; p = 0.04 across the three arms).<sup>[[21]](#ref21)</sup>
- The single-surgeon series that described a simplified GreenLEP technique (n = 108) proposed it for surgeons who are familiar with PVP and are adopting enucleation.<sup>[[30]](#ref30)</sup>

## Where GreenLight PVP fits

### Advantages

- AUA 2026 lists PVP among the options for patients who are at high risk of bleeding or need anticoagulant or antiplatelet therapy (Expert Opinion), and retrospective series support its use on continued antithrombotic therapy.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[9]](#ref9)[[10]](#ref10)[[11]](#ref11)</sup>
- Bleeding is lower than with TURP.<sup>[[15]](#ref15)[[16]](#ref16)</sup>
- Catheterization is short (1–2 days) and stay is short (often same-day or next-day).<sup>[[7]](#ref7)[[16]](#ref16)</sup>
- Efficacy is equivalent to TURP for prostates up to about 80 mL (GOLIATH 24-month noninferiority).<sup>[[14]](#ref14)</sup>
- Hospital-stay cost is lower than with TURP, partially offsetting the cost of the fiber.<sup>[[3]](#ref3)</sup>

### Limitations

- No tissue specimen is available for pathology, which matters when detection of occult prostate cancer is desired (the incidental cancer rate at TURP is roughly 4–8%).
- Retreatment is higher in large prostates (&gt; 80–100 mL) than with enucleation in comparative data.<sup>[[8]](#ref8)[[21]](#ref21)</sup>
- Retrograde ejaculation is similar to TURP with standard technique (about 70–75%), and ejaculatory hood-sparing PVP reduced it to about 15–20% in a small randomized trial.<sup>[[26]](#ref26)</sup>
- Procedure time is about 10 min longer than with TURP.<sup>[[16]](#ref16)</sup>
- Laser fiber costs are significant and are partially offset by shorter stay.
- General or regional anesthesia is usually required, and the procedure is not office-based.
