# Urethrectomy

Urethrectomy is partial or total removal of the urethra. The reconstructive surgeon's role is to define the extent of resection, protect viable tissues and plan urinary diversion or genital reconstruction with the oncologic team.

---

## Indications

### At Radical Cystectomy

The 2024 AUA/ASCO/SUO guideline on non-metastatic muscle-invasive bladder cancer states in the discussion of Statement 11 (a Clinical Principle) that urethrectomy (immediate or delayed) should be performed in two situations. The first is high-grade cancer at the apical urethral margin after prostatectomy, assessed by frozen section or final pathology. The second is women not undergoing neobladder reconstruction. In patients receiving an orthotopic diversion, clinicians must verify a negative urethral margin (Statement 14, Clinical Principle). Following cystectomy with a retained urethra, the urethral remnant is monitored for recurrence (Statement 32, Expert Opinion). In women, the discussion reports urethral tumor involvement in about 12% of patients undergoing cystectomy and identifies involvement of the bladder neck, posterior bladder base or anterior vaginal wall as an important risk factor. It describes intraoperative frozen section of the proximal urethra as a reliable way to confirm that a woman is a candidate for orthotopic diversion.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup>

The EAU guideline on muscle-invasive bladder cancer (2026) makes a strong recommendation against an orthotopic bladder substitute when an invasive tumor is in the urethra or at the level of urethral dissection. A positive preoperative prostatic urethral biopsy alone should not deny a neobladder in men. Frozen section of the distal urethral stump decides, and it can be omitted after a negative preoperative biopsy (both strong). In women, the bladder neck and urethral margin are assessed histologically before or at cystectomy (strong). Concomitant urethral malignancy has been reported in 12% to 16% of women undergoing cystectomy, and a bladder-neck primary correlates strongly with it.<sup>[[22]](#ref22)</sup>

NCCN Bladder Cancer Version 3.2026 describes prostatic-duct CIS and a positive urethral margin as relative contraindications to urethral drainage. Its wording differs from the AUA requirement to verify a negative urethral margin before orthotopic diversion.<sup>[[26]](#ref26)[[1]](#ref1)</sup>

Ahlering and Skinner described the following historical indications in a 174-patient series. They are not all automatic contemporary indications for total urethrectomy:<sup>[[2]](#ref2)</sup>

- Clinically overt urethral involvement.
- Prostatic stromal invasion.
- CIS of the prostatic urethra.
- Bloody urethral discharge after cystectomy.

Zabbo and Montie listed multifocal tumors, concurrent upper-tract tumors, diffuse CIS, trigone or prostatic urethral involvement and a positive urethral frozen section as indications for prophylactic urethrectomy.<sup>[[3]](#ref3)</sup> Contemporary series report a 1% to 17% risk of cancer in the retained urethra, mostly within 2 years of surgery.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup> A systematic review cited by EAU found new urethral tumors after cystectomy in 4.4% (range 1.3% to 13.7%). Risk factors were malignancy in the prostatic urethra or prostate in men and at the bladder neck in women, and orthotopic neobladder was associated with a lower risk (OR 0.44).<sup>[[22]](#ref22)</sup>

Pathologic prostatic stromal involvement is more common than isolated urethral tumor at final pathology: a retrospective review of 211 men treated with radical cystectomy for MIBC found it in 46 (22%) overall, rising with clinical T stage (18% at cT2, 23% at cT3, 48% at cT4), and in half of the men who also had urethral involvement. Most of these men do not need urethrectomy distal to the prostate; radical urethrectomy at cystectomy is reserved for those with clear clinical evidence of urethral cancer distal to the prostatic urethra, not stromal involvement alone.<sup>[[23]](#ref23)</sup>

Concomitant male urethrectomy at the index cystectomy is uncommon in practice, because a negative urethral margin is achieved in the large majority of cases; a urethral margin that turns positive only on final pathology, after frozen section was negative, not performed, or missed disease, is managed with delayed urethrectomy rather than reoperation at the index procedure.

### Primary Urethral Carcinoma

Resection depends on histology, location, stage and the ability to achieve negative margins. The EAU guideline recommends offering distal urethrectomy as an alternative to penile amputation in localized distal urethral tumors when negative margins can be achieved intraoperatively. In women it recommends offering radical urethrectomy unless criteria for organ preservation are met, and urethra-sparing surgery for distal tumors when negative margins can be achieved. For locally advanced squamous cell carcinoma it recommends offering radiotherapy with radiosensitizing chemotherapy for definitive treatment and genital preservation, and multidisciplinary discussion of locally advanced disease. A stage-only table cannot prescribe the same chemoradiation strategy for every histology.<sup>[[21]](#ref21)</sup>

For a malignant stricture-like lesion of the bulbar or penile urethra under 2 cm and clinically confined to the corpus spongiosum (T2), segmental urethrectomy with primary re-anastomosis or a neourethra can be curative rather than requiring total urethrectomy; a longer segment can still be excised but usually needs flap or graft reconstruction. MRI helps distinguish T2 (spongiosum-confined) from T3 (corporal) disease to plan the margin, and an organ-sparing approach is an option in selected low-grade, distal tumors. This segmental, margin-driven approach is a distinct clinical scenario from the total urethrectomy performed at or after cystectomy above.<sup>[[24]](#ref24)</sup>

NCCN lists distal urethrectomy or partial penectomy for T2 pendulous tumors and urethrectomy with or without cystoprostatectomy for T2 bulbar tumors (category 2A). These options depend on tumor location; NCCN does not supply a 2-cm cutoff for segmental reconstruction.<sup>[[26]](#ref26)</sup>

## Surgical Approaches — Male

### Prepubic Approach (workhorse)

A prepubic approach permits urethrectomy during cystoprostatectomy without a separate perineal exposure. The urethra and associated spongiosal tissue are mobilized along the corporal surface and delivered with the planned specimen. Preserve tissue planes appropriate to tumor extent; an en-bloc operation must not first divide the specimen in a way that defeats its oncologic purpose. A 180-patient retrospective series reported a mean 17-minute additional operative time.<sup>[[4]](#ref4)</sup>



**Hiebl urethral-stripping modification.** Hiebl described a modification for difficult bulbar dissection in 21 patients. After periurethral mobilization, the urethra is cannulated with an 18 Fr catheter, sutured distally and stripped free. Blind stripping is avoided where tumor extent requires controlled en bloc margins.<sup>[[5]](#ref5)</sup>

**Prepubic versus perineal comparative outcomes** (Elshal 2011, modified Clavien):<sup>[[6]](#ref6)</sup>

| Metric | Prepubic | Perineal | p |
|---|---|---|---|
| Operative time | 174 min | 209 min | 0.003 |
| Hospital stay | 14.5 d | 17.6 d | 0.047 |
| Grade 4–5 events among reported complications | 2/21 events (9.5%) | 7/25 events (28%) | 0.033 as reported |
| Added OR time vs cystectomy alone | ~17 min (15–25)<sup>[[4]](#ref4)</sup> | Not reported; the total operation was about 35 min longer than prepubic | — |
| Repositioning to lithotomy | No | Yes | — |

### Perineal Approach (traditional)

1. **Lithotomy** positioning.
2. **Midline perineal incision**; dissect through subcutaneous tissue to the bulbospongiosus muscle.
3. Incise the bulbospongiosus muscle longitudinally at the midline and separate from corpus spongiosum.
4. **Circumferentially detach** the urethra from the corporal bodies; mobilize from the penoscrotal angle to the urogenital diaphragm.
5. For the penile urethra, circumferential subcoronal incision approximately 0.5 cm below the glans allows degloving along Buck's fascia.
6. Progressively dissect the corpus spongiosum containing the urethra off the corpora cavernosa; the penis is essentially turned inside out to achieve complete excision.<sup>[[7]](#ref7)</sup>

### En Bloc Transpubic Approach

**Partial pubectomy** allows en bloc urethrectomy with cystectomy and avoids a separate perineal incision and its contamination risk. DeTure described exposure comparable to the perineal route. Pubectomy and urethrectomy took about 30 min, which he reported as comparable to the time needed to expose and close a perineal incision.<sup>[[8]](#ref8)</sup>

**Phallus preservation for locally advanced proximal urethral carcinoma.** March described complete perineal urethrectomy with proximal penectomy, en bloc pubectomy and excision of the pelvic diaphragm, leaving the remnant penis in place, in two patients with pT3 and pT4 proximal urethral cancer and no tumor invasion of the penile shaft. Both had an R0 resection, and there was no ischemic necrosis at 6 months. The remnant penis survives on the superficial penile arteries arising from the external pudendal arteries.<sup>[[9]](#ref9)</sup>

### Minimally Invasive

- **Laparoscopic.** 5-port transperitoneal cystoprostatectomy and lymphadenectomy with perineal urethrectomy and specimen extraction, described in two cases with a total operative time of 4.5–4.8 h.<sup>[[10]](#ref10)</sup>
- **Robot-assisted.** In a 10-case series, side docking gave better perineal access than central docking and allowed simultaneous urethrectomy without undocking (performed in one case), without significant differences in operative time, transfusion, complications or hospital stay. A urethra-preserving robotic technique was reported to have no urethral recurrences (0/134) and favorable perioperative outcomes compared with robotic prophylactic urethrectomy, in a retrospective propensity-matched series.<sup>[[11]](#ref11)[[12]](#ref12)</sup>

## Surgical Approaches — Female

Historical anterior exenteration removed adjacent reproductive organs and vaginal wall with the bladder. The EAU guideline recommends sexual organ-preserving techniques in eligible women, selected by absence of tumor in the area to be preserved (strong); extent is determined by oncologic margins and the agreed diversion or reconstruction plan.<sup>[[22]](#ref22)</sup><sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[13]](#ref13)</sup> NCCN also recommends preserving the uterus, vagina or ovaries when feasible in appropriately selected patients (category 2A).<sup>[[26]](#ref26)</sup> Practice differs by planned diversion: a woman going to an ileal conduit or other non-continent diversion routinely has the urethra removed at the same operation, while a woman being evaluated for orthotopic reconstruction is managed toward urethral preservation with a negative frozen-section margin, as above.

### Key Anatomy

- The vaginal-wall smooth muscle contributes to urethral wall formation, particularly when the detrusor is poorly developed.<sup>[[14]](#ref14)</sup>
- The middle urethra is tightly attached to vaginal smooth muscle with abundant veins at the interface, a major source of bleeding.<sup>[[14]](#ref14)</sup>
- The rhabdosphincter fibers are concentrated in the middle and caudal thirds of the urethra; continence can be maintained if innervation remains intact when only the bladder neck and proximal urethra are removed.<sup>[[15]](#ref15)</sup>

### Technique

- **Antegrade dissection** from bladder neck distally allows direct vision. Hinata’s 965 mL mean blood loss was for 12 radical-cystectomy operations, not urethrectomy alone.<sup>[[14]](#ref14)</sup>
- The vaginal wall is taken with the specimen when oncologically indicated.
- **Reconstruction.** When significant vaginal resection is required, a rectus abdominis myocutaneous flap vaginoplasty has been used to restore vaginal length and provide perineal-pelvic bulk, in a series of four patients.<sup>[[16]](#ref16)</sup>

### Distal Urethrectomy for Distal Lesions

For distal female urethral lesions, distal urethrectomy with advancement meatoplasty may preserve an outlet, but continence depends on resection extent and remaining sphincter function; see [Distal Urethrectomy + Advancement Meatoplasty](/docs/surgical-techniques/04a-urethral-reconstruction/distal-urethrectomy-advancement).

## Oncologic Outcomes

### Urethrectomy at Cystectomy — Risk-Stratified Benefit

- **Multicenter (n = 887, Laukhtina 2022):** urethrectomy at radical cystectomy was not associated with PFS, CSS or OS in the overall cohort. In the subgroup at high risk of urethral recurrence (n = 276; urethral invasion, bladder-neck invasion, multifocality or prostatic urethral involvement), urethrectomy was associated with a lower risk of progression (HR 0.58, p = 0.04).<sup>[[17]](#ref17)</sup>
- **Propensity-matched (Hakozaki 2021, 114 matched patients with incontinent diversion):** 5-year OS did not differ overall. In the 58 patients with multiple tumors or concomitant CIS, prophylactic urethrectomy was associated with higher 5-year OS (p = 0.021), particularly in those not receiving neoadjuvant chemotherapy.<sup>[[18]](#ref18)</sup>
- **Single-center propensity-matched (n = 332, Mennes 2026):** 10-year OS was higher in the prophylactic-urethrectomy group (47.3% versus 27.5%, p = 0.002). CSS and RFS did not differ significantly, and the 10-year urethral recurrence-free survival difference (98.2% versus 95.9%) was not significant after matching.<sup>[[19]](#ref19)</sup>

In the Zennami series, urethra preservation in patients at high risk of urethral recurrence was associated with worse urethral recurrence-free, local recurrence-free and overall survival.<sup>[[12]](#ref12)</sup> These retrospective associations do not establish a survival benefit from routine prophylactic urethrectomy. EAU likewise states that urethral recurrence-related death is rare and that prophylactic urethrectomy has not shown a survival benefit. It advises follow-up of the male urethra in patients at risk, and urethrectomy for invasive urethral recurrence when the urethra is the only site of disease.<sup>[[22]](#ref22)</sup> Recurrence risk depends on margins, urethral involvement and surveillance; older 4–18% estimates should not be applied to every contemporary diversion population.<sup>[[4]](#ref4)[[17]](#ref17)[[19]](#ref19)</sup>

### Managing a Urethral Recurrence After Cystectomy

Following cystectomy with a retained urethra, NCCN advises considering urine cytology every 6–12 months for the first two years and thereafter as clinically indicated. Urethral wash cytology may be considered at the same intervals but is reserved for high-risk disease: a positive urethral margin, multifocal CIS or prostatic urethral invasion (category 2A).<sup>[[26]](#ref26)</sup> A review reports higher-stage recurrence when detection follows symptoms rather than surveillance.<sup>[[27]](#ref27)</sup>

Management of a confirmed urethral recurrence depends on the diversion already in place. With a non-orthotopic diversion (for example an ileal conduit), perineal urethrectomy alone is sufficient. With an orthotopic neobladder, preserving the urethra, and therefore the existing diversion, is preferred when feasible: intraurethral or intraneobladder BCG is a reasonable option for CIS without a concomitant papillary tumor (5 of 6 patients with CIS remained recurrence-free after BCG in one series, while treatment failed in those with papillary or invasive disease), whereas a localized high-grade papillary or invasive (T1) recurrence is treated with urethrectomy and conversion to an alternative diversion. A locally advanced (T3/T4) recurrence is managed with systemic therapy before surgical extirpation, as for a primary urethral tumor.<sup>[[27]](#ref27)[[28]](#ref28)</sup>

### Primary Urethral Carcinoma (rare)

A 2026 meeting abstract described 35 patients with urethral SCC and heterogeneous multimodal treatment. Its five-year survival estimate, based on median 2.4-year follow-up, is not a urethrectomy-specific treatment result or a general prognosis for urethral cancer.<sup>[[20]](#ref20)</sup>

## Complications

Separate urethrectomy-specific morbidity from the entire cystectomy operation. Joniau reported ten local complications in 180 prepubic cases, including penile or scrotal hematomas. Elshal compared 71 prepubic with 115 perineal cysto-urethrectomies: its 9.5% versus 28% high-grade figures were 2/21 versus 7/25 reported complication events, not percentages of all operated patients. Cross-study tables mixing these denominators are misleading.<sup>[[4]](#ref4)[[6]](#ref6)</sup>

Total and segmental resections have different functional consequences. Counsel according to the structures removed and reconstructed; urethrectomy is not assumed to spare continence or sexual function.<sup>[[21]](#ref21)</sup>

## Reconstructive Implications

- **Total urethrectomy precludes an orthotopic neobladder anastomosed to the native urethra.** A reconstructionist asked to perform or assist with the urethrectomy step should confirm the diversion plan first; once the urethra is out, the patient is committed to a [conduit](/docs/surgical-techniques/04c-urinary-diversion/ileal-conduit), [continent cutaneous pouch](/docs/surgical-techniques/04c-urinary-diversion/indiana-pouch) or another suitable diversion.
- **Frozen-section urethral margin.** Intraoperative frozen section of the distal urethral stump, not preoperative prostatic urethral biopsy, decides eligibility for orthotopic diversion (AUA/ASCO/SUO 2024 Statement 14 discussion; EAU 2026 strong recommendation). See the guideline positions above. A systematic review and meta-analysis of 14 studies (8,208 patients) found pooled frozen-section sensitivity of 83% (95% CI 38–97%, reflecting substantial between-study heterogeneity) and specificity of 95% (95% CI 91–97%) for urethral margin involvement, with a patient with a positive final urethral margin over 100 times more likely to have had a positive frozen section.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[22]](#ref22)[[25]](#ref25)</sup>
- **Insufficient urethral length for continence.** When too little urethra remains after urethrectomy to support continence, bladder neck closure with a continent catheterizable channel or an incontinent stoma is an option, the same salvage pathway used elsewhere for a urethra that cannot be reconstructed; see [Bladder Neck Closure](/docs/surgical-techniques/04ab-bladder-neck-reconstruction/outlet-continence/bladder-neck-closure).
- **Urethra-preserving robotic technique** (Zennami 2024) has been used when oncologic criteria do not mandate urethrectomy and neobladder candidacy is being preserved.<sup>[[12]](#ref12)</sup>
- **Phallus preservation in proximal urethral carcinoma** has been described in two patients; the external pudendal to superficial penile arterial supply is the basis of the technique.<sup>[[9]](#ref9)</sup>
- **Female surgery with a substantial vaginal defect** may benefit from a [rectus abdominis flap vaginoplasty](/docs/surgical-techniques/04g-prolapse-repair) or pelvic reconstruction, which is the reconstructive surgeon's contribution to the multidisciplinary cystectomy.<sup>[[16]](#ref16)</sup>
