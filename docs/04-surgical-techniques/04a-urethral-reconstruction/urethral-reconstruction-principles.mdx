# Principles of Urethral Reconstruction

Urethral reconstruction rests on five principles: define the stricture accurately, remove or fully open the diseased segment, reconstruct with healthy, well-vascularized tissue, create a tension-free mucosa-to-mucosa repair, and choose the technique that matches the stricture.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[2]](#ref2)[[3]](#ref3)</sup>

The male and female technique database is on the [Urethral Reconstruction landing page](/docs/surgical-techniques/urethral-reconstruction).

---

## 1. Accurate Preoperative Stricture Staging Comes First

Urethroplasty starts before the incision. The surgeon has to know where the stricture is, how long it is, how narrow it is, and what caused it.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup> That information determines whether the repair is excisional, augmented or staged, or whether perineal urethrostomy is chosen instead.

### Imaging defines the map, but not perfectly

RUG and VCUG remain the standard imaging studies, but radiographic length underestimates the length found at surgery. In 29 men with high-grade anterior stricture imaged after suprapubic tube placement, 11 reconstructive urologists underestimated length by an average of almost 1 cm (radiographic 3.0 cm, intraoperative 3.8 cm), and the deviation increased with stricture length.<sup>[[4]](#ref4)</sup>

### Urethral rest improves operative planning

After recent instrumentation, dilation or repeated manipulation, a period of urethral rest allows the lesion to mature. A suprapubic tube is used when obstruction or retention requires drainage. The EAU guideline cites Terlecki in proposing evaluation after two months and urethroplasty after three months of rest, and notes that the optimal duration is not known and that longer rest is appropriate with marked infection or inflammation.<sup>[[27]](#ref27)</sup> In Terlecki's single-surgeon series, 28 of 128 anterior urethroplasty patients had a median of 3 months of rest, and severely fibrotic segments were excised in 75%.<sup>[[5]](#ref5)</sup> In Moncrief's series, the proportion of strictures read as obliterative rose from 23% to 58% after a period of rest with a suprapubic tube, and the operative plan changed in 47% of cases, including to an excision approach in 37%.<sup>[[4]](#ref4)</sup>

### The final decision is still made in the operating room

Even with good staging, the operative plan stays flexible. Spongiofibrosis, tissue quality and lumen caliber are judged directly at surgery.<sup>[[2]](#ref2)</sup>

## 2. The Disease Is Spongiofibrosis, Not Just a Narrow Lumen

The target of reconstruction is the underlying spongiofibrotic segment that has replaced normal pliable urethral tissue with scar, not the small lumen seen on cystoscopy.<sup>[[6]](#ref6)[[7]](#ref7)</sup>

Two reconstructive strategies follow:

- **Complete excision** of the scar with primary reconnection is used when the diseased segment is short enough.
- **Adequate urethrotomy** through all unhealthy tissue into normal proximal and distal lumen is followed by graft or flap augmentation when excision would create too much tension or too much urethral loss.<sup>[[2]](#ref2)[[3]](#ref3)</sup>

In his 27-year series of anastomotic posterior urethroplasty, Koraitim identified three operative details as the "gold triad" for a successful result:

1. complete excision of scarred tissue,
2. fixation of healthy mucosa at the two urethral ends,
3. a tension-free anastomosis.<sup>[[3]](#ref3)</sup>

---

## 3. Tension-Free, Mucosa-to-Mucosa Repair Is Non-Negotiable

A urethral repair under tension heals poorly. Ischemia at the suture line can lead to breakdown, secondary healing and recurrent scar.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[3]](#ref3)[[8]](#ref8)</sup>

### For anterior urethra

Tension can usually be addressed by:

- generous urethral mobilization,
- careful spatulation,
- and, when needed, ancillary maneuvers such as crural separation or, occasionally, inferior pubectomy.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[8]](#ref8)</sup>

### For posterior urethra / PFUI

Posterior repair follows a progressive perineal strategy:

1. bulbar mobilization,
2. crural separation,
3. inferior pubectomy,
4. and only rarely rerouting or transpubic / abdominal adjuncts.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[8]](#ref8)</sup>

A series of 1,637 patients with pelvic fracture urethral distraction defects treated by progressive transperineal anastomotic urethroplasty reported a success rate of 92.4% (1,363 of the 1,475 with follow-up). After final repair, 8.5% had some degree of incontinence and 1.6% had de novo erectile dysfunction.<sup>[[9]](#ref9)</sup> If a repair cannot be made tension-free, the reconstruction is modified with further mobilization, augmentation or staging.

## 4. Technique Selection Must Match Length, Location, and Etiology

No single technique suits all strictures. Reconstruction is selected according to the length, location, obliterative severity, etiology and tissue quality of the lesion.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[2]](#ref2)[[7]](#ref7)</sup>

| Stricture scenario | Usual reconstructive logic |
|---|---|
| **Short bulbar stricture** | Non-transecting repair or graft when feasible; transecting EPA for short traumatic obliteration with full-thickness scar |
| **Longer bulbar stricture** | Oral mucosal graft augmentation |
| **Bulbar stricture with focal obliteration plus healthier adjacent urethra** | Augmented anastomotic or augmented non-transecting repair |
| **Penile stricture** | Usually graft-based reconstruction rather than transection |
| **Panurethral disease** | One-stage long graft repair, staged repair, flap/graft combination, PU, or salvage options depending on etiology |
| **Lichen sclerosus** | Oral mucosa; one-stage or staged according to tissue quality; avoid genital skin |
| **PFUI** | Delayed posterior anastomotic urethroplasty |
| **Recalcitrant complex disease** | Perineal urethrostomy, which had higher reported success than graft or flap repair in one retrospective series<sup>[[11]](#ref11)</sup> |

For the full reference on incisions and surgical approaches to male urethroplasty, which covers perineal skin incisions (midline, inverted-U and lambda), the three penile-stricture exposure approaches (degloving, Kulkarni invagination, midline ventral), anterior urethrotomy approaches (ventral, dorsal Barbagli, dorsolateral and non-transecting), posterior approaches, graft-placement configurations, and the one-stage versus staged decision, see [Male Urethroplasty — Incisions & Approaches](/docs/surgical-techniques/04a-urethral-reconstruction/male-urethroplasty-incisions-approaches).

## 5. Oral Mucosa Is the Default Graft Material

Oral mucosa is the graft of choice when substitution is needed.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[7]](#ref7)[[13]](#ref13)</sup> The AUA guideline states that surgeons should use oral mucosa as the first choice when grafts are used (Expert Opinion) and that buccal or lingual mucosal grafts may be used as equivalent alternatives (Strong Recommendation; Evidence Level A).<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup>

Advantages of oral mucosa:

- hairless epithelium,
- thick resilient surface,
- thin vascular lamina propria,
- easy harvest,
- and low donor-site morbidity.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[7]](#ref7)</sup>

The AUA guideline recommends against hair-bearing skin for substitution urethroplasty (Clinical Principle) and against genital skin for reconstruction in lichen sclerosus-proven stricture (Strong Recommendation; Evidence Level B).<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup>

## 6. Graft Bed Vascularity Determines Graft Survival

A free graft survives only if its recipient bed is healthy enough to carry it through imbibition, inosculation, and neovascularization.<sup>[[7]](#ref7)[[14]](#ref14)[[15]](#ref15)</sup>

Graft placement follows from this:

- **Dorsal onlay** uses the tunica albuginea of the corpora as a stable, well-supported graft bed.
- **Ventral onlay** relies on the corpus spongiosum and can be used when spongiosal support is good.
- **Dorsal inlay / Asopa** combines ventral access with dorsal support.
- **One-sided dorsolateral approaches** aim to preserve vascular supply while still using a favorable graft bed.<sup>[[14]](#ref14)[[15]](#ref15)[[16]](#ref16)</sup>

The blue graft is directly apposed to its bed. Panels show simplified graft–bed interfaces, not complete operative cross-sections. (Original WARWIKI schematic)*

The comparative literature does not show that either graft side is superior. A systematic review of 41 mostly case-series studies (3,683 patients) found no single technique superior for bulbar free-graft urethroplasty, with recurrence of 20% or less at medium-term follow-up for dorsal and ventral onlay, and it could make no recommendation for dorsal inlay or dorsolateral onlay because of sparse evidence. A meta-analysis of eight studies (655 patients) found similar success for dorsal and ventral onlay (RR 1.00, 95% CI 0.94–1.06) and less transient erectile dysfunction with the ventral technique.<sup>[[14]](#ref14)[[16]](#ref16)</sup> The graft must lie on well-vascularized support and be fixed without hematoma, shear or dead space.<sup>[[14]](#ref14)[[15]](#ref15)[[16]](#ref16)</sup> The [quilting stitch](/docs/foundations/surgical-skills/quilting-stitch) places multiple small interrupted bites to secure the graft to the underlying corpus spongiosum or tunica and is used to obtain this apposition.

The AUA guideline recommends against single-stage tubularized graft urethroplasty (Expert Opinion). One side of a tubularized free graft has no reliable vascular bed.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup>

## 7. Vessel-Sparing and Non-Transecting Techniques Aim to Preserve Function

Modern urethral reconstruction increasingly tries to preserve the urethra’s blood supply and minimize sexual side effects, especially in bulbar repairs.<sup>[[17]](#ref17)[[18]](#ref18)</sup>

This principle appears in several forms:

- **vessel-sparing EPA**,
- **non-transecting bulbar urethroplasty**,
- **augmented non-transecting anastomotic repairs** such as ANTA and MsANTA.<sup>[[17]](#ref17)[[18]](#ref18)[[19]](#ref19)[[20]](#ref20)</sup>

These techniques remove or open the diseased lumen while preserving as much healthy spongiosal vascularity and neural support as possible. The Scandinavian Urethroplasty Study randomized 151 patients with a bulbar stricture of 2 cm or less to transecting EPA (n = 75) or BMG (n = 76). Patients in the transecting EPA group reported more penile complications (p = 0.02), including reduced glans filling and a shortened penis, on a questionnaire that was not validated. Postoperative IIEF-5 scores did not differ. Recurrence was 12.9% in both groups at 12 months, but the trial was not designed to detect differences in recurrence.<sup>[[28]](#ref28)</sup> Transection is used when the stricture requires it.

## 8. Staged Reconstruction Is for Bad Tissue, Not Surgical Defeat

Staged urethroplasty is used when local tissue is too diseased, scarred, ischemic or unreliable to support a one-stage tubular repair.<sup>[[21]](#ref21)[[22]](#ref22)</sup>

Typical scenarios include:

- **lichen sclerosus**,
- **failed hypospadias repair**,
- severe penile scarring,
- repeated prior failed reconstructions.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[10]](#ref10)[[21]](#ref21)[[22]](#ref22)</sup>

The logic is two-step:

1. excise diseased tissue and create a healthy grafted plate,
2. then tubularize only after the tissue has matured and proven it can survive.<sup>[[21]](#ref21)</sup>

## 9. Perineal Urethrostomy Is a Definitive Reconstructive Option

Perineal urethrostomy (PU) is a definitive reconstruction in selected patients. The AUA guideline states that surgeons may offer PU as a long-term alternative to urethroplasty (Conditional Recommendation; Evidence Level C) and should offer it to patients at high risk of failure of urethral reconstruction (Expert Opinion).<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup> In a single-center retrospective series of 403 complex anterior strictures, success (no further operative management) at a median of 50.7 months was 94.8% for PU, 78.5% for BMG and 78.2% for penile skin flaps. PU patients were older and had longer strictures and more lichen sclerosus.<sup>[[11]](#ref11)</sup>

PU is particularly considered in patients with:

- extensive lichen sclerosus,
- multiple failed prior repairs,
- very long complex anterior strictures,
- major comorbidity,
- or a preference for a simpler operation.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[11]](#ref11)</sup>

---

## 10. Definitive Urethroplasty Should Be Offered Earlier

The AUA guideline recommends offering urethroplasty for penile urethral strictures (Moderate Recommendation; Evidence Level C) and as initial treatment for long (2 cm or more) bulbar strictures (Moderate Recommendation; Evidence Level C), given the low success of dilation and direct vision internal urethrotomy. A claims-database analysis of 65,333 men found that after 2016 more men proceeded to urethroplasty after failed endoscopic treatment (HR 2.57).<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[23]](#ref23)</sup>

This is especially true for:

- **penile strictures**,
- **long bulbar strictures**,
- **recurrent strictures after endoscopic failure**.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup>

Repeated dilation or DVIU in a lesion unlikely to respond delays definitive repair and may worsen the field by adding manipulation and scar.

---

### Recurrence after urethroplasty does not rule out another durable repair

An August 2026 matched observational analysis compared 195 revision urethroplasties with 195 primary repairs matched by age, stricture length, etiology and technique. Five-year stricture-free estimates were 83% versus 85%; revision was not significantly associated with recurrence (HR 1.28, 95% CI 0.70–2.34). Recurrence meant inability to pass a 16 Fr flexible cystoscope atraumatically. Reported satisfaction and complications were also similar.<sup>[[26]](#ref26)</sup>

Median follow-up was 138 months in patients without recurrence. The authors conclude that surgeons should not hesitate to offer revision surgery after failed urethroplasty. Matching cannot eliminate selection bias, and the confidence interval does not establish equivalence. The recurrent anatomy, tissue quality and alternatives are discussed with the patient.<sup>[[26]](#ref26)</sup>

---

## 11. Posterior Urethroplasty Works Best as Delayed Reconstruction

For pelvic fracture urethral injury:

- manage the acute injury with suprapubic diversion,
- avoid serial endoscopic salvage,
- and perform definitive anastomotic reconstruction once the patient and injury have matured, typically after 3-6 months.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[8]](#ref8)[[24]](#ref24)</sup>

Delayed perineal anastomotic repair is the standard approach in the AUA guideline. It allows scar definition, safe mobilization and mucosa-to-mucosa repair.<sup>[[3]](#ref3)[[9]](#ref9)[[12]](#ref12)[[24]](#ref24)[[25]](#ref25)</sup>

## Core Principles at a Glance

1. Stage the stricture accurately with imaging, urethral rest when needed, and a flexible intraoperative plan.
2. Treat the spongiofibrosis, not just the lumen.
3. Create a tension-free mucosa-to-mucosa repair.
4. Match the reconstruction to length, location, etiology and severity.
5. Use oral mucosa as the first-choice graft material.
6. Respect graft-bed vascularity and support.
7. Preserve blood supply when possible with vessel-sparing and non-transecting strategies.
8. Use staged reconstruction when tissue biology requires it.
9. Offer perineal urethrostomy as a definitive option in selected patients.
10. Offer urethroplasty earlier when endoscopic success is unlikely.
11. Treat posterior urethral distraction injuries with delayed definitive repair.

---

## Bottom Line for the Reconstructive Surgeon

Urethral reconstruction depends on accurate staging, full scar control, a tension-free repair, healthy vascular support and a technique chosen to suit the stricture.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[2]](#ref2)[[3]](#ref3)</sup> The named techniques in the database apply these rules to different anatomy.
