# 7-Flap Perineal Urethrostomy

The **7-flap perineal urethrostomy** is a perineal-urethrostomy (PU) technique that uses a **single laterally based perineal skin flap shaped like the number "7"**, advanced from a midline perineal incision to the spatulated proximal urethral stump. It was first described by **French, Hudak, and Morey** in 2011<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup> and has become a cornerstone of the **algorithmic midline approach to PU** developed at high-volume reconstructive centers — the 7-flap is selected when the urethra-to-skin distance is too great for a tension-free direct loop anastomosis (typically obese patients or proximally transected urethras).<sup>[[2]](#ref2)</sup>

For the alternative midline-incision technique used when the urethra reaches the skin without tension, see the loop variant described below. For the inverted-U flap PU and the broader PU framework, see [Blandy Perineal Urethrostomy](/docs/surgical-techniques/04a-urethral-reconstruction/blandy-perineal-urethrostomy). For salvage of a failed PU when local perineal tissue is depleted, see [Propeller Flap PU Revision](/docs/surgical-techniques/04a-urethral-reconstruction/propeller-flap).

---

## Concept and Flap Design

The flap derives its name from its **shape resembling the number "7"**: a horizontal limb running along the midline perineal incision (which gets advanced deep into the wound to reach the urethra) and a vertical limb extending laterally (which forms the **pedicle base**, supplied by perineal vasculature).<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup>

The fundamental geometric problem the 7-flap solves: in obese patients or proximally transected urethras, the **healthy proximal urethra sits deep within the perineum**, far from the skin surface. Traditional PU techniques (Johanson, Blandy inverted-U) rely on bringing the urethra up to the surface, which may be impossible without unacceptable tension. The 7-flap inverts the problem — it **advances skin into the depth of the wound** rather than mobilizing the urethra to the surface.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[2]](#ref2)</sup>

## Indications

- **Complex, long-segment anterior urethral strictures** (median ~ 8 cm, range 2.5–18 cm).<sup>[[2]](#ref2)</sup>
- **Failed prior urethroplasty** or revision of a failed prior PU.<sup>[[2]](#ref2)[[3]](#ref3)</sup>
- **Lichen sclerosus** and other adverse-etiology strictures.<sup>[[3]](#ref3)</sup>
- **Older or comorbid patients** who are poor candidates for complex multi-stage urethroplasty.<sup>[[4]](#ref4)[[5]](#ref5)</sup>
- **High BMI** — mean BMI 34.9 kg/m² in the 7-flap cohort vs 30.0 in the loop cohort (p = 0.01).<sup>[[2]](#ref2)</sup>

The **AUA 2023 urethral stricture guideline amendment** endorses PU as a long-term treatment option, either as an alternative to urethroplasty (Conditional Recommendation) or specifically for patients at high risk for reconstruction failure (Expert Opinion).<sup>[[5]](#ref5)</sup>

---

## The Algorithmic Midline Approach

Both the 7-flap and its companion **loop technique** start with the **same midline perineal incision**. The decision between them is made **intraoperatively after the urethra is exposed and transected**, based on the urethra-to-skin distance.<sup>[[2]](#ref2)</sup> This standardized starting point is a key advantage over Blandy / Johanson, which commit the surgeon to a specific flap design from the outset.

In the McKibben 2019 series of 62 patients:<sup>[[2]](#ref2)</sup>

| Decision | Cohort | Mean BMI | Trigger |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Loop technique** | 20 (32.3%) | 30.0 kg/m² | Urethra reaches skin without tension — typically distal strictures or low BMI |
| **7-flap technique** | 42 (67.7%) | 34.9 kg/m² | Long urethra-to-skin distance — obese patients or proximal transection |

## Step-by-Step Technique

### Positioning and exposure

1. Dorsal lithotomy with Allen stirrups; perineum, genitalia, and inner thighs prepped and draped. Suprapubic catheter, if present, left in place.
2. **Vertical midline perineal incision** from base of scrotum toward anus, centered over the bulbar urethra.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[6]](#ref6)</sup>

The midline approach is preferred over the inverted-U: cadaveric studies show it damages **1.6–2.0×** fewer vessels and nerves with equivalent exposure, and clinical series report significantly lower SSI rates (1.9–3.1% vs 16.4–18.6%, p &lt; 0.05).<sup>[[6]](#ref6)</sup>

### Urethral mobilization and transection

3. Divide bulbospongiosus in the midline; expose corpus spongiosum and bulbar urethra.
4. Mobilize the urethra circumferentially.
5. Identify the strictured segment by palpation, visual inspection, and bougie / catheter passage from the meatus.
6. **Transect the urethra at the level of healthy, well-vascularized proximal urethra** — bulbar or membranous, depending on stricture extent.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup>
7. Discard the distal strictured segment.
8. **Spatulate the proximal stump ventrally** to a wide fish-mouth opening (calibrate to **≥ 24–30 Fr**).<sup>[[7]](#ref7)</sup>
9. Confirm healthy mucosa — pink, well-vascularized, pliable.

### Intraoperative decision

10. Test whether the spatulated stump can be brought to the perineal skin without tension.
    - **Yes → Loop technique**: preserve the dorsal urethral plate, mobilize to the skin, and suture the spatulated urethra directly to the perineal skin edges in a "loop" configuration (dorsal plate as posterior wall; skin sutured to lateral and ventral urethral margins).
    - **No → 7-flap technique**: design a laterally based 7-shaped perineal skin flap to bridge the depth.

### 7-flap design, elevation, and inset

11. From the midline incision, design a **laterally based 7-shaped flap** with horizontal limb along the midline (the portion advanced into the wound) and vertical limb extending laterally (the pedicle base).<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup>
12. Elevate the flap **full-thickness** with subcutaneous fat and lateral pedicle blood supply intact.
13. **Advance and rotate the flap medially and deep** into the perineal wound to reach the transected urethra.
14. Suture the **tip of the flap to the spatulated proximal urethral stump** with absorbable suture (typically 4-0 or 5-0 polyglycolic acid).
15. Suture the **lateral edges of the flap to the remaining perineal skin edges** to complete the urethrostomy. End-result: a widely patent, well-vascularized stoma at the perineum.

### Catheter and closure

16. Place a 16–18 Fr Foley through the urethrostomy into the bladder.
17. Close the perineal wound in layers.
18. Catheter typically removed at **1–2 weeks** postoperatively.

## Outcomes

| Series | n | Success | Median FU | Notable |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| French 2011 (first description)<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup> | 10 (7-flap) | 90% | — | Outpatient setting, no perioperative complications |
| McKibben 2019 (algorithmic)<sup>[[2]](#ref2)</sup> | 42 (7-flap) / 20 (loop) | 92.9% / 100% | 30.7 mo | PGI-I 1.0 ("very much improved") |
| Joshi 2024 (midline PU)<sup>[[3]](#ref3)</sup> | 103 | 95.1% | 61 mo | 86% satisfied / very satisfied; 5.8% complication rate |
| Fuchs 2018 (PU overall)<sup>[[4]](#ref4)</sup> | 77 | 94.8% | 50.7 mo | PU > BMG (78.5%) and skin flap (78.2%), p = 0.003 |
| Klemm 2024<sup>[[8]](#ref8)</sup> | 76 | 84% retreatment-free | 55 mo | High satisfaction; preserved continence |

## Functional Outcomes and Quality of Life

- **Voiding** — significant Qmax and LUTS improvement.<sup>[[8]](#ref8)[[9]](#ref9)</sup>
- **Continence** — generally preserved; median ICIQ-UI of 0 in the long-term Klemm cohort.<sup>[[8]](#ref8)</sup>
- **Sexual function** — stable EF in most series with bimodal distributions; **de novo ED ~ 24%**.<sup>[[8]](#ref8)[[10]](#ref10)</sup>
- **Satisfaction** — 85–88% satisfied or very satisfied; 76% unbothered by sitting to urinate.<sup>[[3]](#ref3)[[9]](#ref9)</sup>

---

## Complications and Failure

Complications are generally mild and self-limited (~ 5.8%).<sup>[[3]](#ref3)</sup> The dominant failure mode is **stomal stenosis** (5–16% across series).<sup>[[2]](#ref2)[[8]](#ref8)</sup> Risk factors include **prior radiation** (OR 11.2 on multivariate), elevated BMI, and older age.<sup>[[7]](#ref7)[[11]](#ref11)</sup>

Failed PU can be revised with the 7-flap technique itself, or — when local perineal tissue is depleted — with a [posterior thigh propeller flap](/docs/surgical-techniques/04a-urethral-reconstruction/propeller-flap).<sup>[[12]](#ref12)</sup>

## Loop vs 7-Flap — At a Glance

| Feature | Loop Technique | 7-Flap Technique |
|---|---|---|
| Proportion of cases (McKibben 2019) | 32.3% | 67.7% |
| Mean BMI | 30.0 kg/m² | 34.9 kg/m² |
| Indication | Short urethra-to-skin distance, distal strictures, low BMI | Long urethra-to-skin distance, proximal transection, high BMI |
| Flap design | None — urethral plate mobilized to skin | Laterally based 7-shaped perineal skin flap |
| Blood supply | Dorsal urethral plate longitudinal supply preserved | Lateral perineal pedicle |
| Success | 100% (20 / 20) | 92.9% (39 / 42) |
| Reoperative use | Limited | Can revise failed PU |

## Comparison With Traditional PU Techniques

The midline algorithmic approach (loop / 7-flap) outperforms the **Blandy inverted-U** and **Johanson** techniques across multiple endpoints:

- **Lower SSI** — midline incision damages fewer neurovascular structures (cadaveric 1.6–2.0×); clinical SSI 1.9–3.1% vs 16.4–18.6% for inverted-U (p &lt; 0.05).<sup>[[6]](#ref6)</sup>
- **Higher patency** — midline approach 92.9–100% success vs Blandy 51–75% across mixed-era series.<sup>[[2]](#ref2)[[11]](#ref11)</sup>
- **Algorithmic flexibility** — intraoperative decision rather than pre-committed flap design.<sup>[[2]](#ref2)</sup>
- **Dorsal-plate-preservation option** (loop) — when feasible, preserving the dorsal urethral plate and its longitudinal blood supply (Myers / McAninch) minimizes stomal-stenosis risk: primary success 83%, secondary 93%; stenosis primarily in irradiated patients (OR 11.2).<sup>[[7]](#ref7)</sup>

---

## PU vs Long-Stricture Anterior Urethroplasty

TURNS data show that for **strictures > 6 cm**, PU 2-year failure was **14.5%** vs **30.2%** for long-stricture anterior urethroplasty (p = 0.09), with comparable urinary-function improvement and stable sexual function.<sup>[[10]](#ref10)</sup> PU use has steadily increased at high-volume centers, rising from 4.3% to **38.7% of complex reconstructive case volume** over a decade, while penile-skin-flap use has declined.<sup>[[3]](#ref3)</sup> Contemporary evidence supports PU — and the 7-flap specifically — as a **primary reconstructive option** rather than a last resort, particularly in older, obese, or multiply-operated patients.<sup>[[3]](#ref3)[[5]](#ref5)[[8]](#ref8)</sup>

---

## Technical Pearls

- **Caliber** — urethrostomy calibrated to **≥ 24–30 Fr** to minimize stenosis risk.<sup>[[7]](#ref7)</sup>
- **Skin selection** — perineal skin overlying the perineal artery is non-hirsute or nearly non-hirsute in many men, reducing risk of intra-stomal hair growth.<sup>[[13]](#ref13)</sup>
- **Outpatient feasibility** — the original French 2011 series was performed entirely outpatient without perioperative complications.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup>
- **Revision capability** — failed PU (any technique) can be revised with the 7-flap or, in compromised local tissue, with a posterior thigh propeller flap.<sup>[[12]](#ref12)</sup>
