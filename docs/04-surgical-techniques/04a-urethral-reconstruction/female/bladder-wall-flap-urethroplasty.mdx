# Bladder Wall Flap Urethroplasty (Female)

**Bladder wall flap urethroplasty** is a reconstructive technique reserved for the most complex female urethral defects, particularly those involving complete urethral loss, obliterative strictures, and bladder-neck involvement where local genital tissue (vaginal or labial flaps) is insufficient or unavailable.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[2]](#ref2)</sup> It uses a pedicled full-thickness flap from the anterior or posterior bladder wall, tubularized to create a neourethra or used as an onlay augmentation.

For related bladder-flap principles in the upper tract and outlet, see [Boari Flap & Psoas Hitch](/docs/surgical-techniques/04d-upper-tract-reconstruction/boari-flap-psoas-hitch) and the [bladder-flap foundations article](/docs/foundations/surgical-principles/flaps/bladder-flap).

## Indications

This technique occupies a specific niche in the reconstructive ladder for female urethral defects.

- **Complete urethral loss** (post-traumatic, post-pelvic fracture).<sup>[[3]](#ref3)[[4]](#ref4)</sup>
- **Obliterative bladder neck contracture** (post-pelvic fracture).<sup>[[3]](#ref3)</sup>
- **Complex urethral defects with bladder neck involvement** (congenital or acquired).<sup>[[2]](#ref2)</sup>
- **Vesicovaginal fistulas with urethral involvement** (obstetric trauma).<sup>[[5]](#ref5)</sup>
- **Neurogenic incontinence with low urethral resistance** (myelomeningocele and exstrophy, Pippi Salle procedure).<sup>[[6]](#ref6)[[7]](#ref7)</sup>
- **Failed prior urethral reconstruction** where local tissue is depleted.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup>

## Named Techniques and Variants

### 1. Tanagho Anterior Bladder Tube

The classic technique uses a proximally based anterior bladder wall flap that is tubularized to create a neourethra, requiring a combined vaginal and abdominal approach. Known limitations include the bladder neck being shifted anterosuperiorly (causing voiding difficulty), a posteriorly directed suture line (risking vaginal fistula), and rotational tension on the bladder at the suture line.<sup>[[3]](#ref3)[[4]](#ref4)</sup>

### 2. Nayyar U-Shaped Anterior Bladder Tube

This modification was designed to overcome Tanagho's limitations. A U-shaped flap is raised from the anterior bladder wall and tubularized, keeping the native bladder-neck fibers in their anatomical position. It avoids anterosuperior bladder-neck displacement. Tubes of 3, 3.5 and 3.5 cm were fashioned in the three reported patients.<sup>[[3]](#ref3)</sup> Nayyar and colleagues explain the rationale as incorporating the full circumference of detrusor fibers near the bladder neck, contrasted with the Tanagho tube, which they estimate excludes about 60–70% of that circumference from contributing to continence. As a qualitative anatomical rationale, they suggest basing the flap on the posterior three-fourths of the detrusor and orienting its base toward the trigone, reasoning that neural input arrives from the posterolateral aspect; this is the chapter authors' reasoning rather than a measured comparative benefit.<sup>[[10]](#ref10)</sup>

### 3. Pippi Salle Procedure (Anterior Bladder Wall Flap for Urethral Lengthening)

The Pippi Salle procedure was described for neurogenic incontinence and exstrophy in children (mean age 9 years in the 1997 series). A midline anterior bladder-wall flap is sutured to the posterior wall (trigonal mucosa) in an onlay fashion, creating a flap-valve mechanism that increases urethral resistance. Bladder emptying is by clean intermittent catheterization, and bladder augmentation accompanied the procedure in 13 of 17 patients. Modifications include an anterolateral flap (for exstrophy) and an extended distal mucosal flap (to avoid ureteral reimplantation).<sup>[[6]](#ref6)[[7]](#ref7)[[8]](#ref8)</sup>

### 4. Flipped Anterior Bladder Wall Tube

Described by Mitsui et al. for pediatric patients with complete urethral disruption. The anterior bladder-wall flap is tubularized and flipped caudally to reach the proximal urethral remnant, combined with a fascial sling for continence.<sup>[[9]](#ref9)</sup> An earlier report by Ahmed (1995) described the same concept in two girls with an absent urethra and a closed bladder neck: a distally based anterior bladder-wall flap, tubularized and flipped caudally to the native meatus site, an orientation intended to keep the suture line away from the posterior vaginal wall rather than against it, as in the classic Tanagho configuration. Both patients were continent, one voiding normally and the other by clean intermittent catheterization.<sup>[[11]](#ref11)</sup>

### 5. Anterior Bladder Wall Advancement (Elkins Technique)

A transvaginal approach where the anterior bladder wall is mobilized and advanced into the vagina, then rolled into a neourethra or connected to the urethral remnant. Originally described for vesicovaginal fistulas with urethral loss secondary to obstetric trauma.<sup>[[5]](#ref5)</sup>

### 6. Posterior Bladder Wall Flap

Used when the anterior wall is unsuitable (prior surgery, scarring). Patidar et al. used posterior bladder flaps in 6 of 22 patients with complex urethral defects.<sup>[[2]](#ref2)</sup>

## Surgical Technique — General Principles

1. **Exposure.** Combined abdominal and vaginal approach (Tanagho, Nayyar) or transvaginal alone (Elkins); transpubic approach may be needed for pelvic-fracture cases.
2. **Flap design.** A rectangular or U-shaped full-thickness bladder-wall flap is outlined, typically from the anterior wall, with its base at the bladder neck.
3. **Flap harvest.** Raised preserving its vascular pedicle (based on superior vesical artery branches).
4. **Tubularization.** Flap rolled over a 14–18 Fr catheter and sutured to create a tube of adequate caliber.
5. **Anastomosis.** Neourethra connected distally to the urethral remnant or brought to the perineum / vaginal vestibule.
6. **Reinforcement.** Omental interposition or [Martius flap](/docs/foundations/surgical-principles/flaps/martius) between the neourethra and vagina to prevent fistula.<sup>[[3]](#ref3)</sup>
7. **Bladder closure and reservoir assessment.** Close the donor defect in layers. Assess capacity before bladder-wall tube harvest; Radwan and colleagues selected labial tubes when small capacity or adhesions precluded a Tanagho tube. Augmentation accompanied the separate pediatric Pippi Salle procedure in 13/17 patients.<sup>[[4]](#ref4)[[6]](#ref6)</sup>
8. **Catheter drainage.** Duration varies by repair: Nayyar removed the urethral catheter at 3 weeks in three cases, while Radwan maintained it for 2 weeks, then obtained a voiding cystourethrogram through the suprapubic catheter and removed that catheter after satisfactory voiding without extravasation.<sup>[[3]](#ref3)[[4]](#ref4)</sup>

## Complications

- **Urethrovesical / urethrovaginal fistula.** Fistula occurred in 2/17 patients in the 1997 Pippi Salle series and 1/6 in the 1994 series.<sup>[[6]](#ref6)[[7]](#ref7)</sup>
- **Stress urinary incontinence.** Four of 20 Elkins patients required additional anti-incontinence surgery. In Patidar, 3 of 18 socially dry patients had minimal stress incontinence managed conservatively.<sup>[[2]](#ref2)[[5]](#ref5)</sup>
- **Voiding difficulty / need for self-catheterization.** 2/22 Patidar patients required self-calibration.<sup>[[2]](#ref2)</sup>
- **Catheterization difficulty.** 3/17 Pippi Salle patients had problems with catheterization.<sup>[[6]](#ref6)</sup>
- **Urethral stenosis.** Two of 20 Elkins patients required dilation, and 2 had small bladder capacity with detrusor instability.<sup>[[5]](#ref5)</sup>
- **Bladder capacity.** Concurrent augmentation was performed in 13 of 17 and 14 of 18 patients in the two Pippi Salle series, which were pediatric neurogenic and exstrophy cohorts.<sup>[[6]](#ref6)[[8]](#ref8)</sup>

## Advantages and Limitations

**Advantages**

- Provides tissue for complete urethral replacement; tubes of 3–3.5 cm were reported in three patients.<sup>[[3]](#ref3)</sup>
- Urothelium-lined lumen is physiologically compatible.
- Well-vascularized pedicled tissue.
- Applicable when all local genital tissue options are exhausted.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup>

**Limitations**

- Requires an abdominal approach in the Tanagho and Nayyar techniques, which is more invasive than transvaginal techniques.
- Flap harvest may reduce bladder capacity. In a retrospective series of 16 female anterior bladder-wall-tube repairs, 10 had good voiding and urinary control, 4 had stress incontinence, and 2 had voiding difficulty at mean 46.6 months (range 16–108). That center considered capacity &lt;200 mL or significant bladder inflammation unsuitable for its procedure.<sup>[[12]](#ref12)</sup> Augmentation accompanied the distinct pediatric Pippi Salle procedure in 13/17 patients.<sup>[[6]](#ref6)</sup>
- Fistula is a concern; these small, heterogeneous series do not establish a comparative rate versus vaginal or labial flaps.
- Continence after urethral-loss reconstruction must be distinguished from patency after standard stricture augmentation; those percentages are not comparable endpoints.
- Limited evidence base; small case series only.<sup>[[2]](#ref2)[[3]](#ref3)[[4]](#ref4)</sup>

## Position in the Reconstructive Algorithm

Bladder wall flap urethroplasty is generally a salvage or last-resort technique when vaginal, labial, and buccal tissue sources are unavailable or have failed. Radwan et al. compared the Tanagho bladder tube with a labia minora pedicled tube (with a concomitant TOT sling) in 15 patients with post-traumatic urethral loss. Both groups had a total continence proportion of 66.6% at a mean of 42 months. The groups were very small, and equivalence was not established. The labial approach avoids the abdominal incision.<sup>[[4]](#ref4)</sup> The technique is used mainly for obliterative bladder-neck pathology and total urethral loss when other tissue sources cannot bridge the defect.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[3]](#ref3)</sup>
