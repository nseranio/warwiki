# Primary End-to-End Anastomosis (Female)

**Primary end-to-end anastomosis (EPA) in females** excises the strictured or obliterated urethral segment and directly re-approximates the healthy proximal and distal ends in a tension-free, spatulated, mucosa-to-mucosa fashion without interposition of graft or flap tissue. In women, this technique is most commonly used for pelvic fracture urethral injury (PFUI) rather than for typical female urethral stricture disease; the evidence base consists of case reports, small case series, and one systematic review.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[2]](#ref2)</sup> It differs from the augmentation urethroplasty techniques (BMG, vaginal flap) that dominate the female stricture literature.

## Concept and Rationale

The principle of EPA is identical across sexes: excise the diseased segment, mobilize the healthy urethral ends, and create a direct, tension-free, spatulated anastomosis. This avoids the need for graft or flap tissue. In males, the SIU/ICUD consultation reported 93.8% overall success for EPA of bulbar strictures across 1,234 patients (17 articles).<sup>[[3]](#ref3)</sup> Current selection distinguishes traumatic full-thickness obliteration from nontraumatic short strictures, for which nontransecting or graft options may be preferred. EPA is also the standard repair for posterior urethral distraction defects after PFUI. Koraitim reported success in 90% of perineal and 98% of perineo-abdominal repairs in 155 patients over 27 years.<sup>[[4]](#ref4)</sup>

In females, the rationale is most compelling in two scenarios:

1. **Acute pelvic fracture urethral injury.** The urethra is avulsed or transected and the ends can be directly re-approximated before fibrosis develops.
2. **Short-segment obliterative stricture.** A very short fibrotic segment can be excised with the healthy ends brought together without tension.

## Indications

- **Pelvic fracture urethral injury (PFUI).** The most common indication. Patel et al.'s SR found that 80% (66/83) of immediately repaired female PFUI cases were managed with anastomotic repair.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup>
- **Short-segment urethral obliteration.** Typically ≤ 1–1.5 cm, where healthy ends can be mobilized and approximated tension-free.
- **Traumatic urethral transection.** Iatrogenic (during pelvic surgery) or external trauma causing clean urethral disruption.
- Xu et al. used end-to-end anastomosis in 4 of 44 patients (9%) with female urethral stricture associated with urethrovaginal fistula. The authors based technique selection on fistula location, stricture length and vaginal anatomy.<sup>[[2]](#ref2)</sup>

## Contraindications and Limitations

- **Stricture length > 1–1.5 cm.** The female urethra is only 3–5 cm long, so even modest tissue loss creates a proportionally large gap. Unlike the bulbar urethra (which can be mobilized to bridge 2–3 cm), the female urethra has limited mobility, making tension-free anastomosis difficult beyond very short defects.<sup>[[5]](#ref5)[[6]](#ref6)</sup>
- **Bladder neck or proximal-urethra involvement.** Excision in this area risks damage to the continence mechanism.
- **Extensive periurethral fibrosis.** Prevents adequate mobilization.
- **Concomitant urethrovaginal fistula.** May require tissue interposition rather than direct closure.
- **Lichen sclerosus.** Diseased tissue margins may extend beyond the visible stricture.

## Surgical Technique

The technique in females is adapted from male EPA principles but performed via a vaginal approach (or combined vaginal-abdominal or transpubic approach for proximal injuries).

### Early Anastomotic Repair for Acute PFUI

EAU guidelines recommend early repair (within seven days) for female PFUI, in preference to delayed repair or early realignment (strong recommendation). Early repair is preferred once the patient is haemodynamically stable, and proximal or midurethral disruptions are repaired through retropubic and transvaginal routes. Male delayed-PFUI algorithms do not apply to women.<sup>[[17]](#ref17)</sup> The EAU cites pooled complication rates by strategy (from one review):<sup>[[17]](#ref17)</sup>

| Strategy | Stricture | Fistula | Incontinence | Vaginal stenosis | Permanent diversion |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Early realignment | 59% | 13% | 0% | 0% | 0% |
| Early repair (7 days or less) | 3% | 6% | 9% | 0% | 3% |
| Delayed repair (more than 7 days) | 3% | 4% | 31% | 4% | 7% |

Delayed repair often needs a complex abdominal or combined abdominal-vaginal reconstruction. Associated vaginal lacerations are closed in two layers at the same time, and a distal urethral injury that spares the sphincter mechanism can be left hypospadiac.<sup>[[17]](#ref17)</sup>

**Children.** The pediatric urology literature describes a more conservative default than the adult EAU pathway above: urethral injury is rare in a girl without an associated pelvic fracture, and occurs in about 5% of female pelvic fractures. Suprapubic drainage with delayed repair is described as the usual course in children, though primary realignment over a urethral catheter within a few days is considered acceptable in a hemodynamically stable child, and continence is generally favorable when the bladder neck is spared. This pediatric pattern is expert/textbook guidance rather than comparative evidence, and it has not been tested against the early-repair strategy that the adult evidence above favors; evaluate a girl with gross hematuria, urinary retention, or vaginal or rectal injury under anesthesia with cystoscopy, since imaging adds little here.<sup>[[20]](#ref20)</sup>

1. **Timing.** Performed as soon as the patient is hemodynamically stable. Patel et al. concluded, from very-low-quality literature, that primary anastomotic repair via a vaginal approach "as soon as the patient is haemodynamically stable appears to be optimal." Dorairajan et al. performed same-day repair or realignment in 4 girls or young women with PFUI.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[7]](#ref7)</sup>
2. **Patient positioning.** Lithotomy.
3. **Approach.** Anterior vaginal-wall incision; identification of the disrupted urethral ends.
4. **Debridement.** Devitalized tissue at both urethral ends is debrided back to healthy, bleeding mucosa. Because the female urethra is short, keeping debridement to the minimum needed to reach healthy tissue has been described.<sup>[[18]](#ref18)</sup>
5. **Mobilization.** Both urethral ends are mobilized circumferentially to allow tension-free approximation. The distal end typically has more mobility than the proximal end. When a vaginal laceration accompanies the injury, mobilizing the urethra and vagina as a single unit to bridge the defect and reduce anastomotic tension has been described.<sup>[[18]](#ref18)</sup> When proximal dissection separates the bladder from the anterior vaginal wall, ureteral stents or infant feeding tubes have been used to identify and protect the ureters; if the urethral mucosa is too fragile to hold sutures, further mobilizing the bladder away from the anterior vaginal wall is an option.<sup>[[18]](#ref18)</sup>
6. **Spatulation.** Both ends are spatulated (incised longitudinally for about 5 mm on opposite sides) to widen the anastomosis and reduce circumferential contracture risk.
7. **Anastomosis.** Mucosa-to-mucosa with interrupted or running 4-0 or 5-0 absorbable sutures (polyglactin or poliglecaprone) over a 14–16 Fr urethral catheter.
8. **Reinforcement.** Periurethral fascia or a [Martius flap](/docs/foundations/surgical-principles/flaps/martius) may be interposed between the anastomosis and the vaginal wall for vascular support and fistula prevention.
9. **Vaginal closure.** Layered closure of the anterior vaginal wall.
10. **Catheter management.** Urethral catheter (± SPC) for 2–3 weeks. A four-week urethral-and-suprapubic-catheter interval, followed by pericatheter and voiding studies before catheter removal, has also been described.<sup>[[18]](#ref18)</sup>

### Delayed Anastomosis for Established Obliterative Stricture

Delayed reconstruction is for established defects or when early repair was not feasible; it is not the preferred routine pathway for a stable woman with a newly diagnosed PFUI.

1. **Timing.** 3–6 months after the initial injury, once inflammation has resolved and fibrosis has stabilized. The AUA guideline gives this interval for delayed anastomotic urethroplasty after PFUI.<sup>[[8]](#ref8)</sup>
2. **Preoperative assessment.** VCUG, cystoscopy (antegrade via SP tract and retrograde), and MRI to define gap length and relationship to the bladder neck.
3. **Approach.** Vaginal for most cases; transpubic for proximal or long defects.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[9]](#ref9)</sup>
4. **Scar excision.** Fibrotic obliterated segment is excised completely until healthy urethral mucosa is identified at both ends.
5. **Mobilization and anastomosis.** As above.
6. **Adjunctive maneuvers** when the gap exceeds tension-free vaginal anastomosis:
   - **Inferior pubectomy (male-derived maneuver).** Partial resection of the inferior pubic symphysis can shorten the inter-end distance in male bulboprostatic anastomosis; benefit as a female EPA adjunct has not been established.<sup>[[8]](#ref8)[[10]](#ref10)</sup>
   - **Transpubic approach.** Wider exposure for proximal urethral mobilization.<sup>[[9]](#ref9)</sup>
   - If tension-free anastomosis remains impossible, convert to substitution urethroplasty (vaginal flap, labial flap, BMG, or bladder flap).<sup>[[2]](#ref2)</sup>

For a bladder-neck-level avulsion repaired transabdominally, identifying the bladder neck through the suprapubic tract under endoscopic guidance (a 12-Fr mini-nephroscope has been described) before placing the posterior anastomotic sutures, then passing a 14-Fr silicone catheter across the repair to guide placement of the anterior sutures, with omental interposition over the anastomosis, has been described for selected proximal injuries.<sup>[[19]](#ref19)</sup>

### Key Findings — Patel 2017 Systematic Review

A systematic review of female PFUI management covering 158 female patients across 51 articles; the authors rated the literature as very low quality.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup>

- **83 (53%)** managed with immediate repair: 17 via primary alignment, 66 via anastomotic repair.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup>
- **75 (47%)** managed with delayed repair.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup>
- **Urethral stenosis and fistula rates were highest after primary alignment** (not anastomotic repair).
- **Urethral integrity was similar** between primary anastomosis and delayed repair.
- Incontinence and vaginal stenosis were significantly more common after delayed repair, which favors early anastomotic repair when feasible.
- Delayed repair patients more often required more extensive reconstructive surgery (substitution urethroplasty rather than simple anastomosis).
- Conclusion: "Primary anastomotic repair of a female urethral distraction defect via a vaginal approach as soon as the patient is haemodynamically stable appears to be optimal."

### Primary vs Delayed Repair

| Parameter | Primary Anastomosis | Delayed Repair |
|---|---|---|
| Timing | As soon as hemodynamically stable | 3–6 mo post-injury |
| Urethral integrity | Similar | Similar |
| Incontinence | Lower | Higher |
| Vaginal stenosis | Lower | Higher |
| Complexity of surgery | Simpler (direct anastomosis) | More complex (often requires substitution) |
| Tissue quality | Fresh edges, less fibrosis | Fibrotic; may require extensive excision |
| Patient selection | Hemodynamically stable, identifiable ends | Selected patients when early repair is not feasible |

Source: Patel 2017 SR.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup>

## Comparison with Male EPA

| Feature | Male EPA | Female EPA |
|---|---|---|
| Primary indication | Short bulbar stricture (≤ 2 cm) or PFUI | PFUI primarily; rarely for short stricture |
| Urethral length | ~ 18–20 cm for the whole male urethra, not just the anterior segment | 3–5 cm |
| Mobilization potential | Extensive (bulbar urethra mobilizable several cm) | Limited |
| Adjunctive maneuvers | Crural separation, inferior pubectomy, urethral rerouting | Inferior pubectomy, transpubic approach |
| Success rate | Depends on site, etiology and endpoint | Limited female-specific data; no valid cross-sex equivalence comparison |
| Continence risk | Low (external sphincter preserved) | Higher concern (shorter urethra, less sphincteric redundancy) |
| Non-transecting option | Described (vessel-sparing EPA) | Two-case uncontrolled series described<sup>[[18]](#ref18)</sup> |

### Non-Transecting EPA: Male Rationale and Limited Female Experience

In males, non-transecting, vessel-sparing EPA preserves the bulbar arteries and periurethral vasculature by excising only the fibrotic mucosa or submucosa without transecting the full thickness of the corpus spongiosum. The Scandinavian Urethroplasty RCT (n = 151) showed that transecting EPA was followed by more patient-reported penile complications than BMG (reduced glans filling, p = 0.03; penile shortening, p = 0.001), with no difference in IIEF-5 scores and similar recurrence (12.9% in both arms) at 12 months.<sup>[[14]](#ref14)</sup> The penile complications questionnaire was not validated.<sup>[[14]](#ref14)</sup> AUA notes that non-transecting substitution urethroplasty results in fewer penile complications than transecting urethroplasty for short bulbar strictures.<sup>[[8]](#ref8)[[13]](#ref13)</sup>

Viswaroop and Gopalakrishnan describe performing non-transecting anastomotic urethroplasty in two women with obliterative or near-obliterative strictures under 1 cm, an approach they extrapolated from male experience rather than a standardized female operation. The scarred segment is opened longitudinally, the fibrotic mucosa is excised, and the posterior mucosa is approximated over a catheter without full urethral transection. Both women voided per urethra: one at 18 months of follow-up, and the other required cystoscopy with or without dilation every three months and was later reclassified as primary voiding dysfunction rather than a true stricture recurrence.<sup>[[18]](#ref18)</sup> This uncontrolled, two-case experience does not establish a standardized or validated female technique; the female urethra lacks a corpus spongiosum, and the vascular-preservation rationale and outcomes behind the male trials do not transfer to women.

## Advantages and Limitations

**Advantages**

- No graft or flap harvest is required, which avoids donor-site morbidity.
- Technically simpler when feasible (short defect, identifiable healthy ends).
- No concerns about graft take or flap viability.
- Lower incontinence and vaginal stenosis rates compared to delayed repair (for PFUI).<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup>
- Single-stage procedure.
- No foreign tissue; healing is native urethra to urethra.

**Limitations**

- **Narrow applicability.** The short female urethra (3–5 cm) limits the length of stricture that can be excised while still achieving tension-free anastomosis.
- **Continence risk.** Excision of any urethral segment shortens functional length and may compromise the continence mechanism, particularly if the mid-urethral high-pressure zone is involved.<sup>[[11]](#ref11)</sup>
- **No female-specific large series.** Outcomes data derived from small case reports and a SR of heterogeneous PFUI literature.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup>
- **Not applicable to most female urethral strictures.** The majority are idiopathic, mid-urethral, and best managed with augmentation (BMG or vaginal flap) rather than excision.<sup>[[15]](#ref15)</sup>
- **Risk of tension.** Even modest gaps may preclude tension-free repair, necessitating conversion to substitution urethroplasty.
- **Long-term LUTS.** In Black et al.’s pelvic-fracture cohort of 25 girls and women (21 repaired primarily; four treated nonoperatively), 9/21 assessed patients (43%) had moderate or severe LUTS at a mean of 7.3 years. Sexual dysfunction was also reported among respondents.<sup>[[11]](#ref11)</sup>

## Position in the Female Reconstructive Algorithm

EPA occupies a specific niche distinct from the augmentation techniques used for typical stricture disease.

1. **For acute PFUI.** EPA is used when the urethral ends can be identified and approximated tension-free. EAU guidelines recommend early repair within seven days (strong).<sup>[[17]](#ref17)</sup> The ACS Best Practices 2025 guideline recommends urologic and gynecologic consultation, with management options including urethral catheterization versus immediate primary repair (vaginally, transabdominally, or combined).<sup>[[16]](#ref16)</sup> Patel 2017 favored primary anastomotic repair via a vaginal approach.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup>
2. **For delayed PFUI reconstruction.** AUA recommends delayed urethroplasty over delayed endoscopic procedures, with anastomotic reconstruction via perineal approach (males) using mobilization and crural separation for tension-free repair. In females the vaginal approach is used; transpubic access for proximal defects.<sup>[[8]](#ref8)[[9]](#ref9)</sup>
3. **For typical female urethral stricture (idiopathic, iatrogenic).** EPA is rarely indicated. The AUA states that surgeons may reconstruct female strictures with oral mucosal grafts, vaginal flaps or a combination (Moderate Recommendation; Evidence Level Grade C), with reported success of 69–95%.<sup>[[8]](#ref8)</sup> Augmentation preserves urethral length, whereas EPA is limited by the short female urethra.
4. **For short-segment obliterative stricture without PFUI.** EPA may be considered if the obliterated segment is very short (≤ 1 cm) and surrounding tissue is healthy, but this scenario is uncommon. Xu used EPA in only 4 of 44 patients (9%) with FUS and UVF.<sup>[[2]](#ref2)</sup>

## Summary

Primary end-to-end anastomosis in females has been described mainly for pelvic fracture urethral injury and is rarely applicable to typical female urethral stricture disease. The main evidence is Patel 2017, a systematic review of very-low-quality literature, which concluded that primary anastomotic repair via a vaginal approach as soon as the patient is haemodynamically stable appears optimal for female PFUI, with less incontinence and vaginal stenosis than delayed repair. EAU guidelines recommend early repair within seven days (strong).<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup> For non-traumatic female strictures, the short urethral length (3–5 cm) limits applicability, and augmentation techniques (BMG, vaginal flap) are used instead. When EPA is feasible, the technical principles (complete scar excision, adequate mobilization, spatulation and tension-free mucosa-to-mucosa anastomosis) mirror those established in the extensive male literature.<sup>[[3]](#ref3)[[4]](#ref4)</sup>
