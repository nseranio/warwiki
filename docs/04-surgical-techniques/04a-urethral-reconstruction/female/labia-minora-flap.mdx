# Labia Minora Pedicled Flap (Female Urethroplasty)

The **labia minora pedicled flap** is a technique for female urethral reconstruction that uses tissue from the inner aspect of the labia minora, mobilized on its native vascular pedicle, to augment or replace the strictured urethral segment. Reports have concentrated on complex strictures, obliterative disease and urethral strictures with associated urethrovaginal fistulas.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[2]](#ref2)</sup>

For a different genital flap and its anatomy, see [Labia Majora Fasciocutaneous Flap](/docs/foundations/surgical-principles/flaps/labia-majora-fasciocutaneous). Oral-lip mucosal grafts are a separate donor category.

## Tissue Properties

Labia minora tissue is considered a suitable donor source because it is hairless, wet, elastic, and well-vascularized, properties that closely mimic urethral mucosa.<sup>[[3]](#ref3)</sup> The tissue is easily accessible within the same operative field, avoiding the need for a separate donor site (as with buccal mucosa). The main limitations are co-existing lichen sclerosus or vulvovaginal atrophy, which compromise tissue quality and future outcomes.<sup>[[3]](#ref3)</sup>

## Surgical Technique

Two principal configurations have been described.

### 1. Onlay Augmentation Flap

The pedicled labia minora flap is harvested with its vascular pedicle intact and used as a ventral or dorsal onlay to augment the strictured urethral segment.<sup>[[3]](#ref3)</sup> The EAU 2026 update pooled 29 women treated with labial or vestibular flap urethroplasty in two papers, with stricture-free rates of 88–100% at 6 to 15 months and no adverse effects reported.<sup>[[8]](#ref8)</sup>

### 2. Tubularized Neourethra

For obliterative strictures or complete urethral loss, a flap of approximately 3 × 3.5 × 3 cm from the labia minora (or majora) is tubularized over an 18–22 Fr fenestrated silicone stent to create a neourethra. Bilateral labial flaps may be harvested and pieced together to achieve adequate length. This approach has been described via both transvaginal and transpubic access routes. Transpubic access gives wider exposure for complex obliterative strictures secondary to pelvic fracture.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[2]](#ref2)</sup>

### Key Steps (Tubularized Technique)

1. Flap design on the inner aspect of the labium minus, preserving the lateral vascular pedicle.
2. Careful dissection maintaining the subdermal vascular plexus.
3. Tubularization of the flap over a catheter / stent.
4. Anastomosis of the neourethra to the proximal healthy urethra and bladder neck.
5. Interposition of a labial fat pad (Martius flap) between the neourethra and vaginal wall for additional vascular support and tissue reinforcement when needed.<sup>[[4]](#ref4)</sup>
6. Layered vaginal-wall closure.

## Indications

Xu et al. reported 44 women with urethral stricture associated with urethrovaginal fistula. Labial pedicle flap urethroplasty was the most frequently used technique (24 of 44 patients). The authors conclude that procedure selection should be based on fistula location, stricture length and vaginal anatomy, that a transvaginal approach may be optimal when the vagina is wide and easily dilated, and that the pedicle labial flap is a reliable technique for complex strictures.<sup>[[2]](#ref2)</sup>

| Indication | Configuration Described |
|---|---|
| Standard urethral stricture (non-obliterative) | Onlay augmentation flap |
| Obliterative stricture / complete urethral loss | Tubularized neourethra |
| Urethral stricture + urethrovaginal fistula | Tubularized or onlay + fistula repair |
| Pediatric urethrovaginal fistula (pelvic fracture) | Pedicled labia minora flap |

Sources: Xu 2009; Xu 2013; Bouchard 2025; Radwan 2013; Jiang 2017.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[2]](#ref2)[[3]](#ref3)[[4]](#ref4)[[5]](#ref5)</sup>

## Outcomes

- **Xu 2009 transpubic tubularized technique (n = 8):** Mean follow-up 48.25 mo (range 10–118). No postoperative complications were reported. One patient required urethral dilation at 3 mo (Qmax 13 mL/s); all others voided normally after catheter removal. Transient dysuria (2) and stress incontinence (1) resolved spontaneously.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup>
- **Xu 2013 multi-technique comparison (n = 44; labial pedicle flap in 24):** Anatomical success 93.2% (41/44) and functional success 90.9% (40/44) at mean 42.3 mo. Fistula recurrence in 2 (infection-driven), stricture recurrence in 1, de novo SUI in 1.<sup>[[2]](#ref2)</sup>
- **Radwan 2013 labia minora pedicled tube with TOT sling (n = 10; post-traumatic urethral loss):** Anatomical repair succeeded in all patients. Total continence was achieved in 6 of 9 (66.6%) with follow-up. Complications were meatal stenosis (1) and transient urinary retention (2). Mean follow-up was 42 mo for the 15 patients followed in the whole series.<sup>[[4]](#ref4)</sup>
- **Jiang 2017 labia minora skin flap (n = 4 girls; urethrovaginal fistula after pelvic fracture):** Mean follow-up 27 mo. All voided well with normal urinary control, and 1 had a recurrent fistula.<sup>[[5]](#ref5)</sup>
- **Osman 2013 systematic review (all retrospective case series):** Vaginal or labial flap augmentation had a mean success rate of 91% at 32.1 mo (58 patients). Vaginal or labial graft augmentation had 80% (25 patients, 22 mo), oral mucosal augmentation 94% (32 patients, 15 mo) and urethral dilation 47% (107 patients, 43 mo).<sup>[[6]](#ref6)</sup>
- **Free labial grafts (EAU 2026 pooled data):** Ventral labia minora graft (30 patients) had stricture-free rates of 75–95%. Eight (26.7%) developed urgency incontinence that settled within six months.<sup>[[8]](#ref8)</sup>

## Pedicled Flap vs Free Graft from Labia Minora

The pedicled flap differs from the free labial mucosal graft:

- The pedicled flap retains its own vascular supply, which is the rationale for using it in a poorly vascularized bed. Comparative data are lacking.<sup>[[3]](#ref3)[[7]](#ref7)</sup>
- A free graft loses its native pedicle and depends on revascularization from a healthy recipient bed. Outcomes with oral mucosal grafts do not transfer directly to labial grafts.<sup>[[3]](#ref3)</sup>
- When adequate flap mobilization is not possible (prior surgery, tissue loss, atrophy), a free graft may be substituted.<sup>[[3]](#ref3)</sup>

## Advantages and Limitations

**Advantages**

- Same operative field, no second incision site.
- Preserved blood supply, which may help in a poorly vascularized bed.
- Hairless, elastic, mucosa-like tissue.
- Can be tubularized for complete urethral replacement.
- Used in young girls with post-traumatic urethrovaginal fistula.<sup>[[5]](#ref5)</sup>

**Limitations**

- Tissue quality compromised by lichen sclerosus or vulvovaginal atrophy.
- Limited tissue volume in some patients.
- Potential for donor-site cosmetic changes.
- Smaller evidence base than buccal mucosal graft.<sup>[[3]](#ref3)[[7]](#ref7)</sup>
