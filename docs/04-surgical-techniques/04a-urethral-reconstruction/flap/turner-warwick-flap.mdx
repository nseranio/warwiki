# Turner-Warwick Two-Stage Scrotal Inlay Urethroplasty

The **Turner-Warwick urethroplasty** is a two-stage scrotal inlay technique for bulbomembranous urethral strictures, named after Richard Turner-Warwick (1925–2020) of The Middlesex Hospital and the Institute of Urology, London. Stage 1 marsupializes the strictured urethra to scrotal skin; Stage 2 tubularizes the matured plate approximately 4–6 months later. In Reid's 60-patient series, the overall success rate was approximately 90%.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup> Modern two-stage BMG urethroplasty has largely replaced the technique because of the limitations of hair-bearing scrotal skin. Turner-Warwick's broader contributions remain in use: the staged-reconstruction approach, transpubic posterior urethroplasty and the principles of vascularized tissue transfer.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[2]](#ref2)[[3]](#ref3)[[4]](#ref4)</sup>

For the related Cecil / Culp / Pierce / Johanson scrotal-based two-stage lineage, see [Cecil-Culp Procedure](/docs/surgical-techniques/04e-genital-reconstruction/cecil-culp-procedure). For the longitudinal ventral penile skin flap (often mistakenly attributed to Turner-Warwick), see [Orandi Flap](/docs/surgical-techniques/04a-urethral-reconstruction/orandi-flap). For modern BMG-based staged repair, see [Buccal Mucosa Graft](/docs/foundations/surgical-principles/grafts/buccal-mucosa).

> **Nomenclature note.** A "Turner-Warwick longitudinal ventral penile flap" is not a recognized technique in the reconstructive urology literature. The longitudinal ventral penile skin flap is Amin Orandi's 1968 contribution.<sup>[[12]](#ref12)</sup> Turner-Warwick's principal eponymous urethroplasty technique is the two-stage scrotal inlay described here.

## Richard Turner-Warwick — The Man and His Contributions

**Richard Turner-Warwick (1925–2020)** was a British urological surgeon based at The Middlesex Hospital and the Institute of Urology, London, widely regarded as one of the founding fathers of modern urethral reconstructive surgery. His major contributions:<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[2]](#ref2)[[4]](#ref4)</sup>

1. **Two-stage scrotal inlay urethroplasty** ("the Turner-Warwick urethroplasty"), a staged technique for complex bulbomembranous strictures.
2. **Transpubic approach** for posterior urethral distraction injuries.
3. **Classification systems** for urethral stricture disease.
4. **Principles of urethral reconstruction.** Tissue transfer, the importance of vascularized tissue, the role of staged procedures.
5. **Training and mentorship.** Turner-Warwick trained a generation of reconstructive urologists, including Anthony Mundy, who succeeded him at the Institute of Urology and continued to refine the techniques.

For biographical dates, see the [ICS tribute](https://www.ics.org/news/1163). For the surgeon profile, see [Richard Turner-Warwick](/docs/roots/surgeons/s-z/richard-turner-warwick).

## The Two-Stage Scrotal Inlay Technique

### Stage 1 — marsupialization and scrotal inlay

- The strictured urethra is opened along its ventral surface (ventral urethrotomy) through the full length of the stricture.
- The opened urethral edges are sutured to the surrounding scrotal or perineal skin, creating a marsupialized urethral trough, in which the urethra is laid open and the patient voids through a perineal opening.
- This allows the strictured segment to heal in an open configuration, with the scrotal skin providing a vascularized bed for epithelialization.
- The patient voids through the perineal opening for typically 4–6 months while the tissue matures.

### Stage 2 — tubularization (closure)

- After tissue maturation and once the urethral bed is healthy, the marsupialized trough is tubularized: the scrotal skin edges are rolled inward and sutured together to recreate a tubular urethra.
- A urethral catheter is placed and the patient is followed for stricture recurrence.

### Reid 1975 outcomes (60 patients)<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup>

- **Overall success approximately 90%**
- Restenosis of the proximal or distal stoma after Stage 1 was an early difficulty; the report describes nitrofurazone-hydrocortisone urethral suppositories as practically eliminating it. This uncontrolled 1975 practice is not a current recommendation for routine intraluminal treatment.
- Modifications included placement of urethral sutures prior to scrotal mobilization and abandonment of suprapubic cystotomy at Stage 2.
- Patients with strictures following prostatectomy had problems with urinary control, but not if prostatectomy was performed after Stage 1.

**Alexander 1977** reviewed 59 cases of urethral stricture treated at Charity Hospital of Louisiana from 1965 to 1975. Five procedures were used (internal urethrotomy, Johanson-Leadbetter, patch-graft, Turner-Warwick and dismembered techniques). Results of internal urethrotomy were poor, and the other procedures together had an overall success of 72%. Turner-Warwick outcomes were not reported separately.<sup>[[6]](#ref6)</sup>

## Turner-Warwick vs. Johanson — The Two Staged Techniques

The Turner-Warwick two-stage urethroplasty is closely related to the Johanson 1953 staged urethroplasty. Both involve marsupialization followed by tubularization. The differences are:<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[2]](#ref2)[[7]](#ref7)[[8]](#ref8)</sup>

| Feature | Johanson (1953) | Turner-Warwick |
|---|---|---|
| Primary indication | Anterior (penile / bulbar) strictures | Bulbomembranous strictures |
| Stage 1 | Ventral urethrotomy + marsupialization to penile / perineal skin | Ventral urethrotomy + scrotal inlay |
| Stage 2 | Tubularization of the marsupialized trough | Tubularization with scrotal mobilization |
| Tissue source | Local penile / perineal skin | Scrotal skin |
| Key advantage | Simpler; applicable to anterior strictures | Reaches bulbomembranous junction; more tissue available |
| Key limitation | Limited reach for posterior strictures | Hair-bearing scrotal skin; diverticula; urine-induced dermatitis |

**Olsson and Krane 1978** directly compared single-stage versus multi-staged urethroplasties (including Johanson, Leadbetter-Johanson, Turner-Warwick) and concluded that no advantage was seen when initial or final success rates were compared, while the multi-staged approaches carried the burden of increased hospitalization, absence from employment, and morbidity of multiple anesthetics.<sup>[[2]](#ref2)</sup>

## Limitations of the Scrotal Inlay Approach

The scrotal inlay technique has well-documented limitations that led to its decline:<sup>[[5]](#ref5)[[9]](#ref9)[[10]](#ref10)[[11]](#ref11)</sup>

1. **Hair-bearing scrotal skin.** Scrotal skin is typically hair-bearing; intraurethral hair growth, hair-ball formation, and stone formation are characteristic complications. Provet 1989 emphasized the importance of using hairless skin and aggressive tailoring.<sup>[[10]](#ref10)</sup>
2. **Diverticula formation.** Scrotal skin, being thin and pliable, tends to balloon outward during voiding, creating pseudo-diverticula that trap urine and cause postvoid dribbling.<sup>[[9]](#ref9)[[10]](#ref10)</sup>
3. **Urine-induced dermatitis.** Prolonged contact of urine with scrotal skin during the marsupialized phase can cause chronic dermatitis, compromising tissue quality for Stage 2 closure.<sup>[[9]](#ref9)</sup>
4. **Two-stage morbidity.** Two separate operations with months of perineal voiding between stages impose a quality-of-life burden.<sup>[[2]](#ref2)</sup>
5. **Stomal restenosis.** Restenosis at the proximal or distal stoma after Stage 1 was a frequent early problem in Reid's series.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup>

**Jordan 2002** argued that scrotal and perineal flaps have been "unfairly maligned" for poor waterproofing, diverticula and hair importation. Hairless scrotal areas can be mobilized as skin islands, and the skin overlying the perineal artery is non-hirsute or nearly non-hirsute in many individuals.<sup>[[11]](#ref11)</sup>

## Distinguishing Turner-Warwick from the Orandi Flap

The longitudinal ventral penile skin flap is the Orandi flap (1968), which is not a Turner-Warwick technique:

| Feature | Turner-Warwick Urethroplasty | Orandi Flap |
|---|---|---|
| Year | 1960s–1970s<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[2]](#ref2)</sup> | 1968<sup>[[12]](#ref12)</sup> |
| Stages | **Two-stage** (marsupialization → tubularization) | **Single-stage** (pedicled island flap) |
| Tissue source | Scrotal / perineal skin | Ventral penile shaft skin |
| Pedicle | Broad local attachment supplies the scrotal skin inlay | Dartos fascia pedicle (vascularized island flap) |
| Primary indication | Bulbomembranous strictures | Penile urethral strictures |
| Hair-bearing | Yes (scrotal skin) | No (distal penile skin is hairless) |
| Current status | Largely replaced by modern BMG staged urethroplasty | Still used (Barbagli dorsal modification 2019) |

## Turner-Warwick's Broader Legacy

The scrotal inlay technique has largely been superseded, but Turner-Warwick's broader contributions remain in use:<sup>[[4]](#ref4)[[5]](#ref5)[[13]](#ref13)</sup>

1. **Principles of tissue transfer.** Turner-Warwick is credited with early articulation of the principle of using vascularized tissue for urethral reconstruction (well-vascularized tissue heals better and resists stricture recurrence).
2. **Staged-approach philosophy.** Recognition that some strictures are too complex for single-stage repair. Secrest 2002 emphasized that "staging a urethroplasty should not be considered a step backwards" and listed indications including chronic inflammation, fistula, false passage, urethral stones, diverticula, abscess, failed prior repair, complicated hypospadias, severe trauma, neurologic diseases, extensive BXO strictures, and long strictures.<sup>[[5]](#ref5)</sup>
3. **Transpubic approach** for posterior urethral distraction injuries provided excellent exposure and visualization of the involved anatomy. Brock and Kaplan 1981 used this approach in 5 children, 2 of whom had concomitant closure of a failed first-stage Turner-Warwick scrotal inlay.<sup>[[3]](#ref3)</sup>
4. **Training the next generation.** Turner-Warwick's trainees, particularly Anthony Mundy at the Institute of Urology, continued to advance the field. Greenwell, Venn, and Mundy 1999 documented the transition from one-stage flap to two-stage free-graft procedures. They concluded that buccal mucosal free grafts are the material of choice for patch urethroplasty of an uncomplicated bulbar stricture, that the Orandi procedure remains the "gold standard" for patch urethroplasty of an uncomplicated penile stricture, and that two-stage free-graft repairs give better results than one-stage flap repairs for circumferential reconstruction of the penile urethra.<sup>[[4]](#ref4)</sup>

## Modern Evolution — From Scrotal Inlay to BMG Staged Urethroplasty

The Turner-Warwick scrotal inlay concept has been modernized by replacing scrotal skin with buccal mucosal grafts (BMG) or split-thickness skin grafts (mesh grafts) in the staged approach.<sup>[[5]](#ref5)[[9]](#ref9)[[14]](#ref14)</sup>

### Mesh graft urethroplasty (Schreiter / Noll 1989; Carr / MacDiarmid / Webster 1997)<sup>[[9]](#ref9)[[14]](#ref14)</sup>

- Scrotal skin replaced with meshed split-thickness skin graft from the thigh or full-thickness foreskin.
- Advantages over scrotal inlay: non-hair-bearing, easier to size, seemingly less permeable to urine penetration.
- Carr 1997: 20 men with complex strictures, most after failed urethroplasty, 17 with thigh split-thickness graft and 3 with foreskin. Success was 12 of 15 men (80%) at median 38 months; 5 men had not undergone closure.
- Schreiter and Noll 1989: 96 patients (meshed foreskin or split-thickness skin) with excellent anatomical and functional results in all but 1 patient.

### Modern two-stage BMG urethroplasty

The current standard for staged urethroplasty uses BMG rather than scrotal skin or mesh grafts:<sup>[[4]](#ref4)[[5]](#ref5)[[15]](#ref15)</sup>

- **Stage 1.** Strictured urethra opened; BMG quilted to the corpora cavernosa or urethral bed to create a new urethral plate.
- **Stage 2** (after adequate maturation, commonly at least 4–6 months; timing individualized): the matured BMG plate is tubularized to recreate the urethra.
- BMG avoids the hair-bearing skin problem and is a mucosal surface, and staged BMG repair is used for complex penile strictures, lichen sclerosus and failed prior repairs.

## Contemporary Practice Trends

The evolution from Turner-Warwick-era techniques to modern practice is substantial:<sup>[[4]](#ref4)[[16]](#ref16)[[17]](#ref17)</sup>

- **Greenwell 1999.** Documented the shift at the Institute of Urology (79 anterior urethroplasties, 1992 to 1996) from one-stage flap to two-stage free-graft procedures. Of 45 one-stage bulbar patch urethroplasties, 76% used BMG rather than flaps. Of 34 penile urethroplasties, 82% were two-stage procedures.<sup>[[4]](#ref4)</sup>
- **Cotter 2019.** Multi-institutional analysis of 2,098 anterior urethroplasties (2010–2017): fasciocutaneous flap use in the penile urethra declined (-86%) while single-stage dorsal repairs increased (+280%).<sup>[[17]](#ref17)</sup>
- **Fuchs 2018.** In a retrospective single-center review of 403 complex anterior strictures (2007 to 2017), penile skin flap use declined while perineal urethrostomy use increased from 4.3% to 38.7% of case volume.<sup>[[16]](#ref16)</sup>

## Guideline Context

The AUA Urethral Stricture Disease Guideline (2023 amendment) makes these recommendations relevant to staged urethroplasty:<sup>[[18]](#ref18)</sup>

- Surgeons may reconstruct long multi-segment strictures with one-stage or multi-stage techniques using oral mucosal grafts, penile fasciocutaneous flaps, or a combination (Moderate Recommendation; Grade C).
- Surgeons should use oral mucosa as the first choice when using grafts (Expert Opinion).
- In lichen sclerosus-proven stricture, surgeons should not use genital skin for reconstruction (Strong Recommendation; Grade B).
- Surgeons should not use hair-bearing skin for substitution urethroplasty (Clinical Principle). This addresses the main limitation of the scrotal inlay technique.

## Key Takeaways

The **Turner-Warwick urethroplasty** is a two-stage scrotal inlay technique for bulbomembranous strictures, and not a longitudinal ventral penile flap, which is the Orandi 1968 technique. Reid 1975 reported approximately 90% success in 60 patients.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup> Modern two-stage BMG urethroplasty has largely replaced the technique and avoids importing hair-bearing scrotal skin.<sup>[[5]](#ref5)[[9]](#ref9)[[14]](#ref14)</sup> Turner-Warwick's broader legacy includes the staged-reconstruction approach, the transpubic approach, the principles of vascularized tissue transfer and the training of Anthony Mundy and a generation of reconstructive urologists.<sup>[[2]](#ref2)[[3]](#ref3)[[4]](#ref4)[[5]](#ref5)</sup>
