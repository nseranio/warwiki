# Abdominoperineal (Transpubic) Urethroplasty

**Abdominoperineal urethroplasty** is a combined surgical approach for complex posterior urethral distraction defects, most commonly after pelvic fracture urethral injury (PFUI). It is the most extensive step in the reconstructive ladder for posterior urethral strictures and is reserved for cases in which a perineal-only approach cannot achieve a tension-free anastomosis. Synonymous terms include combined abdominal transpubic perineal urethroplasty, perineo-abdominal urethroplasty, and transpubic urethroplasty.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[2]](#ref2)[[3]](#ref3)</sup>

For the canonical PFUI clinical workflow (epidemiology, MRI, Webster steps, surveillance), see [Pelvic Fracture Urethral Injury (PFUI)](/docs/special-populations/05a-trauma-emergencies/pfui). For the standard perineal anastomotic technique, see [Excision and Primary Anastomosis](/docs/surgical-techniques/04a-urethral-reconstruction/excision-primary-anastomosis). For the robotic alternative for VUAS / BNC, see [Robotic Posterior Urethroplasty](/docs/surgical-techniques/04a-urethral-reconstruction/robotic-posterior-urethroplasty). For the post-prostatectomy VUAS-context abdominoperineal approach, see [Combined Abdominoperineal Approach (BNC / VUAS)](/docs/surgical-techniques/04ab-bladder-neck-reconstruction/bnc-vuas/combined-abdominoperineal).

## Indications

The abdominoperineal approach is reserved for cases in which suitable perineal maneuvers are insufficient or in which intra-abdominal pathology requires direct abdominal access:<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[2]](#ref2)[[4]](#ref4)[[5]](#ref5)[[6]](#ref6)[[7]](#ref7)</sup>

- **Defects that cannot be bridged without tension through an appropriate perineal approach**; a fixed gap length alone does not select an abdominal approach.
- **Previous failed perineal urethroplasty** in salvage cases.
- **Associated complex pathology.** Rectourethral fistulas, periurethral cavities / abscesses, false passages, or bladder-neck laceration.
- **Pediatric cases** in which a small pelvis limits perineal exposure.
- **Proximal urethral stump above the inferior pubic margin** on imaging, associated with step 3 or higher (pubectomy, rerouting or abdominal access; OR 66) in a retrospective study analyzing 102/114 patients, not a specific predictor of abdominal access alone.

The abdominoperineal approach was used in 6/122 (5%) PFUI repairs in the TURNS multicenter series and in 5.6% of 737 repairs in a large single-center series. It is reserved for the most complex defects.<sup>[[8]](#ref8)[[9]](#ref9)</sup>

## Webster Step-Based Classification

The procedure fits within the Webster classification of progressive perineal maneuvers for posterior urethroplasty:<sup>[[7]](#ref7)[[9]](#ref9)</sup>

| Step | Maneuver |
|---|---|
| 1 | Bulbar urethral mobilization alone |
| 2 | Corporal splitting (separation of the crura) |
| 3 | Inferior pubectomy |
| 4 | Supracrural urethral rerouting |
| Combined | Abdominoperineal approach when steps 1–4 are insufficient or intra-abdominal pathology (bladder-neck repair, fistula closure) requires direct abdominal access |

![Progressive perineal maneuvers after PFUI: Male posterior urethral reconstruction, conceptual route map](/img/diagrams/pfui-webster-steps.svg)

*Mobilization, corporal separation, inferior pubectomy and selected rerouting can shorten the path to a tension-free urethral anastomosis. A repertoire, not mandatory escalation. Scar excision, blood supply, corporal and sphincter anatomy require operative judgment; rerouting is selective. (Original WARWIKI schematic; see the figure source record and review limits.)*

Kizer reported recurrence in 3/4 rerouted repairs and success in 4/5 abdominoperineal repairs. These tiny selected subgroups do not establish that rerouting underperforms abdominal access. A larger later series found no significant recurrence-free survival difference among step-4, abdominal and substitution groups.<sup>[[4]](#ref4)[[9]](#ref9)</sup>

## Preoperative Evaluation

- **RGU and VCUG** define distraction-defect length and stump position relative to the pubic symphysis.<sup>[[2]](#ref2)[[7]](#ref7)</sup>
- **Cystourethroscopy** assesses bladder-neck competence and the proximal urethral stump.<sup>[[2]](#ref2)[[5]](#ref5)</sup>
- **Pelvic MRI.** The pubourethral stump angle (PUA) on sagittal T2 was the only independent predictor of an elaborate approach in a retrospective series of 74 men. A low PUA is associated with the need for inferior pubectomy or abdominoperineal repair.<sup>[[10]](#ref10)</sup>
- **Erectile function** is assessed as a predictor of surgical complexity and as a baseline for counseling.<sup>[[7]](#ref7)</sup>
- **Timing.** Definitive reconstruction is planned after major injuries stabilize and the patient can be positioned safely for urethroplasty (AUA 2023, Expert Opinion).<sup>[[11]](#ref11)</sup>

## Surgical Technique

| Step | Detail |
|---|---|
| 1. Positioning | Lithotomy adapted to orthopedic injuries, pressure protection and simultaneous access to perineum and lower abdomen<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[2]](#ref2)</sup> |
| 2. Perineal phase | Bulbar urethra mobilized through midline perineal incision; all scar excised until healthy mucosa is identified distally |
| 3. Abdominal phase | Lower midline or Pfannenstiel incision provides retropubic access; bladder opened to identify the proximal urethral stump from above |
| 4. Partial pubectomy | Wedge of bone subperiosteally resected from medial inferior pubic rami with an osteotome, creating a wider subpubic tunnel. Partial (inferior) pubectomy is preferred over total pubectomy, which was abandoned due to high pelvic-instability and morbidity rates<sup>[[3]](#ref3)[[12]](#ref12)</sup> |
| 5. Anastomosis | Mobilized bulbar urethra passed through the subpubic tunnel (or rerouted around one penile crus when needed) and anastomosed to the prostatic apex under direct vision; spatulated and tension-free<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[3]](#ref3)</sup> |
| 6. Ancillary procedures | Bladder-neck repair when injured (20/25 in one highly selected series), rectourethral fistula closure with interposition flap (gracilis, rectus abdominis, omental pedicle, bulbospongiosus), and drainage of periurethral cavities<sup>[[5]](#ref5)[[6]](#ref6)[[3]](#ref3)</sup> |
| 7. Catheter management | Suprapubic catheter for bladder drainage; urethral silicone stent indwelling for ~ 4 weeks<sup>[[6]](#ref6)</sup> |

### Koraitim's "gold triad"

Koraitim's three principles for successful anastomotic urethroplasty:<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup>

1. Complete excision of scarred tissue.
2. Fixation of healthy mucosa at both urethral ends.
3. Creation of a tension-free anastomosis.

## Outcomes

| Series | n | Stricture Length | Success | Incontinence | New-Onset Impotence |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Koraitim 2005<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup> | 40 | — | 39/40 (98%) | No new incontinence in the full 155-patient cohort | Two potency losses in the full cohort; not a 40-patient subgroup rate |
| Pratap 2006a<sup>[[2]](#ref2)</sup> | 21 | 5.2 cm | 20/21 (95%) radiographic patency | 2/21 | 3/14 initially potent men |
| Pratap 2006b<sup>[[5]](#ref5)</sup> | 25 | 6.5 cm | 24/25 radiographic patency; 92% overall success | 4% | 0% (no change) |
| Koraitim 2010<sup>[[3]](#ref3)</sup> | 64 | 4.2 cm | 98.4% | 0% | 3.1% |
| Yepes 2023<sup>[[9]](#ref9)</sup> | 41 | — | 74.4% | — | — |
| Kizer 2007<sup>[[4]](#ref4)</sup> | 5 | — | 80% | — | — |

Different populations, follow-up and endpoints limit comparison. Koraitim’s 98% abdominal versus 90% perineal results do not prove a causal benefit from abdominal exposure, and the 2005 and 2010 reports may include overlapping patients.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[3]](#ref3)</sup>

## Complications

- **Urinary incontinence.** Baseline bladder-neck and sphincter injury contribute, and surgery cannot guarantee continence. Preoperative function and the injury pattern determine counseling.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[2]](#ref2)</sup>
- **Erectile dysfunction.** Pelvic trauma commonly causes preoperative ED, and additional postoperative impairment is possible. Pratap reported new impotence in 3/14 initially potent men, whereas Koraitim reported 2 direct potency losses in 155 patients (2010: 2/64). Estimates of surgical risk vary by series.<sup>[[2]](#ref2)</sup>
- **Pubectomy-related complications.** Abnormal gait, pelvic instability and bowel herniation are rarely seen with partial inferior pubectomy. Total pubectomy was abandoned because of bleeding, pelvic instability, incontinence and anastomotic stricture.<sup>[[2]](#ref2)[[3]](#ref3)[[12]](#ref12)</sup>
- **Recurrent stricture.** Failure ranged from 1.6% (Koraitim 2010) to 25.6% (Yepes 2023, 74.4% success) depending on series and complexity.<sup>[[3]](#ref3)[[9]](#ref9)</sup>
- **Bladder stones** were a late complication in some series (2/64 at 6 and 14 years after surgery).<sup>[[3]](#ref3)</sup>

---

## Comparison With Alternative Approaches

Abdominal access and supracrural rerouting are selective options, and comparative trials have not established a fixed hierarchy.<sup>[[4]](#ref4)[[9]](#ref9)</sup> The AUA states that anastomotic reconstruction is performed through a perineal approach, that transabdominal or transpubic techniques are rarely required, and that clinicians should refer patients to tertiary care centers when necessary.<sup>[[11]](#ref11)</sup>

For VUAS / BNC after radical prostatectomy or radiation, the contemporary alternative is robotic transabdominal reconstruction; see [Robotic Posterior Urethroplasty](/docs/surgical-techniques/04a-urethral-reconstruction/robotic-posterior-urethroplasty) and [Bladder Neck Reconstruction & VUAS](/docs/surgical-techniques/04ab-bladder-neck-reconstruction).

---

## Key Takeaways

Abdominoperineal access can enable a tension-free reconstruction when a perineal route is inadequate or associated pathology requires abdominal exposure. Outcomes vary with complexity: patency was 74.4% in one large series and 98% in selected series, and continence and potency outcomes depend on the original injury.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[2]](#ref2)[[9]](#ref9)</sup>
