# Core-Through Urethrotomy

**Core-through urethrotomy (CTU)** is an endoscopic technique used to re-establish urethral continuity in completely obliterative urethral strictures, typically post-traumatic posterior (bulbomembranous) strictures, where the lumen is entirely occluded and a standard direct vision internal urethrotomy (DVIU) cannot be performed because no lumen exists to incise. First described by Gupta and Gill in 1986, the technique resects or vaporizes the fibrotic tissue between the two blind-ending urethral segments, guided by a metal sound passed through a suprapubic cystostomy tract.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup> Reported success ranges from 58% to 87% in the series below, depending on patient selection, technique and definition of success. Open anastomotic urethroplasty is more successful (95% perineal and 97% transpubic in Koraitim's series), and Koraitim regarded it as the gold standard for posterior urethral distraction defects.<sup>[[2]](#ref2)[[3]](#ref3)[[4]](#ref4)[[7]](#ref7)</sup>


**Current role:** this page describes a historical technique. For complete posterior obliteration, current guidelines favor reconstruction and advise against endoluminal cut-to-the-light treatment because durable patency is poor and false passage or rectal injury may result.<sup>[[16]](#ref16)</sup> The AUA Expert Opinion is to perform delayed urethroplasty instead of delayed endoscopic procedures after obstruction or obliteration from pelvic fracture urethral injury, and to avoid blind cut-to-the-light procedures because they are rarely successful.<sup>[[15]](#ref15)</sup>

For the open anastomotic repair, see [Pelvic Fracture Urethral Injury (PFUI)](/docs/special-populations/05a-trauma-emergencies/pfui). For standard endoscopic incision of patent strictures, see [DVIU and Urethral Dilation](/docs/surgical-techniques/04a-urethral-reconstruction/dviu). For paclitaxel-coated balloon adjuncts, see [Drug-Coated Balloon Therapy](/docs/surgical-techniques/drug-coated-balloon).

## Definition and Distinction from Standard DVIU

Standard DVIU (Sachse urethrotomy) requires a visible, narrowed but patent lumen through which the urethrotome blade can be advanced and the stricture incised under direct vision. In obliterative strictures, there is no residual lumen; the urethral segments end blindly, separated by a plug of dense fibrotic tissue. CTU addresses this by cutting or vaporizing through the fibrotic plug from the distal (retrograde) end toward the proximal end, using a suprapubic sound as a target and guide.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[3]](#ref3)</sup>

The term "core-through" reflects the concept of coring out a channel through solid scar tissue, analogous to coring an apple, which is fundamentally different from incising a narrowed but patent lumen.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup>

## Historical Patient Selection

- **Complete obliterative post-traumatic posterior urethral strictures** (bulbomembranous)
- **Stricture length ≤2–2.5 cm** on bidirectional uroradiography
- **Good alignment** between proximal and distal urethral segments (no significant lateral displacement)
- **No history of rectal injury** (relative contraindication; risk of rectourethral fistula)
- Patients who have failed initial optical urethrotomy for a passable stricture that subsequently obliterated<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup>
- Patients who are poor candidates for open urethroplasty (comorbidities, patient preference, resource-limited settings)
- An existing suprapubic cystostomy is a prerequisite<sup>[[3]](#ref3)[[4]](#ref4)[[5]](#ref5)</sup>

### Contraindications

- Stricture length &gt;2.5 cm (relative; some authors extend to 3 cm)
- **Poor alignment** between urethral segments or significant lateral / vesical displacement<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup>
- **Complex distraction defects** with fistulae, false passages, or associated rectal injury
- **Prior failed CTU** with re-obliteration (relative; consider open urethroplasty)
- Koraitim's 145-patient series emphasized that optical urethrotomy was successful only in patients with genuine strictures with a persistent opening between bulbar and prostatic areas, not in true distraction defects with complete loss of continuity, for which he considered anastomotic urethroplasty the gold standard.<sup>[[7]](#ref7)</sup>

## Historical Technique Description

### Preoperative requirements

- Suprapubic cystostomy in place (typically placed at initial injury or as a staged procedure)
- Bidirectional urethrography (simultaneous antegrade via SPT and retrograde) to assess length, alignment, and gap<sup>[[3]](#ref3)[[4]](#ref4)[[5]](#ref5)</sup>
- Flexible cystoscopy through the SPT to assess the proximal segment

### Step-by-step

**1. Positioning and setup.** Dorsal lithotomy under general or spinal anesthesia. C-arm fluoroscopy available in some series for real-time guidance.<sup>[[6]](#ref6)</sup>

**2. Suprapubic sound placement.** A **metal sound** (bougie or Van Buren) is passed through the SPT, through the bladder, and into the proximal (prostatic) urethra, advanced until it reaches the proximal end of the obliterative segment, serving as a **tactile and visual target** for the endoscopic approach from below.<sup>[[3]](#ref3)[[4]](#ref4)[[5]](#ref5)</sup>

**3. Retrograde endoscopic approach.** A resectoscope or urethrotome is introduced per urethra and advanced to the distal end of the obliterative segment, identifying the distal blind-ending lumen.

**4. Core-through incision / vaporization.** Depending on energy source:

- **Cold knife (Sachse):** the blade incises the fibrotic tissue at the 12 o'clock position (toward the symphysis pubis), cutting down toward the metal sound. The incision is deepened progressively until the sound is encountered.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[6]](#ref6)</sup>
- **Nd:YAG laser:** a 600 μm contact bare fiber at 15–25 W vaporizes the fibrotic tissue toward the metal sound. Hemostasis is good, with a penetration depth of 3–4 mm.<sup>[[3]](#ref3)[[4]](#ref4)</sup>
- **Holmium:YAG laser:** approximately 0.4 mm penetration depth; more precise tissue ablation with less collateral thermal damage than Nd:YAG.<sup>[[5]](#ref5)</sup>
- **Thulium laser:** 2-μm continuous-wave laser that provides hemostasis and tissue vaporization.<sup>[[9]](#ref9)[[10]](#ref10)</sup>

**5. Establishing continuity.** Incision or vaporization continues until the metal sound is visualized endoscopically, confirming the obliterative segment has been traversed. The channel is widened by further incision or resection. A guidewire may be passed through the new channel into the bladder.

**6. Catheterization.** 18 Fr Foley placed across the reconstructed segment and left for 4–6 weeks (significantly longer than after standard DVIU).<sup>[[3]](#ref3)[[4]](#ref4)[[5]](#ref5)[[6]](#ref6)</sup>

**7. Postoperative assessment.** VCUG at catheter removal (6 weeks); urethroscopy at 1–3 months; uroflowmetry at 3 months and periodically thereafter. Many patients require repeat DVIU or dilation 1–2 times before the stricture stabilizes.<sup>[[3]](#ref3)[[4]](#ref4)[[6]](#ref6)</sup>

## Energy Sources — Comparison

| Energy Source | Penetration Depth | Advantages | Disadvantages | References |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Cold knife (Sachse) | N/A (mechanical) | Widely available, no special equipment | Bleeding, poor visualization in dense scar, less precise | <sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup> |
| Nd:YAG laser | 3–4 mm | Hemostasis, good vaporization, outpatient | Deeper thermal injury, risk of collateral damage | <sup>[[3]](#ref3)[[4]](#ref4)</sup> |
| Holmium:YAG laser | ~0.4 mm | Precise ablation, minimal thermal damage, outpatient | Less hemostatic than Nd:YAG | <sup>[[5]](#ref5)</sup> |
| Thulium laser | ~0.25 mm | Hemostasis, continuous wave, precise | Limited data for CTU specifically | <sup>[[9]](#ref9)[[10]](#ref10)</sup> |

A meta-analysis of 9 comparative studies (659 patients) of laser versus cold-knife urethrotomy for strictures shorter than 2 cm (not CTU-specific) found a higher 12-month Qmax with laser (MD 2.13 mL/s, 95% CI 1.02–3.25), less postoperative bleeding (RR 0.28) and a lower 1-year recurrence rate (RR 0.67, 95% CI 0.46–0.98).<sup>[[11]](#ref11)</sup>

## CTU vs. Open Urethroplasty

The Cochrane review identified one randomized trial comparing urethrotomy with primary urethroplasty, in 50 men with traumatic posterior urethral stricture after pelvic fracture. The review did not give the trial's stricture-length criteria or its technique for the endoscopic arm; the results describe urethrotomy in general and not only core-through technique:<sup>[[12]](#ref12)</sup>

- In the first 6 months, men in the urethrotomy group were more likely to require further surgery (RR 3.39, 95% CI 1.62–7.07).
- At 2 years, 16/25 (64%) of men treated by urethrotomy required continued self-dilation or further surgery for recurrence versus 6/25 (24%) of men treated by primary urethroplasty.
- The review found insufficient data to perform meta-analysis or to determine effect size reliably.

Koraitim's 17-year experience with 145 posterior urethral strictures showed the same pattern in a retrospective series:<sup>[[7]](#ref7)</sup>

| Approach | Success |
|---|---|
| Perineal anastomotic urethroplasty | 95% |
| Transpubic urethroplasty | 97% |
| Optical urethrotomy | 58% (genuine strictures only) |
| Urethroscrotal inlay | 43% |

Koraitim specifically cautioned that "repeated urethrotomy of a long fibrous segment between a widely distracted prostatic and bulbar urethra would not only have a poor result but, by jeopardizing the elasticity of the anterior urethra, it also may undermine the chance for subsequent anastomotic urethroplasty."<sup>[[7]](#ref7)</sup>

## Complications

- **Stricture recurrence.** This is the most common complication. Goel reported recurrence in 69% at 3 months and 25% at 12 months after the initial procedure; many strictures stabilize after one or two repeat dilations or urethrotomies.<sup>[[6]](#ref6)</sup>
- **Urinary incontinence.** Stress incontinence occurred in 2/10 (transient) in Gupta and Gill and in 4 of the 46 men voiding satisfactorily in el-Abd. It was not reported in the Nd:YAG, holmium or Goel series.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[2]](#ref2)[[3]](#ref3)[[5]](#ref5)[[6]](#ref6)</sup>
- **Erectile dysfunction** is generally not caused by the procedure itself, though ED from the original pelvic fracture is common.<sup>[[3]](#ref3)[[4]](#ref4)[[5]](#ref5)</sup>
- **Bleeding and hematuria** are minimal with laser and more common with cold knife.<sup>[[3]](#ref3)[[4]](#ref4)[[11]](#ref11)</sup>
- **False passage** is a risk when cutting through dense scar without adequate guidance.
- **Rectal injury** is rare but serious; the 12 o'clock incision direction (toward the symphysis) is chosen specifically to avoid the rectum posteriorly.<sup>[[3]](#ref3)</sup>
- **Extravasation** occurred in 1/29 in the Dogra Ho:YAG series and resolved conservatively.<sup>[[5]](#ref5)</sup>
- **Re-obliteration** occurred in 2/65 in the largest Nd:YAG series.<sup>[[4]](#ref4)</sup>

## The "Cut to the Light" Variant

A related technique uses a combined antegrade-retrograde approach. A cystoscope is passed through the SPT into the proximal urethra, and its light is the target for the retrograde endoscopic incision instead of a metal sound. It has been described as giving better directional guidance when alignment between segments is suboptimal. The AUA advises avoiding blind cut-to-the-light procedures because they are rarely successful.<sup>[[15]](#ref15)</sup>

## Role in Contemporary Practice

The AUA Urotrauma Guideline (2020) states that clinicians should perform percutaneous or open suprapubic tube placement as the preferred initial management for most pelvic fracture urethral injuries (Recommendation; Grade C). Primary realignment is an option (Grade C) in hemodynamically stable patients, but most men eventually need repeated instrumentation or formal urethroplasty.<sup>[[14]](#ref14)</sup> In one Italian PFUI cohort, laser urethrotomy was among the most-used techniques (21.1% of repairs).<sup>[[13]](#ref13)</sup>

The AUA Urethral Stricture Disease Guideline (2023) states that strictures previously treated with dilation or DVIU are unlikely to be successfully treated with another endoscopic procedure, with failure rates &gt;80%.<sup>[[15]](#ref15)</sup>

The historical series do not establish a modern indication for CTU in complete obliteration. Patients unsuitable for reconstruction require individualized drainage and specialist review; results of early realignment cannot be used to claim that cutting through a mature distraction defect is harmless.<sup>[[14]](#ref14)[[15]](#ref15)[[16]](#ref16)</sup>

## Critical Distinction — Genuine Stricture vs. Distraction Defect

Koraitim's preoperative decision-making framework is used for patient selection:<sup>[[8]](#ref8)</sup>

- **Genuine stricture** (narrowing with some residual continuity): may be considered for standard endoscopic treatment in selected short lesions; this is not CTU through an obliteration.
- **Distraction defect** (complete loss of urethral continuity with a gap filled by fibrosis): requires anastomotic urethroplasty; CTU has poor results in this setting.
- Defects with significant lateral or cephalad bladder displacement, or gaps that cannot be bridged tension-free, should bypass CTU and proceed directly to perineal or transpubic anastomotic urethroplasty.<sup>[[7]](#ref7)[[8]](#ref8)</sup>

---

## Key Takeaways

Core-through urethrotomy documents an earlier attempt to manage obliterated posterior strictures endoscopically. The current distinction is between a selected nonobliterative stenosis that may permit endoscopic treatment and complete loss of continuity requiring reconstructive assessment. Historical success ranges should not be presented as a recommendation to perform CTU.<sup>[[16]](#ref16)</sup>
