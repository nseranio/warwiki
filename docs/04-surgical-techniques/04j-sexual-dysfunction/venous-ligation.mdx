# Penile Venous Ligation Surgery

**Penile venous ligation surgery** treats corporeal veno-occlusive dysfunction (CVOD), also called venous leak, in which inadequate trapping of blood within the corpora cavernosa prevents the achievement or maintenance of erection. The classic operation resects the deep dorsal vein and ligates pathologic drainage pathways. The AUA 2018 guideline does not recommend penile venous surgery for ED (Moderate Recommendation; Grade C), citing inconsistent long-term outcomes, but recent combined open-endovascular approaches and standalone embolization have renewed interest in highly selected patients.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[2]](#ref2)</sup>

The AUA 2018 guideline states: "For men with ED, penile venous surgery is not recommended (Moderate Recommendation; Evidence Level: Grade C)." The guideline adds that venous ligation is unlikely to result in long-term success for the overwhelming majority of men and delays treatment with more reliable options such as penile prosthesis surgery.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup>

Ligation may have a role in carefully selected young men with primary venous leak, normal arterial inflow and no significant comorbidities.<sup>[[5]](#ref5)</sup> Prospective series describe combined ligation plus embolization in this group, and standalone embolization has also been reported. Neither has randomized comparison data.<sup>[[3]](#ref3)[[4]](#ref4)[[15]](#ref15)</sup>

---

## Pathophysiology and rationale

Normal erection requires arterial inflow, sinusoidal smooth-muscle relaxation, and compression of subtunical venules against the tunica albuginea (the veno-occlusive mechanism). In CVOD, blood drains prematurely through incompetent veins, most commonly the deep dorsal vein, cavernous veins, and crural veins. Surgery aims to eliminate these pathologic drainage pathways.<sup>[[6]](#ref6)[[2]](#ref2)</sup>

**Conceptual caveat.** Contemporary understanding suggests that CVOD is often an effect rather than a primary cause of ED, secondary to smooth-muscle degeneration, fibrosis, or arterial insufficiency. This may partly explain the variable surgical outcomes.<sup>[[2]](#ref2)</sup>

## Preoperative diagnosis and patient selection

Diagnosis of venous leak requires hemodynamic confirmation:

| Test | Finding |
|---|---|
| **Pharmacologically challenged penile duplex sonography (PC-PDS)** | Elevated end-diastolic velocity (EDV &gt; 5 cm/s) in cavernosal arteries with normal peak systolic velocity (PSV &gt; 30 cm/s)<sup>[[7]](#ref7)</sup> |
| **Dynamic infusion cavernosometry / cavernosography (DICC)** | Maintenance flow rates and identification of leak sites<sup>[[5]](#ref5)[[9]](#ref9)</sup> |
| **CT cavernosography** | Anatomic mapping of leak sites, used in contemporary series<sup>[[3]](#ref3)[[4]](#ref4)</sup> |

Favorable patient-selection criteria<sup>[[9]](#ref9)[[10]](#ref10)</sup>:

- Young age and primary (often congenital) venous leak.
- Normal arterial inflow on PC-PDS and selective internal pudendal arteriography (SIPA).
- Mild-to-moderate leak on cavernosometry.
- Normal cavernous electromyography (SPACE). Abnormal results predict poor outcomes (full spontaneous erections in 28 of 41 men with normal single-potential analysis of cavernous electrical activity, SPACE, versus 3 of 36 with abnormal results).<sup>[[10]](#ref10)</sup>
- No significant atherosclerosis, diabetes, or smoking.
- Smooth-muscle content preserved on biopsy where assessed.

## Surgical technique

The classic open procedure<sup>[[9]](#ref9)[[5]](#ref5)[[11]](#ref11)</sup>:

| Step | Detail |
|---|---|
| **1. Incision** | Infrapubic or inguinoscrotal approach. |
| **2. Resection of the deep dorsal vein** | From the coronal sulcus to the pubic arch. Resection gave slightly better results than simple ligation alone in one series.<sup>[[11]](#ref11)</sup> |
| **3. Ligation of cavernous veins** | At the penile hilum. |
| **4. Crural ligation** | Banding of the crura proximal to the entrance of the cavernous artery using umbilical tapes (in more extensive procedures, e.g. Cayan 2008). |
| **5. Preservation** | Preserve dorsal arteries and dorsal nerves bilaterally. |

## Outcomes — high initial response, low durability

Results are highly variable and depend heavily on patient selection, technique, and follow-up duration.

| Series | n | Follow-up | Success | Notes |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Wespes 1985**<sup>[[6]](#ref6)</sup> | 20 | Not stated | 16/20 (80%) improved enough for satisfactory intercourse | The 4 failures had serious arterial lesions |
| **Wespes 1992**<sup>[[11]](#ref11)</sup> | 67 | Long-term | 31/67 (46%) potent | 7 relapses from deeper vein leakage; 11 failures from arterial disease |
| **Freedman 1993**<sup>[[12]](#ref12)</sup> | 46 | &gt; 12 mo | 11/46 (24%) sustained potency | 74% initial improvement with high relapse; sustained function in 43% of distal leaks and 16% of proximal leaks |
| **Hwang 1994**<sup>[[13]](#ref13)</sup> | 35 (30 followed) | 27.5 mo (mean) | 12/30 (40%) sustained spontaneous erection | 93% initial success; inadequate elimination of leaking veins, especially crural veins, was the likely cause of recurrence |
| **Stief 1994**<sup>[[10]](#ref10)</sup> | 77 | 21 mo (mean) | 31/77 (40%) full spontaneous erections at 6 mo | Normal SPACE predicted success (28/41 vs 3/36 with full erections) |
| **Kim & McVary 1995**<sup>[[14]](#ref14)</sup> | 15 | 29 mo (mean) | 60% potent | Shorter ED duration predicted success; 40% penile contracture |
| **Cayan 2008**<sup>[[9]](#ref9)</sup> | 26 | ≥ 12 mo | 42% complete + 31% partial | IIEF 6.7 → 16.3; 88% satisfaction with or without PDE5i |
| **Allaire 2021**<sup>[[3]](#ref3)</sup> (combined ligation + embolization) | 45 | 14 mo | 73.3% primary and 82.2% secondary success | Prospective; combined open and endovascular. Primary success was EHS ≥ 3 with no vascular reoperation or prosthesis; secondary success was penetrative intercourse |
| **Allaire 2025**<sup>[[4]](#ref4)</sup> (combined) | 171 | 23 mo (mean) | 80.7% erection improvement | Prospective consecutive series; 14% required vascular reoperation |

**Pattern.** Initial improvement is reported in 74–93% of men, but sustained potency at long-term follow-up is 24–46% in most older series.<sup>[[12]](#ref12)[[13]](#ref13)[[11]](#ref11)</sup> The two Allaire series of combined open-endovascular surgery report 73.3% primary success at a mean 14 months and 80.7% improvement at a mean 23 months. Both come from one group, follow-up is shorter than in the older series, and there are no randomized data.<sup>[[3]](#ref3)[[4]](#ref4)</sup>

## Complications

| Complication | Rate | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| **Penile shortening** | 3/30 (Hwang) to 20/46 (Freedman); 10–43% | Counsel explicitly<sup>[[12]](#ref12)[[13]](#ref13)</sup> |
| **Penile hypoesthesia / glans numbness** | 6–20% | 4/67 (Wespes 1992), 4/30 (Hwang), 9/46 (Freedman)<sup>[[11]](#ref11)[[12]](#ref12)[[13]](#ref13)</sup> |
| **Penile deviation / curvature** | 3/30 (Hwang) | <sup>[[13]](#ref13)</sup> |
| **Wound infection** | 1/30 (Hwang) | <sup>[[13]](#ref13)</sup> |
| **Recurrence of ED** | Sustained potency 24–46% in older series | Attributed to collateral venous drainage or incomplete elimination of leak sites, especially crural veins. Cavernosography after failed stripping has shown a persistent cavernosal vein.<sup>[[13]](#ref13)[[12]](#ref12)[[8]](#ref8)</sup> |
| **Penile contracture** | 40% (Kim & McVary 1995) | <sup>[[14]](#ref14)</sup> |

## Emerging — venous-leak embolization

Endovascular embolization of venous-leak sites has emerged as a less invasive alternative or adjunct:

- **Hoppe 2026 multicenter registry** (n = 175): 82/138 (59.4%) achieved an IIEF-15 improvement of at least 4 points at a mean 9.2 months. Venous leak recurred or persisted in 10.2% at a mean 16 months. Non-target pulmonary embolism occurred in 3/175 (1.7%).<sup>[[15]](#ref15)</sup>
- **Allaire 2021 and 2025** combined open ligation plus embolization in a single procedure (n = 45 and n = 171): 82.2% secondary success in the first and 80.7% erection improvement in the second.<sup>[[3]](#ref3)[[4]](#ref4)</sup>
- **PiVET-ED trial.** The published protocol describes a prospective, randomized, single-blinded, sham-controlled study of pelvic vein embolization for venogenic ED (planned n = 80), designed to provide randomized evidence.<sup>[[16]](#ref16)</sup>

| Body | Position |
|---|---|
| **AUA 2018**<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup> | Not recommended (Moderate Recommendation; Grade C). Unlikely to result in long-term success; delays prosthesis treatment |
| **EAU 2025**<sup>[[17]](#ref17)</sup> | Venous ligation surgery for veno-occlusive dysfunction is "no longer recommended because of poor long-term results" (guideline text, without a strength rating) |

## When to refer / when not to operate

- **Select cautiously.** A young man, primary / congenital venous leak, normal arterial inflow on PC-PDS and SIPA, normal SPACE, mild-to-moderate leak on DICC, no atherosclerosis, no diabetes, a non-smoker, smooth-muscle content preserved, and a patient who is informed about the AUA and EAU non-recommendations and the high recurrence rate.
- **Do not operate** in arterial-component ED, atherosclerosis, abnormal SPACE, smoking, diabetes, prior failed medical therapy where a penile prosthesis would be a more reliable next step.
- **Embolization.** Embolization or combined ligation plus embolization has been reported from experienced centers.<sup>[[3]](#ref3)[[4]](#ref4)[[15]](#ref15)</sup>

---

## Postoperative management

- **Activity restriction.** Avoid intercourse, masturbation, cycling, and strenuous exercise for 4–6 weeks.
- **Postoperative duplex Doppler** at approximately 6 weeks to confirm absence of recurrent leak.
- **PDE5i bridging** is commonly used postoperatively to support tumescence-driven flow.
- **PRO assessment.** IIEF / IIEF-5 at baseline, 3 mo, 6 mo, 12 mo, with annual follow-up given known late attrition.
- **Counseling.** Patients must be told preoperatively about the 10–43% reported penile shortening rate and the high long-term recurrence rate; document the AUA 2018 non-recommendation.
