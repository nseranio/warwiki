# Priapism Shunts & Decompression

This page is the operative atlas companion for refractory [ischemic priapism](/docs/special-populations/05a-trauma-emergencies/priapism). It covers distal corporoglanular shunts, tunneling maneuvers, penoscrotal decompression (PSD), historical proximal shunts, and the transition point to early penile prosthesis. For diagnosis, corporal blood gas interpretation, phenylephrine dosing, sickle-cell considerations, and non-ischemic priapism, use the emergency-condition page: [Priapism](/docs/special-populations/05a-trauma-emergencies/priapism).

The organizing principle is simple: ischemic priapism is a corporal compartment syndrome. Medical therapy evacuates and pharmacologically contracts the corpora; shunt surgery creates outflow when aspiration, irrigation or phenylephrine fails. Current practice favors distal corporoglanular shunts with tunneling, and glans-sparing decompression is an emerging alternative. Proximal shunts are mostly historical: the combined AUA/SMSNA 2022 guideline states that evidence is inadequate to quantify their benefit after distal shunting (statement 12; Moderate Recommendation, Grade C), and the EAU systematic review concluded that they should be abandoned.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[2]](#ref2)[[3]](#ref3)[[53]](#ref53)</sup>

## Algorithmic Placement

| Clinical setting | Preferred operative move | Avoid |
|---|---|---|
| **Persistent ischemic priapism after aspiration / irrigation / phenylephrine** | Distal corporoglanular shunt, usually T-shunt or Al-Ghorab depending on setting and surgeon experience | Repeating ineffective bedside aspiration for hours while ischemic time accumulates |
| **Prolonged priapism with thick clot or failed simple distal shunt** | Add corporal tunneling (Burnett snake maneuver) or proceed to PSD | Tiny needle shunts that cannot clear organized proximal clot |
| **Failed distal shunt / failed tunneling** | PSD or early penile prosthesis, based on duration, corporal viability, patient goals, and local expertise | Proximal shunt as reflex salvage |
| **Very late presentation, nonviable corpora, or failed decompression** | Acute malleable penile prosthesis or planned early prosthesis strategy | Delayed implantation without counseling about fibrosis and length loss |

For ischemic priapism lasting at least 36 hours, non-surgical management is associated with a high retreatment burden. In a 2026 retrospective single-center cohort of 76 patients (38 non-surgical, 38 surgical), reported readmission or reintervention rates were 66.7% with non-surgical care versus 10.4% with surgical care (p &lt; 0.01).<sup>[[4]](#ref4)</sup>

**Predicting who will need a shunt.** Two retrospective cohorts identify factors associated with progression to surgical shunting after failed medical therapy. In a series of 169 ischemic-priapism encounters, longer duration of priapism (odds ratio 1.05 per hour) and a history of prior priapism (odds ratio 3.15) independently predicted the need for a shunt; a 24-hour duration cutoff had 77% sensitivity and 90% specificity for eventual shunting.<sup>[[50]](#ref50)</sup> In a separate cohort of 147 patients presenting with a first episode of ischemic priapism, a total phenylephrine dose above 950 mcg or an erection duration beyond 15.5 hours were each associated with a significantly higher likelihood of requiring corporoglanular shunting.<sup>[[51]](#ref51)</sup> These thresholds can help set expectations for escalation to the operating room rather than repeating ineffective bedside aspiration and phenylephrine.

## Distal Corporoglanular Shunts

Distal shunts create a communication between the distal corpus cavernosum and the glans / corpus spongiosum. They are first-line surgical procedures because they are fast, anatomically accessible, and can often be performed under local anesthesia with dorsal penile nerve block plus ring block.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[5]](#ref5)</sup> AUA/SMSNA 2022 recommends a distal corporoglanular shunt, with or without tunneling, after intracavernosal phenylephrine and aspiration, with or without irrigation, fail (statement 10; Moderate Recommendation, Grade C). Consider tunneling for persistent ischemia after a distal shunt (statement 11; Moderate Recommendation, Grade C).<sup>[[3]](#ref3)[[53]](#ref53)</sup>

### Winter Shunt

| Feature | Detail |
|---|---|
| **Mechanism** | Percutaneous needle core through the glans into the distal corpus cavernosum, removing a core of tunica albuginea to create a small corporo-spongiosal communication |
| **Instrument** | Tru-Cut or large-bore core biopsy needle |
| **Shunt window** | Small (circular core) |
| **Strength** | Simplest bedside shunt under local anaesthesia; minimal equipment |
| **Weakness** | Small opening; prone to closure; highest failure rate among distal shunts in the Nixon series, where 92% of reoperated patients had initially undergone a Winter shunt<sup>[[48]](#ref48)</sup> |
| **Best use** | Early refractory ischemic priapism (< 24 h) when a simple distal decompression is reasonable |

Winter is the least invasive distal shunt but also the easiest to occlude.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[5]](#ref5)</sup> Multiple passes may be made bilaterally to increase the shunt size. The small window may be insufficient for adequate drainage in prolonged priapism with thick, gelatinous, or organised clot; the Burnett "Snake" maneuver cannot be combined with the Winter shunt because the window is too small to accept a Hegar dilator.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[8]](#ref8)</sup>

**Efficacy.** In the Nixon retrospective series (n = 28), 13 patients (46.4%) required more than one operation for failed detumescence after cavernosa-to-spongiosum shunts, and 12 of the 13 (92.3%) had initially undergone a Winter shunt. Reoperation was uncommon after Al-Ghorab or Quackels shunts in the same series.<sup>[[48]](#ref48)</sup> Brant and colleagues reported a 13-patient T-shunt series.<sup>[[6]](#ref6)</sup> Across studies in the EAU systematic review, distal-shunt resolution ranged from 18.7–100% and potency from 20–100%, with heterogeneous priapism durations.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup>

### Ebbehoj Shunt

| Feature | Detail |
|---|---|
| **Mechanism** | Percutaneous scalpel stab through the glans into the distal corpus cavernosum; blade rotated or withdrawn to create a linear incision (rather than a core biopsy) |
| **Instrument** | No. 11 scalpel blade |
| **Shunt window** | Moderate (linear incision) — slightly larger than Winter |
| **Strength** | Slightly larger opening than Winter while remaining simple; no tissue removal |
| **Weakness** | Still a small distal opening; urethral injury risk if directed too ventrally; not easily combined with the Snake maneuver (window too small for dilator passage) |
| **Best use** | Early refractory cases where a percutaneous blade shunt is preferred over needle coring |

First described by Ebbehoj 1974 as a modification of the Winter shunt that uses a scalpel blade rather than a biopsy needle to create the corporo-spongiosal fistula.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup> Conceptually similar to Winter but creates a larger tunical defect; the blade should be directed into the distal corporal tip and kept away from the ventral urethra.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[5]](#ref5)</sup>

**Efficacy.** Most outcome studies group the Ebbehoj with other distal shunts; individual efficacy data are limited. Its window is intermediate in size between the Winter and Al-Ghorab shunts; comparative efficacy data are lacking. A persistent glans-cavernosum fistula can itself cause ED from venous leak; restoration of full erections after closure of a persistent fistula 5 years after shunting has been described in a single case.<sup>[[18]](#ref18)</sup>

### T-Shunt (Lue)

| Feature | Detail |
|---|---|
| **Mechanism** | Scalpel inserted percutaneously through the lateral glans (2 o'clock or 10 o'clock, avoiding the urethra ventrally and the dorsal NVB), advanced into the distal corpus cavernosum; rotated 90° to create a cruciate (T-shaped) opening in the tunica albuginea |
| **Instrument** | No. 10 scalpel blade |
| **Shunt window** | Large (cruciate) — significantly larger than Winter or Ebbehoj |
| **Strength** | Wider, more durable distal opening; compatible with the Burnett Snake maneuver (7–8 mm Hegar dilator); allows simultaneous corporal biopsy for smooth-muscle viability assessment; bedside under local anaesthesia |
| **Weakness** | More tissue disruption; urethral injury risk if trajectory is wrong (mitigated by lateral glans entry + Foley catheter); urethrocutaneous-fistula risk with Snake maneuver<sup>[[10]](#ref10)</sup> |
| **Best use** | Commonly used percutaneous distal shunt for refractory cases, especially when tunneling will be needed |

The T-shunt was designed to create a larger and more durable distal corporal exit than Winter or Ebbehoj. In the Brant / Lue 2009 original retrospective series (n = 13), cavernous blood flow was restored in 12 of 13 (92%) and one further patient needed a second procedure. All but 2 recovered erectile function, and 6 patients had previously undergone unsuccessful distal or proximal shunt procedures.<sup>[[6]](#ref6)</sup>

**Step-by-step (Brant–Lue):**<sup>[[6]](#ref6)</sup>

1. Local anaesthesia (dorsal penile-nerve block and ring block) suitable for bedside or ED use.
2. Foley catheter placed to identify the urethra and prevent urethral injury.
3. **No. 10 blade** inserted through lateral glans at 2 o'clock or 10 o'clock, advanced into distal corpus cavernosum, penetrating the tunica albuginea.
4. **Blade rotated 90°** within the corpus to create the cruciate opening.
5. Dark deoxygenated blood expressed; manual compression facilitates drainage.
6. Performed bilaterally.
7. **Optional tunneling through the T-shunt.** A 20–22 Fr straight urethral sound or dilator is passed through the T-shunt opening toward the crus to clear stagnant corporal blood.<sup>[[6]](#ref6)[[55]](#ref55)</sup> The 7–8 mm Hegar dilator is described for the Al-Ghorab Snake technique.<sup>[[8]](#ref8)[[53]](#ref53)[[55]](#ref55)</sup>
8. **Optional corporal biopsy.** Necrotic smooth muscle on biopsy is highly predictive of severe ED and shunt failure and may guide early prosthesis decisions.<sup>[[7]](#ref7)</sup>
9. Closure with absorbable sutures; compressive dressing.

**Efficacy:**

- **Brant / Lue 2009.** Blood flow restored in 12 of 13 patients.<sup>[[6]](#ref6)</sup>
- **Zacharakis 2014 T-shunt and Snake (n = 45).** Resolution occurred in all patients with priapism &lt; 24 h and in 30% of those with priapism &gt; 48 h. Median 6-month IIEF-5 fell from 24 to 7.7. All patients with priapism &gt; 48 h had necrotic cavernous smooth muscle on biopsy and severe ED requiring prosthesis.<sup>[[7]](#ref7)</sup>
- **Ortaç 2019 T-shunt ± tunneling (n = 25).** All had rapid resolution (median duration 58 h); among 19 with erectile-function follow-up, 16 (84.2%) reported postoperative ED, 3 (15.8%) achieved unaided intercourse, 6 (31.5%) required PDE5i, and 9 (47.4%) had severe ED.<sup>[[19]](#ref19)</sup>
- **Unal / Burnett 2024 Snake versus no Snake (n = 82, retrospective).** Single-intervention resolution was 24/26 (92.3%) with Snake versus 30/56 (53.6%) without (p &lt; 0.001); among initial responders, recurrence was 1/24 (4.2%) versus 8/30 (26.6%).<sup>[[10]](#ref10)</sup>

**Outcomes by priapism duration (T-shunt ± Snake):**

| Duration | Resolution | Erectile function | Smooth-muscle biopsy |
|---|---|---|---|
| **&lt; 24 h** | 100% resolution | ED in about 50%; mild to moderate cases treated with PDE5i | Not stated<sup>[[7]](#ref7)</sup> |
| 24–48 h | Not reported separately | Not reported separately | Not reported separately |
| **&gt; 48 h** | 30% resolution | Severe ED in all, requiring prosthesis | Necrotic smooth muscle in all<sup>[[7]](#ref7)</sup> |

Source: Zacharakis 2014.<sup>[[7]](#ref7)</sup>

**T-shunt and proximal shunts.** Brant and Lue suggested that the T-shunt with tunneling could make proximal shunts obsolete.<sup>[[6]](#ref6)</sup>

### Al-Ghorab Shunt

First described by Al-Ghorab in 1973. Among the distal shunts, the Al-Ghorab produces the largest tunical window because the tunica albuginea is excised under direct vision rather than punctured or incised. It was the original platform on which Burnett later developed the Snake maneuver.<sup>[[5]](#ref5)[[8]](#ref8)[[9]](#ref9)</sup>

| Feature | Detail |
|---|---|
| **Approach** | Open (not percutaneous) |
| **Instrument** | Scalpel + scissors / sharp dissection — excises a disc of tunica under direct vision |
| **Shunt window** | Largest (excised ~ 5 × 5 mm disc of distal tunica) |
| **Visualization** | Direct — glans tissue dissected to expose distal tunical tips |
| **Snake compatible** | Yes — originally described here |
| **Anesthesia** | Local (dorsal-nerve + ring block), regional, or general |
| **Reoperation rate** | Uncommon in the Nixon series |
| **Best use** | Failed percutaneous shunt, prolonged clot burden, or primary distal procedure when open access is preferred |

**Step-by-step (Al-Ghorab):**<sup>[[5]](#ref5)[[9]](#ref9)</sup>

1. Anaesthesia: local (dorsal-nerve and ring block), regional, or general.
2. **Transverse or dorsal glans incision** (circumcoronal or dorsal-glans approach) exposing the distal corpora cavernosa.
3. Dissect glans tissue to expose the distal tips of the tunica albuginea bilaterally.
4. Excise a disc or cone of tunica albuginea (about 5 × 5 mm) from each corporal tip under direct vision.
5. Express dark deoxygenated blood; manual shaft compression facilitates drainage.
6. **Optional Snake maneuver.** 7–8 mm Hegar dilator passed retrograde through the excised window to the crus, bilaterally.<sup>[[8]](#ref8)[[9]](#ref9)[[53]](#ref53)</sup>
7. **Blunt cavernosotomy salvage** (Shiraishi–Matsuyama). For very prolonged (5–7 d) priapism refractory to T-shunt and Snake, Pean forceps may be used through the Al-Ghorab window to bluntly excise necrotic or fibrotic cavernosal tissue, enlarging the drainage route.<sup>[[20]](#ref20)</sup>
8. Optional corporal biopsy: necrotic smooth muscle predicts severe ED and may justify early prosthesis.<sup>[[7]](#ref7)</sup>
9. Close glans incision with absorbable sutures; compressive dressing.

**Efficacy:**

- **Nixon 2003** (n = 28). Across cavernosa-to-spongiosum shunts 46.4% required reoperation, and 92.3% of those had initially undergone a Winter shunt, while reoperation after Al-Ghorab or Quackels was uncommon. As many as 90% of patients with follow-up had ED; only 2 of 20 (10%) preserved pre-morbid erectile function.<sup>[[48]](#ref48)</sup>
- **Burnett / Pierorazio 2009** (original Snake-with-Al-Ghorab, n = 3). 2-, 5-, and 6-day priapism refractory to all prior management; 3/3 resolved.<sup>[[9]](#ref9)</sup>
- **Segal / Burnett 2013** (retrospective, n = 10, mean follow-up 6.7 months). Mean duration 75 h (24–288 h); 6/10 had failed prior surgery; 8 of 10 resolved with no recurrence; 2 with recurrent priapism were treated with IPP; 2 of the 6 men with normal preoperative EF had at least partial postoperative EF.<sup>[[8]](#ref8)</sup>
- Shiraishi / Matsuyama 2013 salvage (n = 2, 5- and 7-day priapism refractory to T-shunt and Snake): Al-Ghorab and blunt cavernosotomy with Pean forceps achieved immediate resolution in both; pathology confirmed fibrotic or necrotic corporal tissue; one patient achieved partial erection on sildenafil.<sup>[[20]](#ref20)</sup>
- Unal / Burnett 2024 distal shunt ± Snake (n = 82): single-intervention resolution 24/26 (92.3%) with Snake versus 30/56 (53.6%) without (p &lt; 0.001); among initial responders, recurrence was 1/24 (4.2%) versus 8/30 (26.6%).<sup>[[10]](#ref10)</sup>

**Distal-shunt comparison:**

| Feature | Winter | Ebbehoj | T-Shunt | Al-Ghorab |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Approach | Percutaneous | Percutaneous | Percutaneous | Open |
| Instrument | Tru-Cut needle | No. 11 blade | No. 10 blade + 90° rotation | Scalpel + excision under direct vision |
| Window | Small (core) | Moderate (linear) | Large (cruciate) | Largest (excised disc) |
| Visualization | Blind | Blind | Blind | Direct |
| Snake compatible | No | No | Yes | Yes (originally described) |
| Reoperation (Nixon series) | Most reoperations followed Winter | Not reported | Not reported | Uncommon |

## Burnett Snake Maneuver

The Burnett "Snake" maneuver is a corporal tunneling adjunct to distal shunt surgery for ischemic priapism. [Burnett](/docs/roots/surgeons/a-g/arthur-burnett) and Pierorazio described it at Johns Hopkins in 2009 as a modification of the Al-Ghorab shunt, and it was later extended to the T-shunt.<sup>[[9]](#ref9)</sup> It is used with a shunt and not alone. It addresses a limitation of a distal window: opening the corporal tip does not clear the clotted or sludged blood filling the full length of the corporal body. A 7–8 mm Hegar dilator passed retrograde from window to crus mechanically disrupts that column and restores a patent intracorporal lumen.<sup>[[8]](#ref8)[[9]](#ref9)</sup>

**Step-by-step (Burnett–Pierorazio):**<sup>[[9]](#ref9)</sup>

1. **Prerequisite: adequate distal shunt window.** Originally described with the Al-Ghorab; now also performed through the T-shunt. Winter and Ebbehoj windows are too small to accept a Hegar dilator.<sup>[[5]](#ref5)[[9]](#ref9)</sup>
2. Anaesthesia per the underlying shunt (local and dorsal-nerve and ring block at the bedside, or regional or general anesthesia in the OR).
3. 7–8 mm Hegar cervical dilator (smooth, blunt, graduated metal) inserted retrograde through the distal tunical window into the corporal tip.
4. Dilator advanced proximally through the full length of the corpus cavernosum to the crus at the ischiopubic ramus; the serpentine advancement is the eponymous "snake."
5. Procedure performed bilaterally.
6. Dark deoxygenated, clotted blood expressed; manual shaft compression assists drainage.
7. Confirm patency: the dilator should pass smoothly along the full corporal length; postoperative penile Doppler may be used to confirm restored cavernosal flow.
8. Optional corporal biopsy: necrotic smooth muscle predicts severe ED and may guide early-prosthesis decisions.<sup>[[7]](#ref7)</sup>
9. Close the shunt site (glans incision or stab wounds) with absorbable suture; compressive dressing.

**Shunt-compatibility matrix:**

| Shunt | Snake-compatible? | Rationale |
|---|---|---|
| **Winter** (Tru-Cut needle) | No | Core too small for Hegar dilator |
| **Ebbehoj** (No. 11 blade) | No | Linear stab still too narrow |
| **T-Shunt** (No. 10 + 90° rotation) | Yes | Cruciate window accommodates dilator |
| **Al-Ghorab** (open excision) | Yes — original platform | Largest window; ideal dilator passage |

**Efficacy across the Snake literature:**

| Series | n | Setting | Resolution | Erectile function / note |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Burnett / Pierorazio 2009** (Al-Ghorab + Snake) | 3 | 2-, 5-, 6-day refractory priapism after failed prior shunting | 3/3 (100%) | Meaningful EF recovery assessable in 1<sup>[[9]](#ref9)</sup> |
| **Segal / Burnett 2013** (Al-Ghorab + Snake) | 10 | Mean duration 75 h (24–288 h); 6/10 prior failed surgery | 8/10 (80%) with no recurrence; 2 with recurrence treated with IPP | 2/6 with normal preop EF achieved at least partial postop EF<sup>[[8]](#ref8)</sup> |
| **Zacharakis 2014** (T-shunt + Snake) | 45 | Refractory ischemic priapism | 100% if &lt; 24 h; 30% if &gt; 48 h | IIEF-5 24 to 7.7 (p &lt; 0.0005); all &gt; 48 h had necrotic smooth muscle on biopsy and required prosthesis<sup>[[7]](#ref7)</sup> |
| **Ortaç 2019** (T-shunt ± tunneling) | 25 | Median duration 58 h | Rapid resolution in all (1 recurrence at 12 h) | ED in 16 of 19 with follow-up (84.2%); 15.8% unaided intercourse; 31.5% PDE5i; 47.4% severe ED<sup>[[19]](#ref19)</sup> |
| **Unal / Burnett 2024** (distal shunt ± Snake) | 82 (26 Snake vs 56 no Snake) | Comparative Johns Hopkins cohort | Single-intervention resolution 24/26 (92.3%) vs 30/56 (53.6%) (p &lt; 0.001) | Among initial responders, recurrence 1/24 (4.2%) vs 8/30 (26.6%) (p &lt; 0.001); among those with documented sexual-function follow-up, functional erections 6/14 (42.8%) vs 13/26 (50%; p = .66)<sup>[[10]](#ref10)</sup> |

In this retrospective single-center comparison, the Snake maneuver was associated with higher single-intervention resolution (24/26 [92.3%] vs 30/56 [53.6%]) and lower recurrence among initial responders (1/24 [4.2%] vs 8/30 [26.6%]). Among patients with documented sexual-function follow-up, functional erections did not differ significantly (6/14 [42.8%] with Snake vs 13/26 [50%] without; p = .66).<sup>[[10]](#ref10)</sup>

**Complications** (Segal / Burnett series, n = 10):<sup>[[8]](#ref8)</sup>

- Wound infection with skin necrosis: 2/10 (one also had urethral injury and fistula)<sup>[[8]](#ref8)</sup>
- Intraoperative urethral injury with subsequent urethrocutaneous fistula: 1/10<sup>[[8]](#ref8)</sup>
- Corporal perforation: theoretical (not reported in published series)
- Penile shortening or deformity: prolonged ischemia and surgical treatment may both contribute; the guideline cannot separate their effects reliably.<sup>[[53]](#ref53)</sup>

**Salvage modification: Al-Ghorab and blunt cavernosotomy (Shiraishi–Matsuyama 2013).** For very prolonged priapism (5–7 d) refractory to T-shunt and Snake, Pean forceps are used through the Al-Ghorab window to bluntly excise necrotic or fibrotic corporal tissue. Both reported patients achieved immediate resolution; one recovered partial sildenafil-responsive EF.<sup>[[20]](#ref20)</sup>

**Practice pattern.** In a survey with 141 responses from members of societies for male genital surgery, 99/139 (71.2%) favored tunneling procedures as first-line surgery for prolonged ischemic priapism; 124/138 (89.9%) had performed corporoglanular tunneling, versus 86/137 (62.8%) who had performed penoscrotal decompression.<sup>[[14]](#ref14)</sup>

Pearls:

- Use blunt, controlled pressure; false passage or tunical perforation defeats the purpose.
- Tunnel only after a real distal exit has been made; forcing a dilator through a tiny puncture increases urethral- and tunical-injury risk.
- Persistent erection after shunting or tunneling requires corporal blood gas or color duplex Doppler before repeat surgery to distinguish persistent ischemia from reactive hyperemia or conversion to non-ischemic priapism (AUA/SMSNA statement 13; Moderate Recommendation, Grade C).<sup>[[53]](#ref53)</sup>

## Penoscrotal Decompression

Penoscrotal decompression (PSD) is a glans-sparing surgical strategy for prolonged or refractory ischemic priapism, first described by Fuchs, Shakir, McKibben et al. in 2018.<sup>[[11]](#ref11)</sup> Instead of communicating the corpus cavernosum with the corpus spongiosum through the glans, PSD makes direct corporotomies through a penoscrotal or proximal shaft incision, evacuating thrombosed blood from the mid-shaft and proximal corpora through a surgical field familiar to surgeons who perform penile-fracture repair or prosthesis implantation. Small retrospective series and a surgeon survey suggest that PSD may outperform corporoglanular tunneling in prolonged priapism while preserving the option of later prosthesis placement.<sup>[[11]](#ref11)[[12]](#ref12)[[13]](#ref13)[[14]](#ref14)</sup>

**Rationale.** PSD was developed to address three weaknesses of the distal-shunt and Snake paradigm:<sup>[[11]](#ref11)</sup>

- In the Zacharakis T-shunt-plus-Snake series, resolution occurred in only about 30% of patients with priapism &gt; 48 h; corporoglanular tunneling may still leave heavily thrombosed mid-shaft and proximal corpora incompletely decompressed.<sup>[[7]](#ref7)</sup>
- In Fuchs's small, nonrandomized comparison after failed distal shunts, 3/8 MPP recipients required eight revision operations over median 41.5 months; distal extrusion accounted for four and impending lateral extrusion for two of those eight revision indications.<sup>[[11]](#ref11)</sup>
- A penoscrotal or proximal-shaft approach gives direct access to the mid-shaft corpora through a familiar plane and spares the glans, preserving future prosthesis options.

**Step-by-step:**<sup>[[11]](#ref11)[[12]](#ref12)[[13]](#ref13)</sup>

1. Regional or general anaesthesia in an OR setting.
2. Penoscrotal incision (Fuchs original) at the penoscrotal junction, similar to implant exposure, or a proximal penile shaft incision (Basile 2025 modification) familiar from penile-fracture repair, which avoids the penoscrotal junction and may facilitate quicker bilateral exposure and later IPP placement.<sup>[[13]](#ref13)</sup>
3. Foley catheter; dissect through dartos to expose tunica albuginea of both corpora cavernosa; incise Buck's fascia.
4. **Bilateral longitudinal (or transverse) corporotomies** in the tunica albuginea.
5. Evacuate dark, clotted blood; the EAU-described PSD technique passes a suction tip proximally and distally through each corporotomy to clear the corporal bodies. Manual shaft compression and saline irrigation assist evacuation.<sup>[[49]](#ref49)</sup>
6. Bilateral decompression is preferred. In the Baumgarten series, priapism recurred after 2 of 10 unilateral PSDs (20%) and after none of 15 primary bilateral PSDs.<sup>[[12]](#ref12)</sup>
7. Close tunical incisions primarily with absorbable suture (3-0 PDS or Vicryl).
8. Layered wound closure; compressive dressing.

**Efficacy and outcomes:**

| Series | n | Duration | Key outcomes |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Fuchs 2018** (PSD vs MPP after failed distal shunt) | 6 PSD / 8 MPP | Median 61 h | PSD: 6/6 resolution and immediate pain relief, with no additional intervention; MPP: 3/8 patients (37.5%) underwent eight revisions over median 41.5 months, including 4/8 revision indications for distal extrusion and 2/8 for impending lateral extrusion<sup>[[11]](#ref11)</sup> |
| **Baumgarten 2020** (multi-institutional) | 25 (27 procedures) | Mean 71 h; 48% had failed prior corporoglanular shunting | Bilateral PSD 0/15 recurrence vs unilateral 2/10 (20%), both salvaged with bilateral PSD; 9/15 (60%) with documented sexual-function follow-up reported spontaneous erections adequate for penetration; median IIEF-5 drop 3.5 points; 2/25 went on to uneventful IPP<sup>[[12]](#ref12)</sup> |
| **Basile 2025** (proximal-shaft PSD) | 26 (22 bilateral, 4 unilateral) | Median 47 h (IQR 36.5–58.8); half &gt; 48 h | 96% immediate detumescence, 92% complete pain relief, 18/26 (69%) success defined as pain resolution without recurrence (73% for bilateral); 2/26 (8%) complications; among 21 with documented sexual function at median 10.4-month follow-up, 86% reported satisfactory EF with or without PP; 2/13 PP recipients had infection requiring explantation; earlier and bilateral PSD were associated with better outcomes<sup>[[13]](#ref13)</sup> |
| **VanDyke 2023/2024 surgeon survey** | 141 responses | Perception study | Among surgeons who had performed both, 47.3% rated PSD "Very/Extremely Effective" vs 18.7% for corporoglanular tunneling (p &lt; 0.001); perceived meaningful sexual recovery was 33/74 (44.6%) for PSD vs 33/75 (44.0%) for corporoglanular tunneling; practice experience was 124/138 (89.9%) for corporoglanular tunneling vs 86/137 (62.8%) for PSD<sup>[[14]](#ref14)</sup> |

**Comparison across surgical options:**

| Feature | Distal shunts | Snake (corporoglanular tunneling) | PSD | Immediate MPP after failed shunt |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Approach | Through glans | Through glans | Penoscrotal / proximal shaft | Penoscrotal |
| Glans-sparing | No | No | Yes | Yes |
| Resolution / success (different cohorts) | Distal shunts: 18.7–100% resolution across mixed durations<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup> | Snake: 24/26 (92.3%) single-intervention resolution<sup>[[10]](#ref10)</sup> | PSD: 18/26 (69%) resolution of pain without recurrence; 6/6 detumescence after failed distal shunt<sup>[[13]](#ref13)</sup><sup>[[11]](#ref11)</sup> | Prostheses: 100% priapism resolution across reviewed studies<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup> |
| Recurrence | Variable | 1/24 (4.2%) among initial Snake responders<sup>[[10]](#ref10)</sup> | 0/15 after primary bilateral PSD<sup>[[12]](#ref12)</sup> | Not reported as a comparable endpoint |
| Erectile function | Distal shunts: 20–100% potency across heterogeneous studies<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup> | Snake: 6/14 (42.8%) functional erections with or without PDE5i among those with follow-up<sup>[[10]](#ref10)</sup> | PSD: 9/15 (60%) spontaneous erections adequate for penetration among those with follow-up<sup>[[12]](#ref12)</sup> | N/A (prosthesis-dependent) |
| Revision rate | Variable | Low | 0% (Fuchs) | 37.5% (Fuchs) |
| Complication rate | Not reported separately in EAU review (all shunts: 0–42.5%)<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup> | 2/10 (20%) in Segal Snake series<sup>[[8]](#ref8)</sup> | 2/26 (8%) in Basile PSD series<sup>[[13]](#ref13)</sup> | 0–13.6% across pooled penile-prosthesis studies, not immediate post-shunt MPP specifically<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup> |
| Preserves future prosthesis | Yes | Yes | Yes (uneventful IPP documented) | N/A |

**Complications.** Postoperative complications occurred in 8% (2 patients) in the Basile series, and no urethral injuries or wound infections were reported in these small PSD series. The Segal Snake series (n = 10) had two patients with wound infection and skin necrosis; one also had an intraoperative urethral injury with subsequent fistula.<sup>[[8]](#ref8)</sup> The glans is not incised, so glans necrosis is not a risk by design. In the Segal Al-Ghorab Snake series, 2 of 6 men with normal preoperative erectile function achieved at least partial postoperative function.<sup>[[8]](#ref8)</sup> Among PSD patients, Baumgarten reported ED in 6/15 (40%) with documented sexual-function follow-up, while Basile reported ED in 38% after successful PSD versus 57% after failed PSD among 21 with documented status.<sup>[[12]](#ref12)[[13]](#ref13)</sup>

**Prosthesis preservation.** Because PSD avoids the glans entirely and closes the corporotomy primarily, future IPP placement remains feasible; Baumgarten documented two uneventful subsequent IPPs, and Basile reported 86% satisfactory EF (with or without PP) among 21 patients with documented sexual-function status at median 10.4-month follow-up, although 15% (2/13) of PP recipients required explantation for infection.<sup>[[12]](#ref12)[[13]](#ref13)</sup>

**Place in the algorithm.** AUA/SMSNA 2022 gives no recommendation for PSD; PSD series predate the guideline, and comparison with distal shunting remains a research question. The sequence below combines AUA/SMSNA guidance, the EAU systematic review, the Schifano 2025 review and PSD series.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[3]](#ref3)[[11]](#ref11)[[12]](#ref12)[[21]](#ref21)[[53]](#ref53)</sup>

1. Intracavernosal phenylephrine and corporal aspiration, with or without irrigation, as combined first-line therapy (AUA/SMSNA statement 8; Moderate Recommendation, Grade C).<sup>[[53]](#ref53)</sup>
2. Distal shunt (T-shunt or Al-Ghorab) ± Snake.
3. PSD when distal shunting fails to fully detumesce, and increasingly considered as a primary alternative to corporoglanular tunneling in prolonged priapism.<sup>[[13]](#ref13)[[14]](#ref14)</sup>
4. Early penile prosthesis when smooth-muscle necrosis is confirmed or duration / clinical signs argue against any decompression attempt.

**EAU 2026 guideline positions.** The EAU guideline (distinct from its 2024 systematic review) recommends proceeding to surgery only after aspiration and sympathomimetic injection have failed (strong), distal shunting first with tunneling if necessary (weak), and penoscrotal decompression as an alternative first-line option (weak). It states that no shunt type has been shown superior and that distal shunts are less invasive with lower ED rates; that proximal shunts are more invasive with higher ED rates; and that shunting beyond 36–48 hours may only relieve pain. Tunneling with a 7 mm sound or 7/8 Hegar dilator is described as necessary when priapism has lasted over 48 hours. For PSD it cites a 2025 systematic review of 62 patients (detumescence about 89%, erectile function preserved in about 55%, nearly half after failed distal shunts) and the Basile series, and cautions that PSD in refractory cases may delay prosthesis placement. The EAU describes perioperative antithrombotic prophylaxis (aspirin 325 mg preoperatively, heparin 5,000 IU intraoperatively, aspirin 81 mg with clopidogrel 75 mg for five days). In one observational shunt subgroup, recurrence was 11% in 9 patients given heterogeneous antithrombotic therapy versus 69% in 13 without it, an 84% relative decrease (level 3).<sup>[[49]](#ref49)</sup>

**Limitations.** Evidence consists of small retrospective cohorts, case reports and abstracts, with no RCTs. A nine-publication review (62 patients) reported detumescence in 89.3%, recurrence in 12.7%, and erectile function preserved in 54.8% of those with follow-up; 46.7% had undergone prior distal shunting. These pooled estimates do not establish superiority to shunts or prosthesis.<sup>[[54]](#ref54)</sup> Efficacy is still time-dependent (better outcomes within 36 h in the Basile series), PSD requires an OR rather than a bedside setting, and 62.8% of surveyed male-genital-surgery society members had personally performed the procedure.<sup>[[13]](#ref13)[[14]](#ref14)</sup>

## Proximal and Venous Shunts

Proximal shunts were historically used after distal shunt failure. They are now largely de-emphasized or abandoned because distal shunts with tunneling, PSD, and early prosthesis have clearer roles and lower anatomic morbidity. The EAU Sexual and Reproductive Health Guidelines Panel systematic review concluded that proximal or venous shunts should be abandoned because of their morbidity and ED rates.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[2]](#ref2)[[5]](#ref5)</sup> AUA/SMSNA 2022 advises counseling that evidence is inadequate to quantify the benefit of a proximal shunt after distal failure (statement 12; Moderate Recommendation, Grade C). Its discussion permits selected proximal shunting after distal shunting and tunneling fail, according to surgeon experience, duration and baseline erectile function.<sup>[[3]](#ref3)[[53]](#ref53)</sup>

### Quackels Shunt

First described by Quackels in 1964 as a proximal cavernospongiosal shunt: a direct side-to-side anastomosis between the corpus cavernosum and the adjacent corpus spongiosum at the proximal penile shaft or perineum, where the two structures lie in immediate anatomic apposition. The original rationale was that in prolonged priapism, the distal corpora may be so heavily thrombosed that distal shunts fail to drain the proximal corporal bodies; opening a window at the proximal level was meant to bypass the thrombosed distal tissue.<sup>[[5]](#ref5)[[22]](#ref22)</sup>

**Step-by-step (Quackels):**<sup>[[5]](#ref5)</sup>

1. General or regional anaesthesia (cannot be performed under local alone, unlike most distal shunts).
2. **Lithotomy position** for perineal and proximal-shaft access; Foley catheter to protect the urethra.
3. **Perineal or penoscrotal-junction incision** exposing the proximal corpora cavernosa and bulbar corpus spongiosum.
4. Dissect through Buck's fascia to expose the tunica albuginea of the corpus cavernosum and the adjacent corpus spongiosum.
5. Excise an elliptical or circular window from the apposed walls of corpus cavernosum and corpus spongiosum; suture the tunical edges together with absorbable suture to create a watertight side-to-side anastomosis.
6. Unilateral or bilateral; bilateral is more commonly performed for maximum drainage.
7. Express thrombosed blood through the new fistula into the spongiosum / systemic venous return.
8. Layered closure over a urethral catheter.

**Quackels versus Grayhack, the two classic proximal shunts:**

| Feature | Quackels | Grayhack |
|---|---|---|
| Year | 1964 | 1964 |
| Drainage pathway | Corpus cavernosum → corpus spongiosum | Corpus cavernosum → saphenous vein |
| Anastomosis | Direct side-to-side corporospongiosal | Vascular graft (saphenous vein interposition) |
| Vein harvest | No | Yes (saphenous from thigh) |
| Technical complexity | Moderate | High (vein harvest + microvascular anastomosis) |
| Urethral-injury risk | Higher (direct manipulation of spongiosum / urethra) | Lower |
| Pulmonary-embolism risk | Lower | Higher (theoretical, from venous-graft thrombosis) |
| Pooled potency rate | 11.1–77.2% across proximal shunts; Quackels-specific rate not given<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup> | 11.1–77.2% across proximal shunts; Grayhack-specific rate not given<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup> |

**Efficacy:**

- **Cosgrove / LaRocque 1974** (early review of 100 shunt cases). Overall potency 61% across shunt types; little difference between Grayhack and Quackels; unilateral saphenous-vein shunts performed slightly better than bilateral; outcomes similar whether performed early or late.<sup>[[23]](#ref23)</sup>
- **Nixon 2003** (n = 28). Reoperation was uncommon after Al-Ghorab or Quackels (92.3% of reoperated patients had had a Winter shunt); as many as 90% had ED at follow-up; only 10% (2 of 20) preserved pre-morbid EF and 15% achieved partial unaided erection.<sup>[[48]](#ref48)</sup>
- **Tabibi 2010** (small comparative, n = 16; ≥ 2-yr follow-up; 5 Winter / 7 Al-Ghorab / 4 Grayhack). Mean duration 51 h; Grayhack 2/4 impotent, 1 potent, 1 sildenafil-responsive; no surgical complications; sample too small for firm conclusions.<sup>[[24]](#ref24)</sup>
- **Zheng 2013** (n = 9 prolonged priapism, mixed shunt types incl. 1 Grayhack). 100% resolution but 66.7% postoperative ED; > 72 h cases had no response to PDE5i; authors recommended early prosthesis instead.<sup>[[25]](#ref25)</sup>
- **EAU systematic review 2024.** Proximal shunts (n = 209): resolution 5.7–100%, potency 11.1–77.2%; distal shunts (n = 274): resolution 18.7–100%, potency 20–100%; prostheses (n = 194): 100% resolution, satisfaction 60–100%. The panel concluded that proximal or venous shunts should be abandoned.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup>

**Quackels-specific complications.** Beyond the duration-driven ED that affects all priapism surgery, Quackels carries distinctive risks from corporospongiosal manipulation:<sup>[[26]](#ref26)[[27]](#ref27)[[28]](#ref28)</sup>

- **Urethral injury.** Direct dissection of the spongiosum places the urethra at risk.
- **Urethrocavernous fistula.** Robbins 1984 reported a late-developing urethrocavernous fistula 3 months post-cavernospongiosum shunt.<sup>[[27]](#ref27)</sup>
- **Combined urethrocutaneous and urethrocavernous fistula.** Manjunath 2015 reported simultaneous urethrocutaneous and urethrocavernous fistulae after proximal corporospongiosal shunt requiring suprapubic urinary diversion.<sup>[[26]](#ref26)</sup>
- **Persistent ("permanent open") shunt with venous leak.** Kulmala 1995 studied 26 patients with post-shunt impotence and found a permanent open shunt on cavernosography in 5/26 (19.2%); all 5 were cured of impotence by shunt closure, suggesting that a subset of post-shunt ED is mechanically reversible if recognized. The 26 patients had undergone various shunt operations.<sup>[[28]](#ref28)</sup>
- Wound infection, cavernositis, penile shortening from corporal fibrosis.

**Current status.** The Quackels shunt is largely obsolete in contemporary practice:

- Burnett / Brant–Lue T-shunt and Snake provide proximal corporal decompression through a distal approach and may reduce the need for proximal shunting.<sup>[[6]](#ref6)[[9]](#ref9)</sup>
- Penoscrotal decompression achieves proximal-corporal drainage with lower morbidity and better EF preservation.<sup>[[11]](#ref11)[[13]](#ref13)[[21]](#ref21)</sup>
- **Penile prosthesis series** in the EAU systematic review (n = 194, not stratified here by timing or device type) reported 100% priapism resolution and 60–100% patient satisfaction.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[17]](#ref17)</sup>
- The urethral-injury and persistent-shunt risk profile is not justified given safer alternatives.<sup>[[26]](#ref26)[[27]](#ref27)[[28]](#ref28)</sup>

Alnajjar & Muneer (2022) frame the consensus succinctly: "proximal shunts (Quackels or Grayhack) are no longer routinely performed."<sup>[[2]](#ref2)</sup> Quackels remains a procedure to recognize in historical literature and to consider only where modern techniques (Snake, PSD, prosthesis) are unavailable.

### Grayhack Shunt

First described by Grayhack, McCullough, O'Conor, and Trippel in 1964 as a proximal cavernovenous shunt: a segment of great saphenous vein is mobilized, tunneled subcutaneously, and anastomosed end-to-side to the corpus cavernosum so that corporal blood drains into the systemic venous circulation rather than into an adjacent penile structure.<sup>[[5]](#ref5)[[29]](#ref29)</sup> Conceptually distinct from Quackels (which drains into the adjacent corpus spongiosum), the Grayhack offers a high-capacity venous conduit but at the cost of vein harvest, microvascular anastomosis, donor-site morbidity, and the unique risk of a persistently patent graft causing venous-leak ED.

**Step-by-step (Grayhack):**<sup>[[5]](#ref5)[[29]](#ref29)</sup>

1. General or regional anaesthesia; supine, ipsilateral leg prepped to access the saphenous vein.
2. **Great saphenous vein** identified at the groin / proximal medial thigh; a segment of adequate length harvested, ligated distally, flushed with heparinised saline.
3. Separate penoscrotal-junction or proximal-shaft incision to expose the tunica albuginea of the corpus cavernosum.
4. Small elliptical or circular corporotomy in the tunica.
5. Saphenous-vein graft tunneled subcutaneously from groin to penile incision and anastomosed end-to-side to the corpus cavernosum with interrupted 5-0 / 6-0 suture.
6. **Unilateral preferred over bilateral.** Cosgrove / LaRocque found unilateral saphenous-vein shunts performed better than bilateral.<sup>[[23]](#ref23)</sup>
7. Confirm free flow of dark deoxygenated blood from corpus to graft to systemic venous return.
8. Layered closure at both sites; compressive penile dressing; urethral catheter optional.

**Efficacy:**

**Grayhack versus Quackels, head to head:**

| Feature | Grayhack | Quackels |
|---|---|---|
| Year | 1964 | 1964 |
| Drainage | Corpus cavernosum → saphenous vein (systemic venous) | Corpus cavernosum → corpus spongiosum |

**Grayhack-specific complications.**<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[10]](#ref10)[[28]](#ref28)[[31]](#ref31)</sup>

- **Persistent ("permanent open") graft with venous-leak ED.** The defining Grayhack-physiology risk. Kulmala documented this in 19.2% of post-shunt-impotence patients; Moloney specifically recommended shunt ligation if erection has not returned at 3 months.
- **Graft thrombosis.** The saphenous graft may occlude, leaving the shunt non-functional.
- **Pulmonary embolism.** Theoretical concern from mobilization of thrombosed corporal blood into systemic venous return or graft-thrombus embolisation.
- **Donor-site morbidity.** Wound infection, hematoma, seroma, lymphocele at the groin; loss of the saphenous segment for future CABG or vascular reconstruction.
- Duration-driven ED, penile shortening / fibrosis, multi-site wound infection.

**Modern cavernovenous variants** (kept brief; see Barry section below for caverno-dorsal-vein detail):

- **Barry shunt (1976).** Corpus cavernosum to dorsal penile vein; smaller surgical field, no saphenous mobilization, cannot cause urethrocutaneous fistula; can convert to Grayhack or Quackels without repositioning if unsuccessful.<sup>[[33]](#ref33)</sup>
- **Chiou 2009 cavernosal-dorsal-vein shunt.** Saphenous-vein graft connecting corpus cavernosum to the deep dorsal vein. In 16 patients (10 with prior failed shunts), priapism resolved or improved in all; 9 of 13 adults with ischemic priapism and follow-up (69%) had erections (6 with intercourse); Doppler confirmed graft patency and restored cavernosal arterial flow in 12/13 studied.<sup>[[34]](#ref34)</sup>
- **Kilinc 2009 temporary cavernosal-cephalic vein shunt.** Minimally invasive angiocath and serum-set construct to the cephalic vein; 13/15 (86.7%) detumescence; the 2 failures required formal saphenocavernous shunting; 3/13 ED at 12 mo.<sup>[[35]](#ref35)</sup>

**Current status.** The Grayhack is largely obsolete:

- The EAU systematic review concluded that proximal or venous shunts should be abandoned.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup>
- T-shunt or Al-Ghorab and Snake achieves proximal corporal decompression through a distal approach, removing the original rationale.<sup>[[6]](#ref6)[[9]](#ref9)</sup>
- **Penoscrotal decompression** drains the proximal corpora with lower morbidity and better EF preservation.<sup>[[11]](#ref11)[[13]](#ref13)</sup>
- **Penile prosthesis series** in the EAU systematic review (n = 194, not stratified here by timing or device type) reported 100% priapism resolution and 60–100% patient satisfaction.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[17]](#ref17)</sup>
- The Grayhack-specific risks (graft thrombosis, persistent open shunt, donor-site morbidity, surgical complexity) are not justified by alternatives.

The Grayhack and its variants persist mainly in historical literature and as the conceptual ancestor of the Barry (caverno-dorsal-vein) shunt, which the recent Soydaş 2025 algorithm and Micoogullari 2021 series have proposed as a possible niche option. Micoogullari's series used elective shunt closure at 2 months to address persistent shunt patency.<sup>[[15]](#ref15)[[16]](#ref16)</sup>

### Barry / Caverno-Dorsal Vein Shunt

First described by John M. Barry in 1976 as a simpler, smaller-field cavernovenous alternative to the Grayhack, using the dorsal vein of the penis (deep or superficial) as the drainage target rather than the saphenous vein.<sup>[[33]](#ref33)</sup> The original Barry technique uses a smaller field than Grayhack, avoids saphenous-vein harvest, and avoids the direct spongiosal dissection of Quackels; Chiou’s later graft modification reintroduces saphenous-vein harvest.<sup>[[33]](#ref33)</sup><sup>[[34]](#ref34)</sup> Recent series, particularly the Micoogullari 2021 planned-closure construct and the Chiou 2009 saphenous-graft modification, have driven a measured resurgence of interest, with some authors advocating Barry shunting as a first-line surgical option for refractory ischemic priapism.<sup>[[15]](#ref15)[[16]](#ref16)[[34]](#ref34)</sup>

**Relevant venous anatomy.** The penis drains via three distinct systems:

- **Superficial dorsal vein.** Subcutaneous; drains skin and prepuce into the external pudendal, then femoral or saphenous, veins.
- **Deep dorsal vein (DDV).** In the midline dorsal groove between the corpora cavernosa, deep to Buck's fascia; drains glans and all three corpora via emissary and circumflex veins into Santorini's preprostatic plexus. Principal drainage of the flaccid corpus cavernosum and the target of the standard Barry shunt.
- **Cavernosal (crural) veins.** Emerge from each crus and drain only the corpora into the internal pudendal vein.

In ischemic priapism the veno-occlusive compression of emissary veins is pathologically sustained; the Barry shunt bypasses that obstruction by creating a direct corpus-cavernosum-to-dorsal-vein communication and restoring venous outflow.

**Step-by-step (original Barry, 1976):**<sup>[[33]](#ref33)</sup>

1. General or regional anaesthesia; supine.
2. **Dorsal mid-shaft penile incision.** Single small field; no patient repositioning required.
3. Identify and mobilize the deep dorsal vein (between Buck's fascia and tunica) and/or the superficial dorsal vein.
4. Small corporotomy in the tunica adjacent to the mobilized vein.
5. Ligate the chosen dorsal vein distally; anastomose the proximal end end-to-side to the corpus cavernosum with fine suture.
6. Confirm free flow of dark deoxygenated blood from corpus to dorsal vein to systemic return; detumescence.
7. Layered closure; compressive dressing.

If the Barry shunt is unsuccessful, a Grayhack or Quackels can be performed without repositioning, a deliberate practical advantage of Barry's original design.<sup>[[33]](#ref33)</sup>

**Modifications:**

- **Micoogullari 2021: planned closure at 2 months.**<sup>[[15]](#ref15)</sup> Protocol: emergency aspiration and corporal blood gas, then 0.01% adrenaline irrigation × 5 at 20-min intervals, then Barry shunt if priapism persists, then elective shunt closure at 2 months. This directly addresses the Grayhack-era "permanent open shunt" problem (Kulmala and Moloney) that drove EAU 2024 to recommend abandonment of cavernovenous shunts more broadly.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[28]](#ref28)[[31]](#ref31)</sup>
- **Chiou 2009: DDV and saphenous-vein-graft interposition.**<sup>[[34]](#ref34)</sup> Hybrid construct using the dorsal vein as drainage target (Barry) but a saphenous-vein graft as the conduit (Grayhack); larger-caliber lumen at the cost of reintroducing vein harvest.
- **Kilinc 2009: temporary external cavernosal-cephalic shunt.**<sup>[[35]](#ref35)</sup> Angiocaths and serum sets bridging the corpora to a forearm cephalic-vein angiocath; bedside, no OR required.
- **Soydaş 2025 algorithm.** Proposes incorporating caverno-dorsal-vein shunting into a prolonged-priapism algorithm.<sup>[[16]](#ref16)</sup>

**Efficacy:**

| Series | n | Setting | Resolution | EF / note |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Barry 1976** (original) | 2 | 6-day (DDV) and 3.5-day (superficial DV) priapism | 2/2 resolved | "Anatomically sound, small surgical field, no saphenous mobilization, cannot cause urethrocutaneous fistula"<sup>[[33]](#ref33)</sup> |
| **Chiou 2009** (DDV + SVG) | 16 (age 15–65; duration 32 h–8 d; 10/16 had prior failed shunting) | Mixed primary and salvage (10 with prior shunts) | Resolved or improved in 16/16 | 69% (9/13) had erections at ≤ 6.5 yr (5 of 16 returned questionnaires); 6 with intercourse; Doppler-confirmed patent shunt in all studied and restored cavernosal arterial flow in 12/13<sup>[[34]](#ref34)</sup> |
| **Micoogullari 2021** (Barry + planned closure) | 10 | Median duration 13.5 h (range 7–38) | 100% (10/10) | 80% (8/10) maintained EF; 2/10 → IPP; shunt electively closed at 2 mo<sup>[[15]](#ref15)</sup> |
| **Kilinc 2009** (temporary cephalic-vein) | 15 | Bedside angiocath construct | 13/15 (86.7%) | 2 failures → saphenocavernous shunt; 3/13 ED at 12 mo<sup>[[35]](#ref35)</sup> |

**Barry versus Grayhack: what Barry was designed to fix:**

| Feature | Barry | Grayhack |
|---|---|---|
| Drainage target | Dorsal penile vein | Saphenous vein |
| Vein harvest | No (original)<sup>[[33]](#ref33)</sup> / yes (Chiou modification)<sup>[[34]](#ref34)</sup> | Yes (Grayhack saphenous-vein shunt)<sup>[[49]](#ref49)</sup> |
| Surgical field | Small (dorsal penis only) | Large (groin + penis) |
| Donor-site morbidity | None (original) | Present (thigh wound) |
| Urethrocutaneous-fistula risk | None | None |
| Surgical complexity | Low–moderate | High |
| Patient repositioning | Not required | May be required |
| Escalation if it fails | Can proceed to Grayhack/Quackels without repositioning | — |

**Complications.** The defining cavernovenous-shunt risk, persistent ("permanent open") shunt with venous-leak ED, applies to Barry as much as to Grayhack. Kulmala documented this in 19.2% of post-shunt-impotence patients with 100% impotence reversal on closure, and Moloney recommended graft ligation if erection had not returned by 3 months.<sup>[[28]](#ref28)[[31]](#ref31)</sup> Micoogullari's ten-patient series used planned shunt closure at two months; 8/10 maintained erectile function after a median priapism duration of 13.5 hours. The uncontrolled series cannot show whether planned closure caused that outcome or be directly compared with the heterogeneous proximal-shunt cohorts.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[15]](#ref15)</sup> Barry-specific reports document no urethrocutaneous fistulae, no saphenous donor-site morbidity (original technique), and no recurrence in the Micoogullari and Chiou series.<sup>[[15]](#ref15)[[33]](#ref33)[[34]](#ref34)</sup>

**Current status and algorithmic placement.** The EAU review's conclusion that proximal or venous shunts should be abandoned rests primarily on Grayhack and Quackels data, not Barry-specific outcomes.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup> The Barry-shunt series are small and uncontrolled (Chiou 16 patients, resolution or improvement in all and erections in 69%; Micoogullari 10 patients, detumescence in all and preserved EF in 80%). Micoogullari suggests the Barry shunt could be considered as a first treatment for refractory low-flow priapism. AUA/SMSNA 2022 includes Barry shunts in its proximal-shunt evidence discussion but does not endorse Barry as a first surgical treatment. It recommends counseling about uncertain benefit after distal shunt failure (statement 12; Moderate Recommendation, Grade C); the small Barry series do not replace that pathway.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[15]](#ref15)[[34]](#ref34)[[53]](#ref53)</sup>

The clearest pragmatic niches:

- **After failed distal shunting.** Chiou's 16-patient cavernosal-dorsal-vein graft series included 10 patients with previous shunt surgery; priapism resolved or improved in all 16, without a separate complete-resolution rate. This describes a salvage technique but does not establish comparative efficacy.<sup>[[34]](#ref34)</sup>
- **When saphenous harvest is undesirable.** Original Barry preserves the saphenous segment.<sup>[[33]](#ref33)</sup>
- **When planned closure is feasible.** Micoogullari's 2-month elective ligation is the construct most likely to preserve EF.<sup>[[15]](#ref15)</sup>
- **As a stepping stone.** Failure converts to Grayhack or Quackels without repositioning.<sup>[[33]](#ref33)</sup>

## Early Malleable Penile Prosthesis for Refractory Ischemic Priapism

Early malleable penile prosthesis (MPP) is a definitive surgical option for ischemic priapism refractory to conventional interventions; a penile prosthesis may also be considered for untreated acute ischemic priapism &gt;36 hours (AUA/SMSNA Expert Opinion).<sup>[[53]](#ref53)</sup> It treats the priapism and the ED that follows prolonged corporal ischemia in one operation. In Ralph's UCLH series of 50 patients with smooth-muscle necrosis, 42 resumed intercourse, satisfaction was 96%, and no patient reported penile shortening.<sup>[[2]](#ref2)[[17]](#ref17)[[36]](#ref36)[[37]](#ref37)</sup>

**Pathophysiological rationale.** Corporal smooth-muscle damage is time-dependent and ultimately irreversible: by > 48 h all biopsied patients in the Zacharakis T-shunt series had necrotic smooth muscle, and EF recovery is unlikely even if shunting achieves detumescence.<sup>[[5]](#ref5)[[17]](#ref17)[[36]](#ref36)</sup> Implantation in the acute soft-corpora window, before fibrotic remodeling consolidates, accomplishes four things at once:

1. **Mechanical detumescence.** The prosthesis displaces the trapped blood.
2. **Length preservation.** The cylinder functions as an internal stent or tissue expander during fibrotic remodeling.
3. **ED treatment.** Addresses the low likelihood of erectile recovery after prolonged confirmed ischemia; AUA/SMSNA advises this counseling after &gt;36 hours (statement 7; Moderate Recommendation, Grade B).<sup>[[53]](#ref53)</sup>
4. **Avoidance of the dense-fibrosis surgery.** Zacharakis showed 80% of delayed implants require a second corporotomy and downsized cylinders due to fibrosis.<sup>[[38]](#ref38)</sup>

**Imaging-guided patient selection with contrast-enhanced penile MRI.** T2-weighted gadolinium-enhanced penile MRI differentiates viable from nonviable corporal smooth muscle. Ralph 2010 reported 100% sensitivity of MRI for predicting nonviable smooth muscle against corporal-biopsy reference; an additional 10 MRI-nonviable patients without biopsy all developed ED, and 5 MRI-viable patients all maintained EF.<sup>[[39]](#ref39)</sup> MRI findings may inform prosthesis counseling in prolonged cases, but AUA/SMSNA states that MRI is unlikely to have a role in the initial diagnostic and treatment phase. Persistent erection after shunting requires corporal blood gas or Doppler before further surgery (statement 13; Moderate Recommendation, Grade C).<sup>[[39]](#ref39)[[47]](#ref47)[[53]](#ref53)</sup> In the ISSM survey (251 respondents; 11.6% response rate), fewer than 40% currently used MRI or corporal biopsy in this setting.<sup>[[40]](#ref40)</sup>

**Guideline positioning.**

- **EAU panel systematic review (2024).** The review favors prosthesis implantation for ischemic priapism &gt;48 hours and concludes that proximal or venous shunts should be abandoned; these are review conclusions.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup>
- **AUA/SMSNA 2022.** A prosthesis may be considered for untreated acute ischemic priapism &gt;36 hours or refractory disease after shunting, with or without tunneling (statement 14; Expert Opinion). Discuss early versus delayed placement (statement 15; Moderate Recommendation, Grade C). The discussion defines early placement as within two weeks and calls for confirming the ischemic timeline, including intermittent detumescence.<sup>[[3]](#ref3)[[53]](#ref53)</sup>
- **EAU 2026.** Discuss prosthesis after delayed presentation (&gt;48 hours) or failed injection/distal-shunt therapy (weak); delay implantation after a shunt to reduce infection and erosion (strong). AUA/SMSNA discusses those risks without prescribing a fixed post-shunt delay.<sup>[[49]](#ref49)[[53]](#ref53)</sup>
- **Pang 2025 / Calopedos 2025.** These narrative reviews favor early prosthesis for RIP.<sup>[[2]](#ref2)[[36]](#ref36)</sup>

**Why malleable over inflatable in the acute setting:**<sup>[[17]](#ref17)[[36]](#ref36)[[37]](#ref37)</sup>

| Feature | Malleable (semi-rigid) | Inflatable (3-piece IPP) |
|---|---|---|
| Acute / early implantation | More often used in AUA-reviewed early series | Also an option; choice depends on patient goals and surgical expertise<sup>[[53]](#ref53)</sup> |
| Surgical complexity | Lower | Higher (reservoir + pump) |
| Operative time | Shorter | Longer |
| Comparative infection risk | No established superiority in the AUA review | No established superiority in the AUA review<sup>[[53]](#ref53)</sup> |
| Corporal dilation | Minimal — soft necrotic tissue | Same, but precise sizing critical |
| Acts as internal stent / tissue expander | Yes | Yes (more complex) |
| Concealment | Poor | Better |
| Elective exchange to IPP later | Yes (~ 6–12 mo) | N/A |
| Cost | Lower | Higher |

**Step-by-step (early MPP for refractory ischemic priapism):**<sup>[[37]](#ref37)[[41]](#ref41)[[42]](#ref42)</sup>

1. Confirm ischemic priapism with corporal blood gas (dark blood, pO₂ &lt;30 mmHg, pH &lt;7.25); MRI may inform viability in selected prolonged cases but must not delay initial treatment. After shunting, obtain blood gas or Doppler before further surgery.<sup>[[39]](#ref39)[[53]](#ref53)</sup>
2. Counsel about permanent loss of native erections in exchange for detumescence, length preservation, and restored ability to have intercourse.
3. Broad-spectrum antibiotic prophylaxis; consider antibiotic-coated devices (InhibiZone / Titan hydrophilic).
4. Penoscrotal or subcoronal incision.
5. **Bilateral corporotomies.** The necrotic corpora are soft and dilate with minimal force; full Hegar progression is rarely needed.
6. Evacuate residual thrombosed blood; irrigate.
7. **Measure full corporal length distally and proximally.** Full-length cylinders maximize length preservation.
8. Insert malleable rods bilaterally, with proximal tip seated in crura, distal tip beneath the glans.
9. **Salem sling-suture distal-erosion prophylaxis** (especially after prior distal shunting that has weakened the tunica): nonabsorbable suture taken through the cylinder and the edges of the opened tunica fixes the cylinder to the corporotomy edges. In Salem's 12-patient series (11 with prior failed shunts) this construct yielded 0% distal erosion at median 15-mo follow-up.<sup>[[42]](#ref42)</sup>
10. Close corporotomies with 2-0 / 3-0 absorbable.
11. Layered wound closure; compressive dressing.
12. **Discharge within 24 h** is typical.<sup>[[41]](#ref41)</sup>

**Clinical evidence:**

| Series | n | Setting | Outcome |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Ralph 2009 UCLH** | 50 (13 prior failed shunts) | Acute ischemic priapism; 86% malleable, 14% IPP | 84% successful intercourse (42/50; median follow-up 15.7 mo); 96% satisfaction; no patient reported shortening; 6% infection; 12% further revision; 12% elective MPP-to-IPP exchange<sup>[[37]](#ref37)</sup> |
| **Zacharakis 2014 UCLH** (early vs delayed) | 95 (68 early at median 7 d vs 27 delayed at median 5 mo) | Refractory ischemic priapism | Satisfaction 96% early vs 60% delayed; 80% of delayed required second corporotomy + downsized cylinders due to dense fibrosis; significant shortening was the main driver of delayed-group dissatisfaction<sup>[[38]](#ref38)</sup> |
| **Barham 2023 multicenter (IPP)** | 62 priapism (15 early, 47 delayed) + 62 controls (no priapism) | Early (≤ 6 mo) vs delayed (&gt; 6 mo) IPP | Postoperative noninfectious complications: 0% early vs 40.5% delayed; all reported cylinder-related complications occurred with full-sized cylinders — supports early referral to prosthetic experts<sup>[[43]](#ref43)</sup> |
| **Tausch 2015 cost-effectiveness** | 14 | RIP at urban safety-net hospital | Mean preop 82 h of RIP, 4 ER visits, 2 admissions, 1.5 shunts, 5 irrigation/drainage, 5 hospital days, estimated preop cost US $83,818; all discharged ≤ 24 h after MPP<sup>[[41]](#ref41)</sup> |
| **Salem 2010 distal-erosion prevention** | 12 (11 prior failed shunts) | Median duration 120 h | 100% successful insertion; 0% distal erosion and 0% infection with sling-suture construct; 100% intercourse and satisfaction at median 15 mo<sup>[[42]](#ref42)</sup> |
| **Dighero 2025 long-term QoL (QoLSPP)** | 39 of 167 post-priapism implants | Median 9 yr post-priapism | Satisfactory scores on 7/16 QoLSPP items; 100% would not regret the prosthesis; 100% satisfied to live the rest of life with device in situ; no significant difference malleable vs inflatable<sup>[[44]](#ref44)</sup> |
| **Butaney 2019 ISSM survey** | 251 ISSM members | Practice patterns | 70.9% more comfortable with MPP than shunt, yet ~ 80% still favor shunts first-line; experience higher with prostheses than shunts — points to underutilization gap<sup>[[40]](#ref40)</sup> |

**Malleable-to-inflatable exchange.** A main advantage of acute MPP is the option for elective exchange to a 3-piece IPP at 6–12 months once corporal remodeling has stabilized; Ralph reported elective exchange in 6 of 50 patients (12%), and the corporal space maintained by the malleable rod makes the later IPP insertion considerably easier than a primary delayed IPP into densely fibrotic tissue.<sup>[[37]](#ref37)[[38]](#ref38)</sup>

**Complications.** Early MPP has a favorable complication profile relative to both shunt surgery and delayed implantation:<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[17]](#ref17)[[37]](#ref37)[[38]](#ref38)[[42]](#ref42)[[43]](#ref43)[[46]](#ref46)</sup>

- Infection: 3/50 (6%) after immediate implantation in Ralph’s mixed-device cohort (43 malleable, 7 inflatable; median follow-up 15.7 months), versus 0/12 in Salem’s malleable sling-suture series (median follow-up 15 months); Ralph’s infections were managed by explantation and delayed reinsertion.<sup>[[37]](#ref37)[[42]](#ref42)</sup> Antibiotic-coated cylinders reduce this risk.<sup>[[46]](#ref46)</sup> Antibiotic-coated cylinders reduce this risk.
- Distal cylinder erosion or protrusion: particular risk when prior shunting has weakened the distal tunica; no distal erosion occurred with the Salem sling suture in 12 patients.<sup>[[42]](#ref42)</sup>
- Intraoperative corporal perforation: 1/12 patients (8.3%) in Salem’s small series; the affected patient had sickle cell disease, and the perforation was managed intraoperatively.<sup>[[42]](#ref42)</sup>
- Curvature: from asymmetric fibrosis or malposition; infection and curvature were the reasons for revision in the Zacharakis early group.<sup>[[38]](#ref38)</sup>
- Mechanical failure: relevant to IPPs only; malleable rods have no mechanical components.
- Penile shortening: none of Ralph’s 50 immediate-implant patients reported it; Zacharakis reported subjective shortening in 3% of 68 early-implant versus 40% of 27 delayed-implant patients, with shortening driving delayed-group dissatisfaction.<sup>[[37]](#ref37)[[38]](#ref38)</sup>

**Three-piece IPP in the acute setting.** Both malleable and inflatable devices are options; AUA/SMSNA does not mandate one device type.<sup>[[53]](#ref53)</sup> Clavijo 2017 detailed techniques for IPP placement into fibrotic corpora including cutting cavernotomes and narrow-base cylinders (Coloplast Titan narrow-base).<sup>[[45]](#ref45)</sup> In Barham’s retrospective multicenter cohort, postoperative noninfectious complications occurred in 0% of 15 men receiving an IPP within 6 months of ischemic priapism versus 40.5% of 47 men receiving it later; the study supports early referral but does not establish a causal effect of timing.<sup>[[43]](#ref43)</sup> A narrative review of 8 studies reported total complication rates of 3.37% with early IPP placement versus 37.23% with delayed placement among the studies reporting complications, supporting early referral to a prosthetic surgeon when an IPP is planned.<sup>[[52]](#ref52)</sup>

**Special populations.**

- **Sickle cell disease** (4 of 13 in the Tausch cohort). The single corporal perforation in the Salem series occurred during dilation in a sickle cell patient. Perioperative hydration and transfusion are managed with hematology.<sup>[[41]](#ref41)[[42]](#ref42)</sup>
- **Prior failed shunt surgery.** Weakened distal tunica; use the Salem sling suture.<sup>[[42]](#ref42)</sup>

**Algorithmic placement and biases.** Per current evidence:<sup>[[2]](#ref2)[[3]](#ref3)[[21]](#ref21)[[36]](#ref36)</sup>

1. Intracavernosal phenylephrine and corporal aspiration, with or without irrigation, as combined first-line therapy (AUA/SMSNA statement 8; Moderate Recommendation, Grade C).<sup>[[53]](#ref53)</sup>
2. Distal shunt (T-shunt or Al-Ghorab) ± Snake, for shorter-duration disease with likely-viable corpora.
3. Consider PSD after failed distal shunting; EAU 2026 also permits it as an alternative first surgical approach in selected cases (Weak). Discuss early prosthesis without requiring PSD first.<sup>[[49]](#ref49)</sup>
4. Consider a penile prosthesis for untreated acute ischemic priapism &gt;36 h or persistent ischemia after shunting, with or without tunneling (AUA/SMSNA Expert Opinion); an early malleable device may be chosen when MRI or biopsy suggests nonviable corpora or the patient prefers definitive treatment with length preservation. After a shunt, delay implantation to reduce infection and erosion (EAU Strong recommendation).<sup>[[49]](#ref49)[[53]](#ref53)</sup>

| Situation | Bias |
|---|---|
| Shorter duration, viable corpora, patient strongly wants native EF chance | Distal shunt with tunneling or PSD |
| Failed shunt / failed PSD | Acute malleable prosthesis |
| Duration > 36–48 h, MRI / biopsy with necrotic corpora | Early MPP (length-preserving) |
| Borderline duration (36–48 h) with patient strongly desiring native-EF chance | Discuss both options with the patient; MRI may inform the choice |
| Patient medically unstable or infected field | Temporize safely; defer implant if risk outweighs length preservation |
| Prior shunting with weakened distal tunica | MPP with Salem sling-suture distal fixation |

**Penile prosthesis versus shunt (ranges from the EAU systematic review; not head-to-head data):**<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[37]](#ref37)[[38]](#ref38)[[41]](#ref41)</sup>

| Parameter | Early prosthesis | Distal shunts | Proximal shunts |
|---|---|---|---|
| Resolution | 100% across reviewed prosthesis series (timing and device types pooled) | 18.7–100% across distal-shunt series | 5.7–100% across proximal-shunt series<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup> |
| Potency / functional intercourse | 26.3–100% across prosthesis series (timing and device types pooled); 42/50 (84%) resumed intercourse in Ralph’s immediate mixed-device cohort after a median follow-up of 15.7 months | 20–100% across distal-shunt series | 11.1–77.2% across proximal-shunt series<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[37]](#ref37)</sup> |
| Satisfaction | 96% in Ralph’s immediate mixed-device cohort (50 patients; median follow-up 15.7 months); the review’s 60–100% range includes prosthesis series with differing timing | Not systematically reported | Not systematically reported<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[37]](#ref37)[[38]](#ref38)</sup> |
| Complications | 0–13.6% across reviewed penile-prosthesis series (not an early-only estimate) | 0–42.5% across shunt series, not distal-specific | 0–42.5% across shunt series, not proximal-specific<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup> |
| Length preservation | Yes | Variable | Variable |
| Treats ED proactively | Yes | No | No |
| Comparative cost-effectiveness | Not established by the cited studies | Not established by the cited studies | Not established by the cited studies |

**Limitations.** The choice is irreversible (native erectile function is permanently lost), which is a meaningful concern in borderline 36–48 h windows where some residual EF recovery might still occur. The evidence base is retrospective with no RCTs; availability of prosthetic surgical expertise on emergency call is not universal; and patient counseling in acute distress is challenging.<sup>[[17]](#ref17)[[36]](#ref36)[[40]](#ref40)</sup>
