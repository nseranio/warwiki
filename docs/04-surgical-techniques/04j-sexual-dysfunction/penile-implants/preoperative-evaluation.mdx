# Preoperative Evaluation for Penile Prosthesis Implantation

Penile implants have among the highest satisfaction rates of any treatment for erectile dysfunction, and those rates depend on patient selection and on expectations set before the first incision. Implanting the wrong patient is the most avoidable error in prosthetic urology. This page covers patient selection, medical optimization, risk stratification, the CURSED framework, consent elements specific to this procedure, and the infection-prevention bundle that starts in clinic.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[2]](#ref2)</sup>

---

## The Decision to Implant

Penile implantation is considered after failure of, intolerance to, or patient rejection of the less invasive rungs of the ED reconstructive ladder: PDE5 inhibitors, intraurethral alprostadil, vacuum erection device, and intracavernosal injection therapy.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[3]](#ref3)</sup> Three pathways in which early implantation is reasonable are:

- **Severe Peyronie's disease with ED.** Mechanical straightening by the implant is part of the operation, and medical therapy for ED is usually futile
- **Corporal fibrosis.** Ischemic priapism, prior infection or prior prosthesis explantation can make dilation harder. AUA/SMSNA permits considering a prosthesis for untreated acute ischemic priapism &gt;36 hours or failure of shunting, with or without tunneling (Expert Opinion). Early placement may limit fibrosis-associated shortening; discuss its risks and benefits against delayed placement (Moderate Recommendation, Grade C).<sup>[[24]](#ref24)</sup>
- **Patient preference for a definitive, on-demand solution.** This is a valid indication when the patient has been adequately counseled, even if they have not formally "failed" medical therapy

See [penile-implants index](/docs/surgical-techniques/04j-sexual-dysfunction/penile-implants) for the indication list.

## Patient Selection: the CURSED Framework

The **CURSED Patient** mnemonic was proposed by Trost, Baum and Hellstrom, from a review of the urologic and cosmetic-surgery literature, to identify seven traits that may indicate a psychologically challenging patient at increased risk of postoperative dissatisfaction: Compulsive/obsessive, Unrealistic, Revision, Surgeon shopping, Entitled, Denial and Psychiatric. Denial and psychiatric disorder are two separate traits in the original formulation, not one combined trait.<sup>[[4]](#ref4)</sup>

| Letter | Trait | What it looks like in clinic |
|---|---|---|
| **C** | **C**ompulsive / controlling personality | Detailed spreadsheets of every device specification; demands specific cylinder lengths; fixation on millimeter-level outcomes |
| **U** | **U**nrealistic expectations | Expects return of natural erections, increased length, enhanced sensation, or improved orgasm — none of which the device provides |
| **R** | **R**evision / previously operated elsewhere | Explanted or revised at another institution; seeking a different outcome from a different surgeon |
| **S** | **S**urgeon shopping | Multiple prior consultations; asks "what would Dr. X do?"; frames the visit as comparison shopping |
| **E** | **E**ntitled / demanding | Interrupts, dismisses nurses and support staff, makes demands about the perioperative experience |
| **D** | **D**enial | Denial of prior erectile function or current disease status |
| **P** | **P**sychiatric disorder | Untreated major depression, body dysmorphic disorder, or active psychiatric decompensation |

None of these is an absolute contraindication. Each warrants additional counseling time and documentation. When several traits cluster, a deferred decision or referral to another surgeon is considered. Unrealistic expectations and revision surgery are among the traits Trost and colleagues list as indicating higher risk of dissatisfaction, and a mixed-method study of 47 men found that 79% were satisfied with their implant.<sup>[[4]](#ref4)[[5]](#ref5)</sup> The CURSED traits are expert-derived rather than validated as a predictive score.

### The partner conversation

Contemporary series report partner satisfaction of 91–95%, and partner dissatisfaction is among the recognized determinants of patient dissatisfaction.<sup>[[4]](#ref4)[[6]](#ref6)</sup> Where possible, the partner attends at least one preoperative visit, handles the demonstration device and takes part in the consent discussion. A 2019 retrospective comparison of 883 men reported patient satisfaction of 99.2% with inflatable versus 90.3% with malleable implants, and partner dissatisfaction of 11.2% with malleable, 3.8% with two-piece inflatable and 3.3% with three-piece inflatable devices; device-subgroup denominators and follow-up were not reported separately in this figure, and these single-center historical rates are a counseling example, not a promised outcome.<sup>[[20]](#ref20)</sup>

## Medical Optimization

### Diabetes

Diabetes is the most extensively studied modifiable risk factor for prosthesis infection. In a multicenter retrospective cohort of 5,261 patients, diabetes was an independent risk factor (OR 1.68).<sup>[[7]](#ref7)</sup> Infection rates rose with HbA1c in a prospective multicenter cohort of 902 implants (1.3% below 6.5% and 22.4% above 9.5%), where an HbA1c of 8.5% predicted infection with 80% sensitivity and 65% specificity.<sup>[[12]](#ref12)</sup> A 2026 meta-analysis of retrospective studies found infection rates more than twice as high when mean HbA1c was 8% or higher (9.1% vs 3.8%).<sup>[[13]](#ref13)</sup> No randomized trial has validated a cutoff. Common practice:

- **HbA1c &lt;8.5%** is the most widely cited threshold (Habous 2018); above this, reassess with endocrinology
- **HbA1c &lt;7%** is ideal where achievable without inducing hypoglycemia
- **Point-of-care glucose &lt;200 mg/dL** on the morning of surgery
- Hold SGLT2 inhibitors before surgery to avoid euglycemic ketoacidosis (the FDA labeling advises at least 3 days, and 4 days for ertugliflozin)

Fructosamine (2–3 week glycemic average) can be used as an adjunct in patients with hemoglobinopathies or when a more recent glycemic snapshot is needed.

### Obesity and metabolic syndrome

Obesity increases operative difficulty (deeper scrotum, harder reservoir access). A review of fungal IPP infections reports diabetes in 69% of cases and obesity in 85% of cases in the series it cites.<sup>[[8]](#ref8)</sup> BMI &gt;30 warrants additional counseling on technical difficulty and infection risk. In a revision candidate with BMI &gt;40, weight reduction before reimplantation is a reasonable consideration.

### MRSA screening and decolonization

Nasal MRSA carriage is more frequent in healthcare workers, dialysis patients and those with recent hospitalization. Many prosthetic programs screen with a nasal swab at the preoperative visit and decolonize carriers with mupirocin 2% ointment twice daily for 5 days plus a daily chlorhexidine body wash for 5 days. This is institutional practice. The AUA best practice statement does not address decolonization for implant surgery; for implanted devices its Table V lists an aminoglycoside with a first- or second-generation cephalosporin, aztreonam with a cephalosporin, or an aminoglycoside with vancomycin.<sup>[[9]](#ref9)</sup>

### Smoking

Smoking cessation for at least 4 weeks preoperatively is commonly advised. The supporting data come from general surgical wound-healing studies rather than IPP-specific trials. A single-center retrospective series of 137 patients associated smoking with explantation.<sup>[[14]](#ref14)</sup>

### Hypogonadism

Symptomatic men with serum testosterone &lt;300 ng/dL are evaluated for hypogonadism, and treatment can be started before or alongside implantation. Testosterone replacement is not usually stopped before surgery, and no IPP-specific data show increased infection risk. In the TRAVERSE trial, testosterone was noninferior to placebo for major adverse cardiac events but was associated with more atrial fibrillation, acute kidney injury and pulmonary embolism, so thrombotic history is reviewed.<sup>[[15]](#ref15)</sup>

### Anticoagulation

Warfarin, DOACs, and dual antiplatelet therapy require individualized perioperative plans coordinated with the prescribing clinician. Aspirin monotherapy (81 mg) can usually be continued. Scrotal hematoma is a risk with uncorrected anticoagulation, and hematoma has been associated with infection in some series.

## Physical Examination

The prosthetic urology preop exam has elements not found in general urology:

- **Penile length in stretched and flaccid states.** Length is documented in clinic and shared with the patient, so later concerns about shortening have a baseline
- **Corporal integrity.** Palpate for Peyronie's plaques, prior surgery scars (especially Nesbit / plication), and any areas of fibrosis from prior priapism or infection
- **Scrotal anatomy and dartos thickness.** The pump sits between the dartos layers; excessively thick or tethered scrota change operative planning
- **Abdominal wall.** Prior low-transverse scars (Pfannenstiel, open prostatectomy, cystectomy, appendectomy, hernia mesh) make virgin space of Retzius reservoir placement riskier and push toward ectopic placement
- **Inguinal canal patency.** Patent processus vaginalis or known inguinal hernia changes reservoir strategy; a reservoir should not be placed through a hernia defect
- **Penile sensation.** Documented because post-op sensory changes are a common complaint and the baseline matters. Biothesiometry, applying a standardized vibratory stimulus to marked dorsal, ventral and shaft sites and recording the first-perceived-vibration threshold against normative values, is one office technique for this and has been used to document sensory change after plaque incision and grafting for Peyronie's disease; there is no standardized protocol and little evidence that the result itself changes ED or implant management, so a normal history and exam remain the baseline documentation for most patients.<sup>[[18]](#ref18)[[19]](#ref19)</sup>
- **Meatus and urethra.** Meatal stenosis or unrecognized stricture can affect postoperative catheterization decisions

## Special Preoperative Considerations

### Prior pelvic / lower abdominal surgery

A patient with prior radical prostatectomy, cystectomy, pelvic radiation, renal transplant, or extensive hernia mesh is not a candidate for reflexive space-of-Retzius reservoir placement. Preoperative planning includes:

- Review of operative notes where available
- Consideration of ectopic submuscular reservoir (behind the rectus abdominis, inferior to the arcuate line, above the posterior rectus sheath / transversalis fascia)
- Consideration of a two-piece device (AMS Ambicor, Rigicon Infla2P) that avoids a separate abdominal reservoir
- Discussion of malleable prosthesis for the highest-risk abdomens

In a matched retrospective cohort of 26 solid-organ transplant recipients (kidney, liver, heart or kidney-pancreas) and 26 age-matched controls, infection occurred in 4% versus 0% (P = 1.00) and reoperation in 11.5% versus 11.5% (P = 1.00), with no difference in reoperation rate between two-piece and three-piece devices. This small cohort, together with a 2021 systematic review, has been used to question a blanket rule against a three-piece device or abdominal reservoir in transplant recipients, while still recognizing altered surgical planes and possible adhesions. This is underpowered evidence, not proof of equivalent risk; keep the reservoir decision individualized.<sup>[[21]](#ref21)[[22]](#ref22)</sup>

See [reservoir placement](/docs/surgical-techniques/04j-sexual-dysfunction/penile-implants/reservoir-placement) for technical options.

### Peyronie's disease with ED

Patients with Peyronie's require preoperative documentation of curvature direction, degree, and deformity complexity (hourglass, hinge, notch, indentation). Decision-making includes whether intraoperative modeling alone will suffice, whether plication is planned at time of implant, and whether a tunica albuginea incision or grafting step is anticipated.<sup>[[10]](#ref10)</sup>

### Prior prosthesis explantation

A patient whose prior device was explanted for infection is at the highest risk of infection. Previous IPP infection was an independent risk factor for infection in the multicenter cohort (OR 4.67).<sup>[[7]](#ref7)</sup> Practice for these patients includes:

- Minimum 3-month (and ideally 6-month) interval from explantation
- Documentation of infection clearance (white count, ESR/CRP, and clinical exam)
- Preoperative imaging in selected cases (pelvic MRI to assess retained reservoir fragments, residual abscess)
- Counseling that reinfection remains a substantial risk despite best practice
- Consideration of malleable rather than inflatable reimplantation in the highest-risk scenarios

### Post-radiation

Pelvic radiation (for prostate or bladder cancer) increases infection risk, impairs wound healing, and can obscure fascial planes. Patients are counseled on higher complication rates, and delayed reimplantation is considered where possible.

### Phalloplasty / neophallus

Placement in a neophallus differs fundamentally: there are no native corpora, erosion rates are higher, and dedicated neocorporal techniques (wrapped cylinders, dedicated designs or malleable devices) are used. This is covered in the gender-affirming surgery section.

## Laboratory and Imaging

**Routine labs:** CBC, BMP, HbA1c, coagulation profile where anticoagulated, UA and culture (preoperative urine must be sterile; treat UTI before proceeding). HIV and hepatitis screening is selective, where clinically appropriate.

Review voiding symptoms and history of retention alongside the routine history, since clinically important bladder-outlet obstruction or retention is worth identifying before implantation; postoperative catheterization in a man with an unrecognized outlet problem raises erosion and infection concerns that should be weighed against the current, model-specific device instructions rather than treated as an absolute contraindication.<sup>[[23]](#ref23)</sup>

**Imaging** is not routinely indicated for primary IPP. Selective use:

- **Penile duplex ultrasound.** Where the ED etiology is unclear or venous leak is suspected
- **Pelvic MRI.** For complex Peyronie's with atypical curvature, for revision cases with suspected reservoir migration or abscess, and occasionally for neophallus planning
- **CT abdomen/pelvis.** Revision cases with suspected retained reservoir or tubing, or where abdominal anatomy is substantially altered
- **Cystoscopy.** Reserved for a clinical indication, such as assessing urethral patency or prostatic enlargement when bladder-outlet obstruction is a concern<sup>[[23]](#ref23)</sup>

## Informed Consent — What Is Unique

Beyond the generic surgical consent elements, IPP implantation requires specific disclosures:

- **Natural erections will no longer be possible.** The corporal dilation destroys the sinusoidal architecture. This is irreversible.
- **Length is what it is.** The implant will not make the penis longer than its maximum stretched state. Many patients report subjective shortening after surgery even with length-expanding cylinders.
- **Orgasm, ejaculation and sensation.** Document each separately before surgery and discuss expectations against that baseline; enhanced function should not be promised. Ejaculation and orgasm are distinct; removal of the prostate and seminal vesicles usually markedly reduces or abolishes antegrade ejaculation, and radiotherapy for prostate cancer can also impair it. Orgasm can occur without ejaculation.<sup>[[25]](#ref25)</sup> For a man being implanted after radical prostatectomy, use the separate orgasmic-function data: in a prospective cohort of 499 men, orgasmic function was worse at a median 36 months in 60% and recovery plateaued at 15 to 21 months, with younger age (50 or younger), bilateral nerve sparing and better preoperative erectile function and desire predicting a better outcome. A comparable decline has been reported after radiation therapy for prostate cancer.<sup>[[16]](#ref16)[[17]](#ref17)</sup>
- **The device is mechanical** and can fail. In a series of 2,384 first-time implants spanning two decades, revision-free survival for all reasons was 68.5% at 10 years and 59.7% at 15 years; newer models perform better.<sup>[[11]](#ref11)</sup>
- **Infection risk** was 1.9% overall in a multicenter cohort, and is higher in diabetic patients, revision cases and patients with prior infection.<sup>[[7]](#ref7)</sup> Infection typically mandates explantation.
- **The penis will feel different.** The glans sits at the end of two rigid cylinders and will not engorge.
- **Partner involvement.** Partner expectations are reviewed again in consent.
- **Activation timing.** Usually 4–6 weeks postop; patients anticipating a specific upcoming event (wedding, vacation) should plan accordingly.

Many high-volume implanters have patients watch a manufacturer-provided device video and handle a demo device during the consent visit, a practice intended to reduce postoperative dissatisfaction and medicolegal exposure.

## Preoperative Infection-Prevention Bundle

The infection-prevention protocol begins weeks before the case. See the [infection article](/docs/surgical-techniques/04j-sexual-dysfunction/penile-implants/infection) for evidence base. The preop elements are:

- **Glycemic optimization** (HbA1c &lt;8.5% target)
- **MRSA nasal screen** and decolonization of carriers (institutional practice)
- **Smoking cessation** ≥4 weeks
- **Chlorhexidine body wash** the night before and the morning of surgery, performed by the patient
- **No shaving at home.** Hair is clipped in the OR
- **Preoperative antibiotics** per institutional protocol. The AUA best practice statement lists an aminoglycoside with a first- or second-generation cephalosporin, aztreonam with a cephalosporin, or an aminoglycoside with vancomycin, for less than 24 hours (the statement text advises no doses after wound closure);<sup>[[9]](#ref9)</sup> perioperative intravenous antifungal use was associated with lower infection risk in a retrospective cohort (OR 0.22)<sup>[[7]](#ref7)</sup>

## Documentation Essentials

A prosthetic urology clinic note before the OR booking should contain:

- Failed or contraindicated medical therapy trials (with dose, duration, response)
- Baseline SHIM / IIEF score
- Stretched and flaccid penile length
- Partner discussion summary
- Specific device selected and rationale (3-piece, 2-piece or malleable; brand)
- Risk factors acknowledged (diabetes, radiation, prior surgery, revision status)
- CURSED screen documented
- All major complications discussed by name
- Patient's stated expectations (verbatim quotes are protective)
