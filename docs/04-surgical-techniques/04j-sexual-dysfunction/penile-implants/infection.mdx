# Penile Prosthesis Infection — Evolution, Evidence, and Modern Controversies

Inflatable penile prosthesis (IPP) implantation is associated with high patient satisfaction in most series, and infection is its most serious complication.<sup>[[16]](#ref16)</sup> An infected device usually means explantation or salvage surgery, with the risks of cavernosal fibrosis, penile shortening and a return to the operating room. This article covers how infection rates have fallen, the factors credited with the decline and the current controversies over prophylaxis and irrigation.

The infection rate was 1.9% across primary and revision cases combined in a retrospective multicenter cohort of 5,261 patients.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup> In a 2025 analysis of 18,475 hospital admissions, the infection rate was 3.1% at a median of 3 years, and 2.5% occurred within the first 6 months. It was 5.6% in patients with three or more risk factors versus 2.8% in those with none.<sup>[[2]](#ref2)</sup> Registry analyses of coated devices reported relative reductions in infection of roughly 50% to 70% compared with uncoated devices (see Historical Evolution).

See also: [Penile Implants overview](/docs/surgical-techniques/04j-sexual-dysfunction/penile-implants), [Inflatable Penile Prosthesis (biomaterial)](/docs/foundations/tools/biomaterials/prosthetics/inflatable-penile-prosthesis), [Artificial Urinary Sphincter](/docs/foundations/tools/biomaterials/prosthetics/artificial-urinary-sphincter).

## Historical Evolution

The trajectory of IPP infection over the last fifty years follows three inflection points.

### Pre-coating era (1970s–2001)

Early series of first-generation inflatable devices reported infection rates in the 8–11% range, falling to 3–5% by the early 2000s with gradual improvements in perioperative antibiotic protocols, sterile technique and patient selection.<sup>[[3]](#ref3)</sup> Registry data for uncoated devices in the same period gave lower figures: 1.61% at 180 days (AMS) and 4.6% at 11 years (Coloplast).<sup>[[4]](#ref4)[[7]](#ref7)</sup> Coagulase-negative *Staphylococcus*, particularly *S. epidermidis*, dominated the microbial profile. Infections were framed as failures of sterile technique, a paradigm that would shift substantially with the recognition of biofilm biology.

### Antibiotic-coating era (2001–2002)

The second major inflection came with the introduction of antibiotic-impregnated devices.

- **American Medical Systems (now Boston Scientific)** released the **InhibiZone** coating, a factory-applied combination of rifampin and minocycline that elutes locally after implantation. In a rabbit implantation model, InhibiZone devices kept larger zones of inhibition against *S. aureus* than vancomycin-dipped Titan implants through day 14.<sup>[[30]](#ref30)</sup>
- **Mentor (now Coloplast)** followed in 2002 with the **hydrophilic Titan**, which uses a polyvinylpyrrolidone surface that absorbs whatever antibiotic solution the surgeon chooses to dip the device in immediately before placement.<sup>[[6]](#ref6)</sup>

Carson's 2004 analysis of manufacturer-collected forms for 4,205 original AMS 700 implants found infection at 180 days of 0.68% with InhibiZone and 1.61% without.<sup>[[4]](#ref4)</sup> Wolter and Hellstrom's 2004 manufacturer-database analysis reported 1.06% (25/2,357) for the coated Titan and 2.07% (10/482) for the uncoated Alpha-1 at one year.<sup>[[6]](#ref6)</sup> Manufacturer registry follow-up reported infection-related revision in 4.6% of uncoated and 1.4% of hydrophilic-coated Coloplast implants at 11 years, and continued follow-up of InhibiZone implants to 7.7 years.<sup>[[5]](#ref5)[[7]](#ref7)</sup> These are retrospective, voluntarily reported data.

From 2003 onward, essentially every IPP sold in the United States has been coated.

### No-touch + antifungal era (2015–present)

The third inflection, more gradual and ongoing, came over the last decade through three convergent forces:
- Widespread adoption of the no-touch surgical technique
- Recognition of the biofilm paradigm
- Emerging understanding that antifungal coverage matters

Infection rates in high-volume series are now around 1%. Eid's single-surgeon series of 3,342 implants (2002–2014) reported a fall from 5.3% in 2002 to 1.99% with antibiotic-impregnated devices and to 0.44% after adding the no-touch technique in 2006.<sup>[[8]](#ref8)</sup>

## The Microbial Landscape Shift

Wilson and Costerton proposed that coated implants reduced infections with indolent coagulase-negative *Staphylococcus* and left a residual infection population of more virulent organisms.<sup>[[3]](#ref3)</sup>

Gross and colleagues' 2017 multicenter analysis of 227 intraoperative cultures from infected three-piece IPPs (25 centers, 2002–2016) described the organisms involved:<sup>[[9]](#ref9)</sup>

- **No growth** occurred in 33% of cultures.
- **Gram-positive** and **gram-negative** organisms were found in 73% and 39% of positive cultures, respectively.
- ***Candida*** species (11.1%), **anaerobes** (10.5%) and **MRSA** (9.2%) together made up nearly one third of the 153 positive cultures.
- **Multi-organism** infection occurred in 25% of positive cultures.

The organisms were not covered by the AUA and EAU guideline regimens in at least 14% to 38% of cases. A review of fungal IPP infections found diabetes in 69% of them and *Candida albicans* in 62% of isolates.<sup>[[10]](#ref10)</sup>

Prophylaxis regimens designed for the older spectrum may therefore leave some causative organisms uncovered.

## Drivers of Decline (Ranked by Impact)

Six factors are credited with the decline. No study has ranked them, so the order below is approximate.

### 1. Antibiotic-coated devices

The largest reduction reported. Registry analyses found relative reductions of roughly 50% to 70% with both InhibiZone and hydrophilic Titan devices.<sup>[[4]](#ref4)[[6]](#ref6)[[7]](#ref7)</sup>

### 2. The no-touch technique

Developed by J. François Eid, this uses an adhesive barrier drape after skin preparation to ensure that neither the surgeon's gloves nor the implant ever contacts the patient's skin during placement. Combined with separate gloves and instruments for skin and device-handling phases, frequent glove changes, and minimization of operative time and OR traffic, this protocol accompanied an infection rate of 0.44% in Eid's single-surgeon series, which compared eras rather than randomized patients.<sup>[[8]](#ref8)</sup>

### 3. Modern skin antisepsis

2% chlorhexidine gluconate in 70% isopropyl alcohol (ChloraPrep) has replaced povidone-iodine for skin preparation. In a randomized trial of 100 patients undergoing genitourinary prosthetic surgery, post-preparation skin cultures were positive in 8% with chlorhexidine-alcohol and 32% with povidone-iodine.<sup>[[32]](#ref32)</sup> A prospective cohort study combining ChloraPrep, fibrin sealant hemostasis and no drain reported 0 infections in 103 primary IPP cases versus 3.5% (4/115) in historical controls, who had povidone-iodine preparation and routine drains.<sup>[[11]](#ref11)</sup>

### 4. Patient optimization

- **Glycemic control.** HbA1c targets generally below 8.5% (no validated cutoff exists)
- **MRSA screening and decolonization** with mupirocin and chlorhexidine body washes for known carriers is institutional practice. ASHP 2013 advises adding a single preoperative vancomycin dose for patients known to be MRSA colonized, and its data on mupirocin decolonization are limited.<sup>[[34]](#ref34)</sup>
- **Smoking cessation** for at least four weeks preoperatively

Diabetes is associated with approximately 1.7-fold increased infection risk in multivariable analysis.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup>

### 5. Center-of-excellence effect

Higher-volume implanters are reported to have better outcomes, including lower infection rates, in registry data. Brant's national cohort found high-volume surgeons were more likely to use AUA-adherent regimens (OR 2.21).<sup>[[13]](#ref13)</sup>

### 6. Antibiotic stewardship

Retrospective data indicate that extended IV courses and postoperative oral antibiotics are not associated with lower infection rates. In the 2025 PUMP analysis, IV prophylaxis beyond 24 hours (P = 0.2) and postoperative oral antibiotics (P = 0.5) showed no protective association, and Dropkin's single-institution series of 222 men found similar infection outcomes with and without postoperative antibiotics.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[20]](#ref20)</sup>

## The AUA Guideline Controversy

The American Urological Association published its Best Practice Statement on urologic surgery antimicrobial prophylaxis in 2008 (reviewed in 2011 and archived in 2019). The current statement is the 2020 Best Practice Statement.<sup>[[12]](#ref12)</sup> For implanted prosthetic devices, including IPPs, its table lists an aminoglycoside with a first- or second-generation cephalosporin, aztreonam with a cephalosporin, or an aminoglycoside with vancomycin, for under 24 hours. A table footnote states that routine vancomycin is not recommended.<sup>[[12]](#ref12)</sup>

Brant and colleagues noted that no level-1 evidence supports the vancomycin-gentamicin combination for IPP surgery.<sup>[[13]](#ref13)</sup>

### Practice adoption

In Brant and colleagues' analysis of 26,574 IPP surgeries in a national database, 67% received an AUA-adherent regimen, with a 42% relative increase in use after the 2008 statement, particularly of vancomycin and gentamicin. Nonadherence to the recommended regimen was not independently associated with 90-day explant (OR 1.16, 95% CI 0.78–1.71).<sup>[[13]](#ref13)</sup>

### The PUMP reassessment

The **Prosthetic Urology Multi-Institutional Partnership (PUMP)** is a multi-institutional collaborative (institutions in North America, Europe and Korea) whose retrospective analyses have challenged the AUA regimen.

**Barham et al., 2023** (*J Urol*, 4,161 primary IPP cases, retrospective) compared vancomycin plus gentamicin alone (2,411 patients) with all other regimens (1,750). Crude infection rates were similar (1% vs 1.2%). In the adjusted model, vancomycin plus gentamicin alone was associated with a higher infection hazard (HR 2.7, 95% CI 1.4–5.4, p = 0.004).<sup>[[14]](#ref14)</sup> Barham defined the AUA regimen as vancomycin plus gentamicin alone, a narrower regimen than the several combinations in the AUA statement.

**Abou Chawareb et al., 2025** (retrospective, 5,261 patients at 16 centers) reported:<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup>

- Perioperative **IV antifungal use** was associated with lower infection risk (OR 0.22, p &lt; 0.001).
- Postoperative oral antibiotics (P = 0.5) and IV prophylaxis beyond 24 hours (P = 0.2) showed no protective association.
- Diabetes (OR 1.68) and prior IPP infection (OR 4.67) were the independent patient-level risk factors.

A multicenter retrospective cohort of 804 diabetic patients (18 institutions) found more device infections with AUA-recommended prophylaxis than with nonstandard prophylaxis (5.6% vs 1.9%; OR 2.8, 95% CI 1.1–7.3).<sup>[[15]](#ref15)</sup> Common nonstandard regimens were vancomycin-gentamicin-fluoroquinolone, clindamycin-fluoroquinolone and vancomycin-fluoroquinolone.

No randomized trial has compared prophylaxis regimens. The 2020 AUA statement predates these analyses. The 2025 *Sexual Medicine Reviews* recommendations from the 5th International Consultation on Sexual Medicine are the most recent expert consensus document.<sup>[[16]](#ref16)</sup>

## Antifungal Prophylaxis — the Strongest Modifiable Signal

In the 2023 PUMP analysis, antifungal use was associated with a markedly lower infection hazard (HR 0.08, 95% CI 0.03–0.19), the largest effect in the modern IPP infection literature.<sup>[[14]](#ref14)</sup> The 2025 cohort found a smaller association (OR 0.22 for IV antifungal use).<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup> Both analyses are retrospective.

A systematic review of nine retrospective studies found fungi responsible for 11.1% of IPP infections and concluded that antifungal prophylaxis is warranted.<sup>[[17]](#ref17)</sup> A 2024 review supports antifungal use, particularly in high-risk populations.<sup>[[10]](#ref10)</sup>

Fluconazole 400 mg preoperatively is a commonly reported regimen; route and duration vary between series, and one regimen described in the 2024 review continued fluconazole 200 mg daily for 14 days.<sup>[[10]](#ref10)</sup> That review reserves liposomal amphotericin B for established infections.

### Adoption patterns

A 2025 multicenter survey of SMSNA, SUPS, and GURS members found antifungal use in:<sup>[[18]](#ref18)</sup>

- **25.9%** of primary cases
- **72.3%** of diabetic cases
- **65.2%** of salvage cases
- **48.2%** of revision cases

In vitro data show that adding fluconazole or amphotericin B to dipping solutions does not reduce antibacterial activity on hydrophilic surfaces.<sup>[[19]](#ref19)</sup>

## The Modern Antibiotic Regimen

There is no universally agreed best regimen, and no randomized controlled trial has identified one. The following principles reflect the PUMP investigators' expert practice, not a guideline recommendation:

### Principle 1 — Tailor to your local antibiogram

When the PUMP investigators applied this principle to three surgeons at three different institutions, they found three different ideal regimens. Pulling the institution's *Candida* and gram-negative resistance data should be a first step in any new practice.

### Principle 2 — Cover MRSA, gram-negatives broadly, and Candida

A reasonable contemporary regimen:

| Agent | Coverage |
|---|---|
| Vancomycin | MRSA + gram-positive |
| Gram-negative agent* | Gram-negatives |
| Fluconazole 400 mg IV | *Candida* |

*Gram-negative options: gentamicin, piperacillin-tazobactam, or ceftriaxone, depending on local sensitivity patterns. The 2024 review describes an adopted regimen of vancomycin plus piperacillin-tazobactam plus fluconazole based on local antibiograms.<sup>[[10]](#ref10)</sup> The 2020 AUA statement states that routine vancomycin is not recommended.<sup>[[12]](#ref12)</sup> The 2013 ASHP guideline advises adding a single preoperative vancomycin dose when MRSA colonization is known.<sup>[[34]](#ref34)</sup>

### Principle 3 — Single perioperative dose, stopped within 24 hours

2025 PUMP data show no association between extended IV courses or postoperative oral antibiotics and lower infection.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup> The 2020 AUA statement also advises against postoperative continuation of prophylaxis.<sup>[[12]](#ref12)</sup>

### Principle 4 — Timing within 60 minutes of incision

For cefazolin or cephalosporin-based regimens. Vancomycin requires longer infusion and should be started earlier.

### Principle 5 — Skip routine postoperative oral antibiotics

Retrospective series found no protective association with postoperative oral antibiotics.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[20]](#ref20)</sup> The AUA statement notes that prolonged prophylaxis has not shown benefit in randomized trials of other procedures.<sup>[[12]](#ref12)</sup>

## Dipping Solutions — Device-Specific

The choice of dipping or irrigation solution is fundamentally device-specific.

### AMS 700 InhibiZone

The AMS 700 instructions for use state that InhibiZone components should not be soaked in saline or other solutions before implantation, and may be briefly rinsed or dipped in a sterile solution immediately before implantation. They also warn that contact with alcohols, acetone and other nonpolar solvents can remove the antibiotics.<sup>[[33]](#ref33)</sup> The antibiotic is applied during manufacture, so surgeons typically irrigate the field around the device with:
- Sterile saline
- Antibiotic-fortified saline (vancomycin and gentamicin)
- Or 0.05% chlorhexidine gluconate, based on bench compatibility data and a retrospective cohort (see Irrisept discussion below)

### Coloplast Titan (hydrophilic)

The device is dipped in the chosen antibiotic solution immediately before placement, commonly for at least 30 seconds. The best-evidenced dip is vancomycin plus gentamicin (commonly vancomycin 1 g/L plus gentamicin 80–160 mg/L). In Towe et al.'s 2020 retrospective cohort of 468 diabetic men receiving a Titan, this dip was associated with lower rates of infection (1.4% vs 6.4%), explantation and revision than all other dips combined, and rifampin-containing dips trended toward more infection (P = 0.057).<sup>[[21]](#ref21)</sup>

In vitro, adding fluconazole or amphotericin B to the dip did not reduce antibacterial activity on the hydrophilic surface.<sup>[[19]](#ref19)</sup>

---

## Irrigation — What NOT to Use

Several solutions historically used in IPP surgery should now be avoided in the wound:

### Povidone-iodine (Betadine) wound irrigation

Associated with increased infection rates. In Manka et al.'s retrospective single-surgeon study (217 patients), half-strength betadine irrigation was associated with more infections than vancomycin-gentamicin irrigation (adjusted OR 9.3, p = 0.025).<sup>[[31]](#ref31)</sup> The same institution's later report found a relative risk of 16.9 with betadine irrigation (infection 0.9% after it was discontinued).<sup>[[22]](#ref22)</sup> Povidone-iodine is used for skin preparation, not as wound irrigation.

### Hydrogen peroxide

Despite its historical inclusion in the original Mulcahy salvage washout protocol, it is now generally avoided because of concerns about:
- Tissue cytotoxicity
- Impaired wound healing
- Corporal air embolism

### Bacitracin-polymyxin and TMP-SMX-gentamicin combinations

As primary dipping solutions, the grouped non-vancomycin-gentamicin dips were associated with more infections than vancomycin-gentamicin in Towe's diabetic cohort.<sup>[[21]](#ref21)</sup>

### 0.05% chlorhexidine gluconate (Irrisept) on hydrophilic devices

Discussed in detail below.

## Irrisept — Introduction and Mechanism

**Irrisept** is a self-pressurized lavage bottle containing 0.05% chlorhexidine gluconate in sterile water. It is FDA-cleared as an antimicrobial wound irrigation device. It was adopted in orthopedic and breast reconstructive surgery before prosthetic urology.

The proposed advantages of CHG irrigation in IPP surgery are these:

- The cationic CHG molecule binds to anionic components of microbial cell walls and disrupts membrane integrity, and in vitro it reduced colony counts 3 to 6 log10 for aerobes, anaerobes and *Candida* on Titan discs.<sup>[[23]](#ref23)</sup>
- The mechanical jet lavage adds physical biofilm disruption.
- Low tissue toxicity and negligible systemic absorption are assumed.
- CHG is not an antibiotic, so it is attractive in an era of antimicrobial stewardship, although long-term resistance data in this setting are lacking.

---

## Irrisept Evidence — InhibiZone (AMS) Devices

**No harm signal in the available data.**

- **Im et al. 2024** (bench study): the InhibiZone surface retained its antimicrobial activity after 2-minute exposure of reservoir discs to 0.05% CHG, compared with saline and vancomycin-gentamicin.<sup>[[24]](#ref24)</sup>
- **Razdan et al. 2023** (4 men who had infected three-piece IPPs salvaged with Irrisept irrigation and malleable prostheses, median follow-up 4.8 months): no infections. Hypothesis-generating only.<sup>[[25]](#ref25)</sup>

**Ivan et al. 2026** (*J Sex Med*, 761 antibiotic-impregnated IPP cases at 14 institutions, retrospective) found that 0.05% CHG was not associated with infection requiring explantation (1.9% versus 2.0%, p = 0.9).<sup>[[26]](#ref26)</sup> This is the largest clinical dataset on CHG with impregnated devices. Follow-up was shorter with CHG (median 4 vs 8 months), the comparator was historical and infections were few, so the study has limited power to exclude a difference.

---

## Irrisept Evidence — Hydrophilic Titan Devices: The 2026 Pivot

The data for 0.05% CHG with hydrophilic Coloplast Titan devices tells a fundamentally different story, and represents the most important recent shift in IPP infection prevention practice.

**Ivan et al. 2026** (*J Urol*, 2,150 hydrophilic IPP cases at 14 institutions, retrospective) found that intraoperative 0.05% CHG was associated with a higher infection rate than antimicrobial irrigation and dip (OR 2.07, 95% CI 1.37–3.76):<sup>[[27]](#ref27)</sup>

| Subgroup | CHG | Antibiotic-only |
|---|---|---|
| Overall hydrophilic IPP | 4.6% | 2.1% (p &lt; 0.001) |
| Primary surgery | 3.5% | — |
| Revision surgery | 12% | — |

The authors concluded that caution with 0.05% CHG is warranted in hydrophilic IPP surgery pending prospective evaluation.

Two other studies point the same way:

- **Helo et al. 2025** (*J Sex Med*, retrospective, Titan implants) compared a period of antibiotic dip and irrigation (0 of 320 infected) with a period of 0.05% CHG dip and irrigation (13 of 377 infected, p &lt; 0.001). Within the CHG cohort, diabetes, revision surgery and longer operative time were independent predictors of infection.<sup>[[28]](#ref28)</sup>
- **Simhal et al. 2024** (*in vitro*): vancomycin-gentamicin dipping gave approximately 5.5 log reduction of bacterial adhesion to hydrophilic surfaces, while 0.05% CHG dipping gave approximately 1.5 log. Irrigation with CHG or saline removed the dip solution adsorbed to the surface.<sup>[[29]](#ref29)</sup>

Karpman et al. found 3 to 6 log10 reductions with Irrisept on Titan discs in vitro, but compared it only with saline, not with antibiotic dips.<sup>[[23]](#ref23)</sup>

---

## Why the Device-Specific Difference?

The two coating chemistries differ in how they respond to CHG irrigation.

### InhibiZone

Rifampin and minocycline are impregnated into the silicone components during manufacture and elute over time. In vitro, InhibiZone retained its antimicrobial activity after CHG exposure, and the clinical cohort found no association with infection.<sup>[[24]](#ref24)[[26]](#ref26)</sup>

### Hydrophilic Titan

The antibiotic protection on a Titan comes from the surgeon-applied dip absorbed by the polyvinylpyrrolidone coating. In vitro, CHG or saline irrigation removed the dip solution from the surface, and CHG alone gave much less bacterial reduction than the antibiotic dip (about 1.5 vs 5.5 log).<sup>[[29]](#ref29)</sup> This may explain the clinical association with more infections, but the mechanism has not been shown in patients.

**Practical implication:** the authors of the 2026 cohort advise caution with 0.05% CHG in hydrophilic devices pending prospective data, particularly in revision and salvage cases where infection risk is already elevated.

## Revision and Salvage

Revision and salvage surgery carry higher infection risk than primary implantation.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup> Historical revision infection rates reached 10–13%, and modern washout-protocol revisions are reported at approximately 3% in experienced hands. Prior IPP infection was the largest patient-level risk factor for subsequent infection in the 2025 PUMP cohort (OR 4.67).<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup>

### Modified-Mulcahy salvage washout protocol

1. Explant all components
2. Sequential antiseptic washes (avoid hydrogen peroxide and excess betadine in modern practice)
3. Change all gloves and instruments
4. Re-prep and re-drape the field
5. Replace with a new antibiotic-coated implant

### Contemporary considerations (2026)

- Capsule and pseudocapsule cultures sent routinely (with next-generation sequencing where available)
- **Fresh antibiotic-coated device** used even in presumed non-infectious revisions
- **Antifungal coverage added routinely** in salvage and revision settings
- 0.05% CHG is avoided by some surgeons in revisions involving hydrophilic devices, given the 12% infection rate with CHG in this subgroup of the Ivan 2026 cohort<sup>[[27]](#ref27)</sup>

## Practical Synthesis — What to Do in 2026

### Preoperatively
- MRSA screening with mupirocin and chlorhexidine decolonization in carriers (institutional practice)
- Glycemic control (HbA1c target generally &lt;8.5%)
- Smoking cessation ≥4 weeks
- **ChloraPrep** skin antisepsis

### Perioperative antibiotics
- **Vancomycin plus gram-negative agent (per local antibiogram) plus fluconazole 400 mg IV**
- Single dose, stopped within 24 hours
- **Skip postoperative oral antibiotics**

### Device selection
- Always include an antibiotic coating
- **InhibiZone:** do not dip
- **Titan:** dip vancomycin 1 g/L plus gentamicin 80 mg/L for ≥30 sec; add antifungal in high-risk patients

### Surgical technique
- **No-touch barrier drape** protocol
- Frequent glove changes
- Minimize OR traffic and operative time
- Copious antibiotic-saline irrigation throughout

### Irrisept use — device-specific
| Device | CHG (Irrisept) |
|---|---|
| **AMS 700 InhibiZone** | Not associated with infection in one retrospective cohort<sup>[[26]](#ref26)</sup> |
| **Coloplast Titan** (hydrophilic) | Associated with more infections in two retrospective cohorts; caution<sup>[[27]](#ref27)[[28]](#ref28)</sup> |
| **Revision of hydrophilic IPP** | Highest signal (12% infection with CHG)<sup>[[27]](#ref27)</sup> |

Use vancomycin-gentamicin-fortified saline irrigation for hydrophilic cases instead.

### Postoperatively
- Avoid prolonged IV antibiotics
- Skip routine oral antibiotics
- Track outcomes in a personal infection registry from the first case; the PUMP analyses come from surgeons' tracked outcomes.

## Future Directions

- The 2020 AUA Best Practice Statement predates the PUMP analyses of prophylaxis regimens.
- **NCT06489431:** Irrisept versus traditional antibiotic irrigation for virgin penile prosthesis placement, a Phase 3 randomized trial at Rush University Medical Center (estimated 200 participants; active, not recruiting; primary completion listed as May 2026).
- Optimal antifungal strategy (universal versus risk-targeted) remains debated, with stewardship concerns about *Candida* resistance pushing back against the strong PUMP signal favoring routine use
- **Next-generation sequencing** of biofilms is revealing distinct microbiome signatures that may eventually enable individualized salvage protocols
- **Surface engineering beyond antibiotics** (anti-adhesion coatings, silver, hydrogels, quorum-sensing inhibitors) is under investigation.

---

## Bottom Line

Penile prosthesis infection rates have fallen to around 1–3% in modern series. Antibiotic-coated devices show the largest reduction in registry data, and the no-touch technique, chlorhexidine-alcohol skin preparation and patient optimization are also credited.

Infected devices show more MRSA, anaerobes and *Candida* than older series, and in retrospective analyses vancomycin plus gentamicin alone was associated with a higher adjusted infection risk than nonstandard regimens (HR 2.7). Prospective data are lacking.

**Antifungal prophylaxis** was associated with the largest reduction in infection in retrospective PUMP analyses (HR 0.08 in 2023; OR 0.22 in 2025).

**CHG irrigation differs by device.** It was not associated with infection on InhibiZone-coated AMS devices and was associated with more infections on hydrophilic Coloplast Titan devices. In vitro, CHG or saline irrigation removes the antibiotic dip from the hydrophilic surface.

Local antibiogram data and a personal infection registry support regimen choice.
