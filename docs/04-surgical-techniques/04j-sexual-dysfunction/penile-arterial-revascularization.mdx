# Penile Arterial Revascularization

**Penile arterial revascularization** is a microsurgical bypass procedure that restores arterial inflow to the penis in men with pure arteriogenic erectile dysfunction caused by focal arterial occlusive disease, most commonly after blunt pelvic, perineal or penile trauma. Vaclav Michal first described it in 1973. It is the only potentially curative surgical treatment for arteriogenic ED and is reserved for a highly selected patient population.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[2]](#ref2)</sup> The contemporary donor is the **inferior epigastric artery (IEA)**, anastomosed end-to-side to the dorsal penile artery (Michal II). Saphenous vein is not the standard donor.

Outcomes in the published series vary with patient selection. In Dabaja's 2014 series of 45 men referred for a second opinion on candidacy, 49% had a significant discrepancy between erectile hemodynamic testing and selective internal pudendal arteriography (SIPA). Repeat testing was normal in 73% of those discrepant cases, so those men were no longer candidates for surgery.<sup>[[3]](#ref3)</sup> The best outcomes have been reported in young (&lt; 55 years), non-smoking, non-diabetic men with pure arteriogenic ED, fewer than 2 vascular risk factors, and a history of focal pelvic, perineal or penile trauma.<sup>[[4]](#ref4)[[5]](#ref5)[[10]](#ref10)</sup>

The AUA 2018 guideline gives a conditional recommendation, Grade C for penile arterial reconstruction in young men with focal pelvic or penile arterial occlusion who lack generalized vascular disease or veno-occlusive dysfunction; it limits the procedure to a highly skilled surgeon at a center of excellence and advises counseling that long-term success is not well established.<sup>[[7]](#ref7)</sup> The ICSM standard-operating-procedures statement (Sohn 2013) limits recommendations to Grade D (expert opinion) given the absence of RCTs.<sup>[[8]](#ref8)</sup>

---

## Pathophysiology and rationale

Arteriogenic ED in this context results from focal endothelial injury and stenosis or occlusion of the distal internal pudendal, common penile, or proximal cavernosal artery, distinct from generalized atherosclerotic disease.<sup>[[2]](#ref2)[[1]](#ref1)</sup> Decreased arterial inflow lowers corporal oxygen tension, reduces cavernosal smooth-muscle volume and is followed by corporoveno-occlusive dysfunction, which current studies regard as an effect rather than the cause of the erectile failure.<sup>[[2]](#ref2)</sup>

The surgical goal is to bypass the occlusion and restore perfusion pressure to the cavernosal bodies via communicating branches between the dorsal and cavernosal arteries.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[9]](#ref9)</sup>

## Patient-selection criteria (locked)

| Criterion | Threshold |
|---|---|
| **Age** | &lt; 55 years (best results &lt; 50)<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[10]](#ref10)</sup> |
| **Etiology** | Pure arteriogenic; typically focal post-traumatic occlusion |
| **Vascular risk factors** | **&lt; 2** (smoking, diabetes, hypertension, hyperlipidemia, atherosclerosis)<sup>[[4]](#ref4)</sup> |
| **Atherosclerosis** | Absent — generalized atherosclerotic disease is a contraindication<sup>[[7]](#ref7)</sup> |
| **Veno-occlusive function** | Intact on DICC; absent corporal-fibrosis evidence<sup>[[11]](#ref11)[[12]](#ref12)</sup> |
| **Focal arterial lesion** | Confirmed on selective internal pudendal arteriography (SIPA); concordant with duplex Doppler findings<sup>[[3]](#ref3)[[13]](#ref13)</sup> |

---

## Preoperative workup

| Step | Purpose |
|---|---|
| **Duplex Doppler penile ultrasound (DUS)** | Screens for arterial insufficiency and veno-occlusive dysfunction. In Wegner's 325 men, peak systolic velocity below 20 cm/s occurred only in arteriogenic ED and end-diastolic velocity above 5 cm/s indicated veno-occlusive dysfunction. Only 14% had at least one hemodynamically relevant dorsal-to-cavernosal connection, which the bypass relies on.<sup>[[9]](#ref9)</sup> Variant arterial anatomy limits duplex accuracy (sensitivity 77%, specificity 85% against arteriography in 42 men).<sup>[[14]](#ref14)</sup> |
| **Dynamic infusion cavernosometry / cavernosography (DICC)** | Excludes corporoveno-occlusive dysfunction.<sup>[[11]](#ref11)[[12]](#ref12)</sup> |
| **Selective internal pudendal arteriography (SIPA)** | Defines focal arterial anatomy and the occlusion site; pudendal arteriography is described as the current gold standard for penile arterial evaluation.<sup>[[13]](#ref13)</sup> Arteriography is needed before arterial surgery because variant penile arterial anatomy was present in 83% of 42 men in one series.<sup>[[14]](#ref14)</sup> |
| **Concordance check** | DUS and DICC findings should agree with SIPA. Discrepancies prompt repeat testing.<sup>[[3]](#ref3)</sup> |

## Surgical techniques

The techniques below use the inferior epigastric artery (IEA) as the donor.

| Technique | Description | Use |
|---|---|---|
| **Michal I** | Direct IEA → cavernosal artery anastomosis | Largely abandoned because of poor results<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup> |
| **Michal II** | End-to-side IEA → dorsal penile artery; perfusion communicated to cavernosal artery via perforating branches | Described for focal post-traumatic occlusion in young men; comparative data are limited (see Outcomes)<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[5]](#ref5)</sup> |
| **Hauri** | IEA → dorsal penile artery with an additional arteriovenous shunt to the deep dorsal vein, which Hauri describes as lowering the risk of anastomotic thrombosis | Used when communicating branches between the dorsal and cavernosal arteries are present<sup>[[11]](#ref11)[[16]](#ref16)</sup> |
| **Furlow-Fisher modification of Virag V (FFV5)** | IEA end-to-side to deep dorsal vein only, which arterializes venous drainage | Used when no communicating branches are identified or the proximal common penile artery is obstructed<sup>[[11]](#ref11)[[6]](#ref6)</sup> |
| **Mannheim triple anastomosis** | IEA anastomosed to the dorsal artery and dorsal vein in sequence (modified by the Mannheim group) | Used in the German Mannheim series<sup>[[4]](#ref4)[[15]](#ref15)</sup> |

## Outcomes

| Series | n | Technique | Follow-up | Success | Notes |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Cookson 1993**<sup>[[12]](#ref12)</sup> | 50 | Microsurgical bypass ± venous | 24 mo (median) | 88% excellent or improved (48% excellent); 67% excellent in pure arterial ED vs 42% in mixed | Pure-arterial etiology significantly better than mixed |
| **Manning 1998**<sup>[[4]](#ref4)</sup> | 62 | Virag (7) / Hauri (13) / modified Mannheim (42) | 41 mo (mean) | 54% overall (34% spontaneous, 20% pharmacologically induced erections); 69% at age ≤ 50; 58% with &lt; 2 risk factors; 43% in diabetics; 39% at age &gt; 50 | Age, diabetes and risk factors predicted outcome |
| **Sarramon 1997**<sup>[[6]](#ref6)</sup> | 114 | Michal II (44) / modified Furlow-Fisher DDVA (70) | 17 mo (mean) | Good 47.8%, improved 14.6%, failed 37.4% | Deep dorsal vein arterialization did better than arterial bypass regardless of etiology; pure arteriogenic ED treated with Michal II did worst |
| **Kawanishi 2004**<sup>[[11]](#ref11)</sup> | 49 | Hauri / FFV5 | 5 yr | 67.5% subjective / 65.5% objective (Kaplan-Meier) | No difference between techniques; strict selection |
| **Kayıgil 2012**<sup>[[10]](#ref10)</sup> | 110 | Modified Furlow-Fisher | 73 mo (mean) | 63.6% at 5 yr; 92.8% with no risk factors | Mean IIEF-5 7.3 → 16.8; improvement defined as a rise of at least 5 IIEF-5 points |
| **Zuckerman 2012**<sup>[[5]](#ref5)</sup> | 17 | IEA → dorsal artery | 3.1 yr (mean) | 14/17 (82%) | Pelvic fracture urethral injury cohort; success defined as erections sufficient for intercourse with or without pharmacologic assistance |

The series above are retrospective and use different definitions of success. Results are better in young men without risk factors.<sup>[[4]](#ref4)[[10]](#ref10)[[11]](#ref11)</sup>

## Complications

| Complication | Rate | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| **Glans hyperemia / hypervascularization** | 4–13% (2/49 Kawanishi; 6.4% Kayıgil; 13% Manning) | Excessive arterial flow through the dorsal artery to the glans<sup>[[4]](#ref4)[[10]](#ref10)[[11]](#ref11)</sup> |
| **Shunt / anastomosis thrombosis** | 8% | Mannheim series<sup>[[4]](#ref4)</sup> |
| **Inguinal hernia** | 6.5% | Mannheim series<sup>[[4]](#ref4)</sup> |
| **Penile edema** | 2 of 17 men | Early postoperative, after pelvic fracture urethral injury<sup>[[5]](#ref5)</sup> |
| **Anastomotic hemorrhage** | 1 of 49 | Kawanishi<sup>[[11]](#ref11)</sup> |
| **Scar contracture** | 1 of 49 | Kawanishi<sup>[[11]](#ref11)</sup> |

## Endovascular alternatives

Older men with atherosclerotic arteriogenic ED are not candidates for microsurgical bypass. Endovascular revascularization with drug-eluting stents has been described for this group.

- **Schönhofen 2021** reported a single-center registry of 100 men (mean age 61.8 years) treated with a sirolimus-eluting stent. At 1 year, 55 of 97 (56.7%) improved by at least 4 IIEF-6 points. In an angiographic substudy, 46 of 52 stented lesions (88.5%) were patent at a mean of 9.6 months.<sup>[[17]](#ref17)</sup>
- **Mohan 2025** extended this registry to 147 men (mean age 63.5 years) with PDE5i-refractory ED. Of the 68 men (46%) who completed follow-up at a mean of 30 months, 54% (37/68) reached the 4-point IIEF-6 improvement.<sup>[[18]](#ref18)</sup>
- **Doppalapudi 2019** pooled 16 case series: clinical success was 63.2% and complications 4.9% for arterial insufficiency (162 patients). Success definitions varied between studies.<sup>[[19]](#ref19)</sup>
- **Diehm 2019** reported a 50-patient registry in which 30 of 46 (65%) improved by at least 4 IIEF-6 points at 12 months. Older men and men with hypertension improved less.<sup>[[20]](#ref20)</sup>

These reports are uncontrolled registries and case series, mostly from one center, in men who had not responded to PDE5 inhibitors.<sup>[[18]](#ref18)[[20]](#ref20)</sup>

## Guideline position

| Body | Position |
|---|---|
| **AUA 2018**<sup>[[7]](#ref7)</sup> | Conditional Recommendation, Grade C — penile arterial reconstruction may be considered for young men with focal pelvic / penile arterial occlusion without generalized vascular disease or veno-occlusive dysfunction |
| **ICSM SOP 2013** (Sohn)<sup>[[8]](#ref8)</sup> | Grade D (expert opinion); no RCTs available |

Guidelines do not treat penile arterial revascularization as a standard therapy. It may be offered in specialized centers to carefully selected patients.<sup>[[2]](#ref2)[[8]](#ref8)</sup>

---

## When to refer / when not to operate

- **Refer.** A young man (&lt; 55) with focal post-traumatic ED, PDE5i non-response, normal DICC, a focal lesion on SIPA, fewer than 2 vascular risk factors and no atherosclerosis.
- **Do not operate.** Generalized atherosclerosis, 2 or more risk factors, age &gt; 55, veno-occlusive dysfunction on DICC, mixed-etiology ED, a smoker who has not quit, or diabetes.
- **Endovascular alternative.** Consider it for atherosclerotic ED in older PDE5i non-responders at experienced centers.<sup>[[17]](#ref17)[[18]](#ref18)</sup>

---

## Postoperative management

Protocols vary by center.

- **Activity restriction.** Avoid intercourse, masturbation, cycling, and strenuous exercise for 4–6 weeks to allow anastomosis healing.
- **Postoperative duplex Doppler** at 4–6 weeks to confirm patency.
- **Anticoagulation / antiplatelet protocol** per local microvascular service.
- **PDE5i bridging** during the early postoperative window to support tumescence-driven flow.
- **PRO assessment.** IIEF / IIEF-5 at baseline, 3 mo, 6 mo, 12 mo, with annual follow-up given known late attrition of patency.
- **Smoking cessation** is a selection criterion in the Mannheim series, and success was highest in men without risk factors in the Kayıgil series.<sup>[[4]](#ref4)[[10]](#ref10)</sup>
