# Hemi-Kock Continent Stoma

A **Hemi-Kock continent stoma** combines an ileal augment with an intussuscepted ileal nipple that permits catheterization while resisting urinary leakage. The term *hemi-Kock* also describes a different configuration using a nipple as an afferent antireflux valve. These are alternative applications: one nipple does not simultaneously provide both a continent abdominal outlet and an antireflux ureteral inlet.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[2]](#ref2)[[3]](#ref3)</sup>

For shared reservoir and catheterization principles, see [Augmentation Cystoplasty](/docs/surgical-techniques/04b-bladder-reconstruction/bladder-augmentation) and [Principles of Continent Catheterizable Channels](/docs/surgical-techniques/04b-bladder-reconstruction/catheterizable-channels).

## Concept and Background

The Hemi-Kock derives from the Kock continent ileal reservoir, which Kock first used for continent cutaneous urinary diversion in Göteborg in 1975.<sup>[[12]](#ref12)</sup> The full Kock pouch is a stand-alone reservoir with two nipple valves: an afferent valve that prevents reflux and an efferent valve that forms the continent stoma.<sup>[[13]](#ref13)</sup> The Hemi-Kock was developed at the University of Southern California by Weinberg and colleagues in 1988. It keeps one nipple valve and joins a detubularized ileal plate to the native bladder.<sup>[[2]](#ref2)</sup>

| Feature | Full Kock pouch | Hemi-Kock |
|---|---|---|
| Reservoir | Stand-alone ileal pouch | Ileal plate joined to the native bladder; can be converted to a complete reservoir<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup> |
| Nipple valves | Two (afferent antireflux and efferent stoma) | One, used as the efferent stoma or as an afferent antireflux limb |
| Ileum | Larger segment | About 40 cm<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup> |
| Ureters | Implanted into the afferent limb | Native insertions can remain when the nipple is the efferent stoma; ureters or a previous conduit are joined to the afferent limb when the nipple is antireflux |
| Typical setting | Diversion after cystectomy | Augmentation with a continent stoma, or undiversion, in a patient with a retained bladder |

## Configurations and Selection

| Configuration | Function | Ureteral implications |
|---|---|---|
| **Continent abdominal stoma with augmentation** | Efferent nipple provides the catheterizable continence mechanism | Native ureteral insertions can remain when appropriate; additional ureteral reconstruction is not automatically required |
| **Antireflux augmentation / undiversion** | Afferent nipple separates a ureteral or previous conduit inlet from the reservoir | Ureters or a previous ileal conduit are connected to the afferent limb; this is not a “no ureteral surgery” operation |

Kreder's 30-patient series included both applications as well as conversion to a complete reservoir. Weinberg's initial seven-patient report concerned a nonrefluxing augmentation. Their outcomes should not be presented as one uniform catheterizable-stoma cohort.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[2]](#ref2)</sup>

Candidates need a reconstructable reservoir, acceptable bowel and metabolic reserve, a stoma they or a caregiver can catheterize, and a dependable lifelong follow-up plan. A damaged outlet may require a separate continence procedure or bladder-neck closure. Closure is a definitive choice requiring a reliable alternate drainage route and is not an obligatory step in every Hemi-Kock. In Herschorn's 18-patient series, the aim was to keep a competent urethra for additional access when possible. Ten patients had an anti-incontinence procedure and three had bladder-neck closure.<sup>[[4]](#ref4)[[3]](#ref3)</sup>

An afferent nipple has been used for markedly dilated ureters or undiversion, but the cited experience does not prove superiority over a tunneled ureteral implant. Nieh's review states that the nipple is superior to a tunneled implant for the dilated ureter, which is an expert opinion and not a comparative result. A continent stoma alone may also be constructed in selected patients without needing a large augment.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[4]](#ref4)[[20]](#ref20)</sup>

### Indications and patient selection

The Hemi-Kock has been used in these settings:<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[3]](#ref3)[[4]](#ref4)</sup>

- Adults with a complex lower urinary tract disorder who need both augmentation and a continent catheterizable channel.
- A devastated bladder outlet that requires bladder-neck closure.
- Dilated ureters that need an antireflux implant.
- Undiversion of a previous ileal conduit or other supravesical diversion.
- An unavailable appendix when a Monti channel alone would not give enough augmentation.

Prerequisites are adequate renal function for the metabolic load of an ileal segment, the ability to catheterize the stoma, motivation for lifelong catheterization and follow-up, and a surgeon familiar with continent ileal reservoir principles.<sup>[[2]](#ref2)</sup>

## Operative Principles

The details depend on whether the nipple is afferent or efferent and on the chosen modification. The following is an orientation guide, not a substitute for the original operative description:

1. **Define the complete reconstruction.** Plan reservoir capacity, ureteral drainage, native outlet management, stoma site, and postoperative access before bowel isolation.
2. **Select a vascularized ileal segment.** The historical hemi-Kock used approximately 40 cm of terminal ileum, divided between the augment and valve/channel. In the original descriptions, about 20–25 cm formed the plate and 15–17 cm the efferent limb and nipple, taken 15–20 cm proximal to the ileocecal valve, with ileoileal continuity restored. Preserve terminal ileum and bowel blood supply as anatomy permits; a fixed length is not appropriate for every patient.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[2]](#ref2)</sup>
3. **Prepare the intussuscepted nipple.** Remove a narrow strip of mesentery, about 7–8 cm along the segment to be telescoped, so that the bowel can intussuscept, and preserve the vascular arcades. Skinner's Kock series removed 8 cm, and Boyd's canine hemi-Kock experiment removed 7 cm. Excessive stripping risks ischemia; historical experimental dimensions are not universal instructions.<sup>[[13]](#ref13)[[14]](#ref14)</sup>
4. **Detubularize and reconfigure the augment.** Open the remaining segment along its antimesenteric border and fold it into a U or W shape. Join it broadly to the opened native bladder to support low-pressure storage.
5. **Orient the valve for its intended role.** An efferent continence nipple and an afferent antireflux nipple have different flow paths. Construct and route the catheterizable limb without tension or kinking and confirm easy catheter passage. The stoma is usually brought out at the umbilicus or the right lower quadrant; Hanna placed it at the umbilicus in children.<sup>[[16]](#ref16)</sup>
6. **Establish drainage and follow-up.** Ensure accessible catheter drainage and an explicit plan for an obstructed or uncatheterizable stoma.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[4]](#ref4)[[3]](#ref3)</sup>

Historical Kock reservoirs used nonabsorbable staples and collars that could become stone foci or contribute to erosion and obstruction. Later modifications altered fixation, collar use, and channel tapering. Results of different full-Kock, Hemi-Kock, and experimental valve designs cannot be combined into a single complication rate or a universally preferred construction.<sup>[[5]](#ref5)[[6]](#ref6)[[7]](#ref7)[[3]](#ref3)</sup>

### Nipple valve compared with flap valve

| | Nipple valve (Kock, Hemi-Kock) | Flap valve (Mitrofanoff, Monti) |
|---|---|---|
| Geometry | Intussuscepted bowel protrudes into the reservoir lumen | Channel runs along the inner wall in a submucosal tunnel |
| Closing force | Filling pressure compresses the nipple from outside | Filling pressure compresses the channel against the backing wall |
| Characteristic failure | Desussception (slippage), stenosis, stones on fixation material | Tunnel inadequacy, stenosis, erosion |

Nieh's review describes the nipple valves as technically demanding, with early and late complications and occasional catheterization problems, and reports that simple modifications in fixation, limited use of staples and mesh collars, and tapering of the stoma resolved much of this.<sup>[[20]](#ref20)</sup>

### Fixation and modifications

Fixation of the intussuscepted nipple has changed over time:

- **Original technique.** Metal staples and a nonabsorbable collar secured the nipple. In canine hemi-Kock reservoirs, calculi formed on exposed metal staples and Marlex collars, while absorbable (Polysorb) staples promoted healing and never served as a stone nidus. The authors recommended proper staple placement and no anchoring collar.<sup>[[14]](#ref14)</sup>
- **Mesentery exclusion and seromuscular diathermy.** In dogs, excluding mesentery from the intussusception and applying deep seromuscular diathermy gave a stable valve. Autosuture GIA stapling shortened operating time but did not improve stability.<sup>[[15]](#ref15)</sup>
- **Seromuscular stripping (Mori).** The nipple was created without metal staples in 17 patients with a Mainz pouch. Results were satisfactory in 15; two were incontinent, one from nipple prolapse and one from ischemic change of the valve.<sup>[[17]](#ref17)</sup>
- **Modified efferent fixation (Soulié).** In a 40-patient Kock pouch series, extussusception (8%) and efferent nipple prolapse (3%) occurred only in the 23 patients with the earlier technique, not in the 17 with modified fixation. Continence at 6 months was 78% and 94%, a difference that was not significant (P = 0.13).<sup>[[18]](#ref18)</sup>
- **Hanna modification in children.** Afferent and efferent limbs were fixed at two points, internally to the pouch wall and externally by apposed incisions and sutures, without staples or a Marlex collar. A continent, nonrefluxing reservoir was achieved in all 10 children.<sup>[[16]](#ref16)</sup>
- **Contemporary Hemi-Kock.** Herschorn's later cohort describes modified valve construction and tapering of the catheterizable limb (see below).<sup>[[3]](#ref3)</sup>

## Best Direct Long-Term Evidence

**Herschorn 2022** retrospectively reviewed 109 adults, median age 38 years, after Hemi-Kock catheterizable stoma with cystoplasty. At mean follow-up 10.4 years:<sup>[[3]](#ref3)</sup>

| Outcome | Finding | Interpretation |
|---|---|---|
| Managing with CIC, with or without pads | 98/109 (90%) | Functional use, **not a 90% complete-dryness rate** |
| Any secondary intervention | 70/109 (64.2%) | A substantial long-term maintenance burden |
| Less invasive interventions | More than 60% were endoscopic or under local anesthesia, mainly for stones | This describes the interventions, not a denominator of all patients |
| Valve revision | 14/109 (12.8%) | Valve revision is not synonymous with proven desussception |
| Revision before versus after modifications | 8/44 (18.2%) versus 6/65 (9.3%) | Sequential observational groups; cannot isolate the effect of one modification |

The earlier 18-patient Herschorn series reported 17 dry patients and one lost to follow-up at mean 26 months. Fifteen had augmentation and three a stoma alone; some also had outlet procedures. It should not be added to the later institutional cohort without assessing overlap.<sup>[[4]](#ref4)</sup>

The broader Cochrane review of intestinal urinary reconstruction found sparse, low-quality comparative trials. It does not establish Hemi-Kock superiority over appendicovesicostomy or Monti, or validate a particular nipple length or fixation method.<sup>[[8]](#ref8)</sup>

### Long-term durability of the Kock system

In the Göteborg series of 176 patients who had a Kock reservoir for continent urinary diversion between 1975 and 1999, almost 90% of the 126 known survivors had a well-functioning reservoir. Reoperation was frequent, mainly for nipple problems.<sup>[[12]](#ref12)</sup> This is full Kock experience, not Hemi-Kock experience. Nieh's review describes the Hemi-Kock reservoir as one of the most dependable and stable neobladders.<sup>[[20]](#ref20)</sup>

## Complications and Troubleshooting

- **Difficult catheterization, retention, or obstruction.** Assess promptly for channel angulation, stenosis, valve dysfunction, mucus, or stones. Avoid repeated forceful catheter attempts. Acute loss of drainage requires urgent urologic management.
- **Leakage.** Reassess reservoir pressure, emptying, and valve integrity before assuming the valve alone has failed.
- **Valve slippage, stenosis, ischemia, or erosion.** Management may require endoscopic treatment, valve revision, channel replacement, or conversion to a different drainage strategy.
- **Stones and recurrent symptomatic infection.** They remain causes of reintervention.
- **Augmentation-related morbidity.** Bowel obstruction, metabolic acidosis, nutritional deficiency, perforation, and long-latency malignancy still require lifelong attention.<sup>[[3]](#ref3)[[9]](#ref9)</sup>

The following figures come from full Kock pouch series and give the range of nipple-valve problems. They are not Hemi-Kock rates.

| Complication | Reported frequency | Source and setting |
|---|---|---|
| Urinary stones | 18/68 (26.5%) of Kock patients followed 12 months or longer, usually on exposed staples or the eroded nonabsorbable collar; 5.4% after Indiana pouch | Arai 1993 (mean follow-up 53 months)<sup>[[5]](#ref5)</sup> |
| Urinary stones | 42/95 (44%) after Kock pouch and 19% after Indiana pouch | Okada 1996<sup>[[6]](#ref6)</sup> |
| Efferent nipple malfunction | 21/95 (22%), mostly in the first year | Okada 1996<sup>[[6]](#ref6)</sup> |
| Afferent nipple malfunction | 26/95 (27%), arising several years after surgery | Okada 1996<sup>[[6]](#ref6)</sup> |
| Afferent nipple malfunction | 10/42 (24%): collar erosion (5), afferent limb stenosis (2), obstruction by a mucous plug or fungus ball (3) | Arai 1991 (mean follow-up 38 months)<sup>[[7]](#ref7)</sup> |
| Afferent antireflux valve complications | 79/802 (9.8%) patients: stones on staples 5.2%, stenosis 4.3%, prolapse 0.9%. 56 patients (7.0%) were treated endoscopically and 25 (3.1%) needed open revision | Stein 1996 (USC)<sup>[[19]](#ref19)</sup> |

Arai attributed most late afferent malfunction to the nonabsorbable collar and considered it avoidable.<sup>[[5]](#ref5)[[7]](#ref7)</sup> In the fecal Kock continent ileostomy, Fasth reported a 29% revision rate for late nipple valve dysfunction, mainly from sliding, and stapling did not significantly reduce sliding. That is a different organ system and is cited only for the fixation question.<sup>[[21]](#ref21)</sup>

Urinary salvage reports include conversion of a failed efferent limb to a flap-valve channel and construction of a secondary ileal nipple. Waldner transversely retubularized the short intact ileal segment of the original efferent limb in five women with a Kock pouch and embedded it in an extramural trough as a Mitrofanoff-type flap valve. All five were continent, without catheterization problems, at 6–28 months.<sup>[[10]](#ref10)</sup> A 28-patient ileocecal-pouch series reported 24 fully continent patients, four using a safety pad, and two needing a third continence mechanism at mean 65.4 months. Its reported 92% overall success is not the same as complete dryness and is not Hemi-Kock-specific.<sup>[[11]](#ref11)</sup> These are specialist salvage options.

Evidence from fecal continent ileostomies should not be used as a urinary Hemi-Kock salvage rate or operative recommendation. In particular, colorectal reports involving conversion to a Brooke ileostomy address a different organ system.

## Choosing Among Catheterizable Channels

| Option | Relevant distinction |
|---|---|
| [Appendicovesicostomy](/docs/surgical-techniques/04b-bladder-reconstruction/appendicovesicostomy) | Uses a suitable appendix and a flap-valve tunnel; additional augmentation may be needed |
| [Yang-Monti](/docs/surgical-techniques/04b-bladder-reconstruction/yang-monti) | Uses a short retubularized bowel segment; availability depends on bowel health, mesenteric reach, and prior surgery |
| Hemi-Kock | Provides an ileal augment with an intussuscepted continence mechanism; the trade-offs are greater bowel use and valve-related maintenance |

The Hemi-Kock has not been widely adopted compared with the flap-valve and ileocecal-valve procedures.<sup>[[3]](#ref3)</sup> Nieh's review cites its technically demanding valve construction and its early and late complications.<sup>[[20]](#ref20)</sup> Its niche is the adult who needs simultaneous augmentation and a continent stoma, particularly when a nipple valve is wanted for dilated ureters. The direct Hemi-Kock evidence is largely institutional observational experience. Cross-study percentages for dryness, stenosis, or revision are not a valid head-to-head ranking.<sup>[[3]](#ref3)[[8]](#ref8)</sup>

## Lifelong Follow-Up

For adult NLUTD reconstruction incorporating bowel, AUA/SUFU recommends annual clinical assessment, basic metabolic panel, and urinary tract imaging (Expert Opinion). Review catheterization, mucus management, stoma function, and nutritional risks. Cystoscopy is recommended for gross hematuria or symptomatic recurrent urinary tract infection (AUA/SUFU Moderate Recommendation). Unexplained suprapubic pain also warrants evaluation, and acute abdominal pain or sepsis may indicate perforation.<sup>[[9]](#ref9)</sup>
