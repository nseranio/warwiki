# Sigmoid Cystoplasty (Sigmoidocystoplasty)

**Sigmoid cystoplasty** is a form of [augmentation cystoplasty](/docs/surgical-techniques/04b-bladder-reconstruction/bladder-augmentation) that uses a detubularized segment of sigmoid colon to enlarge the bladder; it retains the native bladder, which distinguishes it from the [sigmoid colon neobladder](/docs/surgical-techniques/04c-urinary-diversion/sigmoid-neobladder), a total orthotopic replacement performed after cystectomy. It can improve capacity and compliance, with practical advantages including pelvic proximity and a thick wall for selected ureteral implantation. A retrospective comparison reported a trend toward less small-bowel obstruction (SBO) with sigmoid than ileal augmentation.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[2]](#ref2)</sup> The authors of that comparison favored sigmoid. Segment selection remains individualized.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup>

---

## Indications

The indications are the same as for other forms of augmentation cystoplasty: refractory low-compliance or overactive bladder. Common underlying conditions are neurogenic bladder, exstrophy, posterior urethral valves and refractory IC/BPS.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[3]](#ref3)[[4]](#ref4)</sup> For adults with NLUTD, the 2021 AUA/SUFU guideline states that clinicians may offer augmentation cystoplasty to select patients refractory to or intolerant of less invasive therapy (Statement 51, Conditional Recommendation; Grade C).<sup>[[22]](#ref22)</sup> For children with non-progressive neurogenic dysfunction and impaired storage, the 2023 CUA/PUC guideline recommends considering intestinal augmentation after clinical and urodynamic assessment and discussion of alternatives and complications (weak recommendation, level III); it does not prefer sigmoid.<sup>[[26]](#ref26)</sup> The EAU Neuro-Urology guideline (limited update, March 2026) recommends offering bladder augmentation for low bladder compliance or refractory neurogenic detrusor overactivity (strong).<sup>[[23]](#ref23)</sup>

Sigmoid is favored when:

- **Ureteral reimplantation** is needed, because the thick sigmoid wall accommodates a submucosal tunnel.<sup>[[3]](#ref3)[[5]](#ref5)</sup>
- **Bowel anatomy or prior surgery favors colon.** Improvement in constipation was reported in one selected series; constipation alone is not an established indication to choose a sigmoid augment.<sup>[[4]](#ref4)</sup>
- **Preserving small bowel** is desirable, although colonic surgery still carries bowel obstruction and anastomotic risks.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup>
- A Mitrofanoff channel is planned, and the sigmoid "tail" modification can stabilize the catheterizable stoma.<sup>[[5]](#ref5)</sup>

## Surgical Technique (Goodwin cup-patch)

1. **Segment isolation.** Approximately 15–25 cm of sigmoid is isolated on a perfused mesenteric pedicle. In Sato’s retroperitoneal-pedicle technique, a sigmoid artery is retained while selected branches, including the marginal artery at the oral end, are divided to gain tension-free reach.<sup>[[24]](#ref24)</sup>
2. **Bowel continuity restoration.** End-to-end or side-to-side colocolonic anastomosis.
3. **Detubularization.** Opened along the antimesenteric border. In a small 1986 comparison, volume-dependent contractions were present in 15/16 patients with tubular sigmoid versus 1/8 with sigmoid cup-patch at mean 19.4-month follow-up.<sup>[[6]](#ref6)</sup> These are selected cohort results, not guaranteed rates.<sup>[[6]](#ref6)[[2]](#ref2)</sup>
4. **Reconfiguration.** Folded into a U-shaped or cup-patch configuration (Laplace's law).
5. **Cystotomy.** The native bladder is bivalved.
6. **Anastomosis.** The sigmoid patch is sutured to the widely opened native bladder with watertight apposition.<sup>[[6]](#ref6)[[24]](#ref24)</sup>

**Sigmoid "tail" modification.** A 3–5 cm segment of nondetubularized colon is left intact and fixed to the posterior aspect of the anterior abdominal wall to stabilize a catheterizable stoma constructed from appendix or tapered ileum. It was described in seven children.<sup>[[5]](#ref5)</sup>

Concomitant ureteral reimplantation, outlet surgery, or a catheterizable channel may be appropriate for specific anatomy and function. Reported reimplantation frequencies reflect selected cohorts, not a requirement for all reflux.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[3]](#ref3)[[5]](#ref5)[[7]](#ref7)[[8]](#ref8)</sup>

## Outcomes

| Study / population | Reported outcome | Interpretation |
|---|---|---|
| Zhang 2012, 47 patients, mean 24 months | Mean capacity 160.6 → 468.5 mL; maximal detrusor pressure 31.1 → 10.9 cm H₂O | Retrospective; selected concomitant reimplantation<sup>[[3]](#ref3)</sup> |
| Zhang 2014, 52 patients, mean 49 months | Safe capacity 113.8 → 373.1 mL; compliance 2.96 → 14.07 mL/cm H₂O | Retrospective; not a direct ileal comparison<sup>[[4]](#ref4)</sup> |
| Hayashi 2006, 86 patients, mean 13.1 years | Normal creatinine clearance in 80/86; continence score improved in 61/86 | Improvement is not the same as complete dryness; eight patients had increased renal scarring<sup>[[7]](#ref7)</sup> |

In an 86-patient long-term series (Hayashi 2006, n=86, mean follow-up 13.1 yr), CrCl was normal in 93% and continence improved in 71%; bladder stones occurred in 21% but were absent at final review.<sup>[[7]](#ref7)</sup> Zhang 2014 (n=52, mean 49 mo) reported significant improvements in capacity, compliance, and renal function with all 52 reporting satisfaction and improved constipation; no overt acidosis or perforation was observed.<sup>[[4]](#ref4)</sup> Zhang's uncontrolled series had no ileal comparator or long-term risk estimate.<sup>[[4]](#ref4)</sup>

## Sigmoid vs. Ileum

| Feature | Sigmoid | Ileum |
|---|---|---|
| Bowel obstruction | Less small-bowel handling; lower-SBO trend in one retrospective comparison | Bowel handling and adhesions remain relevant |
| Anatomic proximity to bladder | Closer | Requires mobilization |
| Ureteral reimplantation | Thick wall; accommodates a submucosal tunnel | Thinner wall |
| Ileocecal valve | Preserved | Preserved |
| Bowel function | Improvement in constipation reported in one selected cohort | Bowel effects vary with segment, length, and baseline disease |
| Pathologic contractions in one retrospective study of colonic augmentation (segment not specified)<sup>[[9]](#ref9)</sup> | 43% after colocystoplasty overall (35 patients; sigmoid-specific rate unavailable) | 26% after ileocystoplasty (31 patients) |
| Mucus production | Clinically relevant mucus burden | Clinically relevant mucus burden; no dependable rank from these sources |

The disadvantage is a higher rate of persistent pathologic contractions: 43% with colocystoplasty versus 26% with ileocystoplasty in a long-term urodynamic study of 84 patients. All groups improved in this nonrandomized comparison.<sup>[[9]](#ref9)</sup>

## Complications

- **Persistent reservoir contractions.** Also occur with ileum and stomach. The 12.5% (1/8, cup-patch), 43% and 26% figures above describe separate cohorts and configurations.<sup>[[6]](#ref6)[[9]](#ref9)</sup> Zhang’s 52-patient sigmoid series required anticholinergics in 6/52 (11.5%).<sup>[[2]](#ref2)[[4]](#ref4)[[6]](#ref6)[[9]](#ref9)</sup>
- **Urinary tract stones.** Approximately 21% (18/86) in the largest long-term series; risk factors include continent stomas and exstrophy.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[7]](#ref7)</sup>
- **Recurrent febrile UTIs.** Approximately 21% (11/52).<sup>[[4]](#ref4)</sup>
- **Bowel dysfunction.** Approximately 10.6% in Zhang 2012; in Zhang 2014 all 52 patients reported improved constipation.<sup>[[3]](#ref3)[[4]](#ref4)</sup>
- **Adhesive intestinal obstruction.** Approximately 8.5% in Zhang 2012; the SBO rate trended lower than with ileocystoplasty in Shekarriz's comparison.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[3]](#ref3)</sup>
- **Bladder perforation.** The rate was comparable to that with ileocystoplasty and highest in neurogenic bladder.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup>
- **Vesicoureteral reflux.** Hayashi reported low-grade recurrence in 6/58 renal units reimplanted during augmentation (in five patients), and grade I reflux in two of 44 patients without reimplantation. These are different denominators and not a randomized comparison.<sup>[[7]](#ref7)</sup>
- **Deteriorating renal function.** 6.4% in Zhang 2012 (sigmoid) and 5.2% in Wang 2018 (augmentation cystoplasty, not restricted to sigmoid).<sup>[[3]](#ref3)[[8]](#ref8)</sup>

---

## Metabolic Disturbances

- Animal-model data: no metabolic advantage of ileum over colon; both cause comparable acidosis.<sup>[[10]](#ref10)</sup>
- Pediatric series: significant hyperchloremic acidosis in 10%, borderline acidosis in 27% after sigmoidocystoplasty.<sup>[[11]](#ref11)</sup>
- Calcium and phosphorus abnormalities in 16.5% and 43%, respectively, more frequent in acidotic patients, likely reflecting bony buffer activation; may delay growth in children.<sup>[[11]](#ref11)</sup>
- Renal insufficiency increased the need for acidosis treatment in a mixed-segment series, but 3/30 children with sigmoidocystoplasty and normal renal function developed significant hyperchloremic acidosis during 1–10 years of follow-up.<sup>[[2]](#ref2)[[11]](#ref11)</sup> Monitor bicarbonate regardless of baseline renal function.<sup>[[2]](#ref2)[[11]](#ref11)</sup>
- Zhang 2014 reported no obvious metabolic acidosis at mean 49 mo.<sup>[[4]](#ref4)</sup>

Treat documented acidosis with individualized alkali therapy and dependable emptying. Potassium-containing alkali requires caution in renal impairment or hyperkalemia.

## Histopathology and Malignancy Risk

Long-term histopathology of sigmoidocystoplasty:<sup>[[7]](#ref7)[[12]](#ref12)</sup>

- **Inflammation** in all cases at \>10-yr follow-up.
- In Hayashi’s 86-patient cohort, hyperplasia occurred in 9/86 (approximately 10%) and metaplasia in 4/86 (approximately 5%).<sup>[[7]](#ref7)</sup>
- No malignancy was reported in these follow-up cohorts; their size, duration, biopsy selection, and possible cohort overlap prevent a conclusion of zero lifetime risk.
- PCNA staining was significantly more intense in patients who stopped regular bladder irrigations and in those with bladder stones, suggesting these factors may promote mucosal proliferation.

Overall malignancy risk after augmentation:<sup>[[13]](#ref13)[[14]](#ref14)[[15]](#ref15)</sup>

- **Urothelial cell carcinoma** is the most common histologic type after colocystoplasty (versus adenocarcinoma after gastrocystoplasty).
- A retrospective German survey reported tumors in 1.58% of cystoplasties versus 0.05% of ileal neobladders and 0.02% of ileal conduits.<sup>[[14]](#ref14)</sup> These are crude reported proportions across different diseases and follow-up periods, not adjusted comparative or sigmoid-specific lifetime risks.
- A systematic review reported mean latency of 19 years.<sup>[[15]](#ref15)</sup> In a separate 16-patient malignancy series, median latency was 20 years, tumors were often advanced at diagnosis, and one-year survival was 56%.<sup>[[13]](#ref13)</sup>
- Older reports recommended endoscopy from five years; current AUA/SUFU guidance favors symptom-triggered evaluation in stable NLUTD. Gross hematuria, recurrent unexplained symptomatic UTI, or suprapubic pain warrant investigation without waiting for an anniversary.<sup>[[22]](#ref22)[[14]](#ref14)[[16]](#ref16)</sup>

## Seromuscular Colocystoplasty Lined With Urothelium (SCLU)

SCLU is a variant designed to avoid incorporating intestinal mucosa into the urinary tract. See [Seromuscular Colocystoplasty (SCLU)](/docs/surgical-techniques/04b-bladder-reconstruction/seromuscular-colocystoplasty) for the full technique and evidence.<sup>[[17]](#ref17)[[18]](#ref18)</sup>

1. A de-epithelialized (mucosa-stripped) sigmoid seromuscular patch is prepared, preserving the submucosa (to prevent patch contraction and fibrosis).<sup>[[18]](#ref18)</sup>
2. Native bladder urothelium is exposed by detrusorectomy at the dome.
3. The seromuscular patch is placed over the exposed urothelium, which then lines the augmented segment.

### SCLU outcomes<sup>[[19]](#ref19)[[20]](#ref20)[[21]](#ref21)</sup>

- Capacity increased 1.8–2.96-fold.
- These small series reported no perforations, bowel obstruction, or metabolic abnormalities during their follow-up; those risks remain possible.
- Mucus production was not clinically significant at short-term follow-up in Jednak's series.
- Continence achieved in 71–81% after initial or secondary procedures.<sup>[[20]](#ref20)</sup>
- **Hourglass deformity** occurred in 7/32 (22%) in an early series.<sup>[[20]](#ref20)</sup> Augmentation failed in 4/32 (12.5%) at mean cohort follow-up of 1.6 years in that series; 8/37 (22%) underwent reaugmentation at mean follow-up 61 months in a later retrospective SCLU cohort. These are separate populations, endpoints, and follow-up periods.<sup>[[20]](#ref20)[[25]](#ref25)</sup>
- Biopsy shows colonic mucosal regrowth in some patients (5/7 interpretable biopsies in one series).<sup>[[20]](#ref20)</sup>
- A 20-patient SCLU study advised against a simultaneous catheterizable channel after 3/5 required reaugmentation; conventional augmentation and pure autoaugmentation were outside its scope.<sup>[[21]](#ref21)</sup>
- Selected SCLU series combined it with an [AUS](/docs/surgical-techniques/04f-incontinence-procedures/procedures/artificial-urinary-sphincter). They do not establish AUS as universally necessary or superior; storage safety and an effective emptying route remain necessary.

## Long-Term Follow-Up

The AUA/SUFU NLUTD guideline states that after lower urinary tract reconstruction incorporating bowel the clinician should assess the patient annually (Statement 58, Expert Opinion) with:<sup>[[22]](#ref22)</sup>

- Focused history, physical examination and symptom assessment.
- Basic metabolic panel (electrolytes, bicarbonate, renal function).
- Urinary tract imaging, commonly renal ultrasound for hydronephrosis and stones.

The guideline states that clinicians should perform cystoscopy when gross hematuria or symptomatic recurrent UTI develops after such reconstruction (Statement 60, Moderate Recommendation; Evidence Level: Grade C). It also states that clinicians should not perform screening or surveillance cystoscopy in NLUTD patients (Statement 19, Strong Recommendation; Evidence Level: Grade B). Cystoscopy is not automatically started at five years; see the surveillance discussion above.<sup>[[22]](#ref22)[[14]](#ref14)[[16]](#ref16)</sup>

Measures described in the literature but not required by the guideline:

- Regular bladder irrigations to reduce mucus accumulation and stone formation.<sup>[[12]](#ref12)</sup>
- Monitoring for calcium and phosphorus abnormalities and bone health, particularly in children.<sup>[[11]](#ref11)</sup>

## Sigmoid vs. Ileum — When to Choose Sigmoid

Sigmoid cystoplasty is well suited when:<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[4]](#ref4)[[5]](#ref5)[[6]](#ref6)</sup>

- Ureteral reimplantation is needed, because the thick wall accommodates a submucosal tunnel.
- Bowel assessment and available anatomy favor using colon.
- Preserving small bowel is important, while accepting colonic surgical risks.
- The ileocecal valve and terminal ileum should be preserved (B₁₂ or bile-salt malabsorption risk).
- SCLU is planned (sigmoid is the standard segment for this technique).

The main disadvantage is a higher rate of pathologic contractions than with ileum, which makes detubularization and cup-patch reconfiguration necessary.<sup>[[6]](#ref6)[[9]](#ref9)</sup>
