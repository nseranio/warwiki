# Ileocystoplasty

**Ileocystoplasty** is the most commonly performed type of [augmentation cystoplasty](/docs/surgical-techniques/04b-bladder-reconstruction/bladder-augmentation), using a detubularized segment of ileum anastomosed to the native bladder to increase bladder capacity and improve compliance.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[2]](#ref2)</sup> Cheng and Myers describe enterocystoplasty as the gold standard for augmentation when conservative therapies have failed.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup>

---

## Indications

For NLUTD, augmentation may be considered when detrusor overactivity or poor compliance is refractory to, or the patient cannot tolerate, appropriate less invasive therapy, including antimuscarinics and intradetrusor botulinum toxin where suitable.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[27]](#ref27)</sup> β3 agonists may relieve neurogenic OAB symptoms, but EAU 2026 strongly advises against using them to reduce urodynamically proven neurogenic detrusor overactivity.<sup>[[32]](#ref32)</sup> For severely impacted idiopathic OAB, AUA/SUFU 2024 permits augmentation only after all other therapeutic options have failed (Expert Opinion).<sup>[[33]](#ref33)</sup> For selected adults with neurogenic lower urinary tract dysfunction, AUA/SUFU says clinicians may offer augmentation cystoplasty for refractory detrusor overactivity or poor compliance (Conditional Recommendation; Grade C).<sup>[[27]](#ref27)</sup> Common underlying conditions:

- **Neurogenic bladder** (myelodysplasia / spina bifida, spinal cord injury, tethered cord, sacral agenesis)
- **Bladder exstrophy / cloacal exstrophy**
- **Posterior urethral valves**
- **Idiopathic detrusor overactivity**<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[2]](#ref2)[[3]](#ref3)</sup>

The primary goals are upper-tract protection, continence, and quality of life.

---

## Contraindications

Contraindications and practical barriers to consider include the following.

- Significant renal insufficiency increases the consequences of metabolic acidosis. Smith's 1977 review listed azotemia (creatinine clearance below 40 mL/min) as a contraindication, together with vesical malignancy, a neurogenic bladder with a spastic pelvic floor that cannot be modified, and young boys (mucus problems).<sup>[[4]](#ref4)</sup> A single contemporary creatinine-clearance cutoff has not been validated; renal reserve, baseline acidosis, and alternatives are assessed individually.
- Inability or unwillingness to perform clean intermittent catheterization (CIC) when CIC is anticipated postoperatively.
- Active inflammatory bowel disease, prior pelvic radiation precluding healthy bowel use, or prior extensive ileal resection resulting in short bowel.<sup>[[35]](#ref35)</sup>
- Patient or caregiver unable to commit to lifelong follow-up.

---

## Surgical Technique

The standard open technique:<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[5]](#ref5)[[6]](#ref6)</sup>

1. **Bowel-segment selection.** Approximately 15–40 cm of ileum, selected to preserve as much terminal ileum and the ileocecal valve as feasible. Some chapter authors recommend preserving the final 20 cm of terminal ileum specifically to protect bile-salt and vitamin B₁₂ absorption.<sup>[[28]](#ref28)</sup> A fixed distance does not guarantee preserved vitamin B₁₂ or bile-salt absorption; record the actual segment and length used.
2. **Bowel continuity restoration:** Side-to-side ileoileal anastomosis (stapled or hand-sewn).
3. **Detubularization.** The isolated ileal segment is opened along the antimesenteric border, disrupting coordinated peristaltic contractions and converting the segment into a low-pressure reservoir.<sup>[[6]](#ref6)</sup>
4. **Reconfiguration.** The opened segment is folded into a U- or S-shaped patch to maximize volume and minimize pressure.
5. **Cystotomy.** Bivalve the native bladder sagittally or coronally down to the ureteric orifices. Inadequate opening can leave the ileal patch as a diverticulum; for a very thick, fibrotic bladder, consider supratrigonal cystectomy to avoid patch exclusion.<sup>[[35]](#ref35)</sup>
6. **Anastomosis:** Reconfigured ileal patch is sutured to the opened bladder in a watertight fashion. Taking slightly more serosa and muscularis than mucosa with each running bite, rolling the mucosa inward, and inspecting the full suture line for protruding mucosa has been described; after closure, the augment can be filled modestly through the suprapubic tube and any leak repaired with interrupted sutures.<sup>[[28]](#ref28)</sup>

### Concomitant procedures

Frequently performed at the time of ileocystoplasty:<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[7]](#ref7)</sup>

- [**Catheterizable channel**](/docs/surgical-techniques/04b-bladder-reconstruction/catheterizable-channels) (Mitrofanoff appendicovesicostomy or Monti channel) for patients who cannot catheterize per urethra.
- **Ureteral reimplantation** for selected anatomic indications; high-pressure reflux can improve with augmentation alone, so reimplantation is not automatic.
- **Bladder-neck procedures** for incontinence ([sling](/docs/surgical-techniques/04f-incontinence-procedures/procedures/male-urethral-slings), [bladder-neck closure](/docs/surgical-techniques/04ab-bladder-neck-reconstruction/outlet-continence/bladder-neck-closure)).

### Minimally invasive approaches

Laparoscopic and robot-assisted ileocystoplasty are technically feasible. Procedural complexity remains substantial.<sup>[[3]](#ref3)[[5]](#ref5)[[8]](#ref8)[[9]](#ref9)</sup>

## Outcomes

- **Bladder capacity:** increased from mean 111 to 788 mL at 12 months in a 23-patient laparoscopic series; this is not a target capacity for every patient.<sup>[[8]](#ref8)</sup>
- **Maximum detrusor pressure:** significant decrease from mean 92 to 15 cm H₂O at 12 months in the 23-patient laparoscopic series.<sup>[[8]](#ref8)</sup>
- **Continence:** older mixed-segment cohorts reported 82–95%, often after additional outlet procedures; these are not isolated ileocystoplasty cure rates.<sup>[[6]](#ref6)[[7]](#ref7)</sup>
- **Upper-tract outcomes:** In a historical mixed-reconstruction cohort of 129 children and young adults, renal function was maintained or improved in 91% with CIC at mean 44-month follow-up.<sup>[[6]](#ref6)</sup> A separate 22-child mixed-augmentation cohort reported increased estimated GFR and resolution of hydronephrosis in 17/19 affected patients at mean 13.4-year follow-up.<sup>[[10]](#ref10)</sup> In a selected series of 80 patients with spina bifida who had ileocystoplasty plus a bladder-outlet procedure, 32/80 developed new renal scars and 12/80 developed stage 3 or worse CKD during median 14-year follow-up; 22/32 with new scars had been nonadherent to CIC.<sup>[[31]](#ref31)</sup>
- **Patient satisfaction:** in a long-term SCI cohort (mean 14.7 yr), 13/14 questionnaire respondents (of 19 patients) would consider the operation again or recommend it to a friend.<sup>[[11]](#ref11)</sup>

In a retrospective 84-patient mixed-segment study, postoperative pathologic contractions occurred in 26% of 31 ileal, 43% of 35 colonic, and 50% of 18 gastric reconstructions. Within this series, ileal reconstructions had the lowest contraction frequency, with residual contractions.<sup>[[12]](#ref12)</sup>

## Complications

Significant long-term morbidity requiring lifelong surveillance.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[13]](#ref13)[[14]](#ref14)</sup>

- **Urinary tract stones.** In Szymanski's 413-patient spina bifida augmentation cohort (80.9% ileocystoplasty), the 10-year bladder-stone risk was 28.2%, with recurrence in 52.4% of stone formers.<sup>[[14]](#ref14)</sup> In Chang's 22-child cohort (19 ileal and 3 sigmoid augmentations), urinary stones were the most frequent late complication, occurring in 8/22 at mean 13.4 years.<sup>[[10]](#ref10)</sup> Continent catheterizable stomas and bladder or cloacal exstrophy are reported risk factors; mucus and urinary stasis may contribute.<sup>[[7]](#ref7)[[10]](#ref10)</sup>
- **Urinary tract infections.** Distinguish asymptomatic bacteriuria from symptomatic infection. Balanca reported at least one febrile UTI in 55.8% of 77 SCI patients over median 13 years, not recurrent UTI in every affected patient. Taghavi reported symptomatic UTI in 36/71 (51%) children with mixed augmentation types over median 4.5 years.<sup>[[15]](#ref15)[[16]](#ref16)</sup>
- **Bladder perforation.** Szymanski estimated 10-year risk of 9.6% with detubularized reconfigured ileocystoplasty versus 23.7% without it in a spina bifida cohort.<sup>[[14]](#ref14)</sup> These are observational time-to-event estimates, not universal rates. Overdistension and unreliable emptying are important preventable risks, but perforation may have other causes.<sup>[[8]](#ref8)[[14]](#ref14)</sup> New abdominal or shoulder pain after augmentation warrants urgent evaluation for perforation; in a nine-case series, six were initially misdiagnosed as UTI or bowel obstruction, and the one undiagnosed patient died.<sup>[[34]](#ref34)</sup>
- **Hyperchloremic metabolic acidosis.** Most common metabolic abnormality, from chloride and ammonium reabsorption by ileal mucosa. Severity depends on segment length, contact time with urine (regular CIC matters), and baseline renal function. Treat documented acidosis with an individualized alkali regimen; potassium-containing therapy requires particular caution with renal impairment or hyperkalemia.<sup>[[13]](#ref13)[[17]](#ref17)[[18]](#ref18)</sup>
- **Vitamin B₁₂ deficiency.** Monitor according to ileal use and nutritional risk. The cited 32% supplementation figure comes from 94 Mainz ileocecal-pouch diversion patients, including low-normal levels; it is not an ileocystoplasty deficiency rate.<sup>[[17]](#ref17)</sup>
- **Bowel dysfunction.** Ileal resection can cause diarrhea or altered bowel habits through bile-acid dysfunction even when the ileocecal valve is preserved.<sup>[[29]](#ref29)</sup> Loss of the ileocecal valve and right colon increases diarrhea after comparable ileal resection; it is a separate risk of ileocecal harvest.<sup>[[30]](#ref30)</sup> Bowel obstruction requiring laparotomy had an estimated 10-year risk of 4.5% in Szymanski’s mixed spina bifida augmentation cohort.<sup>[[7]](#ref7)[[14]](#ref14)</sup>
- **Reoperation.** Szymanski estimated a 43.9% 10-year risk in a mixed-era spina bifida augmentation cohort. A separate 114-adult continent cutaneous ileocecocystoplasty cohort had 42.1% additional surgery over median 40 months and 20.2% channel revision.<sup>[[14]](#ref14)[[19]](#ref19)</sup>

## Malignancy Risk

Malignancy after intestinal augmentation is uncommon. A systematic review across augmentation types reported probabilities of 0–5.5% and mean latency 19 years; this is not an ileocystoplasty-specific pooled risk.<sup>[[20]](#ref20)[[21]](#ref21)</sup>

- **Adenocarcinoma** was the most common histologic type (51.6% of post-augmentation malignancies in a systematic review); signet-ring features appeared in 9/10 selected post-augmentation carcinomas (two after ileocystoplasty) in a multi-institutional pathology series.<sup>[[20]](#ref20)[[22]](#ref22)</sup>
- About half of the tumors arise at the entero-vesical anastomosis.<sup>[[20]](#ref20)</sup>
- Frequently diagnosed at advanced stage with poor prognosis (1-year survival approximately 56% in one series).<sup>[[23]](#ref23)</sup>
- Whether augmentation itself is an independent risk factor (versus the underlying congenital bladder abnormality) remains debated; one matched cohort study found no significant difference in cancer incidence between augmented patients and controls (4.6% versus 2.6%, p=0.54).<sup>[[24]](#ref24)</sup>
- In a selected ten-tumor post-augmentation series that excluded diagnosed urothelial carcinomas, nine tumors had gastrointestinal-type adenocarcinoma profiles and seven had potentially targetable alterations; TP53, KRAS, and MYC were among the most frequently altered genes.<sup>[[25]](#ref25)</sup>

Older observational publications recommended scheduled endoscopy after five years.<sup>[[26]](#ref26)</sup> AUA/SUFU states that clinicians should not perform screening or surveillance cystoscopy in NLUTD patients (Statement 19, Strong Recommendation; Evidence Level: Grade B) and should perform cystoscopy after reconstruction with bowel when gross hematuria or symptomatic recurrent UTI develops (Statement 60, Moderate Recommendation; Evidence Level: Grade C). The statement text adds unexplained suprapubic pain to the signs that warrant full evaluation.<sup>[[27]](#ref27)</sup> For women who undergo augmentation for non-neurogenic OAB or UUI, EAU 2026 weakly recommends offering cystoscopic surveillance from ten years after augmentation.<sup>[[36]](#ref36)</sup> AUA/SUFU's Strong Recommendation against screening or surveillance cystoscopy applies to NLUTD, a different population.<sup>[[37]](#ref37)</sup> Some surgical chapter authors recommend considering surveillance after ten years without citing an interval-comparison study.<sup>[[28]](#ref28)</sup> The indication for surveillance is clearer after gastrocystoplasty.<sup>[[2]](#ref2)</sup>

---

## Long-Term Follow-Up

For NLUTD patients who have had reconstruction incorporating bowel, AUA/SUFU advises annual assessment (Statement 58, Expert Opinion):<sup>[[27]](#ref27)</sup>

- Focused history, physical exam, and symptom assessment.
- **Basic metabolic panel** to monitor for acidosis and electrolyte disturbances.
- **Urinary tract imaging** (renal ultrasound for hydronephrosis and stones).

The statement text advises monitoring B₁₂ over time, with supplementation if appropriate, when terminal ileum is used.<sup>[[27]](#ref27)</sup> Review catheterization and mucus-clearance practices at follow-up. In 75 patients with spina bifida, prior bladder stones, and abdominal-stoma catheterization (48 ileal and 27 colonic augments) who were stone-free three months after stone removal, random allocation to 240 mL daily saline washouts yielded fewer recurrent bladder stones and symptomatic UTI episodes over ten years than allocation to 60 or 120 mL.<sup>[[31]](#ref31)</sup> Bone density assessment in at-risk patients and cystoscopy as clinically indicated are additional considerations.<sup>[[17]](#ref17)[[18]](#ref18)</sup> AUA/SUFU recommends repeat urodynamics at an appropriate interval after treatment for NLUTD patients whose impaired storage or voiding places the upper tracts at risk (Expert Opinion).<sup>[[27]](#ref27)</sup>

---

## Alternatives

Other bowel segments (sigmoid, stomach) can be used for augmentation, each with distinct complication profiles. When regular catheterization cannot be arranged, evaluate an incontinent drainage or diversion option, including [ileovesicostomy](/docs/surgical-techniques/04b-bladder-reconstruction/ileovesicostomy) in selected patients with NLUTD; counsel about its high risk of further treatment or surgery.<sup>[[27]](#ref27)</sup> Tissue-engineering approaches using biodegradable scaffolds seeded with urothelial and smooth muscle cells remain experimental.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup>
