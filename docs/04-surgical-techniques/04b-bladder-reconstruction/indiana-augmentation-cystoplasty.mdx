# Indiana Augmentation Cystoplasty (IAC)

**Indiana augmentation cystoplasty** (IAC), also described within the continent cutaneous ileocecocystoplasty (CCIC) family, uses a colonic augment joined to the native bladder and a tapered terminal-ileal channel with a reinforced ileocecal continence mechanism. It provides reservoir enlargement and an abdominal catheterizable route from one bowel harvest. The ureters stay in the native bladder, so no ureteral surgery is required.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[2]](#ref2)</sup>

This differs from the [Indiana Pouch](/docs/surgical-techniques/04c-urinary-diversion/indiana-pouch), a separate urinary reservoir commonly constructed after cystectomy. Post-cystectomy survival, ureteroenteric stricture rates, and diversion-specific continence cannot be treated as direct IAC outcomes. For design rules common to every catheterizable channel, see [Principles of Continent Catheterizable Channels](/docs/surgical-techniques/04b-bladder-reconstruction/catheterizable-channels).

## Lineage and Concept

The Indiana pouch was developed in 1984 as a modification of the Gilchrist ileocecal reservoir. Full detubularization of the reservoir was recognized early and was achieved either with an ileal patch on the cecal reservoir or with an antimesenteric incision of the cecum, folding and transverse closure. The efferent limb and continence mechanism were modified to ease catheterization and improve continence, and these changes gave lower reoperation rates.<sup>[[5]](#ref5)[[6]](#ref6)</sup>

| Step | Modification |
|---|---|
| 1984 | Original Indiana pouch, developed from the Gilchrist procedure<sup>[[5]](#ref5)</sup> |
| 1991 (Ahlering) | Modified Indiana pouch: complete detubularization of the colonic segment, an easier appendectomy, transcolonic ureteral reimplantation and reinforced plication of the ileocecal junction<sup>[[7]](#ref7)</sup> |
| 1994 (Rowland and Kropp) | Full detubularization, stapled tapering of the efferent limb, plication of the ileocecal valve and tunneled tenial implants<sup>[[6]](#ref6)</sup> |
| 1996 (Rowland) | Absorbable GIA and TA55 staples used to detubularize and close the reservoir in 20 patients, with shorter operating times<sup>[[5]](#ref5)</sup> |
| IAC (Khavari, Boone) | Adaptation of the Indiana principle to augment a native bladder in neurogenic patients<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup> |

Video-urodynamic study of the Indiana pouch found that continence depends on the combined effect of ileocecal valve resistance, resistance from plication of the ileal segment, ileal peristalsis and the low intraluminal pressure of the detubularized bowel.<sup>[[8]](#ref8)</sup>

## Selection

Consider IAC when both a low-pressure reservoir and a catheterizable abdominal channel are needed, with suitable bowel anatomy and the ability of the patient or caregiver to manage lifelong catheterization. Assess renal and metabolic reserve, bowel function, prior ileocecal resection, abdominal access, and stoma reach. A channel alone may be sufficient when storage capacity and pressures are already safe. AUA/SUFU states that clinicians may offer augmentation cystoplasty to select NLUTD patients who are refractory to or intolerant of less invasive therapies (Conditional Recommendation, Grade C), and may offer continent catheterizable channels with or without augmentation to facilitate catheterization (Conditional Recommendation, Grade C).<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[3]](#ref3)[[4]](#ref4)</sup>

The ureters may remain implanted in the native bladder when no separate ureteral reconstruction is indicated. IAC does not automatically correct obstruction, reflux independent of storage pressure, or an unsafe outlet. Bladder-neck closure, a sling, or an AUS may be selected for outlet dysfunction, but none is an obligatory step.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup>

## Operative Principles

1. **Plan the whole urinary and bowel reconstruction.** Determine reservoir size, outlet management, stoma site, and catheterization access. Mark the site with the patient's usual posture and mobility aids in mind.
2. **Harvest a well-vascularized ileocecal segment.** Length and extent of colonic mobilization depend on the chosen configuration and anatomy. Preserve perfusion and restore bowel continuity without tension. Ahlering's modified Indiana pouch used the terminal 8 to 12 cm of ileum and 26 to 30 cm of right colon on the ileocolic pedicle, with an appendectomy.<sup>[[7]](#ref7)</sup>
3. **Detubularize the reservoir component.** Open the colon along its antimesenteric border, reconfigure the augment and leave the intended ileal channel tubular. Incomplete detubularization of the cecum produced pouch deformity in the first two Indiana pouches in one series.<sup>[[13]](#ref13)</sup> Avoid narrowing the junction with the native bladder.
4. **Construct the continence mechanism.** Taper the terminal ileum by suture plication or by linear stapling along the antimesenteric border, and reinforce the ileocecal junction with seromuscular plication according to the selected technique. Confirm easy catheter passage; excessive narrowing risks obstruction.<sup>[[9]](#ref9)[[7]](#ref7)</sup>
5. **Join the augment to the widely opened bladder.** Tailor accompanying ureteral or outlet procedures to documented anatomy and function.
6. **Route and mature the stoma.** Avoid tension, mesenteric twist, and angulation. Establish dependable early drainage and a plan for later catheterization or stoma failure.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[3]](#ref3)</sup>

Historical Indiana-pouch descriptions used different bowel lengths, sutures, and stapling modifications. A named stapler does not imply that its staples are absorbable, and historical dimensions should not be presented as mandatory for native-bladder augmentation.<sup>[[5]](#ref5)[[6]](#ref6)[[7]](#ref7)</sup>

The continence mechanism depends on the valve or channel construction and low reservoir pressure. A 21-reservoir urodynamic comparison of plicated versus stapled ileum found only a nonsignificant trend toward higher mean contraction pressure with stapling (p=0.054); it does not prove one method is more durable or safer.<sup>[[8]](#ref8)[[9]](#ref9)</sup>

## Direct Outcomes and Maintenance Burden

| Study | Findings | Limits |
|---|---|---|
| **Khavari 2012**, 34 neurogenic patients, median 31 months | All were continent at last follow-up; 15/34 (44.1%) had long-term complications, including four stomal revisions | Retrospective, selected single-center cohort; additional outlet procedures in some patients; 1993–2010 was the treatment period, not each patient's follow-up<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup> |
| **Cheng 2020**, 114 adults, median 40 months | Major complications by three months in 18/114 (15.8%); 24/114 (21.1%) readmitted; 48/114 (42.1%) had additional related surgery; 23/114 (20.2%) had channel revision; 11/114 (9.6%) abandoned the channel | Multicenter retrospective CCIC experience; not a randomized comparison with other channels<sup>[[2]](#ref2)</sup> |
| **Redshaw 2014**, 61 adults, median 16 months | Channel intervention in 4/31 CCIC versus 15/30 tunneled-channel patients (OR 6.4, 95% CI 1.8–28 for tunneled channels); reported stomal leakage 29% versus 43% was not significantly different | Short, nonrandomized comparison; does not establish lifelong superiority of the ileocecal valve<sup>[[10]](#ref10)</sup> |

A dry stoma does not mean freedom from reoperation, catheterization difficulty, symptomatic infection, or metabolic effects. Patients need to understand both functional benefit and the long-term care burden.

## Standard Indiana Pouch and IAC Compared

| Feature | Standard Indiana pouch | IAC |
|---|---|---|
| Usual indication | Diversion after cystectomy | Neurogenic bladder needing augmentation and a continent stoma |
| Native bladder | Removed | Preserved and augmented |
| Reservoir | Stand-alone ileocecal pouch | Native bladder plus ileocecal augment |
| Ureters | Reimplanted into the pouch | Remain in the native bladder |
| Continence mechanism | Plicated ileocecal valve and tapered ileum | Same |
| Outlet | Not applicable | Closed, slung, given an AUS or left intact according to function |

In the Khavari series, concurrent procedures were bladder neck closure (n = 3), pubovaginal sling (n = 4), hysterectomy (n = 3), artificial urinary sphincter (n = 1) and cystolithotomy (n = 1) among 34 patients.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup>

## Indiana Pouch Reference Data

The post-cystectomy and benign-indication Indiana pouch has a longer literature than IAC and uses the same continence mechanism. These series describe the pouch, not IAC, and their ureteral and cancer-related outcomes do not transfer.

| Study | Population | Findings |
|---|---|---|
| **Rowland and Kropp 1994**<sup>[[6]](#ref6)</sup> | First 69 patients treated with the evolved technique, minimum 2-year follow-up | Day and night continence 97% by 1 year; pouch-related reoperation 17% (early plus late) |
| **Arai 1993**<sup>[[13]](#ref13)</sup> | 76 Kock and 39 Indiana pouches; long-term group 68 Kock (mean 53 months) and 37 Indiana (mean 34 months) | Surgical revision 22.1% (Kock) vs 10.8% (Indiana); stones 26.5% vs 5.4%; continence satisfactory in both. Kock complications were mostly nipple-valve malfunction and reactions to a nonabsorbable collar |
| **Holmes 2002**<sup>[[14]](#ref14)</sup> | 125 modified Indiana pouches, mean follow-up 41.1 months | Any complication 89.6%; 58.4% of complications were efferent limb (leakage 28.0%, stomal stenosis 15.2%, difficult catheterization 9.6%); pouch stones 10.4%, perforation 3.2%, ureteral anastomotic stricture 7.2%, gallstones 25.6%, kidney stones 6.4%, small bowel obstruction 4.8%; reoperation 52.0%, of which 60% were minimally invasive |
| **Wilson 1994**<sup>[[15]](#ref15)</sup> | 130 modified Indiana pouches | Late complications 12%; ureteral obstruction 7%. Balloon dilation with or without incision of ureteral strictures failed in 83%, and ureteral reimplantation succeeded in 10 of 11. Of the late complications, 56% occurred in patients with 3,000 to 6,500 rad of prior pelvic radiation; the authors advised shorter ureteral tunnels and an ileal patch at initial construction in irradiated patients |
| **Burns 2022**<sup>[[16]](#ref16)</sup> | 137 open cystectomy with Indiana pouch, 2012 to 2018 | Reoperation 7.3% within 90 days and 8% at 90 to 365 days; readmission 18.2% early and 13.1% midterm |
| **Kim 2024**<sup>[[17]](#ref17)</sup> | 97 pouches after robot-assisted cystectomy, median follow-up 93 months | Continence 99.0%; Clavien III to V complications 17.5% at 30 days and 22.7% at 90 days; readmission 21.4% |
| **Polm 2024**<sup>[[18]](#ref18)</sup> | 33 patients (23 female), benign indications, median follow-up 258 months | 22/33 (67%) had at least one surgical revision (45 revision procedures); estimated mean revision-free survival 198 months |

Complications accumulate with follow-up, and short series understate the burden. The ureteral findings apply only to pouches with ureteral reimplantation; IAC avoids that failure mode; the ureters stay in the native bladder.

## Complications

- **Stomal or channel obstruction.** Stenosis, angulation, redundancy, or a tight continence mechanism can impair catheterization. Repeated forceful catheter attempts risk false passage, and an acutely undrainable reservoir needs prompt urologic care.
- **Leakage.** Evaluate reservoir pressure, emptying, and the outlet or channel rather than assuming a single mechanical cause.
- **Stones, mucus, and symptomatic infection.** They are common reasons for ongoing treatment or intervention. Bacteriuria alone is not an indication for antibiotics in a stable NLUTD patient.
- **Bowel and abdominal complications.** Ileus, obstruction, bowel anastomotic problems, and hernia remain relevant after bowel harvest.
- **Metabolic and nutritional effects.** Intestinal urine contact may cause hyperchloremic acidosis. Terminal ileal or ileocecal harvest may contribute to B12 or bile-salt malabsorption and altered bowel function; these are risks, not inevitable outcomes in every patient.
- **Perforation and malignancy.** Long-term augmentation risks persist and require symptom awareness and follow-up.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[11]](#ref11)[[12]](#ref12)[[3]](#ref3)[[2]](#ref2)[[4]](#ref4)</sup>

Ileocecal harvest sacrifices the ileocecal valve. Transit time shortens, and malabsorption and diarrhea may follow, particularly after prior bowel resection or in patients with myelomeningocele who already have frequent defecation. Fisch described functional reconstruction of the valve by embedding ileum in the ascending colon through a submucosal tunnel; the report combined experiments in 15 dogs with first clinical experience in 12 patients.<sup>[[11]](#ref11)</sup>

Terai measured B12 in 27 Indiana pouch patients. None had a serum level below 200 pg/mL, and the mean was lower than in ileal conduit patients (536 vs 727 pg/mL). On the Schilling test, 4 of 6 Indiana pouch patients (and 3 of 9 with Kock pouches) were B12 malabsorbers, and none had megaloblastic anemia or neurologic symptoms.<sup>[[12]](#ref12)</sup> These are small numbers from continent diversion cohorts and should not be used as an IAC-specific B12 deficiency rate. Record the actual bowel segment used and monitor nutritional status over time.<sup>[[4]](#ref4)</sup>

## Follow-Up

For NLUTD patients who have had lower urinary tract reconstruction incorporating bowel, the AUA/SUFU guideline states that the clinician should assess the patient annually with a focused history, physical examination and symptom assessment, a basic metabolic panel and urinary tract imaging (Expert Opinion). Clinicians should perform cystoscopy when gross hematuria or symptomatic recurrent UTI develops after such reconstruction (Moderate Recommendation, Grade C).<sup>[[4]](#ref4)</sup> The guideline also states that asymptomatic bacteriuria in NLUTD patients should not be treated (Moderate Recommendation).<sup>[[4]](#ref4)</sup>

Reviews of catheterization, stoma function, irrigation needs and B12 status fit into the same visit; B12 monitoring is a reasonable addition rather than a guideline requirement. Sudden abdominal pain or sepsis may indicate reservoir perforation.
