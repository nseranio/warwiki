# Principles of Continent Catheterizable Channels

Continent catheterizable channels (CCCs) are built on one central idea: a narrow conduit implanted into a compliant reservoir in a way that allows catheter passage but resists leakage as the reservoir fills.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[2]](#ref2)</sup> This is the modern expression of the **Mitrofanoff principle** and it deliberately borrows from the same valve logic that makes the ureterovesical junction competent.

This page focuses on the design rules that make CCCs work. For the broader role of these channels inside bladder reconstruction, see [Principles of Bladder Augmentation](/docs/surgical-techniques/04b-bladder-reconstruction/augmentation-principles) and [Principles of Bladder Neck Reconstruction](/docs/surgical-techniques/04ab-bladder-neck-reconstruction/bladder-neck-reconstruction-principles).

---

## 1. The Flap-Valve Mechanism Is the Central Concept

A **flap valve** is a central operative design in tunneled CCCs: the proximal channel runs within or against the reservoir wall so filling pressure can compress it.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[2]](#ref2)</sup> A 17-patient pressure-profilometry study supported a role for the intravesical tunnel but could not establish its relative contribution against other closure sites.<sup>[[40]](#ref40)</sup>

Hinman classified four hydrodynamic principles of continence in conduits leading from intestinal reservoirs:<sup>[[2]](#ref2)</sup>

1. **Flap valve**: the conduit is compressed against the reservoir wall as filling pressure rises.
2. **Sphincteric compression**: a narrow lumen is easier to occlude.
3. **Peristalsis**: a minor contributor in most modern channels.
4. **Pressure equilibration / nipple mechanisms**: more relevant in specific diversion designs than in standard Mitrofanoff-type channels.

In practice, the flap valve is the main mechanism.

---

## 2. Tunnel Length and Muscular Backing Determine Channel Continence

The flap valve only works if the conduit has enough **backed length** lying against the reservoir wall.<sup>[[4]](#ref4)[[5]](#ref5)[[6]](#ref6)</sup>

### Tunnel length

Watson studied 21 mixed conduits in 20 children. Functional profile length correlated with maximal closure pressure, and clinical continence differed above and below a functional profile length of 2.0 cm (p = 0.05). This is a small observational physiologic finding and is not a validated surgical cutoff.<sup>[[4]](#ref4)</sup> Published extravesical techniques describe a 3–6 cm detrusor incision, and one robotic series emphasized 4 cm of detrusor backing; neither length is a validated universal minimum.<sup>[[5]](#ref5)[[6]](#ref6)</sup>

### Detrusor or reservoir backing

The tunnel also needs a firm posterior support. In a native bladder this usually means **detrusor backing**; in other reservoirs it may come from seromuscular or serosal tunnel construction.<sup>[[5]](#ref5)[[6]](#ref6)[[8]](#ref8)[[9]](#ref9)</sup>

The principle is simple:

- inadequate tunnel length leads to a weak valve,
- inadequate backing leads to a collapsible but not reliably occlusive tunnel,
- and both failures show up clinically as stomal leakage.<sup>[[4]](#ref4)[[5]](#ref5)</sup>

### Fixation helps preserve the tunnel

VanderBrink et al. describe fixing the bladder wall to the abdominal wall to preserve tunnel length and limit angulation.<sup>[[6]](#ref6)</sup> In a retrospective pediatric comparison restricted to channels implanted into bladder, subfascial revision or replacement for difficult catheterization occurred in 1/21 hitched channels versus 8/84 unhitched channels over median 5.8-year follow-up (p = 0.68); no statistically significant difference in time to complications or revisions was found.<sup>[[41]](#ref41)</sup>

## 3. Implantation Technique Must Match the Reservoir

There is no single best implantation method. The correct technique depends on whether the conduit is entering native bladder, augmented bladder, or bowel reservoir.

| Implantation technique | Best use case | Principle |
|---|---|---|
| **Intravesical submucosal tunnel** | When cystotomy is already being performed, especially with concurrent augmentation | Classic Mitrofanoff-style tunnel under bladder mucosa<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[7]](#ref7)</sup> |
| **Extravesical detrusor tunnel** | Isolated channel creation without major cystotomy | Seromuscular flaps are closed over the channel, analogous to Lich-Gregoir<sup>[[6]](#ref6)[[7]](#ref7)</sup> |
| **Serosal trough / serous-lined tunnel** | Channel implantation into bowel reservoir without true detrusor backing | Uses an extramural serosal tunnel to recreate the valve effect on bowel<sup>[[8]](#ref8)[[9]](#ref9)[[10]](#ref10)</sup> |

### Intravesical tunneling

Intravesical tunneling is the classic approach and was used with concurrent augmentation in one small robotic pediatric series; comparative superiority in this setting was not tested.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[7]](#ref7)</sup>

### Extravesical tunneling

The extravesical approach avoids a large cystotomy and is attractive when creating an isolated channel. VanderBrink et al. reported stomal continence in 79 of 84 patients (94%) using seromuscular flaps over the channel in a 3-6 cm detrusor incision, with revision in 26% at mean 45 months (mostly skin-level or endoscopic).<sup>[[6]](#ref6)</sup>

### Serosal trough techniques

When the channel enters bowel rather than native bladder, there may be no meaningful detrusor to tunnel beneath. In that setting, serous-lined extramural tunnel techniques solve the same problem by different anatomy and can still deliver continence.<sup>[[8]](#ref8)[[9]](#ref9)[[10]](#ref10)</sup>

The reconstructive lesson is that a CCC should be implanted according to the reservoir it enters, not by forcing one implantation method into every anatomy.

## 4. Channel Tissue Selection Follows a Practical Hierarchy

The conduit itself must be reliable, narrow caliber, well vascularized, and long enough to reach the skin without tension. In most reconstructions, tissue choice follows a clear hierarchy.

<a id="appendix-remains-the-gold-standard"></a>
### Appendix is often preferred when suitable

Appendix is often preferred when suitable because it is already tubular, accepts a catheter easily, has dependable blood supply, and usually avoids the bowel-manipulation burden of ileal channel construction.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[14]](#ref14)[[15]](#ref15)</sup> In O'Connor's retrospective adult series, any channel incontinence occurred in 25/87 appendiceal channels (28.7%) versus 45/75 ileal channels (60.0%; p &lt; 0.001) at mean follow-up of 78.6 months.<sup>[[15]](#ref15)</sup> Revision for incontinence occurred in 19.5% versus 36.0% (p = 0.03), while stenosis-related revision was 17.2% versus 22.7% (p = 0.39).<sup>[[15]](#ref15)</sup> In children, Abdelhalim reported higher odds of reoperation with ileal channels (OR 3.4).<sup>[[16]](#ref16)</sup> Polm found no significant difference in revision-free survival between appendix, Monti and tubularized bladder flap. Findings vary by endpoint and follow-up.<sup>[[14]](#ref14)[[15]](#ref15)[[16]](#ref16)[[21]](#ref21)[[23]](#ref23)</sup>

### Monti-family channels are the workhorse backup

When appendix is unavailable, the **Yang-Monti** principle uses a short ileal segment opened and retubularized transversely to create a longer, narrow channel.<sup>[[17]](#ref17)[[18]](#ref18)[[19]](#ref19)</sup> Monti channels are versatile when suitable healthy bowel is available. In a retrospective pediatric series of 92 patients undergoing 94 Mitrofanoff procedures (25 Monti and 69 appendiceal), catheterization problems occurred in 15 Monti-group patients (reported as 60%) and 18 appendiceal-group patients (reported as 27%); mean follow-up was 25 and 37 months, respectively.<sup>[[20]](#ref20)</sup> In a retrospective adult series, difficult catheterization occurred with 53.3% of ileal channels and 57.5% of appendiceal channels (p = 0.6).<sup>[[15]](#ref15)</sup> In Polm's cohort of 173 channels in adults attending surveillance, 106 channels had been created before age 18; major revision was needed in 31 of 51 Monti channels (61%) versus 27 of 90 appendicovesicostomies (30%).<sup>[[21]](#ref21)</sup> In a separate pediatric series, pouch-like dilation was observed in 7/25 Yang-Monti channels (28%; mean follow-up 25 months); all seven had catheterization problems, and two required pouch resection.<sup>[[20]](#ref20)</sup>

### Double Monti and spiral / Casale variants solve length problems

These variants are useful when a standard channel will not reach comfortably, especially in obese patients or those needing umbilical siting across a long abdominal wall. Their tradeoff is additional construction and routing complexity. Galansky's single-center series preferred a tapered Monti channel over spiral or double Monti because of a better blood supply, and no comparative data establish a vascular-safety ranking against appendix.<sup>[[19]](#ref19)[[22]](#ref22)</sup>

### Tubularized bladder flap is a useful bowel-free alternative

When bladder capacity is generous and native tissue is available, **tubularized bladder flap** offers a bowel-sparing option. In one pediatric series (median follow-up 85 months), revision was needed in 52% of all channel types, without a significant difference between appendix, tubularized bladder flap and Monti.<sup>[[23]](#ref23)</sup>

### Ureter can work, but is not first-line

Redundant distal ureter can be used in selected reconstructions, but comparative pressure data suggest it is a less reliable continence conduit than appendix.<sup>[[4]](#ref4)</sup>

## 5. The Channel Must Be Narrow Enough to Close and Wide Enough to Catheterize

A continent channel has to satisfy two opposing demands:

- it must be compressible enough for the flap valve to work,
- but wide enough to permit easy repeated catheterization.<sup>[[2]](#ref2)[[17]](#ref17)</sup>

Catheter size varies by reconstruction: O’Connor’s adult series generally used a 12–16 Fr indwelling channel catheter after surgery (16 Fr when possible), while Castellan described a 16–18 Fr lumen as adequate in a mixed-age Monti series.<sup>[[15]](#ref15)[[17]](#ref17)</sup>

Too wide:

- resists compression,
- leaks more easily,
- loses the valve advantage.

Too narrow:

- stenoses more easily,
- traumatizes with repeated catheterization,
- and becomes difficult to use long term.

For ileal channels, transverse retubularization also reorients the muscle fibers and reduces effective peristaltic behavior, which is another reason the Monti concept works as a continent conduit rather than as a bowel tube simply transplanted to the skin.<sup>[[17]](#ref17)[[19]](#ref19)</sup>

## 6. Reservoir Compliance Is a Prerequisite, Not a Bonus

Channel continence depends on the relationship between reservoir pressure and channel closure pressure. If the reservoir stores at high pressure, even a well-constructed CCC can leak or place the upper tracts at risk.<sup>[[5]](#ref5)[[11]](#ref11)[[12]](#ref12)[[13]](#ref13)</sup>

This principle overlaps with augmentation, but its implication for CCCs is specific:

1. **A high-pressure reservoir can overpower the channel valve and cause stomal leakage.**
2. **A continent stoma does not protect the kidneys if storage remains unsafe.**

That is why augmentation accompanies CCC creation so often in practice.<sup>[[14]](#ref14)[[24]](#ref24)</sup> When reservoir compliance is poor, the operation is the creation of a safe catheterizable reservoir.

---

## 7. Stomal Construction Matters Because Stenosis Is the Commonest Failure Mode

Stomal stenosis is the commonest CCC complication in most series; in Welk's series, channel leakage was at least as frequent.<sup>[[25]](#ref25)[[26]](#ref26)[[27]](#ref27)</sup> Skin-level stenosis is only one failure mechanism. Deeper channel stenosis, redundancy, angulation and valve failure may require different operations.

### Principles that reduce stenosis risk

- Use vascularized skin flaps when appropriate rather than a simple circular maturation.<sup>[[28]](#ref28)</sup>
- **Preserve distal conduit blood supply**, especially with appendix.<sup>[[26]](#ref26)</sup>
- **Avoid tension at the skin exit**.
- **Choose a stoma site the patient can catheterize repeatedly without torque or awkward reach**. Before surgery, assess hand function, mobility and available caregiver support to establish a sustainable catheterization plan.<sup>[[13]](#ref13)</sup>

Kurzrock's retrospective comparison found 0/30 stenoses with tip preservation versus 12/93 with standard maturation, including urinary and enema channels. Median follow-up differed (3.3 versus 9.4 years); this supports an approach, not proof of causality or guaranteed prevention.<sup>[[26]](#ref26)</sup>

---

## 8. The Channel Path Must Be Straight and Non-Angulated

Even a continent channel can fail functionally if it is difficult to catheterize. The conduit therefore needs a straight, tension-free, non-kinked trajectory from reservoir to skin.<sup>[[6]](#ref6)[[20]](#ref20)[[23]](#ref23)</sup>

Common sites of trouble include:

- the reservoir entry point,
- the fascial level,
- the abdominal wall turn,
- and the skin exit.

Poor alignment leads to difficult catheterization, creation of false passages, and eventual revision. This is why channel routing and reservoir fixation are not minor technical details. They are part of the long-term success of the reconstruction.<sup>[[6]](#ref6)[[23]](#ref23)</sup>

---

## 9. CCCs Are Durable, but They Are Maintenance Reconstructions

CCCs are lifelong reconstructions with a meaningful revision burden.<sup>[[16]](#ref16)[[21]](#ref21)[[24]](#ref24)[[25]](#ref25)[[27]](#ref27)</sup>

Long-term series show that:

- many patients need at least one revision (53% of 173 channels at median 12.4 years in Polm's adult cohort, with 40% needing major revision)<sup>[[21]](#ref21)</sup>,
- complications continue to emerge years after the index surgery,
- and both minor or endoscopic treatment and major open revision can be required; their proportions vary across cohorts.<sup>[[16]](#ref16)[[21]](#ref21)[[24]](#ref24)[[27]](#ref27)</sup>

Counseling therefore covers surveillance and occasional maintenance. In a retrospective cohort of 561 people whose channels were created at a median age of 8 years, estimated continued use was 89% at 10 years and 81% at 20 years; catheterization by others was associated with disuse, and lack of transition-clinic attendance with nonmechanical disuse.<sup>[[39]](#ref39)</sup> In a 2025 adult series of 44 channels, 28 of 37 evaluable patients (75.7%) still used the channel at median follow-up of 61 months.<sup>[[30]](#ref30)</sup>

A channel can also be the difference between continence and none in patients with severely limited hand function. In a series of six women with tetraplegia after spinal cord injury who remained incontinent despite maximal anticholinergic treatment and clean intermittent catheterization, all six catheterized from their wheelchairs after channel creation; mean catheterization time fell from 27 minutes (range 10–40) before surgery to 7.8 minutes (range 1–15) afterward. At a mean 44-month survey follow-up, all six reported improvement in continence, body image, independence, convenience, time saving and satisfaction.<sup>[[38]](#ref38)</sup>

**Parastomal hernia** can affect access or require repair, but the cited channel cohorts do not establish it as a principal failure mode or show that it usually disrupts continence. See [Parastomal Hernia After Urinary Diversion](/docs/surgical-techniques/04c-urinary-diversion/parastomal-hernia) for the separate repair discussion.

### Working up new-onset stomal incontinence

New leakage from the stoma is common enough that it deserves a stepwise workup rather than an automatic revision. Most is due to either valve-mechanism dysfunction, usually a detrusor tunnel that has become too short, or a change in reservoir dynamics rather than a tunnel problem. Check catheterization frequency, completeness of emptying, UTI, bladder calculus, and mucus impaction before urodynamics.<sup>[[32]](#ref32)</sup> If leakage remains unexplained, urodynamics can assess reservoir storage and channel closure before revision; address elevated storage pressure when present.<sup>[[4]](#ref4)[[5]](#ref5)[[21]](#ref21)</sup> In some augmented patients with stomal leakage, imaging has shown a narrow connection and hourglass-shaped reservoir associated with incomplete bowel detubularization and/or insufficient bladder bivalving at the original reconstruction; revising the augment with complete detubularization and a wide sagittal bladder opening has been described to resolve leakage in this setting, though without a denominator or comparative outcome.<sup>[[32]](#ref32)</sup> If infrequent catheterization contributes to leakage, increase frequency while assessing emptying, infection, stones, mucus and reservoir pressure.<sup>[[32]](#ref32)</sup> Reserve surgical revision for leakage confirmed to occur without elevated intravesical pressure and that remains bothersome; options are bulking-agent injection into the channel to increase outlet resistance, or revision of the detrusor tunnel (wrapping additional detrusor around the existing channel, or fashioning a new tunnel).

For channel bulking, one small series in patients being considered for surgical revision of stomal incontinence reported 10 of 14 patients (71%) dry after a single submucosal dextranomer/hyaluronic-acid injection and 11 of 14 (79%) after a second, at mean follow-up of one year.<sup>[[33]](#ref33)</sup> A literature table of four small series (5–14 patients each) reported 67–89% success across varied bulking materials and follow-up; this heterogeneous range is not a dependable contemporary success estimate.<sup>[[34]](#ref34)</sup> Checking reservoir function and inspecting the channel endoscopically before bulking is standard; injecting from proximal toward the skin has been reported as technically easier, treating only a short segment of the channel may be inadequate, and implantation has been described as more difficult in a thin-walled Yang-Monti tube than in an appendiceal channel.<sup>[[34]](#ref34)</sup>

## 10. Ileocecal Continence Mechanisms Are a Separate Option

Continent cutaneous ileal cecocystoplasty combines a tapered ileal outlet with ileocecal anatomy. It differs from a standard tunneled Mitrofanoff channel and has its own bowel and revision burdens.<sup>[[29]](#ref29)</sup>

Redshaw's retrospective 61-adult comparison found fewer patients needing a secondary procedure with 31 ileocecal constructions than with 30 tunneled channels (4 of 31, 13%, versus 15 of 30, 50%; OR 6.4) at median follow-up of 16 months. Thirty-day postoperative complications occurred in 51.7% of ileocecal cecocystoplasty patients and 40% of tunneled-channel patients in Redshaw's retrospective adult comparison. The median 16-month follow-up does not establish a lifetime reduction in maintenance or superiority of the ileocecal continence valve.<sup>[[29]](#ref29)</sup>

A distinct muscle-compression alternative, the Hadera mechanism, uses the native appendix as the catheterizable limb from a detubularized right-colon reservoir. Continence is created by crossing two non-detached right rectus-muscle strips around the appendix rather than relying on a submucosal tunnel.<sup>[[35]](#ref35)</sup> In the original 17-patient series, all patients were continent at a mean follow-up of 32 months (range 4–52), catheterizing every 2–4 hours even during straining.<sup>[[35]](#ref35)</sup> This has also been proposed as a possible salvage option when an existing tunneled valve fails, though the cited series describes primary construction only, and salvage effectiveness is not established.

---

**Evidence limits:** the Cochrane review found limited, poor-quality comparative trial evidence for diversion and reconstruction choices; it does not validate a particular channel tunnel or prove one material best.<sup>[[3]](#ref3)</sup>

## Core Principles at a Glance

1. **Build a flap valve**: the channel should be compressed by reservoir filling.
2. **Create enough backed tunnel length**: 3–5 cm is a common technical target; the 2-cm physiological observation is not a validated universal minimum.
3. **Match implantation method to the reservoir**: intravesical, extravesical, or serosal trough.
4. **Prefer appendix when available** and use Monti-family or bladder-flap alternatives when it is not.
5. **Balance channel caliber** so it closes reliably but remains easy to catheterize.
6. **Ensure the completed reservoir stores at low pressure**, adding augmentation at the same operation when indicated.<sup>[[13]](#ref13)</sup>
7. **Construct the stoma to resist stenosis** and preserve blood supply.
8. **Route the conduit straight and without tension**.
9. **Counsel for lifelong maintenance**, because revision is common.
10. **Consider native continence valves** such as the ileocecal valve when the reconstructive setting favors them.

---

## Bottom Line for the Reconstructive Surgeon

Continent catheterizable channels are valved access routes to a safe reservoir.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[2]](#ref2)[[6]](#ref6)</sup> The required components are a compressible narrow channel, a well-backed tunnel, a low-pressure reservoir, a straight route to the skin, and realistic long-term follow-up.

## Positioning, Catheter Management, and VTE

A CCC may be created alone or with augmentation or an outlet procedure, according to storage and access needs; positioning follows the planned operation.<sup>[[13]](#ref13)</sup> One published robotic pediatric protocol for combined augmentation and appendicovesicostomy uses supine semi-lithotomy with about 10° Trendelenburg so that catheter and cystoscopic access do not require repositioning.<sup>[[31]](#ref31)</sup>

In a patient who may need a future kidney transplant, siting the stoma so it will not conflict with a likely transplant incision, and identifying the location and orientation of the channel mesentery during operative planning, have been described as transplant-coordination considerations to work through with the transplant team; no comparative outcome data support one particular site.<sup>[[36]](#ref36)</sup>

A small feeding tube is left indwelling through the new channel to stent it during healing; in that same protocol, the channel catheter was capped and secured to the abdomen while two suprapubic catheters and one urethral catheter drained freely for 4 weeks. The channel catheter was then removed and CIC teaching began; the suprapubic catheters were removed after competence with CIC was shown.<sup>[[31]](#ref31)</sup> No consensus channel-specific stenting duration was found across the wider literature reviewed here; the period above is one published protocol. When reservoir surgery is concomitant, default to its drainage plan; see [Augmentation Cystoplasty](/docs/surgical-techniques/04b-bladder-reconstruction/bladder-augmentation) when augmentation is performed at the same setting.

No CCC-specific VTE recommendation exists in the AUA/SUFU NLUTD guideline.<sup>[[13]](#ref13)</sup> Because the operation is usually combined with bowel-based augmentation, apply the general risk framework (age, obesity, prior VTE, malignancy, prolonged operation or immobility) on the [VTE Prophylaxis](/docs/foundations/pharmacology/perioperative-eras/vte-prophylaxis) page, weighted toward the higher end when augmentation is performed concurrently.

## Follow-up and loss of access

Ensure a sustainable catheterization plan and an urgent response for inability to catheterize, especially after outlet closure. Avoid repeated forceful attempts; seek prompt reconstructive assessment and safe drainage. A continent stoma does not prove safe reservoir pressure.<sup>[[13]](#ref13)</sup>

A stepwise approach to a channel that will not catheterize has been described: first ensure bladder drainage through a patent native urethra when one exists, then attempt gentle passage with the patient's usual or a smaller lubricated catheter. If that fails, a hydrophilic guidewire may help, but a low threshold for small-caliber endoscopy and catheter placement over the wire is favored over repeated traumatic attempts; wire-guided dilation is limited to the channel's usual caliber, and dilation is preferred over incision because of the fragile channel and adjacent bowel. If urethral drainage and endoscopic access both fail, suprapubic drainage is the fallback, with open or ultrasound-guided placement considered when anatomy is altered or bowel is nearby; a catheter is typically left 1–2 weeks after dilation. This is one described specialist workflow; prompt reconstructive assessment and avoidance of force still apply.<sup>[[32]](#ref32)</sup>

For a continent cutaneous channel with proximal but segmentally limited stenosis, one described salvage option takes the channel down, mobilizes the reservoir, raises a U-shaped vascularized flap from the pouch, tapers it over a 16-Fr catheter, and imbricates the pouch around the base of the efferent channel to re-establish the valve; this is contrasted with complete channel replacement when fibrosis is longer or the channel is obliterated. No comparative outcome data support one approach over the other.<sup>[[37]](#ref37)</sup>

AUA/SUFU states that clinicians may offer continent catheterizable channels, with or without augmentation, to select NLUTD patients to facilitate catheterization (Statement 52, Conditional Recommendation; Evidence Level: Grade C). The statement text describes a systematic review of 11 retrospective studies (213 patients, median follow-up 36 months) in which reoperation was required in 40% and channel stenosis occurred in 4% to 32%, and it advises preoperative counseling that channel revision is common.<sup>[[13]](#ref13)</sup>

Maintain lifelong clinical and upper-tract follow-up. After bowel augmentation, follow the [augmentation surveillance plan](/docs/surgical-techniques/04b-bladder-reconstruction/augmentation-principles). For adults with NLUTD, AUA/SUFU recommends against screening or surveillance cystoscopy (Statement 19, Strong Recommendation; Evidence Level: Grade B).<sup>[[42]](#ref42)</sup> For NLUTD patients after lower urinary tract reconstruction using bowel, it recommends cystoscopy for gross hematuria or symptomatic recurrent UTI (Statement 60, Moderate Recommendation; Evidence Level: Grade C).<sup>[[13]](#ref13)</sup> The statement text adds unexplained suprapubic pain to the signs that warrant full evaluation.<sup>[[13]](#ref13)</sup>
