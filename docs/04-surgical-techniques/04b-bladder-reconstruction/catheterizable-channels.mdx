# Continent Catheterizable Channels

Continent catheterizable channels (CCCs) are surgically created conduits that provide a reliable route for clean intermittent catheterization (CIC) when the native urethra is inaccessible, unsafe, or impractical.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[2]](#ref2)[[3]](#ref3)</sup> They are built on the **Mitrofanoff principle**: a narrow supple conduit implanted into a **low-pressure reservoir** in a non-refluxing, tunneled fashion and brought to the skin as a continent catheterizable stoma.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[2]](#ref2)[[3]](#ref3)</sup>

For the reconstructive urologist, CCC creation is rarely a standalone trick. It usually sits inside a larger reconstruction: augmentation cystoplasty, bladder neck closure, exstrophy salvage, devastated urethra management, or combined urinary and bowel reconstruction.<sup>[[4]](#ref4)[[7]](#ref7)[[8]](#ref8)</sup> The channel only succeeds if the reservoir is low pressure, the outlet plan is coherent, and the patient can use the channel reliably for life.

---

## The Mitrofanoff Principle

Mitrofanoff's original insight was that continence can be created by combining three elements:<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[2]](#ref2)</sup>

1. A **narrow, supple conduit** such as appendix or retubularized bowel
2. **Submucosal tunneling** into the bladder or reservoir to create a flap-valve anti-reflux mechanism
3. A **low-pressure reservoir** drained by intermittent catheterization

As the reservoir fills, pressure compresses the tunneled conduit against the reservoir wall, preventing leakage. Passage of the catheter splints the tube open and permits drainage.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[3]](#ref3)</sup>

This is why CCCs are best understood as **continence mechanisms attached to a reservoir**, not merely as abdominal stomas.

## Indications

Per the AUA/SUFU NLUTD guideline, CCCs may be offered to selected patients who cannot catheterize the native outlet safely or effectively, or who strongly prefer a stoma for catheterization despite preserved urethral access.<sup>[[4]](#ref4)</sup>

| Indication | Practical example |
|---|---|
| **Devastated urethra** | Urethral destruction from chronic catheterization, pressure injury, stricture, or erosion<sup>[[4]](#ref4)</sup> |
| **Need for bladder neck closure** | Complete loss of urethral continence mechanism or unsalvageable urethra<sup>[[4]](#ref4)[[7]](#ref7)</sup> |
| **Neurogenic bladder** | Spina bifida and spinal cord injury are the commonest modern indications<sup>[[5]](#ref5)[[7]](#ref7)[[8]](#ref8)</sup> |
| **Bladder exstrophy / epispadias complex** | Reoperative continence reconstruction with difficult native outlet anatomy<sup>[[5]](#ref5)[[7]](#ref7)</sup> |
| **Posterior urethral valves or complex congenital dysfunction** | Channel creation as part of childhood-to-adult bladder preservation pathways<sup>[[5]](#ref5)[[7]](#ref7)</sup> |
| **Functional inability to catheterize urethrally** | Wheelchair dependence, severe body habitus, pelvic access difficulty, or limited hand function<sup>[[6]](#ref6)[[8]](#ref8)</sup> |
| **Patient preference** | Normal dexterity but preference for abdominal catheterization because it is easier, cleaner, or socially more manageable<sup>[[4]](#ref4)</sup> |

Neurogenic bladder accounts for the majority of channels in most series, but adult reconstructive practice increasingly includes non-neurogenic salvage indications as well.<sup>[[7]](#ref7)[[8]](#ref8)</sup>

## Tissue Options and Technique Families

### 1. Appendicovesicostomy (Mitrofanoff Appendicovesicostomy)

Appendix remains the **gold-standard channel** when available.<sup>[[9]](#ref9)[[10]](#ref10)</sup>

- The appendix is mobilized on its mesentery
- The proximal end is implanted into bladder or reservoir via a tunneled non-refluxing mechanism
- The distal end is matured to the skin, commonly at the umbilicus for cosmesis<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[9]](#ref9)</sup>

Why appendix is favored:

- Near-ideal caliber for 10–14 Fr catheterization
- Robust blood supply
- No separate bowel anastomosis required
- Lowest long-term revision burden among common channel types<sup>[[9]](#ref9)[[14]](#ref14)[[16]](#ref16)</sup>

Reported continence rates are typically **94–98%**, with long-term revision rates roughly **16–32%**, most often for stomal stenosis.<sup>[[9]](#ref9)[[12]](#ref12)[[13]](#ref13)[[14]](#ref14)</sup>

### 2. Yang-Monti (Monti) Ileovesicostomy

When the appendix is absent, too short, or already committed to a Malone channel, the Monti procedure is the standard substitute.<sup>[[11]](#ref11)[[15]](#ref15)</sup>

- A **2–3 cm** ileal segment is isolated
- Opened along the antimesenteric border
- **Transversely retubularized** to create a longer narrow tube
- Implanted into the bladder using the same Mitrofanoff principle<sup>[[11]](#ref11)[[15]](#ref15)</sup>

Monti channels are highly continent, but the price of appendix unavailability is often a higher long-term revision burden than APV.<sup>[[13]](#ref13)[[16]](#ref16)[[21]](#ref21)</sup>

### 3. Double Monti

When extra length is needed, two short ileal segments can be retubularized and joined to make a longer channel — especially useful in obese adults or patients with a thick abdominal wall.<sup>[[17]](#ref17)</sup>

### 4. Casale (Spiral Monti)

The Casale or spiral Monti uses one longer ileal segment opened in a spiral fashion and retubularized to gain length from a single bowel segment.<sup>[[13]](#ref13)[[17]](#ref17)</sup> It is attractive in adults who need longer reach, but some series suggest higher subfascial revision rates than shorter appendix-based channels.<sup>[[13]](#ref13)</sup>

### 5. Tubularized Bladder Flap (TBF)

TBF avoids bowel entirely by tubularizing a flap of native bladder wall into a catheterizable outlet.<sup>[[18]](#ref18)[[19]](#ref19)</sup> It is most useful when bladder capacity is already substantial and there is enough bladder tissue to spare. Its major advantage is avoiding bowel harvest; its limitation is obvious — it depends on having a good bladder to work with in the first place.

### 6. Reservoir-Based Variants

In continent cutaneous ileal cecocystoplasty and related constructs, the reservoir and the catheterizable outlet are built together using ileocecal tissue, rather than by adding a tunneled channel to an existing bladder.<sup>[[20]](#ref20)</sup> These variants may reduce secondary procedures in selected patients, but they remain major reconstructions with substantial postoperative morbidity.

## Stoma Location and Routing

The **umbilicus** is the commonest stomal site because it hides well and is usually easy to access.<sup>[[7]](#ref7)[[21]](#ref21)</sup> Other common sites are the right lower quadrant and suprapubic abdomen.

Stoma siting should be individualized:

- **Wheelchair users** need a site accessible while seated
- **Obese adults** may need longer channels and a flatter abdominal exit
- **Umbilical placement** offers excellent cosmesis but may carry higher subfascial revision rates in some longer Monti/Casale constructs<sup>[[13]](#ref13)[[17]](#ref17)</sup>

As with continent diversions, the best stoma is the one the patient can reliably use every day.

---

## Concomitant Procedures

CCCs are frequently created as part of a larger reconstructive package:<sup>[[7]](#ref7)[[22]](#ref22)</sup>

| Concomitant procedure | Why it matters |
|---|---|
| **Augmentation cystoplasty** | A channel only works well if the reservoir is low pressure; many patients need both<sup>[[7]](#ref7)</sup> |
| **Bladder neck plasty or closure** | Often required when urethral continence is impossible or the urethra must be abandoned<sup>[[4]](#ref4)[[7]](#ref7)</sup> |
| **Ureteral reimplantation** | Sometimes necessary in the same reconstructive setting |
| **Malone antegrade continence enema (MACE)** | Common in neurogenic bowel + bladder patients; appendix may need to be split or alternative tissue chosen<sup>[[23]](#ref23)</sup> |

This is why CCCs belong in bladder reconstruction. They are usually not "extra"; they are the route by which the reconstructed system becomes usable.

## Outcomes

### Continence

Channel continence is generally excellent across modern series.

| Channel type | Continence | Revision profile |
|---|---|---|
| **Appendicovesicostomy** | ~94–98%<sup>[[9]](#ref9)[[12]](#ref12)[[13]](#ref13)</sup> | Lowest revision burden |
| **Monti** | ~98%<sup>[[13]](#ref13)</sup> | Higher long-term revision rate than APV<sup>[[16]](#ref16)</sup> |
| **Casale / Spiral Monti** | Similar continence to Monti<sup>[[13]](#ref13)[[17]](#ref17)</sup> | Useful when extra length is needed |
| **Tubularized bladder flap** | Comparable continence in selected series<sup>[[18]](#ref18)</sup> | Attractive when avoiding bowel matters |

### Continued channel use

Long-term channel use remains high but not perfect. Continued use is reported around **76–96%** in most series, and roughly **75.7%** in the recent adult-focused study by Morris et al.<sup>[[8]](#ref8)[[24]](#ref24)</sup>

### Revision burden

The long-term burden is substantial. Across series, **39–53%** of patients undergo at least one surgical revision over time.<sup>[[16]](#ref16)[[18]](#ref18)[[24]](#ref24)[[25]](#ref25)</sup> This is the central counseling point: channels work well, but they are not low-maintenance.

---

## Complications

### Stomal stenosis

The commonest complication, reported roughly in the **10–50%** range depending on cohort and follow-up.<sup>[[5]](#ref5)[[14]](#ref14)[[21]](#ref21)[[25]](#ref25)</sup> It may present early, but late stenosis years after the original operation is well documented.

### Stomal leakage / incontinence

Occurs in roughly **6–36%** of patients.<sup>[[5]](#ref5)[[24]](#ref24)[[25]](#ref25)</sup> It may reflect stomal widening, inadequate tunnel length, a high-pressure reservoir, or failure of the continence mechanism.

### False passage and traumatic catheterization

False passage formation occurs in a meaningful minority and is usually the result of traumatic catheterization or difficult stomal access.<sup>[[18]](#ref18)</sup>

### Channel stricture / subfascial narrowing

Subfascial stenosis requiring formal open revision occurs in a subset of patients, often around **6–15%** in longer-term series.<sup>[[13]](#ref13)[[25]](#ref25)</sup>

### Prolapse

Late stomal prolapse is less common, but when present it often requires revision rather than simple office management.<sup>[[25]](#ref25)</sup>

### Channel type matters

On multivariable analysis, ileal channels carry higher reoperation risk than appendicovesicostomy, reinforcing the hierarchy many reconstructive surgeons already follow intuitively: **appendix first when available**.<sup>[[16]](#ref16)[[21]](#ref21)</sup>

---

## What Predicts Success?

### Compliance is king

Patient adherence to regular catheterization is the strongest predictor of durable success. Better compliance is associated with lower infection rates, lower stenosis rates, and fewer revisions.<sup>[[22]](#ref22)</sup>

### Body habitus matters

In adults, obesity affects continued stomal use and channel selection. A thicker abdominal wall often pushes reconstruction away from short appendix channels toward longer Monti/Casale designs, but those longer channels may carry more revision burden.<sup>[[13]](#ref13)[[17]](#ref17)</sup>

### Tissue type matters, but less than usability

Appendix generally outperforms ileal channels over time, but even a technically perfect channel fails if the patient cannot or does not catheterize it regularly.<sup>[[16]](#ref16)[[21]](#ref21)[[22]](#ref22)</sup>

## Malone Antegrade Continence Enema (MACE)

The same principle is used for bowel management in the **Malone antegrade continence enema** procedure, in which a catheterizable channel is implanted into the cecum to permit antegrade enemas.<sup>[[3]](#ref3)[[26]](#ref26)</sup>

This matters to bladder reconstruction because:

- Neurogenic bladder and neurogenic bowel commonly travel together
- Appendix may be needed for both urinary and bowel channels
- Surgeons may need to split the appendix or choose Monti-type alternatives when both channels are required simultaneously<sup>[[23]](#ref23)</sup>

MACE is not a bladder-reconstruction operation per se, but it strongly influences planning for CCC creation.

---

## Robotic Construction

Robot-assisted creation of catheterizable channels is now established in experienced hands.<sup>[[12]](#ref12)[[27]](#ref27)</sup> Continence appears equivalent to open reconstruction, with the usual MIS tradeoffs of shorter stay, less pain, and improved cosmesis. Its biggest limitation is not principle but complexity: many of these are combined reconstructions, and not every center can reproduce them minimally invasively.

---

## Postoperative Management

- CIC usually begins after initial healing and catheter removal, often **2–4 weeks** postoperatively
- Patients and caregivers need explicit teaching on catheter size, frequency, lubrication, and signs of traumatic catheterization
- Regular catheterization is essential to prevent channel closure
- Lifelong follow-up is mandatory, including surveillance of stomal patency, continence, reservoir function, and upper tracts<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[4]](#ref4)</sup>

The main postoperative message is simple: **a continent channel that is not used regularly is a channel at risk of failing**.

---

## Bottom Line for the Reconstructive Surgeon

Continent catheterizable channels are one of the signature operations of bladder reconstruction because they make otherwise unsalvageable lower urinary tract systems manageable. Appendix remains the best conduit when available, Monti-family channels are the workhorse backup, and all of them succeed or fail according to the same variables: a low-pressure reservoir, a usable stoma, and reliable lifelong catheterization.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[4]](#ref4)[[16]](#ref16)[[21]](#ref21)</sup>

The operation is worth offering, but only with honest counseling: continence is usually excellent, revision is common, and surveillance never ends.
