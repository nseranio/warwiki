# Appendicovesicostomy (Mitrofanoff Procedure)

**Appendicovesicostomy (Mitrofanoff procedure)** is a continent catheterizable urinary diversion using the **vermiform appendix as a conduit** between the bladder and an abdominal-wall stoma, allowing emptying by clean intermittent catheterization (CIC). First described by **Paul Mitrofanoff in 1980**, it implants a narrow, supple tube into a low-pressure reservoir via a **submucosal flap-valve mechanism** — analogous to a ureteral reimplantation — to achieve continence while permitting easy catheterization.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[2]](#ref2)</sup> It is the **gold standard** for creating a continent catheterizable channel (CCC) in patients unable to catheterize through the native urethra.<sup>[[3]](#ref3)</sup>

For broader CCC design rules (including Monti, double-Monti, tubularized bladder flap, and continent vesicostomy), see [Catheterizable Channels](/docs/surgical-techniques/04b-bladder-reconstruction/catheterizable-channels).

---

## The Mitrofanoff Principle

Three essential components:<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[2]](#ref2)[[4]](#ref4)</sup>

1. A **narrow, supple conduit** (appendix, ureter, Fallopian tube, or retubularized bowel) that acts as a flap valve when compressed against the reservoir wall during filling.
2. **Antireflux implantation** of the conduit into a low-pressure reservoir (native bladder, augmented bladder, or continent pouch) via a submucosal tunnel.
3. A **catheterizable cutaneous stoma** that is cosmetically acceptable and easily accessible.

![Appendicovesicostomy: the appendix on its mesoappendix tunneled into the bladder as a flap-valve and brought to a catheterizable skin stoma, with a flap-valve cross-section](/img/diagrams/appendicovesicostomy.svg)

*The Mitrofanoff principle realized with the appendix. The appendix — kept on its **mesoappendix** — is implanted into the bladder through a **submucosal tunnel** (a 4–5:1 tunnel-to-diameter flap-valve) and brought to a **catheterizable stoma** (umbilicus or RLQ). The cross-section shows why it stays dry: a filling bladder presses the channel flat against its detrusor backing, so continence is passive while a catheter still passes easily. If the appendix is unavailable, a [Yang-Monti channel](/docs/surgical-techniques/04b-bladder-reconstruction/yang-monti) substitutes. (Original WARWIKI schematic)*

The appendix is the **preferred conduit** — natural tubular shape, small caliber (10–14 Fr), reliable blood supply from the appendicular artery, and sufficient length (typically 6–10 cm).<sup>[[4]](#ref4)[[5]](#ref5)[[6]](#ref6)</sup>

## Indications

The AUA/SUFU NLUTD guideline supports CCC in patients who can perform self-catheterization but have a **devastated urethra** that cannot be catheterized, require **bladder neck closure**, or prefer a CCC for **ease of catheterization**.<sup>[[7]](#ref7)</sup>

- **Neurogenic bladder** (myelomeningocele, SCI, tethered cord, sacral agenesis) — most common, \>90% of cases in pediatric series.<sup>[[5]](#ref5)[[8]](#ref8)[[9]](#ref9)</sup>
- **Bladder exstrophy / epispadias** — particularly after [bladder neck closure](/docs/surgical-techniques/04ab-bladder-neck-reconstruction/outlet-continence/bladder-neck-closure), where the appendicovesicostomy provides the sole route for emptying. Largest exstrophy series (n=432): BNC + CCC achieves **93% continence** vs 64% with BN reconstruction alone.<sup>[[10]](#ref10)</sup>
- **Cloacal anomalies**.<sup>[[5]](#ref5)[[11]](#ref11)</sup>
- **Posterior urethral valves** with urethral damage.<sup>[[12]](#ref12)</sup>
- **Urethral stricture or destruction** from chronic catheterization.<sup>[[7]](#ref7)</sup>
- **Wheelchair-bound patients** for whom urethral CIC is difficult.<sup>[[2]](#ref2)[[13]](#ref13)</sup>
- **Adult neurogenic and non-neurogenic** bladder dysfunction (median age 42 in the largest adult series).<sup>[[14]](#ref14)</sup>
- **Oncologic patients** requiring continent diversion after cystectomy.<sup>[[11]](#ref11)[[15]](#ref15)</sup>

## Surgical Technique

### Step 1 — Appendiceal mobilization

- Identify the appendix; carefully preserve the **mesoappendix** to maintain blood supply from the appendicular artery.
- Divide the appendix at its base from the cecum; close the cecal defect (purse-string suture or stapler).
- Irrigate and calibrate the lumen (should accept a **10–14 Fr catheter**).<sup>[[2]](#ref2)[[6]](#ref6)[[16]](#ref16)</sup>

### Step 2 — Submucosal tunnel (antireflux implantation)

- Cystotomy (or open the augmented bladder).
- Create a **submucosal tunnel 3–5 cm in length** (4:1 or 5:1 tunnel-to-diameter ratio, similar to ureteral reimplantation).<sup>[[2]](#ref2)[[16]](#ref16)</sup>
- Draw the distal (cut) end of the appendix through the tunnel; anastomose to the bladder mucosa at the hiatus.
- **Detrusor backing ≥ 4 cm** is critical for continence — Wille demonstrated that inadequate detrusor backing (\< 4 cm) is the most significant predictor of stomal incontinence.<sup>[[17]](#ref17)</sup>
- Tunnel may be on the **posterior bladder wall** (intravesical approach, preferred when concomitant augmentation is performed) or the **anterior bladder wall** (extravesical approach, preferred for isolated appendicovesicostomy).<sup>[[18]](#ref18)</sup>

### Step 3 — Stoma creation

- **Umbilical stoma:** most popular — excellent cosmesis (hidden in the umbilicus). Used in 90% of cases in the Abdelhalim series. Higher stenosis rate than abdominal-wall stomas (25% vs lower with VQZ).<sup>[[19]](#ref19)[[20]](#ref20)[[21]](#ref21)</sup>
- **Right lower quadrant stoma:** traditional location; easier construction, less cosmetic.
- **VQZ plasty:** skin-flap technique creating a **concealed, skin-lined stoma** in the RLQ. The V-flap creates the posterior wall, the quadrilateral (Q) flap creates a skin tube that buries the appendiceal mucosa, and the Z-plasty prevents linear scar contracture. Landau showed **0% stomal stenosis** with VQZ vs 25% umbilical and 45% tubular skin flap. König 2025 confirmed no superficial stenosis in 29 patients.<sup>[[21]](#ref21)[[22]](#ref22)</sup>
- **VQ plasty:** simplified VQZ that eliminates the Z-component — neater linear scar with comparable functional outcomes.<sup>[[23]](#ref23)</sup>
- **VR flap:** González simplification with the quadrilateral flap at 90° from the V-flap, creating a linear closure.<sup>[[24]](#ref24)</sup>

### Step 4 — Concomitant procedures

Frequently performed simultaneously:<sup>[[5]](#ref5)[[11]](#ref11)[[13]](#ref13)</sup>

- [Augmentation cystoplasty](/docs/surgical-techniques/04b-bladder-reconstruction/bladder-augmentation) (52% in Cain).
- Bladder-neck reconstruction (48% in Cain) or [bladder-neck closure](/docs/surgical-techniques/04ab-bladder-neck-reconstruction/outlet-continence/bladder-neck-closure).
- **MACE (Malone antegrade continence enema)** for fecal incontinence (17% in Cain) — when both urinary and fecal channels are needed, the appendix can be **split** to create both channels.<sup>[[5]](#ref5)[[25]](#ref25)[[26]](#ref26)</sup>

## Stomal Stenosis Prevention

The most common complication, reported in **10–50%** depending on technique.<sup>[[11]](#ref11)[[21]](#ref21)[[27]](#ref27)</sup>

| Technique | Stenosis rate | Key feature |
|---|---|---|
| Standard (appendiceal tip excision) | 13% (12/93)<sup>[[27]](#ref27)</sup> | Traditional; tip amputated, lumen matured to skin |
| Kurzrock tip-preservation<sup>[[27]](#ref27)</sup> | **0%** (0/30) | Preserves distal appendiceal tip and vasculature; opens lumen proximally |
| VQZ plasty<sup>[[21]](#ref21)</sup> | 0% (0/8) | Skin-lined channel buries mucosa; prevents contracture |
| VQ plasty<sup>[[23]](#ref23)</sup> | Similar to VQZ | Simplified; neater scar |
| Umbilical stoma<sup>[[20]](#ref20)[[21]](#ref21)</sup> | 14–25% | Cosmetically superior but higher stenosis risk |
| Buccal mucosa graft (salvage)<sup>[[29]](#ref29)</sup> | 0% re-stenosis (n=10) | For established stenosis |

Kurzrock 2020: preserving the distal tip and its vasculature while opening the lumen in a more proximal, better-vascularized area eliminated stomal stenosis (0/30, median 3.3 yr) vs 13% with the standard technique (p=0.04). 75% of stenoses with the standard technique occurred within the first year.<sup>[[27]](#ref27)</sup>

### Timing of complications

- Jacobson 2017 (n=81, 119 stomas, 80.1 mo): unique complications cluster in the first 2 years, then **statistically significant decline** (p=0.0013); high-grade complications similarly cluster.<sup>[[31]](#ref31)</sup>
- Reuvers 2017 (n=128, 10.1 yr): **peak re-intervention in the first year**, decreased yearly thereafter; 63% of re-interventions superficial / endoscopic.<sup>[[9]](#ref9)</sup>
- Thomas 2006 (n=78, 117 stomas): stomal stenosis at mean **6.2–9.4 mo**; most complications within the first year.<sup>[[28]](#ref28)</sup>

## Complications

| Complication | Incidence | Timing | Management |
|---|---|---|---|
| Stomal stenosis | 10–54% | Peak 6–13 mo; 75% within 1 yr | Dilation, L-stent, VQ/VQZ revision, BMG graft |
| Difficult catheterization | 10–20% | Mean 29.9 mo | Catheter change, dilation, revision |
| Stomal incontinence | 6–8% | Variable | Endoscopic bulking (Deflux), revision, reimplantation with longer tunnel |
| False passage | 6–10% | Mean 3.6–6.5 mo | Catheter drainage; rarely requires revision |
| Conduit stricture | 9–15% | Variable | Endoscopic incision, revision |
| Bladder/reservoir stones | 6–32% | Increases with time | Lithotripsy, surgical removal |
| Conduit angulation/kinking | 3–6% | Variable | Surgical revision |
| Parastomal hernia | Rare | Variable | Surgical repair |
| Appendiceal perforation | 4% | Variable | Surgical repair |
| Small-bowel obstruction | 2–5% | Early | Conservative or surgical |
| Conversion to ileal conduit | 5–18% | Late (mean 82 mo) | Cystectomy + ileal conduit |

### Stomal stenosis management

- **L-stent** — short knotted catheter lying flush with skin: 100% improvement reported; significantly decreases need for surgical revision.<sup>[[33]](#ref33)</sup>
- **Topical betamethasone cream** during catheterization — common adjunct.<sup>[[33]](#ref33)</sup>
- **Buccal mucosa grafting** — salvage for refractory stenosis without creating a new channel; Radojicic reported no re-stenosis in 10 patients (mean 22 mo).<sup>[[29]](#ref29)</sup>

## Alternatives When the Appendix Is Unavailable

Appendix may be unavailable due to prior appendectomy, use for MACE, or unsuitable anatomy. See [Catheterizable Channels](/docs/surgical-techniques/04b-bladder-reconstruction/catheterizable-channels) for full coverage of alternatives:

| Alternative | Description | Continence | Notes |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Yang-Monti channel**<sup>[[34]](#ref34)[[35]](#ref35)</sup> | 2–3 cm ileal segment transversely retubularized | 93.8% | Always available; predictable length; higher subfascial revision (18.5% vs 0%) |
| **Spiral / double Monti**<sup>[[35]](#ref35)</sup> | Two ileal segments joined for length | Similar | Longer distances (e.g., obese patients) |
| **Casale technique**<sup>[[36]](#ref36)</sup> | Single ileal segment divided and unfolded for length | Similar | Avoids second bowel anastomosis |
| **Tubularized bladder flap**<sup>[[37]](#ref37)</sup> | Flap raised from bladder wall, tubularized | Similar | No bowel needed; requires adequate bladder volume |
| **Split appendix**<sup>[[25]](#ref25)[[26]](#ref26)</sup> | Appendix divided for simultaneous Mitrofanoff + MACE | No difference vs whole appendix | Shorter OR; avoids bowel anastomosis |
| **Continent vesicostomy**<sup>[[5]](#ref5)</sup> | Bladder wall to skin | 98% | Higher stomal complication rate than appendix |

Abdelhalim 2022 (n=120, median 11.4 yr): **ileal channels had 3.37× higher odds** of needing reoperation vs appendicovesicostomy on multivariate analysis (95% CI 1.24–9.17, p=0.037).<sup>[[19]](#ref19)</sup> Polm: Monti channels required significantly more **major revisions** (27%) than appendicovesicostomy.<sup>[[37]](#ref37)</sup> O'Connor 2019: ileal channels had higher channel-incontinence rate than appendiceal (36% vs 19.5%, p=0.03).<sup>[[14]](#ref14)</sup>

## Minimally Invasive Approaches

### Robot-assisted laparoscopic Mitrofanoff (RALMA)

- Gundeti's group: largest robotic experience, 38 cases. Detailed outcomes in 18: stomal continence **94.4%**, mean OR 494 min, mean LOS 5.2 d.<sup>[[18]](#ref18)</sup>
- The appendicovesical anastomosis can be **extravesical** (anterior bladder, preferred for isolated appendicovesicostomy) or **intravesical** (posterior bladder, preferred when concomitant augmentation is performed).<sup>[[18]](#ref18)</sup>
- Improved cosmesis, reduced pain, shorter LOS vs open; longer OR times; technically complex.<sup>[[8]](#ref8)[[38]](#ref38)</sup>

### Laparoscopic / laparoscopic-assisted

Cerchia 2026: both fully laparoscopic and laparoscopic-assisted Mitrofanoff techniques are safe and effective.<sup>[[3]](#ref3)</sup> Chua: laparoscopic-assisted approach offered lower EBL (50 vs 100 mL, p=0.048) and shorter LOS (3.5 vs 6 d, p=0.029) vs open in patients with prior abdominal surgeries.<sup>[[39]](#ref39)</sup>

## Adult-Specific Considerations

- O'Connor 2019 (n=176, median 142 mo): **75.9% of channels remained in use** with 90.2% continence among functioning channels; major revision rate 38.7%.<sup>[[14]](#ref14)</sup>
- Indications in adults: neurogenic bladder, urethral dysfunction, post-cystectomy continent diversion.<sup>[[14]](#ref14)[[15]](#ref15)[[40]](#ref40)</sup>
- **Higher stomal stenosis rates** in adults (up to 54%) than in pediatric series — body habitus, wound healing, longer bladder-to-skin distance.<sup>[[30]](#ref30)</sup>
- Quality of life generally high — 87% of adults did not regret the surgery (Rey).<sup>[[40]](#ref40)[[41]](#ref41)</sup>
- **Conversion to ileal conduit** in 5–18% of adults, typically for recurrent stomal complications or persistent incontinence; mean 82 mo postop.<sup>[[15]](#ref15)[[30]](#ref30)</sup>

---

## Bladder Exstrophy

- Largest exstrophy continence study (n=432, Johns Hopkins): 40% ultimately underwent BNC + CCC, achieving **93% continence** (95% CI 87–97%) — significantly higher than isolated BN reconstruction (64%).<sup>[[10]](#ref10)</sup>
- Only ~25% of exstrophy patients are expected to void normally per urethra.<sup>[[10]](#ref10)</sup>
- The ceco-appendiceal unit can be used for both augmentation and CCC in exstrophy, with long-term continence in all 11 patients in Tillem (2–11 yr).<sup>[[42]](#ref42)</sup>

---

## Split Appendix Technique (Mitrofanoff + MACE)

- Daugherty 2021 (n=106): split-appendix had **no difference in revision-free survival** vs whole-appendix Mitrofanoff + Monti MACE.
- Shorter OR (447 vs 619 min) and shorter LOS (9 vs 12–13 d).
- More subfascial revisions of urinary CCC in Monti channels (18.5%) vs split-appendix (0%).<sup>[[25]](#ref25)</sup>
- Kajbafzadeh & Chubak 2001 (n=40): divided appendix with separate mesotheliums achieved **100% continence** with 7.5% overall complication rate when the appendix was ≥9 cm with suitable branching mesothelium.<sup>[[26]](#ref26)</sup>

---

## Long-Term Surveillance

- Regular catheterization assessment — difficulty, bleeding, or pain should prompt evaluation for stenosis, false passage, or conduit stricture.
- Stomal inspection at each visit for stenosis, prolapse, or skin changes.
- Renal function monitoring and upper-tract imaging annually.
- If augmentation cystoplasty was performed concurrently, annual cystoscopy beginning 10 yr postoperatively for malignancy screening.
- Stone surveillance — particularly with augmented bladders (rates up to 32%).<sup>[[16]](#ref16)</sup>
- Patient/caregiver education — high likelihood of at least one re-intervention over the channel's lifetime.<sup>[[9]](#ref9)[[11]](#ref11)</sup>

---

## Key Recommendations

1. **The appendix remains the preferred conduit** — lower reoperation rates and better continence vs ileal alternatives.<sup>[[14]](#ref14)[[19]](#ref19)</sup>
2. **Submucosal tunnel ≥ 4 cm with adequate detrusor backing** is essential for stomal continence.<sup>[[2]](#ref2)[[17]](#ref17)</sup>
3. **VQZ/VQ plasty** or the **Kurzrock tip-preservation technique** should be considered to minimize stomal stenosis.<sup>[[21]](#ref21)[[22]](#ref22)[[27]](#ref27)</sup>
4. **The split-appendix technique** is preferred when both urinary and fecal channels are needed.<sup>[[25]](#ref25)</sup>
5. Counsel patients/families that approximately **one-third to one-half** of channels require at least one surgical revision over their lifetime.<sup>[[9]](#ref9)[[11]](#ref11)[[19]](#ref19)[[37]](#ref37)</sup>
6. **Lifelong urological surveillance** is mandatory.<sup>[[9]](#ref9)[[31]](#ref31)[[32]](#ref32)</sup>
