# Appendicovesicostomy (Mitrofanoff Procedure)

**Appendicovesicostomy** (the Mitrofanoff procedure) uses a vascularized appendix between the bladder or reservoir and an accessible skin stoma to permit clean intermittent catheterization (CIC). Mitrofanoff described it in 1980, with the distal appendix implanted in the bladder in a submucosal nonrefluxing fashion and the proximal end brought out as an inconspicuous stoma.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup> The tunneled reservoir insertion provides a flap-valve continence mechanism. The appendix is often preferred when it has suitable length, caliber and blood supply, but successful long-term use may require revision.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[2]](#ref2)[[3]](#ref3)</sup>

See [channel principles](/docs/surgical-techniques/04b-bladder-reconstruction/catheterizable-channels) for reservoir pressure, access and follow-up, and [Yang-Monti channels](/docs/surgical-techniques/04b-bladder-reconstruction/yang-monti) for bowel alternatives.

## The Mitrofanoff principle

The principle is a narrow, supple tube implanted in a nonrefluxing manner in a reservoir and brought to a catheterizable stoma for intermittent emptying. The appendix is the commonest source of the tube. A ureter, Fallopian tube or tailored intestine may be used instead. The tube is buried in the reservoir wall in a tunnel about 5 cm long.<sup>[[25]](#ref25)</sup> The reservoir can be the native bladder, an augmented bladder or a continent pouch. In Woodhouse's review, about 90% of patients were continent and up to 30% had conduit complications, particularly stenosis at the skin level. That review found no significant difference in results by diagnosis, source of the tube or type of reservoir.<sup>[[25]](#ref25)</sup> Sumfest's 47-patient series supported the appendix as the preferred catheterizable segment.<sup>[[7]](#ref7)</sup>

## Selection

AUA/SUFU supports catheterizable channels, with or without augmentation, in selected NLUTD patients to facilitate catheterization. The guideline statement carries a Conditional Recommendation (Evidence Level: Grade C). Confirm that the patient or a dependable caregiver can catheterize and manage the stoma long term. Indications include difficult urethral access, urethral destruction, selected outlet closure and patient preference for easier abdominal access.<sup>[[4]](#ref4)</sup>

Neurogenic disease, exstrophy or epispadias, cloacal anomalies, outlet obstruction and selected urinary diversions are common settings. Cain's 100-patient cohort was more than 90% neurogenic bladder, exstrophy-epispadias or cloacal anomaly combined. In it, augmentation was performed in 52 patients, bladder neck reconstruction in 48 and a Malone antegrade colonic enema (MACE) in 17.<sup>[[5]](#ref5)</sup> Harris's 50 patients had myelomeningocele (31), bladder exstrophy (6), prune-belly syndrome (2), posterior urethral valves (2) and other disorders (10).<sup>[[26]](#ref26)</sup> Faure's 54 patients had neurogenic bladder (21), epispadias-exstrophy (12), bladder outlet obstruction (13), malignancy (6) and cloaca (2).<sup>[[6]](#ref6)</sup>

Choose a stoma site that can be reached in the patient's usual seated position. The umbilicus can conceal the stoma, but convenience, channel reach, prior scars and body habitus take priority over a universal cosmetic preference. A bladder-neck closure makes an alternative dependable drainage route necessary; a CCC is one option, alongside selected permanent suprapubic drainage or diversion.<sup>[[2]](#ref2)[[4]](#ref4)</sup>

## Operative construction

### Appendix and vascular pedicle

Assess appendix length and caliber before committing to the reconstruction. Preserve the mesoappendix and appendicular vascular supply while separating the appendix from the cecum and securely closing the cecal defect. Calibrate for atraumatic passage of the planned catheter; do not force a fixed catheter size through an unsuitable appendix.<sup>[[2]](#ref2)[[7]](#ref7)[[8]](#ref8)</sup>

Plan orientation around pedicle reach and the selected stomal technique. The cecal base and distal appendiceal tip should be named explicitly during construction; “distal cut end” is ambiguous. Avoid twisting, tension and unnecessary skeletonization.<sup>[[2]](#ref2)[[8]](#ref8)</sup>

### Reservoir implantation

A submucosal or extravesical tunnel provides backed channel length without compressing it enough to impede catheterization. Published techniques commonly use several centimeters of backing; dimensions depend on the channel, reservoir and available anatomy.<sup>[[2]](#ref2)[[9]](#ref9)[[10]](#ref10)</sup>

Wille's early robotic experience included 11 completed robotic cases. The one patient with less than 4 cm of backing developed leakage and subsequently became continent after bulking injection. This supports attention to backing. The series was too small to establish a 4-cm cutoff.<sup>[[9]](#ref9)</sup>

In Famakinwa's 18-child series, isolated channels were implanted extravesically in the anterior bladder, while channels accompanying augmentation were implanted intravesically on the posterior wall. These are useful approach options, not mandatory sites for every reconstruction.<sup>[[10]](#ref10)</sup>

### Stomal maturation

Preserve distal blood supply and create an accessible, tension-free opening without a constricting circular scar. V-flap, VQ/VQZ and other skin-flap techniques can conceal bowel mucosa. Select the construction according to tissue and reach; no method eliminates stenosis.<sup>[[11]](#ref11)[[12]](#ref12)[[13]](#ref13)</sup>

In De Ganck's 53 patients, women and umbilical stomas had more complications than men and abdominal-wall stomas on multivariable analysis.<sup>[[27]](#ref27)</sup> The comparisons of skin-flap techniques are small and nonrandomized:

- The VQ plasty omits the Z component of the VQZ. In England's 21 patients (11 VQZ, 12 VQ stomas), it left a neater skin-lined stoma with less scarring and did not change the complication rate or stomal continence.<sup>[[28]](#ref28)</sup>
- The VR flap places the quadrilateral flap at 90 degrees to the V flap and allows a shorter conduit and a linear closure. In 12 children (24 channels, mean follow-up 18 months), all channels were continent, one patient needed revision and one needed dilation.<sup>[[29]](#ref29)</sup>
- Landau reviewed 40 patients with 50 urinary or enema conduits: none of eight VQZ stomas required treatment for stenosis, compared with 8/31 umbilical conduits requiring dilation or revision for six stenoses and two obstructions.<sup>[[11]](#ref11)</sup>
- Kurzrock compared 93 standard and 30 tip-preserving appendicostomies, including both urinary and enema channels. Stenosis occurred in 12/93 versus 0/30, with unequal median follow-up of 9.4 versus 3.3 years. The finding supports the modification; it does not guarantee prevention.<sup>[[13]](#ref13)</sup>
- König reported 31 children, 30 appendiceal channels and 29 VQZ stomas. Six children had seven channel dysfunctions, and no superficial stenosis was observed. Among 21 survey responders, 97% used the channel regularly and none reported channel incontinence.<sup>[[12]](#ref12)</sup>

### Combined urinary and fecal access

A split appendix can provide urinary and antegrade-enema channels when its length and branching vascular supply permit. Confirm both segments remain perfused and reach their destinations without tension. Kajbafzadeh's 40-patient series used appendices 9–15 cm long (average 10.3 cm), all patients were continent at mean follow-up of 22 months, and complications occurred in 7.5%. The authors considered a divided appendix suitable when it is 9 cm or more with a suitable branching mesentery.<sup>[[14]](#ref14)</sup>

Daugherty retrospectively compared 106 simultaneous urinary and fecal reconstructions: 64 split appendices, 27 urinary Monti channels and 15 whole appendices used for the urinary channel. At median 44.5 months, no significant difference in revision-free survival was detected. Urinary subfascial revision occurred in 5/27 Monti channels versus none in the other two groups. Shorter operating time and stay in the split group are observational associations.<sup>[[15]](#ref15)</sup>

## What long-term outcomes mean

Distinguish stomal dryness, continued channel use, urethral continence and freedom from revision. High use or continence after secondary treatment should not be reported as primary uncomplicated success.

| Study | Population and follow-up | Findings and limits |
|---|---|---|
| Reuvers 2017 | 128 pediatric appendicovesicostomies; mean 10.1 years | All but one still used the channel; 32% required reintervention. Stomal incontinence occurred in 6.3%. Continued use was 99%; the report does not give a dry rate.<sup>[[16]](#ref16)</sup> |
| Abdelhalim 2022 | 120 channels: 74 appendix, 33 Monti, 13 tapered ileum; median 11.4 years | Eventual dryness at intervals ≥3 hours in 90.8%; 26/120 required 42 interventions. About one-third of interventions occurred after five years. Ileal channels had higher adjusted odds of reoperation; tissue selection was not randomized.<sup>[[17]](#ref17)</sup> |
| O'Connor 2019 | 176 consecutive adults; 173 included; mean follow-up 78.6 months, median 60 | 75.9% of channels remained in use and 90.2% of those were continent; 38.7% underwent major revision. Channel incontinence was more frequent with ileum than appendix. The separate median 142-month interval since creation is not the reported clinical follow-up.<sup>[[3]](#ref3)</sup> |
| Polm 2024 | 173 channels under adult surveillance; median 12.4 years | 92/173 required any revision. Major revision occurred in 27/90 appendiceal, 31/51 Monti and 12/32 bladder-flap channels. Major includes open subfascial or complete revision, not only channel replacement; no significant difference in revision-free survival was found.<sup>[[18]](#ref18)</sup> |

These cohorts differ in diagnoses, techniques and ascertainment and may overlap earlier institutional publications. Their percentages are not pooled lifetime risks. Earlier series with shorter follow-up reported similar continence:

| Study | Population and follow-up | Findings and limits |
|---|---|---|
| Harris 2000 | 50 consecutive patients, one surgeon; mean age 13.1 years; mean follow-up 4.3 years | 96% continued to catheterize and 98% were continent. Stomal stenosis 10% (5 patients); revision 16%<sup>[[26]](#ref26)</sup> |
| Cain 1999 | 100 patients (57 appendicovesicostomy); mean follow-up 2 years | Stoma continent in 98 of 100. Complications requiring another procedure in 20, including stomal stenosis in 12. Continent vesicostomy had the most stomal problems (6 of 21)<sup>[[5]](#ref5)</sup> |
| Faure 2017 | 54 children; median follow-up 4.3 years | Operative revision in 33 (61%), mostly in the first 2 years. Stomal stenosis was the commonest complication, followed by leakage and conduit stricture<sup>[[6]](#ref6)</sup> |
| Gowda 2008 | 65 adults; mean age 38.4 years; mean follow-up 75.2 months | 46% had catheterization problems, most easy to treat. Five patients (8%) were converted to an ileal conduit. At the end, 92% had a conduit, of which 97% were catheterizable and 95% continent<sup>[[31]](#ref31)</sup> |
| Sahadevan 2008 | 29 adults; median follow-up 126 months | 89% (25 of 28 asked) continent. Stomal stenosis needing intervention in 54% (nine needed stomal reconstruction), calculi in 29%, and conversion to ileal conduit in 18% (five patients, mean 82 months)<sup>[[32]](#ref32)</sup> |

Across series, revision was reported in 16% to 61% of channels (Harris, Abdelhalim, Reuvers, O'Connor, Polm and Faure, at different follow-up). Counsel that at least one reintervention is likely over the channel's lifetime. In a 15-patient adult laparoscopic and robotic series, 13 (87%) reported no regret about the surgery.<sup>[[33]](#ref33)</sup>

### Exstrophy context

Maruf's cohort included 432 classic-exstrophy patients who underwent successful closure and a continence procedure; continence was assessed in 350. Among evaluated patients, 124/133 after bladder-neck closure with a CCC were continent versus 91/142 after isolated bladder-neck reconstruction. Continence meant at least three hours dry without nighttime leakage. This was a selected observational comparison. The authors concluded that only about 25% of patients are expected to void normally per urethra.<sup>[[19]](#ref19)</sup>

## Complications and practical response

Skin stenosis, deeper channel stenosis, angulation, redundancy, false passage and reservoir or channel leakage require different treatment. Complications can arise years after construction, even when early follow-up was uncomplicated.<sup>[[16]](#ref16)[[17]](#ref17)[[20]](#ref20)[[18]](#ref18)</sup>

Timing and frequency differ by series:

- **Timing.** In Jacobson's 81 patients (119 stomas, mean follow-up 80.1 months), 48 unique channel events occurred, clustered in the first 2 years and then declined significantly (P = 0.0013). Difficult catheterization occurred in 20.1% of channels at an average of 29.9 months and stomal stenosis in 12.6% at an average of 19.9 months.<sup>[[20]](#ref20)</sup> In Thomas's 78 patients (117 stomas), stomal stenosis appeared at a mean of 6.2 months (antegrade enema channels) and 9.4 months (bladder channels), and half were treated with surgical revision.<sup>[[30]](#ref30)</sup> Abdelhalim, by contrast, found 32.5% of interventions more than 5 years after surgery.<sup>[[17]](#ref17)</sup>
- **Stenosis.** Reported rates include 10% (Harris), 13% of standard appendicostomies (Kurzrock, 75% within a year) and 54% in adults (Sahadevan). Kurzrock cited a literature range of 12% to 45% after appendicostomy.<sup>[[26]](#ref26)[[13]](#ref13)[[32]](#ref32)</sup>
- **False passage.** It occurred in 6% of continent bladder channels (mean 6.5 months) and 10% of antegrade enema channels (mean 3.6 months) in Thomas's series, and was usually managed by catheter drainage alone.<sup>[[30]](#ref30)</sup>
- **Stones.** They formed in 32% of Duckett's 41 patients at average follow-up of 3.2 years (mucus and bacteriuria as the nidus) and in 29% of Sahadevan's adults.<sup>[[8]](#ref8)[[32]](#ref32)</sup>
- **Conduit stricture and stenosis at the bladder level.** Reuvers reported 9.4%.<sup>[[16]](#ref16)</sup>

**Inaccessible sole drainage channel.** This is urgent. Avoid repeated forceful catheterization. Obtain prompt reconstructive-urology assessment and establish safe drainage, using endoscopic guidance or another route when needed. Assess storage pressure and incomplete emptying before treating leakage as an isolated tunnel defect.<sup>[[4]](#ref4)</sup>

- **Superficial stenosis:** selected dilation, a temporary stomal device or skin revision. The L-stent report included only seven treated patients with subjective improvement; most also used topical betamethasone. It does not prove a 100% cure rate or reduction in surgery.<sup>[[21]](#ref21)</sup>
- **Recurrent stenosis:** buccal graft revision is a salvage option. Radojicic reported no recurrent stenosis in ten mixed channel types at mean 22 months; only three were appendiceal channels.<sup>[[22]](#ref22)</sup>
- **Deep stricture, false passage or angulation:** guided catheter drainage, endoscopic treatment or surgical revision according to anatomy. Avoid assuming that repeated blind dilation will address a structural kink.<sup>[[16]](#ref16)[[20]](#ref20)</sup>
- **Leakage:** review reservoir pressure, emptying and tunnel integrity. Bulking injection may help selected patients; evidence is too limited to promise durable success.<sup>[[4]](#ref4)[[9]](#ref9)</sup>

## Alternatives when the appendix is unavailable

The appendix may be absent, needed for a MACE or unsuitable. [Channel principles](/docs/surgical-techniques/04b-bladder-reconstruction/catheterizable-channels) and the [Yang-Monti page](/docs/surgical-techniques/04b-bladder-reconstruction/yang-monti) cover the alternatives. In Lemelle's 46 patients (65 conduits), 93.8% of conduits were in use at follow-up, with no significant difference between appendix and Yang-Monti channels, and the authors recommended the Yang-Monti channel when the appendix is unsuitable.<sup>[[34]](#ref34)</sup> In Polm's 112 children (117 channels), surgical revision was required in 52% with no difference in revision-free survival among appendix, tubularized bladder flap and Monti channels. Major revision (27%) was significantly more frequent with Monti channels.<sup>[[35]](#ref35)</sup> Ileal channels had higher odds of reoperation than appendix channels in Abdelhalim's series, and higher channel incontinence in O'Connor's adult series (36.0% vs 19.5%, P = 0.03).<sup>[[17]](#ref17)[[3]](#ref3)</sup>

## Minimally invasive approaches

Open, laparoscopic-assisted and robotic construction are feasible in experienced hands. Comparative evidence is predominantly retrospective and confounded by concomitant operations and case selection.

Chua compared 12 laparoscopic-assisted with 11 open isolated appendicovesicostomies after prior abdominal surgery. Median blood loss (50 vs 100 mL, P = 0.048) and stay (3.5 vs 6 days, P = 0.029) were lower in the laparoscopic-assisted group. No significant difference in major complications or continence was detected.<sup>[[23]](#ref23)</sup> Famakinwa's robotic series comprised 18 children (10 intravesical, 8 extravesical), with mean operative time of 494 minutes, mean stay of 5.2 days, median follow-up of 24.2 months and stomal continence of 94.4%.<sup>[[10]](#ref10)</sup>

Cerchia's exploratory series of seven boys describes fully laparoscopic and laparoscopic-assisted techniques for settings without robotics.<sup>[[24]](#ref24)</sup>

## Follow-up

Review catheter passage, drainage schedule, leakage, stoma changes, infections, renal function and upper tracts throughout life. Adjust catheter size and timing to the reconstruction; teach an urgent access-failure plan before discharge.<sup>[[4]](#ref4)[[18]](#ref18)</sup>

For NLUTD with lower urinary tract reconstruction incorporating bowel, AUA/SUFU advises annual history, examination and symptom assessment, a basic metabolic panel and urinary tract imaging (Statement 58, Expert Opinion). The guideline distinguishes an isolated catheterizable channel from a large urine-exposed bowel reservoir. Follow [augmentation surveillance](/docs/surgical-techniques/04b-bladder-reconstruction/augmentation-principles) when relevant.<sup>[[4]](#ref4)</sup>

Annual screening cystoscopy from year ten is not routine. AUA/SUFU states that clinicians should not perform screening or surveillance cystoscopy in NLUTD patients (Statement 19, Strong Recommendation; Evidence Level: Grade B), and should perform cystoscopy after bowel reconstruction when gross hematuria or symptomatic recurrent UTI develops (Statement 60, Moderate Recommendation; Evidence Level: Grade C). Unexplained suprapubic pain also warrants investigation.<sup>[[4]](#ref4)</sup>
