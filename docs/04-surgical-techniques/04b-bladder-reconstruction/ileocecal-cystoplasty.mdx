# Ileocecal Cystoplasty (Ileocecocystoplasty)

**Ileocecal cystoplasty** is a form of [augmentation cystoplasty](/docs/surgical-techniques/04b-bladder-reconstruction/bladder-augmentation) that uses ileocecal bowel to enlarge the bladder. In Indiana-type CCIC, detubularized cecum and ascending colon form the augment, while terminal ileum is tapered into a catheterizable channel.<sup>[[24]](#ref24)</sup> Its principal advantage over standard [ileocystoplasty](/docs/surgical-techniques/04b-bladder-reconstruction/ileocystoplasty) is the ability to simultaneously create a continent catheterizable channel using the ileocecal valve mechanism, while the colonic wall can support a constructed submucosal ureteral tunnel. Ureteral antireflux function is not supplied automatically by the native ileocecal valve.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[2]](#ref2)</sup> Mainz Pouch I and Indiana-type continent cutaneous ileocecocystoplasty (**CCIC**) are related ileocecal reconstructions with different configurations and continence mechanisms; their operative steps and outcome cohorts should not be conflated.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup>

---

## Indications

Indications include selected neurogenic patients with detrusor overactivity and/or poor compliance refractory to or intolerant of less invasive therapy (AUA/SUFU Conditional Recommendation; Grade C).<sup>[[27]](#ref27)</sup> For idiopathic OAB, the 2024 AUA/SUFU guideline says clinicians may offer augmentation cystoplasty or urinary diversion to severely impacted patients who have not responded to all other therapeutic options (Expert Opinion).<sup>[[28]](#ref28)</sup> Ileocecal reconstruction may be selected when:<sup>[[2]](#ref2)[[3]](#ref3)[[27]](#ref27)[[28]](#ref28)</sup>

- A continent catheterizable stoma is needed because urethral catheterization is inaccessible or impractical; augmentation may be limited if native capacity is adequate. Before choosing CCIC, confirm that the patient or a dependable caregiver can perform regular channel catheterization.<sup>[[2]](#ref2)[[27]](#ref27)</sup>
- **Ureteral reimplantation when separately indicated:** a cecal or ascending-colon submucosal tunnel is a Mainz option; Sutton's CCIC series preferred reimplantation into native bladder when feasible. The need for simultaneous reimplantation with augmentation in high-grade reflux remains unclear.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[2]](#ref2)[[29]](#ref29)</sup>
- The appendix is unavailable for a Mitrofanoff channel (the ileocecal valve itself can serve as the continence mechanism).

Before ileocecal harvest, review bowel disease and prior resection, nutritional and renal status, and the expected course of catheterization ability.<sup>[[24]](#ref24)</sup>

Reported indications include neurogenic bladder (including SCI and myelodysplasia) and bladder exstrophy.<sup>[[4]](#ref4)</sup> In IC/BPS, major surgery is an option for carefully selected patients with bladder-centric symptoms or, rarely, an end-stage small fibrotic bladder after other therapies fail (AUA Option; Grade C).<sup>[[26]](#ref26)</sup> The cited ileocecal series involved severe ulcerative disease. Before a trigone-preserving augment, assess trigonal involvement and tolerance of trial catheterization; painful or unacceptable catheterization may favor a different reconstruction.<sup>[[5]](#ref5)[[26]](#ref26)</sup>

## Surgical Technique

Before reservoir construction, assess outlet competence and plan concomitant continence surgery when indicated; 11/23 patients in Sutton's CCIC series received urethral closure, sling, or bladder-neck injection.<sup>[[2]](#ref2)</sup> The following operative outline is configuration-specific; its steps are not interchangeable across ileocecal reconstructions.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[4]](#ref4)</sup>

1. **Segment isolation.** In the original Mainz configuration, cecum and two ileal loops form the reservoir. Indiana-type CCIC uses a colonic augment and a tapered terminal-ileal channel with an ileocecal continence mechanism. Segment lengths, detubularization limits, and colonic mobilization depend on the selected design.<sup>[[6]](#ref6)</sup>
2. **Bowel continuity restoration:** perform the ileocolonic (ileoascending) anastomosis. A 2024 retrospective series reported an ileocolic anastomotic leak in 2/56 CCIC patients (3.6%); a reinforced stapled ileocolic anastomosis was associated with fewer anastomotic leaks than a single-layer ileocolic anastomosis across CCIC and right-colon-pouch cases, without establishing a universal anastomosis method.<sup>[[30]](#ref30)</sup>
3. **Detubularization.** Open the bowel forming the reservoir along its antimesenteric border. In a Mainz reservoir, the cecum and two ileal loops are detubularized; in Indiana-type CCIC, retain the terminal ileal limb for tapering into a catheterizable channel.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[24]](#ref24)</sup> Early nondetubularized ileocecal series found cecal or volume-dependent contractions in 8/13 urodynamically assessed patients (62%) in Mayo’s series and all 10 patients (100%) in Sidi’s intact-ileocecal group; reflux accompanied cecal contractions in some Mayo patients.<sup>[[7]](#ref7)[[8]](#ref8)[[9]](#ref9)</sup>
4. **Reconfiguration:** opened segments folded and sutured into a spherical, low-pressure pouch.
5. **Bladder preparation and anastomosis:** bivalve the native bladder for conventional clam augmentation. Luangkhot's modified neurogenic-bladder series used a posterior-based bladder flap anchored to the psoas for a wide anastomosis; the cited ulcerative IC/BPS series instead performed supratrigonal cystectomy and joined the pouch to the preserved trigone.<sup>[[4]](#ref4)[[5]](#ref5)</sup>
6. **Continent catheterizable channel** (when performed as continent cutaneous ileocecocystoplasty, **CCIC**). The ileocecal valve can be used as the continence mechanism. Indiana-type CCIC tapers the ileal channel and reinforces the ileocecal continence mechanism with interrupted imbricating slow-dissolving sutures.<sup>[[24]](#ref24)</sup> Mainz-type ileoileal nipple intussusception is a distinct option, not the same as leaving a native valve intact. Alternatively, the appendix can be tunneled submucosally into the cecal wall as a Mitrofanoff-type channel.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[10]](#ref10)[[11]](#ref11)</sup>
7. **Ureteral reimplantation:** when needed, ureters reimplanted into the cecal or ascending-colon wall via a standard submucosal tunnel.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup>

**Minimally invasive approach.** A hand-assisted laparoscopic CCIC has been described using a Pfannenstiel incision as the hand port and the umbilicus as the future stoma site; a retrospective comparison of 32 hand-assisted versus 21 open procedures found fewer 90-day Clavien grade 2 or higher complications (18.8% versus 47.6%). The open group had more prior augmentation/channel surgery; this does not prove a causal access benefit or long-term equivalence.<sup>[[6]](#ref6)</sup>

## Outcomes

- **Bladder capacity:** mean 185 to 595 mL in one series; +277 mL average in another.<sup>[[2]](#ref2)[[4]](#ref4)</sup>
- **Mean maximum detrusor pressure:** decreased from 53 to 16 cm H₂O (p&lt;0.0001).<sup>[[4]](#ref4)</sup>
- **Continence:** Sutton reported urethral and stomal continence in 22/23 CCIC patients at mean 26.9 months (21/22 assessed in the follow-up table); 11/23 also had concomitant urethral-outlet procedures, so this is a combined-reconstruction outcome.<sup>[[2]](#ref2)</sup> Luangkhot reported continence in 20/21 adults after modified ileocecocystoplasty at mean 3.1 years.<sup>[[4]](#ref4)</sup> In the mixed Mainz series, 32/34 augmentation patients were dry day and night; its substitution and diversion outcomes are separate.<sup>[[12]](#ref12)</sup>
- **Upper-tract protection:** in a historical 129-patient mixed-segment augmentation or replacement cohort, 91% maintained or improved renal function in combination with clean intermittent catheterization at average 44-month follow-up; this is not an ileocecal-specific estimate.<sup>[[13]](#ref13)</sup>
- **Voluntary voiding:** among 26 followed women with severe ulcerative IC/BPS after supratrigonal cystectomy, 12/18 ileocecal recipients versus 2/8 ileal recipients emptied voluntarily (passive voiding or abdominal straining; p = 0.036) at a cohort median of 171 months. This nonrandomized comparison does not establish a segment effect.<sup>[[5]](#ref5)</sup>

A retrospective series of over 800 continent cutaneous Mainz Pouch I diversions, rather than native-bladder augmentations, reported 92.8% day-and-night continence at mean 7.6 years. Use its valve/stoma data as related diversion evidence, not direct augmentation outcomes.<sup>[[14]](#ref14)</sup>

## Advantages Over Ileocystoplasty

| Feature | Ileocecal Cystoplasty | Ileocystoplasty |
|---|---|---|
| Catheterizable channel | Tapered ileum plus a surgically constructed/reinforced valve mechanism in CCIC | Separate channel may be added when needed |
| Antireflux ureteral reimplantation (when indicated) | Cecal or ascending-colon tunnel is possible in selected Mainz reconstructions; CCIC may use native bladder | Native-bladder reimplantation is also possible; neither augment automatically requires it <sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[2]](#ref2)[[29]](#ref29)</sup> |
| Secondary procedure for channel leakage, obstruction or stenosis | 4/31 CCIC vs 15/30 tunneled channels in one retrospective adult comparison | The comparison concerns channel types, not isolated ileocystoplasty <sup>[[15]](#ref15)</sup> |
| Metabolic acidosis | Risk depends on segment, contact time, renal function, and emptying | No established augmentation-specific advantage from the cited neobladder trial |
| Emptying | Depends on outlet, native bladder, and catheterization strategy | Neobladder comparisons do not establish augmentation superiority |
| Surgical complexity | Greater (hepatic flexure mobilization) | Less extensive dissection |
| Bowel morbidity | Loss of ileocecal valve → diarrhea risk | Preserves ileocecal valve |

In a retrospective 61-adult multicenter study at median 16-month follow-up, a secondary procedure for stomal leakage, channel obstruction or stomal stenosis was required in 4/31 CCIC versus 15/30 tunneled-channel patients (OR 6.4, 95% CI 1.8–28 for tunneled channels). Postoperative complications were 51.7% after CCIC and 40% after tunneled channels. This short, nonrandomized comparison does not establish lifelong superiority.<sup>[[15]](#ref15)</sup>

## Complications

- **Delayed bladder perforation.** Peritonitis or clinical deterioration after augmentation warrants urgent assessment for perforation; management depends on stability and the extent of extravasation.<sup>[[3]](#ref3)[[25]](#ref25)</sup>
- **Postoperative complications.** In a contemporary multicenter series of 114 adult CCIC (median follow-up 40 months), 15.8% had major complications at 3 months. Additional related surgery was needed in 42.1%, and 20.2% had at least one channel revision, most commonly for obstruction (13.2%) or incontinence (3.5%). 9.6% abandoned the catheterizable channel.<sup>[[19]](#ref19)</sup>
- **Vesicoureteral reflux.** In Mitchell’s mixed-segment series, reflux in the ileocecal cystoplasty group was the most common surgical complication. In Mayo’s nondetubularized ileocecal series, reflux was observed at capacity with a cecal contraction in 4/13 urodynamically assessed patients (31%).<sup>[[7]](#ref7)[[13]](#ref13)</sup>
- **Persistent cecal contractions.** With non-detubularized segments, volume-dependent contractions were universal in Sidi's series (all 10 intact ileocecal segments, compared with 12% of sigmoid cup-patch reconstructions)<sup>[[8]](#ref8)</sup>, and Mayo reported cecal contractions in 8 of 13 patients despite adequate anticholinergic doses.<sup>[[7]](#ref7)</sup> Goldwasser found contractions more common with tubular than with detubularized reconstruction.<sup>[[7]](#ref7)[[8]](#ref8)[[9]](#ref9)</sup>
- **Reservoir stones and access:** in a retrospective CCIC cohort published in 2024, 7/56 patients (12.5%) underwent bladder or reservoir stone removal more than 90 days after surgery (median follow-up 46 months).<sup>[[30]](#ref30)</sup> Wiesner's related Mainz Pouch I diversion series reported calculi in 10.8% of intussuscepted ileal-nipple reservoirs and 5.6% of appendix-stoma reservoirs; those rates are not augmentation-specific.<sup>[[14]](#ref14)</sup> Transstomal stone treatment can damage the continence mechanism; EAU notes that percutaneous or open access is typically preferred when a continent channel is present.<sup>[[30]](#ref30)[[14]](#ref14)[[31]](#ref31)</sup>
- **Stomal stenosis in related continent diversion:** Wiesner reported stenosis in 23.5% of appendix stomas and 15.3% of intussuscepted ileal nipples after Mainz Pouch I diversion; these are not augmentation-specific rates.<sup>[[14]](#ref14)</sup>
- **Metabolic disturbances.** Hyperchloremic acidosis remains a concern. The cited 60-patient randomized comparison studied orthotopic neobladders after cancer cystectomy, not augmentation; it cannot establish a metabolic advantage of ileocecal augmentation.<sup>[[16]](#ref16)</sup> Clinically significant acidosis primarily seen with pre-existing renal insufficiency.<sup>[[13]](#ref13)</sup>
- **Bowel-related complications from ileocecal segment harvest:**
  - **Diarrhea.** Loss of ileocecal valve shortens transit time and impairs bile-salt reabsorption, leading to mixed secretory-osmotic diarrhea, fat malabsorption, steatorrhea. Functional reconstruction of the ileocecal valve has been described to mitigate.<sup>[[17]](#ref17)[[18]](#ref18)</sup>
  - **Vitamin B₁₂ deficiency.** Terminal ileal incorporation and prior ileal disease/resection increase risk; monitor over time rather than treating a fixed 30 cm boundary as a guarantee of safety. IBD nutrition guidance is adjacent evidence, not a specific augmentation protocol.<sup>[[17]](#ref17)[[20]](#ref20)</sup>
  - **Cholelithiasis:** bile-salt derangements increase pigment-stone propensity.<sup>[[17]](#ref17)</sup>
  - **Hyperoxaluria and urolithiasis.** Fat malabsorption leads to free oxalate absorption, then calcium-oxalate stones.<sup>[[17]](#ref17)</sup>
- **Hernia.** Open CCIC requires a long midline laparotomy, leading to high rates of ventral and parastomal hernias; the hand-assisted laparoscopic approach was specifically designed to address this.<sup>[[6]](#ref6)</sup>

## Malignancy Risk

An ileocecal-specific equivalence to other augmentation types has not been established. Adenocarcinoma was the most common histologic type (51.6% of post-augmentation malignancies in a systematic review); a separate, selected 10-case pathology series found signet-ring features in 9 of 10 selected non-urothelial carcinomas.<sup>[[21]](#ref21)[[22]](#ref22)</sup> About half of the tumors arise at the entero-urinary anastomosis, and the mean latency is approximately 19 years.<sup>[[21]](#ref21)[[22]](#ref22)</sup> Whether augmentation itself is an independent risk factor remains debated; one matched cohort study of 153 augmented patients with congenital bladder dysfunction and matched catheterization controls found no significant difference (4.6% versus 2.6%, p=0.54).<sup>[[23]](#ref23)</sup>

## Long-Term Follow-Up

- Annual history, examination, symptoms, and review of reliable emptying; a continent cutaneous channel requires catheterization. Review mucus management and the saline irrigation plan; the 2026 EAU urolithiasis guideline recommends regular irrigation for augmented or continent reservoirs without a history of autonomic dysreflexia to reduce stone recurrence (Weak).<sup>[[2]](#ref2)[[31]](#ref31)</sup>
- Annual metabolic panel (electrolytes, bicarbonate, renal function).
- Annual urinary tract imaging, usually renal ultrasound, for hydronephrosis and stones.
- Periodic vitamin B₁₂ levels.
- In adults with neurogenic lower urinary tract dysfunction who have undergone bowel reconstruction, perform cystoscopy for gross hematuria or symptomatic recurrent UTI (AUA/SUFU Moderate Recommendation; Grade C).<sup>[[27]](#ref27)</sup>
- Repeat urodynamics at an appropriate interval after treatment when impaired storage or voiding places the upper tracts at risk (AUA/SUFU Expert Opinion).<sup>[[27]](#ref27)</sup>
- Channel assessment for stenosis or continence issues.<sup>[[3]](#ref3)[[19]](#ref19)</sup>
