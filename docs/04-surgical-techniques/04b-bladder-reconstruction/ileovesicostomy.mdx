# Ileovesicostomy

**Ileovesicostomy** is an incontinent cutaneous urinary diversion that connects the bladder dome to an abdominal stoma through an ileal conduit ("bladder chimney") without ureteroenteric anastomosis. It aims to provide continuous low-pressure drainage.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[2]](#ref2)</sup> It was popularized by Schwartz, Kennelly, McGuire, and Faerber in 1994 for patients with severe neurogenic LUTD who cannot perform CIC and have suffered chronic-catheter morbidity.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup> Its advantage over an [ileal conduit](/docs/surgical-techniques/04c-urinary-diversion/ileal-conduit) is that it preserves the native bladder and ureterovesical junctions, avoiding a new ureteroenteric anastomosis and sometimes permitting later undiversion. Native ureterovesical junctions do not guarantee absence of reflux or upper-tract obstruction.<sup>[[2]](#ref2)[[3]](#ref3)</sup>

---

## Concept and Rationale

It may be offered to selected patients with neurogenic bladder who cannot perform CIC because of poor hand function, immobility, body habitus, or social or cognitive limitations.<sup>[[5]](#ref5)[[19]](#ref19)</sup> Retrospective series included patients with prior catheter-associated urosepsis, calculi, urethrocutaneous fistulae, autonomic dysreflexia, or progressive hydronephrosis.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[4]](#ref4)</sup>

The procedure creates a tubularized ileal segment connecting the bladder dome to the skin for continuous low-pressure drainage into a urostomy bag. Unlike an ileal conduit, the ureters are not transected or reimplanted; they remain in their native position within the bladder wall.<sup>[[2]](#ref2)[[3]](#ref3)</sup>

---

## Indications

AUA/SUFU states that clinicians may offer ileovesicostomy to select NLUTD patients and must counsel them on risks, benefits, alternatives and the high risk of needing additional treatment or surgery (Statement 53, Conditional Recommendation; Evidence Level: Grade C). It is not the default for everyone unable to catheterize; obesity and a long poorly draining channel can make it unsuitable.<sup>[[5]](#ref5)</sup> Specific indications:

- **High cervical SCI or tetraplegia.** Most common indication.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[3]](#ref3)</sup>
- **MS** with progressive disability precluding CIC.<sup>[[2]](#ref2)</sup>
- **Myelomeningocele** with failed prior management.<sup>[[2]](#ref2)[[6]](#ref6)</sup>
- Complications of chronic indwelling catheter drainage.<sup>[[4]](#ref4)</sup>
- Failed prior bladder management (sphincterotomy, augmentation, AUS).<sup>[[2]](#ref2)[[7]](#ref7)</sup>
- Pediatric patients unable to perform reliable CIC.<sup>[[6]](#ref6)</sup>
- Patients who are poor candidates for or refuse continent diversion or augmentation.<sup>[[2]](#ref2)</sup>

## Surgical Technique (Open, McGuire/Schwartz)<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[8]](#ref8)[[9]](#ref9)</sup>

Before surgery, review upper-tract imaging and urodynamics to assess storage pressure, reflux, expected emptying, and outlet competence; use the findings to plan drainage and any outlet procedure.<sup>[[8]](#ref8)[[19]](#ref19)</sup>

1. **Ileal segment harvest:** Select a well-perfused 15–20 cm distal-ileal segment on its mesentery while preserving the ileocecal valve and distal-most ileum when feasible; restore bowel continuity.<sup>[[8]](#ref8)[[20]](#ref20)</sup>
2. **Proximal detubularization.** The proximal 6–8 cm is opened along the antimesenteric border to create a wide funnel for the bladder anastomosis.
3. **Cystotomy:** dome opened widely.
4. **Ileovesical anastomosis.** Detubularized proximal ileum sutured to the bladder dome in a wide funnel to prevent obstruction at the ileovesical junction.
5. **Stoma:** Mark a pouch-compatible site with the patient in usual positions, including wheelchair seating when relevant.<sup>[[21]](#ref21)</sup> The right lower quadrant is a described location when pouch fit and mesenteric reach permit.<sup>[[3]](#ref3)</sup> Bring the tubular distal ileum through the wall and mature an everted, protruding stoma when feasible; an ileal-conduit technique report suggests that a flush stoma can impair pouch fit.<sup>[[22]](#ref22)[[23]](#ref23)</sup>

Principles: the ileal segment functions as a conduit and not a reservoir; ureters are not touched; a wide funnel reduces junctional narrowing, but stomal stenosis, angulation, redundancy, and retained debris can still impair drainage.

### Concomitant procedures

- **Pubovaginal sling (PVS)** in women with intrinsic sphincter deficiency from prolonged catheterization to achieve functional urethral closure.<sup>[[8]](#ref8)[[10]](#ref10)</sup>
- **Bladder-neck closure** may be considered for a nonreconstructible leaking outlet; it is not obligatorily preceded by a sling. Closure sacrifices native urethral access and makes dependable alternate drainage necessary.<sup>[[11]](#ref11)[[12]](#ref12)</sup>
- **Bladder calculus removal** when stones are present.<sup>[[8]](#ref8)</sup>
- **Postoperative anticholinergic treatment.** Four women with mild urethral urge leakage after ileovesicostomy were treated with oxybutynin or tolterodine in Atan's 15-patient series.<sup>[[2]](#ref2)</sup>

### Minimally invasive approaches

- **Laparoscopic** (Hsu 2002): a single-patient report of a 5-port transperitoneal approach with a 17 cm ileal segment and an operative time of about 4 hours.<sup>[[13]](#ref13)</sup>
- **Robotic-assisted** (Vanni/Stoffel 2009, n=8): 5-port; intracorporeal enterovesical anastomosis; extracorporeal bowel reanastomosis through stoma site. Median EBL 100 mL, OR 330 min, LOS 7.7 d; four patients had postoperative complications (urethral incontinence in 2, ileus in 2). All were functioning at median 14 months, with median residual bladder volume 10 mL.<sup>[[9]](#ref9)</sup>
- **Open versus robotic** (7 versus 8 patients): this retrospective comparison found no statistically significant difference in operative time (293 versus 330 minutes; p = 0.24), blood loss (257 versus 100 mL; p = 0.09), or hospital stay (11 versus 8 days; p = 0.14). Robotic inpatient cost was $17,344 versus $14,356 (p = 0.05), largely from operating-room supplies. The robotic cohort may overlap with the 2009 series.<sup>[[14]](#ref14)</sup>

A later single-center pediatric cohort (n=17), likely overlapping Ching's series, reported median 6.4-year follow-up and surgical complications in 7/17 (41%). Its retrospective design and lack of a comparator limit treatment comparisons.<sup>[[26]](#ref26)</sup>

Leng/McGuire 1999 compared 38 patients with their preoperative catheter-management state; this cohort includes the earlier 23-patient series and is not independent evidence from it. The exact before/after complication-count reduction of 3.38 to 1.16 came from Tan’s 50-patient cohort, not Leng.<sup>[[4]](#ref4)</sup>

Before surgery, Leng reported poor compliance in 50%, urosepsis in 45%, hydronephrosis in 21%, renal struvite stones in 18%, urethrocutaneous fistula in 18%, and autonomic dysreflexia in 13%. After surgery, 83–91% maintained normal upper tracts and storage compliance; the abstract does not provide a separate resolution rate for every preoperative complication.<sup>[[4]](#ref4)</sup>

**Comparative caution:** in Husmann’s 48-patient retrospective cohort with complete urethral destruction, urosepsis hospitalization occurred in 14/17 after closure+ileovesicostomy, 6/10 after cystectomy+conduit, and 6/21 after closure+suprapubic drainage. Additional surgery occurred in 15/17, 5/10, and 11/21 respectively over median 8-year follow-up. Selection differed between groups; this is not a randomized ranking, but it directly challenges assumptions that ileovesicostomy invariably reduces long-term morbidity. The authors abandoned ileovesicostomy after this experience.<sup>[[19]](#ref19)</sup>

### Urodynamic outcomes

- Stomal leak point pressure: mean 7.7 cm H₂O (Mutchnik).<sup>[[3]](#ref3)</sup>
- Mean detrusor leak point pressure fell from 42.7 cm H₂O preoperatively to 16.7 cm H₂O postoperatively (p=0.0061, Gauthier).<sup>[[8]](#ref8)</sup>
- Normal upper tracts and bladder storage compliance were maintained in 83–91% (Leng).<sup>[[4]](#ref4)</sup>
- Median residual bladder volume 10 mL postoperatively (robotic series).<sup>[[9]](#ref9)</sup>

## Advantages Over Ileal Conduit

| Feature | Ileovesicostomy | Ileal Conduit |
|---|---|---|
| Ureteroenteric anastomosis | Not required | Required; ureteroenteric stricture is a recognized risk |
| Native UVJs | Preserved anatomically; reflux may still occur | Replaced by ureteroenteric anastomoses |
| Future reconstruction | Selected undiversion may be possible if native anatomy/function permits | Depends on prior cystectomy, remaining anatomy, and clinical goals |
| Cystectomy | Not required; bladder preserved | Consider with supravesical diversion according to bladder condition and indication<sup>[[5]](#ref5)</sup> |
| Surgical complexity | Simpler (no ureteral dissection) | More complex |
| Upper-tract obstruction | No new ureteroenteric junction, but other obstruction remains possible | Ureteroenteric stricture is one recognized risk |

The AUA/SUFU guideline notes that with longer follow-up, ileovesicostomy patients have an increased risk of requiring revision or alternate surgery. A 2026 perspective by Ginsberg favors conduit diversion for selected poorly compliant refractory bladders. This is an expert argument, not a comparative trial; conduit patients still require stoma, renal, and metabolic follow-up.<sup>[[5]](#ref5)[[15]](#ref15)</sup>

## Complications

- **Stomal complications.** Occurred in 19/50 (38%) in Tan 2008, with a mean of 1.47 stomal complications per affected patient; stomal stenosis occurred in 3/23 (Schwartz) and 2/15 (Atan).<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[2]](#ref2)[[3]](#ref3)[[11]](#ref11)</sup>
- **Urethral incontinence.** Atan reported four women with mild urge leakage managed medically and a separate woman converted to an ileal conduit with concurrent cystectomy for severe leakage.<sup>[[2]](#ref2)</sup> Tan reported 36/50 (72%) continent per urethra at last follow-up.<sup>[[11]](#ref11)</sup> Atan's 15-patient series suggested that severe preoperative detrusor hyperreflexia with urge incontinence may predict persistent postoperative urge leakage in women; selected patients with outlet incompetence received concurrent outlet procedures in other series.<sup>[[2]](#ref2)[[10]](#ref10)[[11]](#ref11)</sup>
- **Mechanical obstruction after ileovesicostomy.** Tan reported this composite complication in 11/50 patients (22%); it includes sites beyond the ileovesical junction. A wide funnel is intended to reduce junctional narrowing, but stomal stenosis, limb angulation, and debris can also impair drainage.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[11]](#ref11)</sup>
- **Calculus formation.** 33% in Atan (urinary stasis, chronic bacteriuria, mucus).<sup>[[2]](#ref2)</sup>
- **UTIs.** Symptomatic 20% (Atan); 58% reduced antibiotic use or hospitalization (Hellenthal).<sup>[[2]](#ref2)[[7]](#ref7)</sup>
- **Inflammatory or infectious complications.** 54% (Tan).<sup>[[11]](#ref11)</sup>
- **Early ileus and wound infection.** Gauthier's seven-patient adult series and Ching's nine-patient pediatric series both reported these events; Ching described an ileus requiring parenteral nutrition.<sup>[[8]](#ref8)[[6]](#ref6)</sup>
- **Additional procedures.** In Tan's 50-patient retrospective cohort, 27/50 (54%) required reoperation or another procedure, averaging 2.85 such interventions per affected patient. The mean count of measured urinary-tract comorbid conditions fell from 3.38 before surgery to 1.16 afterward; postoperative UTIs were excluded from this comparison.<sup>[[11]](#ref11)</sup>
- **Conversion to ileal conduit.** Reported for intractable urethral incontinence or persistent fistula. Rates in these small early cohorts are not reliable estimates for all patients, particularly those with a devastated outlet.<sup>[[2]](#ref2)[[7]](#ref7)</sup>

### Risk factors for adverse outcomes

Tan 2008: BMI trended toward significance (p=0.0569). Age, tobacco, diabetes, etiology, preoperative indwelling catheterization, and urethral closure were not significant predictors.<sup>[[11]](#ref11)</sup>

## Managing the Urethral Leakage Problem

Persistent urethral leakage is a recognized problem after ileovesicostomy. Options:<sup>[[2]](#ref2)[[10]](#ref10)[[11]](#ref11)[[12]](#ref12)</sup>

1. Assess emptying, obstruction, reservoir pressure and outlet competence; treat demonstrated detrusor overactivity as appropriate.
2. **Occlusive pubovaginal sling** is a selected salvage option with a preplanned alternate emptying strategy, not a standard tensioning instruction. Chancellor’s 14-woman series included only five ileovesicostomies; all were dry across mixed drainage strategies.<sup>[[10]](#ref10)</sup>
3. **Bladder-neck closure** may suit a devastated outlet. The cited 82–86% results concern closure with a suprapubic catheter, not a direct ileovesicostomy outcome estimate.<sup>[[17]](#ref17)</sup>
4. **Conduit diversion with appropriately considered cystectomy** may be needed for refractory leakage, poor drainage, or an unsalvageable reservoir. These options are not a mandatory sequential ladder.<sup>[[2]](#ref2)</sup>

## Continent Ileovesicostomy (Casale Modification)

A separate continent modification using the Yang-Monti principle: a long, narrow catheterizable tube from a short ileal segment.<sup>[[18]](#ref18)</sup> Distinct from the standard incontinent procedure; essentially a [Monti channel](/docs/surgical-techniques/04b-bladder-reconstruction/catheterizable-channels).

---

## Reversibility — A Unique Advantage

Because the native bladder and UVJs are preserved:<sup>[[2]](#ref2)[[16]](#ref16)</sup>

- After neurologic recovery, reassess catheterization ability and bladder storage and outlet function before undiversion. Simple chimney excision and bladder closure may be feasible in selected patients; a three-patient SCI series instead converted the limb to augmentation ileocystoplasty with intermittent catheterization.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[24]](#ref24)</sup>
- The procedure can be converted to an ileal conduit if it fails.
- Particularly attractive for younger patients and those with potentially reversible neurologic conditions.

---

## Long-Term Surveillance

AUA/SUFU advises annual assessment after lower urinary tract reconstruction incorporating bowel (Statement 58, Expert Opinion), and states that clinicians should perform cystoscopy for gross hematuria or symptomatic recurrent UTI (Statement 60, Moderate Recommendation; Evidence Level: Grade C):<sup>[[5]](#ref5)</sup>

- Focused history, exam, symptom assessment (stoma, leakage, UTI, autonomic dysreflexia).
- Basic metabolic panel for electrolyte and acid-base abnormalities, including hyperchloremic acidosis; monitor vitamin B12 over time if terminal ileum was used.<sup>[[5]](#ref5)</sup>
- Urinary tract imaging, usually renal ultrasound for hydronephrosis and calculi.

Stoma assessment for stenosis, retraction and parastomal hernia is added in practice. AUA/SUFU also advises repeating urodynamics at an appropriate interval after treatment when impaired storage or voiding places the upper tracts at risk (Statement 56, Expert Opinion).<sup>[[5]](#ref5)</sup>

Hellenthal: all 12 patients in the long-term cohort experienced some form of urinary tract problem during 5.5 yr of follow-up; continued surveillance is needed.<sup>[[7]](#ref7)</sup>

## Current Status

A valuable but niche procedure:<sup>[[2]](#ref2)[[5]](#ref5)[[16]](#ref16)</sup>

- Before choosing ileovesicostomy, assess whether an accessible continent catheterizable stoma would permit self- or caregiver catheterization; also discuss suprapubic drainage and ileal conduit diversion. Reversibility is one factor when choosing among drainage options.<sup>[[5]](#ref5)</sup>
- A severely fibrotic, very small, high-pressure bladder is not an automatically favorable candidate. Some experts also caution that a completely acontractile bladder may empty poorly through the chimney. Assess expected drainage before retaining the bladder; another diversion may be more appropriate.<sup>[[25]](#ref25)</sup>
- Atan's 15-patient series suggested that severe preoperative detrusor hyperreflexia with urge incontinence may predict persistent postoperative urge leakage in women; concomitant outlet procedures (PVS or BNC) can be considered after assessment of outlet competence and the drainage plan.<sup>[[2]](#ref2)[[10]](#ref10)[[11]](#ref11)</sup>
- In Tan's 50-patient series, 27/50 (54%) needed reoperation or another procedure; lifelong surveillance remains necessary.<sup>[[11]](#ref11)[[5]](#ref5)</sup>
