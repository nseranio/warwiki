# Bladder Diverticulectomy

**Bladder diverticulectomy** is excision of an acquired bladder diverticulum, usually combined with treatment of the bladder outlet obstruction or neurogenic process that caused it. The goal is to remove a reservoir of stasis that drives infection, stone formation, and incomplete emptying, and in some patients to remove a site of malignancy.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup> For indications, imaging, and the obstruction-first management sequence, see [Bladder Diverticula](/docs/clinical-conditions/03b-voiding-outlet/bladder-diverticula).

---

## Indications

Diverticulectomy is considered for a diverticulum with persistent attributable symptoms, recurrent urinary tract infection, bladder or diverticular stones, upper-tract deterioration from ureteral involvement, or suspected or confirmed malignancy within the sac, after the underlying outlet or neurogenic cause has been addressed or is being treated concurrently. Diverticulum size alone is not an absolute indication.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup> Most patients with a documented diverticulum are managed without surgery; one large single-institution series operated on only 13.6% of 764 patients, and those selected for surgery were younger and had larger diverticula.<sup>[[2]](#ref2)</sup>

---

## Preoperative Evaluation

- **Cystoscopy** confirms the diverticular neck location and its relationship to the ureteral orifices, and inspects the diverticular lining.
- **Cross-sectional imaging** (CT or MR urography) maps diverticulum number, position, wall thickness, and proximity to the ureter, and screens for an intradiverticular mass.
- **Urodynamics** characterizes detrusor contractility and voiding pressure when the contribution of the diverticulum versus the underlying outlet or detrusor process is unclear.
- **Suspected intradiverticular tumor.** When imaging or cystoscopy raises concern, obtain urine cytology and cautiously biopsy a suspicious lesion. The thin or absent muscle wall increases biopsy perforation risk and complicates pathologic staging; endoscopic resection, diverticulectomy or partial cystectomy, and radical cystectomy are options according to tumor features.<sup>[[13]](#ref13)</sup> A population cohort associated documented diverticula with later bladder cancer.<sup>[[3]](#ref3)</sup>

## Surgical Technique

### Open intravesical approach

An open transvesical approach has been described as follows. The bladder is distended, for example to approximately 200 mL through an 18-Fr Foley catheter; the diverticular sac can be catheterized and filled separately over a guidewire to aid localization, and the ipsilateral ureter is catheterized when the diverticulum lies close to the ureteral orifice. The bladder mucosa is incised circumferentially about 0.5 cm from the diverticular mouth, and the sac is dissected and excised along the plane between the diverticular wall and its surrounding pseudocapsule. The bladder is closed in two layers and the repair is tested by filling; a urethral catheter (for example 20-22 Fr) is left for postoperative drainage.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup> These are illustrative technique details from one description of the operation, not fixed specifications; actual catheter size, filling volume, and closure technique vary with the diverticulum's size and location and with surgeon preference.

Open surgery has traditionally been favored for complex posterior or multiple diverticula, or when ureteral reimplantation is anticipated because the diverticulum abuts or distorts the ureteral orifice.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup>

### Laparoscopic and robotic approaches

Laparoscopic and robot-assisted diverticulectomy are options for surgeons with appropriate minimally invasive experience, usually through an extravesical dissection of the diverticular neck after bladder distension localizes the sac; some series use concomitant flexible cystoscopy or fluorescence imaging for localization, and some describe an intravesical (transvesical) port approach instead.<sup>[[4]](#ref4)[[5]](#ref5)[[6]](#ref6)</sup> A modified internal dissection technique enters the diverticulum directly rather than freeing it externally first. In one retrospective series, operative time was shorter in five internal-dissection cases than in eleven external-dissection cases (median 149 versus 228 minutes); the groups were not randomized.<sup>[[7]](#ref7)</sup>

Management of benign prostatic obstruction may be concurrent with or staged before robotic diverticulectomy, using transurethral resection or enucleation where appropriate.<sup>[[8]](#ref8)[[9]](#ref9)[[6]](#ref6)</sup> In a 2026 scoping review of 458 men, 75.1% underwent concomitant outlet surgery overall, but 48.3% of robotic cases did; robotic series more often staged outlet treatment.<sup>[[14]](#ref14)</sup> The heterogeneous data do not establish a preferred sequence.<sup>[[14]](#ref14)</sup>

### Single-port transvesical approach

The single-port (SP) robot allows the whole excision to be done from inside the bladder. Chavali and colleagues describe the technique as follows. Through a 3.5 to 4 cm suprapubic incision about two fingerbreadths above the pubis, the bladder is filled with about 200 mL, held with four stay sutures and opened with a 2 cm vertical cystotomy. The access-port wound retractor sits directly in the bladder, the robot is floating-docked at an acute angle toward the pelvis and the bladder is insufflated to 12 mmHg. The camera is at 12 o'clock, bipolar Maryland forceps at 6 o'clock, monopolar scissors on the right and Cadiere forceps on the left.<sup>[[12]](#ref12)</sup>

After the ureteral orifices are identified, the diverticulum is inverted through its neck. The mucosa around the neck is incised, and the sac is dissected off the surrounding structures, with attention to the ureter, and excised intravesically; the extraperitoneal fat is pushed back. When prostate enucleation is planned in the same sitting, it is done first. Because the sac comes out through its own neck, the residual defect is usually small and is closed with a running 3-0 barbed suture. After undocking, the cystotomy is closed in two absorbable layers without a drain and tested with 120 to 150 mL.<sup>[[12]](#ref12)</sup>

The authors reserved this route for posterior or posterolateral diverticula with a narrow neck and no ureteral involvement on imaging, and used an extraperitoneal approach when the ureter lay close to or wrapped around the sac. If there is doubt, the ureters can be cannulated under direct vision without a separate cystoscopy.<sup>[[12]](#ref12)</sup> See [Single-Port Robotics](/docs/foundations/tools/technology/robotics/single-port) for transvesical access and bladder-insufflation precautions.

| Giannarini 2022<sup>[[9]](#ref9)</sup> | 16 | Robotic, transperitoneal extravesical, with BOO treatment before or concurrent | Median IPSS fell from 25 to 5 and postvoid residual from 195 to 30 mL at 6 months (both p &lt; 0.001); two patients had a 90-day urinary infection requiring prolonged antibiotics |
| Gibson 2024<sup>[[6]](#ref6)</sup> | 28 (13 surgeons) | Robotic, predominantly transperitoneal extravesical | Mean operative time 106 minutes, mean hospital stay 2.67 days; early complications in 14%, mostly Clavien-Dindo grade II; negative pathologic margins in 9/10 malignant cases (a surgical margin endpoint) |
| Chavali 2025<sup>[[12]](#ref12)</sup> | 3 men (66 to 74 years) | Single-port transvesical; one with concurrent single-port enucleation of the prostate, two after prior TURP or PVP | Operative time 140 to 180 minutes, blood loss 10 to 50 mL, discharge after 4 to 5 hours, catheter 4 to 7 days; no conversions, complications or 90-day readmissions; voiding symptoms improved in all (postoperative IPSS 1 to 3; one had been self-catheterizing) at 12 to 44 months; benign pathology |

These are retrospective, mostly single-center or multi-surgeon case series without a randomized comparison between open, laparoscopic, and robotic approaches, or between concurrent versus staged outlet treatment.<sup>[[4]](#ref4)[[6]](#ref6)[[7]](#ref7)[[8]](#ref8)[[9]](#ref9)[[12]](#ref12)</sup> No randomized trial establishes a preferred route; selection currently follows diverticulum complexity, surgeon experience and equipment, and whether concurrent outlet surgery is planned. In Giannarini et al.’s 16-patient robotic series, a cystogram on postoperative day 4 assessed for leak before catheter removal (median postoperative day 5); this was the series protocol.<sup>[[9]](#ref9)</sup>

### Transurethral endoscopic treatment

Transurethral incision of the diverticular neck with fulguration of the lining, usually combined with transurethral treatment of the obstructing prostate, has been used as a lower-morbidity alternative to excision in patients who are poor candidates for a formal diverticulectomy. See [Bladder Diverticula](/docs/clinical-conditions/03b-voiding-outlet/bladder-diverticula#nonoperative-and-minimally-invasive-alternatives) for the supporting series and its limitations. This approach does not remove the diverticular wall, so a retained tumor risk and the possibility of worsened emptying from altered bladder hydrodynamics are relevant counseling points.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[10]](#ref10)</sup>

## Complications

- Urine leak or persistent fistula at the closure site
- Ureteral injury, particularly when the diverticular neck abuts the ureteral orifice, which may require concomitant ureteral reimplantation
- Recurrent or persistent diverticulum from incomplete excision
- Acute urinary retention after an endoscopic-only approach, from the altered bladder hydrodynamics created by opening the diverticular neck<sup>[[10]](#ref10)</sup>
- Standard risks of the chosen approach: bleeding, infection, and, for the minimally invasive routes, the usual access-related complications
