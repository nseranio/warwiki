# Augmentation Cystoplasty

Augmentation cystoplasty (AC) is the standard operation for increasing bladder capacity and lowering storage pressure in patients with refractory neurogenic or non-neurogenic bladder dysfunction when appropriate less invasive treatment is ineffective, unsuitable, or not tolerated.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[2]](#ref2)[[3]](#ref3)[[4]](#ref4)</sup> The modern operation is usually a **detubularized ileocystoplasty**: a short ileal segment is isolated, opened along its antimesenteric border, reconfigured into a low-pressure patch, and anastomosed to a widely bivalved bladder.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[8]](#ref8)[[9]](#ref9)</sup>

The core indication for augmentation is an unsafe reservoir: high storage pressures, poor compliance, and progressive upper-tract deterioration despite optimized conservative therapy.<sup>[[2]](#ref2)[[4]](#ref4)[[5]](#ref5)</sup> AC lowers storage pressure and protects the upper tracts, with conversion to diversion in 2.7% at 10 years in one spina bifida cohort, but it exchanges one disease state for a lifelong reconstructed system that requires reliable emptying (often clean intermittent catheterization [CIC]), mucus management, metabolic surveillance, and vigilance for stones, perforation, and late malignancy.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[17]](#ref17)[[19]](#ref19)[[20]](#ref20)</sup>

---

EAU 2026 makes a strong recommendation to offer augmentation for low compliance and/or refractory neurogenic detrusor overactivity when less invasive treatments have failed. The supporting evidence is level 3. EAU states that lifelong follow-up is required given the morbidity of the procedure.<sup>[[33]](#ref33)</sup>

## Indications

Augmentation cystoplasty is indicated when the bladder remains hostile despite appropriate medical and minimally invasive management:

| Indication | Practical meaning |
|---|---|
| **Refractory low-capacity / poorly compliant bladder** | Failure or intolerance of appropriate storage therapy and optimized emptying; botulinum toxin is considered where suitable<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[4]](#ref4)</sup> |
| **Unsafe storage pressure** | Persistent detrusor leak point or storage pressure high enough to threaten the upper tracts<sup>[[2]](#ref2)[[4]](#ref4)</sup> |
| **Upper-tract deterioration** | Hydronephrosis, vesicoureteral reflux, recurrent pyelonephritis, or declining renal function attributable to a hostile bladder<sup>[[2]](#ref2)[[5]](#ref5)[[17]](#ref17)</sup> |
| **Refractory incontinence** | Incontinence despite CIC and medical therapy, especially in neurogenic bladder patients with salvageable outlet function or a plan for concomitant outlet surgery<sup>[[2]](#ref2)[[17]](#ref17)[[18]](#ref18)</sup> |

Common underlying disease states include spina bifida, spinal cord injury, posterior urethral valves, bladder exstrophy, genitourinary tuberculosis, and severe idiopathic detrusor overactivity or fibrosis.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[3]](#ref3)[[6]](#ref6)</sup>

### Practical contraindications

Selection depends on disease, renal reserve, bowel health, and a workable lifelong care plan:

- **Significant renal insufficiency or azotemia.** Increases the consequences of bowel-mediated solute exchange, especially with colon and ileum<sup>[[2]](#ref2)[[7]](#ref7)[[23]](#ref23)</sup>
- **No dependable emptying and irrigation plan.** Patients or caregivers must be able to perform catheterization if needed; spontaneous voiding cannot be guaranteed, and an inaccessible or unmanageable reservoir risks retention, stones, and perforation<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[4]](#ref4)</sup>
- **Short bowel, major prior bowel resection, or active inflammatory bowel disease.** May limit usable segment choice or make bowel incorporation unacceptable<sup>[[2]](#ref2)[[3]](#ref3)</sup>
- **Untreated outlet obstruction or unresolved outlet incompetence.** Capacity alone does not solve a hostile outlet; many patients need a concomitant outlet procedure<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[16]](#ref16)</sup>

## Preoperative Decision Framework

Before offering AC, the reconstructive question is whether the patient needs a bigger low-pressure reservoir, a different outlet, or an incontinent bypass altogether.

### The classic augmentation candidate

- Safe renal reserve, or at least enough reserve to tolerate bowel incorporation
- Proven low-capacity / poor-compliance bladder on urodynamics
- Failure of optimized medical therapy and usually botulinum toxin
- Patient or caregiver ability to catheterize if required, with frequency tailored to urine production, capacity, pressures, and residuals
- Willingness to irrigate mucus and accept lifelong follow-up<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[4]](#ref4)</sup>

### The patient who may be better served by diversion

The alternative is often an incontinent diversion rather than another bladder salvage maneuver. In patients unable to catheterize, medically fragile patients, or those with recurrent complications from a hostile native bladder, ileal conduit or ileovesicostomy may be preferable to augmentation.<sup>[[4]](#ref4)[[21]](#ref21)</sup>

### Required preoperative workup

- History focused on catheterization ability, continence goals, bowel history, prior abdominal surgery, and prior radiation or tuberculosis
- Urodynamics documenting low capacity and/or poor compliance
- Upper-tract imaging for hydronephrosis or reflux
- Baseline renal function and electrolytes
- Counseling that augmentation is a lifelong reconstructed state, not a one-time cure<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[2]](#ref2)[[4]](#ref4)</sup>

## Technique Overview

### Gold standard: enterocystoplasty

Enterocystoplasty remains the reference standard because bowel provides a large compliant patch that can be configured into a low-pressure reservoir.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[3]](#ref3)</sup> The main technical principle is **detubularization**: the isolated bowel is opened so that coordinated tubular contractions are reduced; residual pressure waves may persist.<sup>[[8]](#ref8)[[9]](#ref9)</sup>

### Core steps of augmentation cystoplasty

1. Mobilize and widely bivalve the bladder to create a broad native plate.
2. Isolate the chosen bowel segment with preservation of mesenteric blood supply.
3. Re-establish bowel continuity.
4. Detubularize and reconfigure the segment into a patch or cup.
5. Anastomose the bowel patch to the opened bladder in a tension-free, watertight fashion.
6. Add concomitant procedures as needed: catheterizable channel, ureteral reimplantation, or outlet surgery.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[8]](#ref8)[[16]](#ref16)</sup>

---

## Segment Selection and Configuration

| Technique | Reconstructive role | Key advantages | Main tradeoffs |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Ileocystoplasty** | Standard modern augmentation | Familiar segment, reliable mesentery, low-pressure dynamics after detubularization<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[8]](#ref8)[[9]](#ref9)</sup> | Hyperchloremic metabolic acidosis, mucus, B12 deficiency risk with longer segments<sup>[[22]](#ref22)[[23]](#ref23)</sup> |
| **Sigmoidocystoplasty** | Alternative when colon is preferred | Can reduce small-bowel handling; cup-patch configuration outperforms tubular colon<sup>[[8]](#ref8)[[9]](#ref9)</sup> | Similar acidosis / mucus issues; colon can be bulky in a deep pelvis |
| **Ileocecal cystoplasty** | Useful when ureteral reach is difficult | Helpful when a wide gap exists between ureters and bladder or when massively dilated ureters require implantation<sup>[[9]](#ref9)</sup> | More complex bowel work; same bowel-related surveillance burden |
| **Gastrocystoplasty** | Historical niche, mostly renal-insufficiency logic | Lower chloride reabsorption, less mucus, lower stone burden, avoids further small-bowel resection<sup>[[3]](#ref3)[[10]](#ref10)[[11]](#ref11)</sup> | Hematuria-dysuria syndrome, metabolic alkalosis, concerning malignancy signal<sup>[[10]](#ref10)[[11]](#ref11)[[28]](#ref28)</sup> |

### Why ileum remains the default

Ileum is the default segment for its reach, ease of detubularization and reservoir dynamics. Comparative evidence is limited: in one series of 133 patients, sigmoid colon showed a trend toward less small-bowel obstruction and no difference in perforation or stone formation compared with ileum.<sup>[[8]](#ref8)</sup> A typical segment length is 15–40 cm, but the exact length is driven by bladder plate size, target capacity, mesenteric reach, and whether a concomitant channel is needed.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[8]](#ref8)[[9]](#ref9)</sup>

### Configuration matters more than segment name

The major technical lesson from the classic enterocystoplasty literature is that detubularized reconfigured bowel behaves better than intact tubular bowel. Low-pressure storage, continence, and perforation avoidance all improve when the augment is opened and refashioned rather than sewn in as a tube.<sup>[[8]](#ref8)[[9]](#ref9)[[20]](#ref20)</sup>

## Alternative and Salvage Augmentation Strategies

### Autoaugmentation

Autoaugmentation (vesicomyotomy / detrusorectomy) removes detrusor over the dome while leaving the urothelium intact, allowing the mucosa to bulge outward as a pseudodiverticulum.<sup>[[12]](#ref12)[[13]](#ref13)</sup> It avoids bowel morbidity and does not burn the bridge to later enterocystoplasty, but its effect on capacity and compliance is generally more modest and less durable than bowel-based augmentation.<sup>[[12]](#ref12)[[13]](#ref13)</sup>

### Seromuscular colocystoplasty lined with urothelium (SCLU)

SCLU combines autoaugmentation with a demucosalized colonic patch placed over preserved urothelium, aiming to avoid bowel-mucosa complications such as mucus and metabolic exchange.<sup>[[8]](#ref8)</sup> Conceptually attractive, but used in selected centers rather than as mainstream reconstructive practice.

### Ureterocystoplasty

Ureterocystoplasty uses a suitably dilated ureter to augment the bladder without bowel. Many reported operations use the ureter of a poorly functioning renal unit; selected approaches preserve the kidney with a separate drainage reconstruction.<sup>[[14]](#ref14)</sup> Applicability is limited to patients with a suitable ureteral scenario.

### Robot-assisted augmentation

Robot-assisted laparoscopic ileocystoplasty has been reported in both adult and pediatric series, often combined with Mitrofanoff creation.<sup>[[15]](#ref15)[[16]](#ref16)</sup> Potential recovery and cosmetic benefits must be weighed against operative complexity and time. The cited reports do not establish superior pain, length of stay, or long-term outcomes over open augmentation.

## Concomitant Procedures

Augmentation often succeeds only when paired with additional reconstructive steps:

| Concomitant procedure | Why it is added |
|---|---|
| **Continent catheterizable channel (Mitrofanoff / appendicovesicostomy)** | For patients who cannot catheterize the native urethra reliably<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[16]](#ref16)</sup> |
| **Ureteral reimplantation** | For selected anatomic obstruction or ureteral reconstruction needs; high-pressure reflux can improve with augmentation alone. EAU notes uncertainty about routine simultaneous reimplantation for high-grade reflux<sup>[[33]](#ref33)[[1]](#ref1)[[5]](#ref5)[[9]](#ref9)</sup> |
| **Outlet procedure** | Sling, AUS, bladder-neck reconstruction, or bladder-neck closure when continence will not be achieved by augmentation alone<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[16]](#ref16)[[18]](#ref18)</sup> |

In many patients the real construct is a rebuilt reservoir plus a rebuilt outlet.

## Outcomes

### Reservoir function

Across modern series and reviews, AC reliably increases bladder capacity and compliance while reducing storage pressure.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[17]](#ref17)[[19]](#ref19)</sup> Renal protection is attributed to the lower storage pressure rather than to the larger volume alone.

### Continence

Reported continence rates range from 78% to 95%, especially when concomitant outlet procedures are used appropriately. Venn and Mundy reported 78% continence with cystoplasty alone in neuropathic bladders, rising to 90% with an artificial urinary sphincter, and 93% for detrusor instability; Shekarriz reported 95% overall.<sup>[[8]](#ref8)[[17]](#ref17)[[18]](#ref18)</sup> Reported success varies because some series define success as daytime dryness, others as social continence, and others include patients with catheterizable channels or AUS.

### Upper-tract preservation

Hydronephrosis and reflux improve or resolve in most patients, and renal function often stabilizes when the reservoir is made safe early enough.<sup>[[5]](#ref5)[[17]](#ref17)</sup>

### Durability

In Szymanski et al.’s retrospective cohort of 413 patients with spina bifida, estimated 10-year conversion to diversion was 2.7%, but any reoperation was 43.9%. These are time-to-event estimates from a mixed-era cohort; in the 222-patient modern subgroup, corresponding estimates were 4.0% and 46.0%.<sup>[[20]](#ref20)</sup>

### Quality of life

Most patients report better continence, greater independence, and relief of upper-tract anxiety after successful augmentation, although this benefit is inseparable from the burden of CIC, irrigation, stone surveillance, and reintervention.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[19]](#ref19)</sup>

## Complications

Augmentation cystoplasty requires lifelong maintenance. The main long-term complications follow.

### Stones

Bladder or reservoir stones were the most frequent late surgical complication in the 413-patient Szymanski spina bifida cohort, with an estimated 28.2% 10-year risk (32.9% in its modern subgroup) and recurrence in 52.4%. Chang et al. reported stones in 8/22 patients (36.4%) over mean follow-up 13.4 years; this is not a 10-year actuarial estimate.<sup>[[17]](#ref17)[[20]](#ref20)</sup> Continent catheterizable channels and exstrophy anatomy increased stone risk in Shekarriz's series;<sup>[[8]](#ref8)</sup> mucus and bacteriuria are also implicated.

### Perforation

Reservoir perforation is a potentially fatal late event. In Szymanski et al.’s spina bifida cohort, estimated 10-year risk was 9.6% with detubularized reconfigured ileocystoplasty versus 23.7% without it, and 8.8% in the modern subgroup. These observational estimates should not be generalized to all augmentation populations or interpreted as randomized technique comparisons.<sup>[[8]](#ref8)[[20]](#ref20)</sup>

### Chronic bacteriuria and symptomatic UTI

Asymptomatic bacteriuria is common in catheterized augmented systems and should not routinely be screened for or treated in stable NLUTD patients. Assess new symptoms for UTI or pyelonephritis; bacteriuria alone does not establish infection.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[19]](#ref19)</sup>

### Mucus

Mucus production is inherent to bowel incorporation. It contributes to catheter blockage, irrigation burden, and stone formation.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[21]](#ref21)</sup>

### Bowel morbidity and reoperation

In the Szymanski spina bifida cohort, the 10-year risk of bowel obstruction requiring laparotomy was 4.5%, and the 10-year risk of any additional surgery was 43.9%. A systematic review of adult neurogenic augmentation reported bowel dysfunction in 15%.<sup>[[19]](#ref19)[[20]](#ref20)</sup>

## Metabolic and Nutritional Consequences

| Segment | Major issue | Practical consequence |
|---|---|---|
| **Ileum / colon** | Hyperchloremic metabolic acidosis | The commonest chronic abnormality; monitor BMP lifelong<sup>[[2]](#ref2)[[22]](#ref22)[[23]](#ref23)</sup> |
| **Ileum** | Vitamin B12 deficiency / bile salt malabsorption | Higher risk with longer ileal segments; periodic B12 monitoring is reasonable<sup>[[2]](#ref2)[[23]](#ref23)</sup> |
| **Ileum / colon** | Bone demineralization | Chronic acidosis can mobilize bone calcium and contribute to osteoporosis<sup>[[22]](#ref22)[[23]](#ref23)</sup> |
| **Stomach** | Hypochloremic metabolic alkalosis | Particularly relevant in patients with renal insufficiency<sup>[[3]](#ref3)[[11]](#ref11)</sup> |
| **Stomach** | Hematuria-dysuria syndrome | Especially troublesome in sensate patients with preserved pelvic sensation<sup>[[3]](#ref3)[[11]](#ref11)</sup> |
| **Jejunum** | Severe electrolyte derangement | Hyponatremia, hyperkalemia, and acidosis make jejunum an unattractive and generally avoided segment<sup>[[2]](#ref2)[[22]](#ref22)</sup> |

## Malignancy Risk and Surveillance

### What is the actual risk?

Malignancy after augmentation is uncommon but real. Across heterogeneous studies in a systematic review, the reported probability of malignancy ranged from 0% to 5.5%, with a mean latency of 19 years and most tumors diagnosed more than 10 years after surgery. The level of evidence was usually poor.<sup>[[25]](#ref25)</sup> Adenocarcinoma was the commonest histologic type (51.6%), and half of the tumors arose at the entero-urinary anastomosis.<sup>[[25]](#ref25)[[26]](#ref26)</sup>

The controversy is whether augmentation itself is independently oncogenic or whether congenital bladder dysfunction, chronic inflammation, stones, and infection already confer part of the risk.<sup>[[24]](#ref24)[[27]](#ref27)</sup> The practical answer for follow-up is the same: these patients need lifelong vigilance.

### Gastrocystoplasty deserves extra concern

Gastrocystoplasty has a particularly concerning malignancy signal, with multiple series reporting adenocarcinoma arising in augmented reservoirs.<sup>[[10]](#ref10)[[28]](#ref28)</sup>

### Surveillance in practice

Routine surveillance cystoscopy for every asymptomatic augmented patient remains controversial:

- **AUA/SUFU NLUTD guideline.** No routine cystoscopy in asymptomatic patients; perform cystoscopy for gross hematuria, recurrent symptomatic UTI, or suprapubic pain<sup>[[4]](#ref4)</sup>
- Some centers advocate annual cystoscopy after year 10, but decision analyses and retrospective studies have questioned its effectiveness and cost-effectiveness in asymptomatic patients.<sup>[[29]](#ref29)[[30]](#ref30)[[31]](#ref31)</sup>
- **Gastrocystoplasty** warrants specialist discussion because of its malignancy signal; these reports do not establish a validated annual cystoscopy schedule for every asymptomatic patient.<sup>[[3]](#ref3)[[28]](#ref28)</sup>

## Postoperative Management and Lifelong Follow-Up

Patients with bowel-incorporating bladder reconstructions require structured lifelong surveillance.<sup>[[4]](#ref4)</sup>

### Core follow-up elements

- **Annual** focused history, examination, and symptom assessment
- **Annual basic metabolic panel**
- **Annual urinary tract imaging**, usually ultrasound, with additional evaluation when risk or symptoms require it
- CIC education and adherence review
- Regular bladder irrigation to clear mucus
- Monitor vitamin B12 over time when terminal ileum is incorporated; assess additional nutritional risks according to the segment and length used<sup>[[4]](#ref4)[[23]](#ref23)</sup>

### Functional follow-up

Repeat urodynamics can be helpful when symptoms, leakage, or upper-tract changes raise concern that the augment is no longer low pressure or the outlet strategy is failing.

### Red-flag symptoms

Immediate evaluation is warranted for:

- New gross hematuria
- Recurrent unexplained symptomatic UTI
- Increasing suprapubic pain
- Difficult catheterization or acute retention
- Sudden abdominal pain / sepsis suggesting perforation<sup>[[4]](#ref4)[[20]](#ref20)</sup>

---

## Alternatives to Augmentation

| Alternative | Role |
|---|---|
| **Intradetrusor botulinum toxin** | Useful before augmentation, but many severely noncompliant bladders remain refractory; repeat treatment is required<sup>[[4]](#ref4)[[32]](#ref32)</sup> |
| **Ileal conduit** | A reasonable alternative for the patient who cannot catheterize reliably, has major comorbidity, or would be poorly served by lifelong augmented-reservoir maintenance<sup>[[21]](#ref21)</sup> |
| **Ileovesicostomy** | Incontinent low-pressure outlet preserving the native bladder; useful in selected NLUTD patients<sup>[[4]](#ref4)</sup> |
| **Sacral neuromodulation** | Considered only in selected NLUTD populations; not a substitute for augmentation in severe hostile bladders from complete SCI or spina bifida<sup>[[4]](#ref4)</sup> |

---

## Bottom Line for the Reconstructive Surgeon

Augmentation cystoplasty remains the reference bladder-salvage operation for the unsafe, refractory low-capacity bladder. The decision is whether the patient can manage a lifelong bowel-incorporating reservoir with reliable emptying or is better served by diversion.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[4]](#ref4)[[20]](#ref20)[[21]](#ref21)</sup>

In the cited series, AC lowered storage pressure, protected renal function, and improved continence for most patients. Stones, metabolic complications, perforation, and late malignancy remain risks that require lifelong follow-up.
