# Rectoneovaginal Fistula (RNVF) Repair

**Rectoneovaginal fistula (RNVF)**, an abnormal epithelialised communication between the rectum and the neovaginal canal, is one of the most clinically significant major complications after feminizing genital surgery, reported in about 0.1–1% of primary penile inversion vaginoplasties in single-centre series and in 6.3% of revision vaginoplasties.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[2]](#ref2)[[3]](#ref3)[[4]](#ref4)</sup> In the largest published experience (Amsterdam, van der Sluis 2016; 1,082 patients with 13 RNVFs), fistulectomy with primary closure or a local advancement flap was sufficient in most patients, with few complications and no apparent impairment of neovaginal function.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup>

For the host operations, see [Penile Inversion Vaginoplasty](/docs/surgical-techniques/04k-gender-affirming-surgery/penile-inversion-vaginoplasty), [Peritoneal Pull-Through Vaginoplasty](/docs/surgical-techniques/04k-gender-affirming-surgery/peritoneal-pull-through-vaginoplasty), and [Intestinal Vaginoplasty](/docs/surgical-techniques/04k-gender-affirming-surgery/intestinal-vaginoplasty). For the related stenosis-management pathway, see [Neovaginal Stenosis Management](/docs/surgical-techniques/04k-gender-affirming-surgery/neovaginal-stenosis-management).

---

## Definitions and Classification

**Types of neovaginal fistulas after vaginoplasty:**<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup>

- **Rectoneovaginal fistula (RNVF).** Rectum to canal; flatus / stool / feculent discharge through the neovagina (most clinically significant).
- **Urethroneovaginal fistula (UNVF).** Urethra to canal; continuous urinary leakage.
- **Pouch-neovaginal fistula.** Canal to peritoneal pouch (rare; 1/1,082 in Amsterdam).
- **Prostato-vaginal fistula.** Prostatic urethra / prostate to canal (4 patients underwent robot-assisted sigmoid vaginoplasty for this in the Sljivich series).<sup>[[5]](#ref5)</sup>

**By location** (adapted from general RVF classification):<sup>[[6]](#ref6)[[7]](#ref7)</sup>

- **Low.** At or near introitus / perineal body; most accessible for perineal repair.
- **Mid-canal.** Within the canal body (mean 5.4 cm from the introitus in the Pansritum series).<sup>[[8]](#ref8)</sup>
- **High / apical.** Near apex; may require transabdominal approach.

**By complexity:**<sup>[[6]](#ref6)</sup>

- **Simple.** Small (< 1 cm), single tract, healthy surrounding tissue.
- **Complex.** > 1 cm, multiple tracts, significant scarring, recurrent after prior repair, concurrent stenosis.

## Pathophysiology and Risk Factors

### Mechanism

The neovaginal canal is dissected in the rectoprostatic / rectovesical space, with the rectum at greatest risk because the canal lies directly anterior to the anterior rectal wall, separated only by Denonvilliers' fascia.<sup>[[2]](#ref2)[[11]](#ref11)</sup> RNVF develops through two primary mechanisms:<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[10]](#ref10)</sup>

1. **Intraoperative rectal injury.** Direct perforation either unrecognised or failing to heal despite primary repair.
2. **Postoperative ischemic necrosis.** Devascularisation of the rectoneovaginal septum with delayed tissue breakdown.

### Risk factors

- **Revision vaginoplasty.** OR 8.6 for RNVF versus primary (p < 0.01).<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup>
- **Intraoperative rectal perforation.** 4/23 (17.4%) of intraoperatively recognised and oversewn perforations subsequently developed an RNVF (Amsterdam); recognised injuries still carry significant fistula risk.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup>
- **Prior perineal surgery.** The single patient with RNVF in the Stark 1,011-patient series (1 of 9 rectal injuries) had prior perineal surgery.<sup>[[10]](#ref10)</sup>
- **No intraoperative sigmoidoscopy** with air-leak test after rectal injury. The same single Stark patient had no sigmoidoscopy.<sup>[[10]](#ref10)</sup>
- **Diabetes.** Diabetes was associated with local infection after vaginoplasty (OR 9.8, p = 0.003, 475 patients), which has been proposed to predispose to fistula. A direct association with RNVF has not been shown.<sup>[[4]](#ref4)</sup>
- **Concurrent vaginal stenosis.** Vaginal stenosis developed in 3 of 6 patients (50%) with rectal injury who proceeded to full-depth vaginoplasty. A contribution of tissue ischemia to fistula formation is speculative.<sup>[[10]](#ref10)</sup>
- **Smoking.** Smoking was the only negative predictor of fistula-repair success in the Pastier retrospective three-centre series of general RVF (p = 0.02).<sup>[[12]](#ref12)</sup>

## Clinical Presentation and Diagnosis

### Symptoms<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[6]](#ref6)</sup>

- Passage of flatus through the neovagina (earliest and most specific).
- Stool or feculent discharge through the neovagina.
- Foul-smelling discharge.
- Recurrent UTI.
- Perineal pain.
- Difficulty / pain / bleeding during dilation.

### Diagnostic workup

In the Amsterdam experience, most patients are diagnosed on symptoms and exam alone.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup> Systematic evaluation:<sup>[[7]](#ref7)[[13]](#ref13)[[14]](#ref14)</sup>

- **Neovaginal speculum exam.** Direct fistula visualisation on the posterior canal wall; feculent material or bubbling.
- **Digital rectal exam.** Palpate the tract on the anterior rectal wall.
- **MRI pelvis.** Modality of choice; 87% accuracy alone, 100% combined with EUA.<sup>[[7]](#ref7)[[13]](#ref13)</sup>
- **Endoanal / endorectal ultrasound (EAUS).** 91% accuracy; assesses tract and sphincter integrity.<sup>[[7]](#ref7)</sup>
- **Examination under anaesthesia (EUA).** 91% accuracy; direct visualisation and hydrogen-peroxide instillation.<sup>[[7]](#ref7)[[15]](#ref15)</sup>
- **Sigmoidoscopy / proctoscopy.** Rectal side and concurrent pathology.
- **Fluoroscopic or CT vaginography.** For upper / mid-canal fistulas.<sup>[[14]](#ref14)</sup>
- **Combining any two modalities** achieves 100% diagnostic accuracy.<sup>[[7]](#ref7)</sup>

## Prevention — Intraoperative Strategies

### Anatomically guided dissection (OHSU / Shoureshi–Dugi)

200-patient series with 1.0% full-thickness rectal injury and 1.0% fistula rate:<sup>[[2]](#ref2)</sup>

- Dissection begins at the central tendon of the perineum.
- Plane developed between rectum and bulbospongiosus / bulbar urethra.
- **Denonvilliers' fascia** identified as the landmark to maintain correct plane.
- Cephalad dissection in the avascular rectoprostatic plane.
- **Finger in the rectum** for continuous tactile feedback.

### Morris algorithmic approach to intraoperative rectal injury (n = 146; 6.8% rectal-injury rate)<sup>[[16]](#ref16)</sup>

1. Mechanical bowel prep in all patients.
2. Continuous rectal-finger palpation during dissection; immediate injury recognition.
3. **If rectal injury identified:**
   - **Immediate multilayer repair** (two-layer in 8 of 10 patients, three-layer in 2).
   - **Intraoperative CRS consultation**, then sigmoidoscopy with air-leak test.<sup>[[10]](#ref10)[[16]](#ref16)</sup>
   - **Bulbospongiosus muscle interposition** between rectal repair and canal lining (2/9 patients in Stark).<sup>[[10]](#ref10)</sup>
   - **Convert decision:** if repair is secure and air leak negative, proceed with full-depth (counsel re approximately 50% stenosis risk); if insecure / extensive, convert to minimal-depth (zero-depth) vaginoplasty.<sup>[[10]](#ref10)</sup>
   - **Temporary fecal diversion** for large injuries / insecure repairs (2/9 in Stark).<sup>[[10]](#ref10)</sup>
4. **Stark finding:** when injuries are identified intraoperatively, multilayer-repaired, and CRS-evaluated, patients do not develop RVF despite completing full-depth GAV; the single RVF in the series had prior perineal surgery and no intraoperative sigmoidoscopy.<sup>[[10]](#ref10)</sup>

### Rectoprostatic fascia reinforcement flap (Pansritum)<sup>[[8]](#ref8)</sup>

The rectoprostatic (prostaticovesicular) fascia is used as a vascularised flap interposed between the rectal repair and the canal lining. In this retrospective series (2006–2018), 14 intraoperative rectal injuries and 5 RNVFs occurred among 2,059 neovaginoplasties, and 2 of the fistulas followed surgery done elsewhere. Mean distance from the introitus was 5.2 cm for injuries and 5.4 cm for fistulas, and mean size was 2.5 cm and 2.3 cm. No fistula recurrence was reported at 1 year. The flap can be used for intraoperative repair and for delayed RNVF, and it uses tissue in situ with no distant donor site.

## Stepwise Management Algorithm

### Step 1 — Assessment and optimisation<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[6]](#ref6)[[7]](#ref7)</sup>

- Define anatomy (MRI and EUA).
- Identify concurrent pathology (stenosis, granulation, infection, abscess).
- Optimise tissue (resolve inflammation, treat infection, nutrition).
- **Smoking cessation.** Smoking was the only negative predictor of success in a retrospective Martius and gracilis series (p = 0.02).<sup>[[12]](#ref12)</sup>
- **Timing.** Wait 3–6 mo from primary surgery or fistula onset to allow inflammation to settle and tissues to mature.<sup>[[6]](#ref6)[[7]](#ref7)</sup>

### Step 2 — Conservative management

For small, minimally symptomatic fistulas:<sup>[[7]](#ref7)</sup>

- Wound care, sitz baths, fibre supplements.
- Antibiotics if infection.
- Cessation of dilation until repair.
- 52–66% non-operative healing reported for benign RVFs in general populations (limited GAS-specific data).

### Step 3 — Seton placement (selected cases)

Per ASCRS 2022 (Grade 1C):<sup>[[7]](#ref7)</sup> draining seton for narrow fistulas, small vaginal-side openings, multiple tracts, or to settle acute inflammation / infection prior to definitive repair.

### Step 4 — Definitive repair

**A. Fistulectomy and primary closure ± local advancement flap (first-line).** Amsterdam first-choice; tract excision and multilayer closure (rectal layers, interposition, canal wall).<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup>

**B. Endorectal advancement flap (ERAF).** ASCRS-preferred procedure for most RVFs (Grade 1C). Success is 41–78%; failure factors are sphincter abnormalities, prior radiation and recurrence. Repeat flaps succeed in 55–93%. In a 57-patient single-centre series, fistula diameter > 1 cm predicted ERAF failure (p < 0.05).<sup>[[7]](#ref7)[[17]](#ref17)</sup>

**C. Rectoprostatic fascia reinforcement flap (Pansritum).** No recurrence among the 5 fistulas at 1 year; uses tissue in situ.<sup>[[8]](#ref8)</sup>

**D. Martius (bulbocavernosus) flap.** Typically recommended for recurrent or complex RNVF (ASCRS Grade 1C). Success 65–94%. In the Pastier retrospective three-centre series (62 patients; 55 Martius and 24 gracilis procedures), success was 69% after each at a median of 23 months, the stay was 2 days shorter with Martius, and 27% of Martius repairs were done without a stoma with no difference in morbidity (p = 0.763). In GAS, the bulbospongiosus muscle and surrounding labia-majora fat pad are harvested from the reconstructed labia majora and tunneled subcutaneously to the fistula site.<sup>[[7]](#ref7)[[12]](#ref12)[[10]](#ref10)[[18]](#ref18)</sup>

**E. Gracilis muscle interposition.** Salvage after Martius failure or for complex / recurrent fistulas. Maspero SR pooled success 64% (95% CI 53–74%) across 384 patients. Failure factors: smoking, Crohn's, > 2 prior repairs. Stoma associated with improved outcomes. Postop complications 0–37% (SSI, thigh numbness, hematoma). Longer LOS than Martius (+ 2 d, p = 0.01). Success of gracilis after failed Martius not significantly different from initial gracilis (p > 0.99); reserve as salvage.<sup>[[12]](#ref12)[[19]](#ref19)</sup>

**F. Robot-assisted sigmoid vaginoplasty for concurrent fistula and stenosis.** In the Sljivich series of 36 robot-assisted sigmoid vaginoplasties (2020–2024), revision indications included prostato-vaginal fistula in 4 patients. No fistulas developed in the revisions done for stenosis, and mean vaginal depth for the whole series was 17.6 cm. Fistula closure in the 4 fistula patients is not reported separately. The operation has been described for a severely stenosed or obliterated canal when the fistula cannot be reached for local repair.<sup>[[5]](#ref5)</sup> Cross-link to the [intestinal vaginoplasty page](/docs/surgical-techniques/04k-gender-affirming-surgery/intestinal-vaginoplasty) sigmoid-revision section.

**G. Robotic transanal minimally invasive surgery (R-TAMIS).** R-TAMIS is an emerging technique using the da Vinci Xi through a GelPOINT Path. The vaginal wall is closed with barbed sutures reinforced with fibrin sealant and acellular dermal mesh. The report describes one patient, discharged the next day, and it has not been studied in the neovaginal setting.<sup>[[20]](#ref20)</sup>

**H. Transabdominal salvage (last resort).** Completion proctectomy with coloanal anastomosis (ASCRS Grade 2C) or Turnbull–Cutait staged coloanal anastomosis for the most refractory cases.<sup>[[7]](#ref7)</sup>

## Fecal Diversion

The role of diversion is selective:<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[7]](#ref7)[[12]](#ref12)[[21]](#ref21)</sup>

**Amsterdam experience (GAS-specific):**<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup>

- **4 of 13 RNVF patients** required diversion: 1 with concurrent closure, 3 staged (diversion, then delayed closure).
- Remaining 9 patients repaired without diversion.

**General RVF evidence:**<sup>[[12]](#ref12)[[21]](#ref21)[[7]](#ref7)</sup>

- **Obi 2026 (158 patients; 424 procedures).** Multiple prior repairs and interposition-flap repairs were associated with diversion use, and healing and recurrence did not differ significantly between diverted and non-diverted patients.<sup>[[21]](#ref21)</sup>
- **Pastier 2024.** 27% of Martius flap repairs performed without stoma with no morbidity impact (p = 0.763).<sup>[[12]](#ref12)</sup>
- **ASCRS 2022.** A diverting stoma has not been shown to improve ERAF outcomes. It is generally recommended as an adjunct to Martius and gracilis flaps despite weak evidence.<sup>[[7]](#ref7)</sup>

**Practical guidance:**

- For small / simple RNVF, repair without diversion is reasonable.
- For large, complex or recurrent RNVF, consider diversion.
- With concurrent stenosis, diversion may be needed for staged repair (fistula, then stenosis).
- Stoma closure typically within 3 mo after confirming healing.<sup>[[7]](#ref7)</sup>

## Comparison of Repair Techniques

| Technique | Success | Stoma? | Best indication |
|---|---|---|---|
| Fistulectomy + primary closure | High (Amsterdam) | Usually no | Small, simple, first-time RNVF<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup> |
| ERAF | 41–78% | Usually no | Simple-to-moderate RNVF<sup>[[7]](#ref7)</sup> |
| Rectoprostatic fascia flap | No recurrence (5 fistulas, 1 yr) | No | Intraoperative injury or delayed RNVF; tissue in situ<sup>[[8]](#ref8)</sup> |
| Martius flap | 65–94% | Optional (27% without) | Recurrent or complex RNVF<sup>[[7]](#ref7)[[12]](#ref12)</sup> |
| Gracilis interposition | 64% (pooled) | Usually yes | **Salvage** after Martius failure<sup>[[12]](#ref12)[[19]](#ref19)</sup> |
| Robot-assisted sigmoid vaginoplasty | Not reported separately (4 fistula patients in 36) | No | RNVF with severe stenosis or obliteration<sup>[[5]](#ref5)</sup> |
| R-TAMIS | Single case report | No | Selected RVF; not studied in neovaginal setting<sup>[[20]](#ref20)</sup> |

## Outcomes and Prognosis

In the Amsterdam series, surgical RNVF repair was associated with few intraoperative and postoperative complications and did not seem to impair neovaginal function.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup> Almost all patients can ultimately be closed, though recurrences are common and multiple procedures may be required.<sup>[[6]](#ref6)</sup>

### Factors associated with success (general RVF, applicable to RNVF)<sup>[[22]](#ref22)[[12]](#ref12)[[21]](#ref21)</sup>

- Major procedure (versus conservative): OR 6.4 (95% CI 2.9–14.2), per-procedure analysis in Corte.
- Diverting stoma: OR 3.5 (95% CI 1.4–8.7, p = 0.009) for success in the same analysis.
- First surgery at the authors' centre: OR 3.2 (p = 0.003) in the same analysis.
- Smoking: only negative predictor in the Pastier Martius and gracilis series (p = 0.02).
- Fistula diameter > 1 cm: predictor of ERAF failure in a 57-patient series.

### Impact on neovaginal function

In the Amsterdam series, RNVF repair did not seem to impair neovaginal function. Concurrent stenosis (3 of 6 patients with rectal injury who proceeded to full-depth vaginoplasty in the Stark series) requires separate management.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[10]](#ref10)</sup>

## Special Considerations

- **Concurrent stenosis.** Staged approach (fistula closure ± diversion, then stenosis management) or RSV addressing both simultaneously.<sup>[[5]](#ref5)[[10]](#ref10)</sup> See [Neovaginal Stenosis Management](/docs/surgical-techniques/04k-gender-affirming-surgery/neovaginal-stenosis-management).
- **Multidisciplinary team.** The team includes the primary vaginoplasty surgeon, colorectal surgery and urogynecology. Stark recommends intraoperative colorectal consultation with sigmoidoscopy and air-leak testing when rectal injury is identified.<sup>[[10]](#ref10)[[15]](#ref15)</sup>
- **Dilation during fistula management.** Suspend or modify during active management to avoid mechanical disruption; resume gradually after closure. Creates tension between fistula healing and stenosis prevention.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup>
- **Recurrence.** Corte 2015 (79 patients; 286 procedures; mean follow-up 33 months): early use of a temporary stoma and a major procedure after failure of local treatment was associated with an overall success rate of 72% (57 of 79). First surgery at the authors' centre independently predicted success.<sup>[[22]](#ref22)</sup>

## Evidence Limitations

- **No GAS-specific guidelines.** Practice adapted from general RVF guidelines (ASCRS 2022) and limited case series.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[7]](#ref7)</sup>
- **Very small sample sizes.** Largest GAS-specific RNVF series has 13 patients (Amsterdam).<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup>
- **No comparative trials** between repair techniques in the neovaginal context.
- **Unique anatomy.** Prostate retention, absence of native vagina, skin-/peritoneum-/bowel-lined canal; fundamentally different surgical environment than cisgender RVF.
- **Limited long-term follow-up.** Most series report 1-year outcomes.
- **Rectoprostatic fascia flap** (Pansritum) is the only fistula technique described specifically for the neovaginal setting, based on 5 fistula repairs.<sup>[[8]](#ref8)</sup>
- **No standardised outcome measures.** Definitions of clinical healing, radiographic closure and patient-reported outcome vary.
- **Quality of evidence is low.** The Göttgens 2014 systematic review found no randomized studies and closure rates from 0% to more than 80%, and it called the quality of published studies "disappointing".<sup>[[23]](#ref23)</sup>
