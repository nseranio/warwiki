# Feminizing Urethroplasty — Urethral Shortening and Feminizing Repositioning

**Feminizing urethroplasty** shortens the distal penile urethra and positions the neomeatus in the vestibule between the neoclitoris and introitus to direct the urinary stream downward. The prostatic, membranous, and proximal bulbar urethral segments remain in situ.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[2]](#ref2)[[3]](#ref3)</sup>

This is the dedicated atlas page. For the cohort-level framework, see [Feminizing Gender-Affirming Surgery](/docs/surgical-techniques/04k-gender-affirming-surgery/feminizing-surgery). For the host operation, see [Penile Inversion Vaginoplasty](/docs/surgical-techniques/04k-gender-affirming-surgery/penile-inversion-vaginoplasty). For the clitoro-urethral-complex framework, see [Clitoroplasty (Preputial-Skin Flap, Dorsal NVB)](/docs/surgical-techniques/04k-gender-affirming-surgery/clitoroplasty-preputial-flap).

## Anatomic Foundation

**Male urethra (preoperative).** Four segments totalling about 18–20 cm:<sup>[[3]](#ref3)</sup>

- **Prostatic** (about 3–4 cm): transitional epithelium.
- **Membranous** (about 1–2 cm): through the external urethral sphincter or urogenital diaphragm; the continence zone, preserved unchanged during feminizing urethroplasty.
- **Bulbar** (about 3–4 cm): within the bulb of corpus spongiosum.
- **Pendulous (penile)** (about 10–15 cm): within the penile-shaft corpus spongiosum to the glanular meatus.

The corpus spongiosum surrounds the bulbar and pendulous segments, providing vascular supply and structural bulk; the rhabdosphincter at the membranous urethra is the primary mechanism of voluntary continence.<sup>[[3]](#ref3)</sup>

**Female target anatomy.** Length about 3–4 cm; opens in the vestibule between clitoris (superior) and introitus (inferior); short length produces a downward-directed seated stream.<sup>[[3]](#ref3)[[4]](#ref4)</sup>

**Reconstructive transformation.** Three simultaneous goals:<sup>[[2]](#ref2)[[5]](#ref5)</sup>

1. **Shorten** the distal penile urethra while preserving the prostatic and sphincteric segments.
2. **Reposition** the neomeatus to the vestibule between neoclitoris and introitus.
3. **Create a patent, non-stenotic neomeatus** producing a downward-directed stream without spraying.

## Surgical Technique — Standard Penile Inversion Vaginoplasty

Integral to the overall PIV procedure; details vary by surgeon but the core steps are consistent.<sup>[[5]](#ref5)[[6]](#ref6)[[7]](#ref7)</sup>

1. **Urethral dissection from corpora.** During penile disassembly, the urethra with its surrounding corpus spongiosum is dissected free from the ventral groove between the corpora cavernosa, from glans distally to penoscrotal junction proximally, preserving spongiosal blood supply ("dismembering of the urethra from the corpora", Baudet step 6).<sup>[[6]](#ref6)[[7]](#ref7)</sup>
2. **Urethral transection and shortening.** Distal penile urethra transected at the glans (original meatus excised with the glans tissue used for clitoroplasty); pendulous segment excised, leaving the bulbar and membranous segments and a short distal stump for the neomeatus. The prostatic and sphincteric segments remain proximal to the shortened distal urethra; the total length should not be equated with cisgender female urethral length.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[5]](#ref5)</sup>
3. **Spatulation.** A described 0.5–1.5 cm ventral incision opens the distal stump into an elliptical meatus, with the intent of widening the opening and reducing stenosis.<sup>[[5]](#ref5)[[8]](#ref8)</sup> Peters and colleagues describe extending inferior bulbar spatulation until the course back to the sphincter is straight to avoid an anteriorly directed stream; the needed length varies with anatomy.<sup>[[29]](#ref29)</sup>
4. **Neomeatus creation and positioning.** Spatulated end sutured to surrounding skin or mucosal tissue at the vestibular position, between neoclitoris (superior) and introitus (inferior), to produce a downward-directed seated stream.<sup>[[2]](#ref2)[[5]](#ref5)[[6]](#ref6)[[7]](#ref7)</sup>
5. **Relationship to the clitoro-urethral complex.** Neomeatus and neoclitoris form the clitoro-urethral complex together; in the described urethral-flap clitoroplasty, the urethra is transected about 1 cm distally after dorsal urethrotomy.<sup>[[8]](#ref8)</sup>
6. **Catheter.** Foley 14–16 Fr through the neomeatus for 5–14 days to allow anastomotic healing and prevent stenosis; some surgeons use suprapubic in addition.<sup>[[6]](#ref6)[[9]](#ref9)</sup>

**Stump hemostasis.** Closing the corpus spongiosum with an overcast suture, or applying a firm compressive dressing for more than 2 days, has been described for urethral-stump bleeding; keeping urethral preparation in the midline avoids the lateral bulbar arteries. Dressing pressure is a balance between hemostasis and preserving perfusion to the neoclitoris, since excessive compression may compromise it. These are unvalidated, experience-based suggestions rather than tested techniques.<sup>[[24]](#ref24)</sup>

### Technique-specific variants

| Variant | Urethral fate | Neomeatus | Unique consideration |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Standard PIV** | Pendulous excised; bulbar / membranous preserved | Spatulated distal stump sutured to vestibule | Most common; meatal stenosis 4–40%<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[2]](#ref2)</sup> |
| **Amend / Tübingen** | Urethra **incorporated into the ventral penile-skin flap** rather than completely separated; assembled neovagina inverted as a composite | Positioned at anatomic location | 24-patient retrospective series; mean depth 11 cm (range 10–14); authors reported good/excellent neoclitoral sensation in 97%; no major complications; median follow-up 39.7 mo<sup>[[6]](#ref6)</sup> |
| **Perovic penile-disassembly** | Long vascularised **urethral flap embedded into the penile-skin tube to line the deep canal**; separate shorter segment for neomeatus | Separate shorter segment | Dual urethral use; mucosal canal surface; n = 89 (mean follow-up 4.6 yr), good cosmetic and functional results in 87%<sup>[[10]](#ref10)</sup> |
| **Brassard urethral-flap clitoroplasty** | Urethra used for **clitoral hood** via dorsal urethrotomy; transected about 1 cm distal to clitoro-urethral complex | Same vestibular | More urethral tissue is used; comparative meatal-stenosis outcomes were not reported<sup>[[8]](#ref8)</sup> |
| **Zero-depth vulvoplasty** | Same shortening; no canal | Same vestibular | Simpler procedure; same urethral principles |
| **Peritoneal-flap / Intestinal** | Same shortening | Same vestibular | Urethral management identical to PIV |

### Raigosa five-step framework

Raigosa et al. characterise feminizing genital surgery as five major steps common to every technique: orchiectomy, penile disassembly, neovaginal-cavity creation, urethral meatus repositioning, and clitorolabioplasty.<sup>[[11]](#ref11)</sup>

## Postoperative Neourethral Anatomy

Long-term gynecologic and primary-care management depends on the following anatomy:<sup>[[4]](#ref4)[[12]](#ref12)</sup>

- **Neourethra:** native prostatic, membranous and bulbar urethra (unchanged) plus a short distal segment to neomeatus.
- **Prostate gland** remains in situ, anterior to the neovaginal canal; prostate examinations are performed vaginally, not rectally.<sup>[[12]](#ref12)</sup>
- **External urethral sphincter** preserved; voluntary continence is maintained.
- **Neomeatus** in vestibule between neoclitoris and introitus, sometimes partially obscured by clitoral hood or labial tissue.<sup>[[4]](#ref4)</sup>
- **UTI risk.** Urinary tract infection remains a postoperative diagnostic consideration; evaluate symptoms and treat infection according to the relevant anatomy and site.<sup>[[12]](#ref12)</sup>
- **Residual corpus spongiosum** around the neomeatus can cause periurethral fullness or rare arousal-related engorgement.<sup>[[4]](#ref4)</sup>

## Complications by Reporting Source

| Complication | Primary-surgeon cohorts | Other reports | Notes |
|---|---|---|---|
| Meatal stenosis | 5–16.3% (mean incidence in systematic reviews) / 4–40% (individual series) | Not well characterised | Most common urethral complication; usually requires meatotomy<sup>[[13]](#ref13)[[15]](#ref15)</sup> |
| Misdirected stream | 9.5–33% | 33–55% | Higher in cohorts outside the operating surgeon's reports<sup>[[15]](#ref15)</sup> |
| Voiding dysfunction | 5.6–33% | 47–66% | Hesitancy, weak stream, straining<sup>[[15]](#ref15)</sup> |
| Urinary incontinence | 4–19.3% | 23–33% | Stress and/or urge<sup>[[15]](#ref15)</sup> |
| Urethral stricture | 10–18% | — | May present as obstructive voiding<sup>[[18]](#ref18)</sup> |
| Urethrocutaneous / urethrovaginal fistula | Infrequently reported | — | May require operative repair |
| Spraying stream | Within "urinary symptoms" | Common | Meatal-configuration and periurethral-tissue related |
| Dribbling | Within "urinary symptoms" | Common | Residual spongiosum or urethral diverticulum |

The Blasdel 2024 narrative review of systematic reviews ("surgical complication blind spots") compared surgeon-reported cohorts with other reports. Voiding dysfunction was 5.6–33% in surgeon-reported cohorts and 47–66% in other reports, and misdirected stream was 9.5–33% versus 33–55%. The authors conclude that urinary complications are incompletely described in the literature and recommend the IDEAL framework (Idea, Development, Exploration, Assessment, Long-term study) for future research.<sup>[[15]](#ref15)</sup>

### Meatal stenosis — the most common urethral complication

Rates of 4–40% are reported across series.<sup>[[13]](#ref13)</sup>

**Pathophysiology.** Circumferential scarring; inadequate spatulation; suture-line tension; distal-stump ischemia; chronic skin-mucosal-junction inflammation.

**Risk factors.** Inadequate spatulation, excessive tension, wound infection or dehiscence, circumcision status (less periurethral tissue).

**Management:**<sup>[[14]](#ref14)[[16]](#ref16)</sup>

- **Office meatotomy.** A reported repair option for meatal stenosis; may be performed under local anaesthesia.<sup>[[13]](#ref13)</sup>
- **Formal meatoplasty.** For recurrent or severe stenosis; scar excision and local tissue flap reconstruction.
- **Serial dilation.** Temporising only; high recurrence.
- In Levy 2019 (n = 240; median follow-up 87 days), 1 patient (0.4%) required reoperation for meatal stenosis.<sup>[[9]](#ref9)</sup>

### Misdirected urinary stream

Other reports describe misdirected stream in 33–55%, compared with 9.5–33% in primary-surgeon cohorts:<sup>[[15]](#ref15)</sup>

- **Meatal position:** too anterior (close to clitoris) gives an anterior-directed stream.
- **Periurethral tissue architecture.** Labial or clitoral-hood deflection.
- **Residual corpus spongiosum.** Bulky periurethral spongiosum alters stream dynamics.
- **Meatal shape.** Circular versus slit-like.
- **Labial fusion or webbing.** Posterior introital webbing redirects stream.

Management: voiding-position adjustment, tissue manipulation, or surgical revision (meatoplasty with repositioning, periurethral debulking, labial revision). Simple conservative maneuvers for a forward or widely spraying stream include gently separating the labia before voiding and, when needed, rotating the pelvis forward to direct the stream into the toilet; persistent or obstructive symptoms still warrant examination for meatal stenosis, periurethral tissue, or other anatomic causes.<sup>[[25]](#ref25)</sup> In the NYU vulvar-revision series, urologic complaints were present in 48.6% (17/35) of revision patients.<sup>[[17]](#ref17)</sup>

### Voiding dysfunction

Other reports describe voiding dysfunction in 47–66%, versus 5.6–33% in primary-surgeon cohorts.<sup>[[15]](#ref15)</sup> Includes obstructive (hesitancy, weak stream, straining, incomplete emptying), irritative (frequency, urgency, nocturia), and incontinence. Contributors:<sup>[[15]](#ref15)[[18]](#ref18)</sup>

- Shortened urethra reduces functional length and closure pressure.
- Pelvic-floor dysfunction affects up to 94.1% of transgender men, according to a narrative review of urogynecologic complications (Motiwala 2026); comparable figures for transfeminine patients are not given.<sup>[[18]](#ref18)</sup>
- Altered pelvic anatomy from canal creation changes urethra–pelvic-floor relationships.
- **Pelvic-floor physical therapy (PFPT)** before and after surgery is reported to reduce dysfunction rates in the same review.<sup>[[18]](#ref18)</sup>

### Stress incontinence and the artificial urinary sphincter

Penile disassembly mobilizes the corpus spongiosum off the corpora cavernosa and repositions the shortened urethra into the vestibule, so the bulbar urethra no longer sits in the native perineal position that placement of a de novo [artificial urinary sphincter](/docs/surgical-techniques/04f-incontinence-procedures/procedures/artificial-urinary-sphincter) (AUS) cuff requires. An AUS is not placed as a primary treatment for stress incontinence after vaginoplasty. When a transfeminine patient already carries an AUS placed for post-prostatectomy incontinence before vaginoplasty or vulvoplasty, erosion during the urethral dissection is a recognized risk. A capsule-sparing technique that deactivates the device, diverts urine with a suprapubic tube, and dissects around the bulbospongiosus muscle while sparing the AUS capsule and tubing has been reported to preserve device function through the genital surgery.<sup>[[23]](#ref23)</sup> An autologous fascial sling has been described as technically feasible in selected patients with new SUI after neovaginal creation, but the operation is challenging because retropubic dissection and sling-trocar passage must avoid the neovagina, especially with prior retropubic surgery or scarring.<sup>[[27]](#ref27)</sup> The supporting evidence is a single case report of a pubovaginal sling using autologous rectus-sheath fascia that resolved stress incontinence after gender-reassignment surgery, so this remains a possibility rather than an established treatment.<sup>[[28]](#ref28)</sup>

## Impact of Surgical Technique on Urethral Outcomes — Raigosa Refinement Data

In a retrospective comparison of two surgical eras at one centre (64 patients with labia minora and clitoral hood creation, 103 without), adding labia-minora and clitoral-hood creation to the standard technique was associated with the following changes:<sup>[[19]](#ref19)</sup>

- Neomeatal stenosis 15.5% to 1.5% (p = .003)
- Hemorrhage 31% to 12.5%
- Aesthetic-revision need 20.3% to 4.6%
- Wound dehiscence 9.7% without versus 20.3% with labia-minora/clitoral-hood creation<sup>[[19]](#ref19)</sup>

The tissue surrounding the neomeatus, in addition to urethral shortening and spatulation, may influence meatal patency. The design was not randomized and follow-up was short, so causation is not established.

## Revision Urethroplasty

Urologic complaints were present in 17 of 35 patients selected for vulvar revision at NYU (48.6%); this is not the rate of urethral revision among all vaginoplasty patients.<sup>[[17]](#ref17)</sup>

**Indications.** Misdirected stream, meatal stenosis, spraying or splashing, periurethral fullness from residual spongiosum, urethral diverticulum.

**Techniques:**<sup>[[16]](#ref16)[[17]](#ref17)[[20]](#ref20)[[21]](#ref21)</sup>

- **Meatoplasty.** Stenosis-tissue excision, re-spatulation and reconstruction.
- **Meatal repositioning.** To a more anatomically favourable position.
- **Periurethral tissue debulking.** Excision of excess corpus spongiosum.
- **Dorsal meatotomy.** Simple widening incision.
- **V-flap meatoplasty.** Advancement flap to widen and redirect.
- **Buccal mucosal graft meatoplasty.** For recurrent or severe stenosis, analogous to cisgender female urethral reconstruction.<sup>[[21]](#ref21)</sup>

**Symptomatic urethral-bulb bulge.** A bulge at the distal anterior neovaginal canal can arise from excess corpus spongiosum or incompletely resected bulbospongiosus muscle. A transvaginal revision opening the overlying penile-skin flap to excise residual bulbospongiosus, with a catheter used to delineate the urethra while excessive ventral spongiosum is tapered to parallel it and the cut edges closed, has been described; for a large anterior-wall incision, vaginal packing and a urethral catheter left in place for about 5 days can support flap adherence. No comparative outcome data are reported for this approach.<sup>[[26]](#ref26)</sup>

## Special Considerations

- **Prior hormone therapy.** Long-term estradiol may cause urethral-mucosal atrophy and corpus-spongiosum thinning, but Gaither 2018 (n = 330) found years on HRT were not associated with complications.<sup>[[22]](#ref22)</sup>
- **Prior orchiectomy.** Scrotal-skin contracture may indirectly affect periurethral tissue architecture.
- **Prostate.** Remains in situ, anterior to the neovaginal canal; examined vaginally.<sup>[[12]](#ref12)</sup> Prostatic urethra unchanged. PSA-screening guidelines apply as for cisgender males but PSA may be lower under estradiol.
- **Continence mechanism.** The external urethral sphincter is preserved across all techniques, yet incontinence is reported in 4–19.3% of surgeon-reported cohorts and 23–33% in other reports.<sup>[[15]](#ref15)</sup> Altered pelvic-floor anatomy and the shortened urethra may contribute in a subset. PFPT before and after surgery is suggested.<sup>[[15]](#ref15)[[18]](#ref18)</sup>

## Urinary Tract Infections

After penile inversion vaginoplasty, a systematic review of 27 studies and 3,388 patients estimated pooled UTI prevalence at 5.6% (95% CI 2.7–9.4); this does not establish a rise from baseline.<sup>[[30]](#ref30)</sup> Evaluate and treat symptoms with site-specific testing.<sup>[[12]](#ref12)</sup> Neovaginal microbiome differs from cisgender vaginal microbiome and may lack protective *Lactobacillus* species, potentially contributing to periurethral colonisation with uropathogens. Standard UTI evaluation and treatment apply with site-specific testing based on symptoms and sexual history. Persistent incontinence should prompt urology or surgical-team referral.<sup>[[12]](#ref12)</sup>

## Evidence Limitations

Gaps in the urethroplasty-specific evidence base:<sup>[[15]](#ref15)</sup>

- **No dedicated studies** focus on the urethroplasty component. Urethral outcomes are reported as secondary endpoints within larger vaginoplasty series.
- **Surgeon-reported rates** may underestimate the urinary-complication burden, since rates for voiding dysfunction, incontinence and misdirected stream are higher in other cohorts.<sup>[[15]](#ref15)</sup>
- **No standardised definitions** for "meatal stenosis," "voiding dysfunction," or "misdirected stream."
- **Patient-reported measures** are limited. AFFIRM has a validated urological domain, but no measure is specific to urinary function after feminizing GAS.<sup>[[15]](#ref15)[[18]](#ref18)</sup>
- **Long-term outcomes** beyond 5 years poorly characterised.
- **No comparative studies** evaluate different urethroplasty techniques (spatulation length, meatal position, periurethral-tissue management).
- Blasdel 2024 explicitly recommends the IDEAL framework for future surgical-innovation research in this field.<sup>[[15]](#ref15)</sup>
