# Bilateral Simple Orchiectomy (Gender-Affirming)

**Bilateral simple orchiectomy** is a low-risk outpatient gender-affirming procedure for transgender women and gender-diverse individuals. It eliminates endogenous testicular testosterone production, allows reduction or discontinuation of antiandrogen therapy, and serves as either a standalone procedure or a bridge to future vaginoplasty.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[2]](#ref2)</sup>

This is the dedicated atlas page for the scrotal-approach gender-affirming orchiectomy. For the cohort-level decision framework and treatment database, see [Feminizing Gender-Affirming Surgery](/docs/surgical-techniques/04k-gender-affirming-surgery/feminizing-surgery). For the clinical-conditions overview, see [Feminizing Procedures](/docs/special-populations/05c-gender-affirming/feminizing-procedures). When a future vaginoplasty is contemplated, scrotal-skin preservation is the main technical issue; see the [Penile-Inversion Vaginoplasty](/docs/surgical-techniques/04k-gender-affirming-surgery/penile-inversion-vaginoplasty) technique page for donor-tissue assessment and prior-orchiectomy data.

---

## Role in Gender-Affirming Care

Bilateral orchiectomy is one of three genital gender-affirming options for transgender women (alongside vulvoplasty and full-depth vaginoplasty).<sup>[[3]](#ref3)</sup> It is suited to regions with limited access to specialized GAS centers. Hehemann and Walsh describe gender-affirming orchiectomy as a generalizable procedure; Rahman and Ferrando describe a technique that draws on vaginal surgical skills for gynecologists and urogynecologists.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[4]](#ref4)</sup> The 2015 U.S. Transgender Survey reported that 11% of transgender women had undergone orchiectomy and 47% wanted it some day; the ACOG opinion cited here gives different figures for that survey.<sup>[[3]](#ref3)[[17]](#ref17)</sup>

### Eligibility (WPATH SOC8 and Endocrine Society 2017)

For gonadectomy, WPATH SOC8 suggests considering at least 6 months of hormone therapy when appropriate to the person’s gender goals, unless hormones are not clinically indicated (statement 5.6).<sup>[[15]](#ref15)</sup> The Endocrine Society 2017 guideline advises at least 1 year of consistent hormone treatment before genital surgery, unless hormones are not desired or are medically contraindicated (ungraded good practice statement 5.2).<sup>[[15]](#ref15)[[5]](#ref5)</sup> For hormone-treated transgender individuals, the Endocrine Society recommends referral for genital surgery when the patient has had a satisfactory social role change, is satisfied with the hormonal effects and desires definitive surgical change (recommendation 5.4, strong).<sup>[[5]](#ref5)</sup> It suggests delaying gonadectomy, which includes bilateral orchiectomy, until the patient reaches the age of majority in their country (recommendation 5.5, weak).<sup>[[5]](#ref5)</sup>

A mental-health assessment letter is commonly required for insurance authorization.<sup>[[3]](#ref3)</sup> WPATH SOC8 (2022) revised the readiness criteria and is the current standard of care; insurer policies may still cite the earlier 12-month thresholds.<sup>[[15]](#ref15)</sup>

## Approach Selection

When testicular malignancy is not suspected, gender-affirming orchiectomy commonly uses a scrotal approach.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup> A testicular mass suspicious for neoplasm calls for Doppler scrotal ultrasound (AUA Strong Recommendation; Grade B); a solid mass suspicious for malignancy also calls for AFP, hCG, and LDH measurement before treatment (Moderate Recommendation; Grade C).<sup>[[18]](#ref18)</sup> For a lesion suspicious for malignancy with a normal contralateral testis, the AUA recommends radical inguinal orchiectomy and discourages transscrotal orchiectomy (Strong Recommendation; Grade B).<sup>[[18]](#ref18)</sup> For early standalone bridge orchiectomy, an inguinal approach has also been described as an alternative to the scrotal route; selection is based on patient preference and surgeon experience.<sup>[[16]](#ref16)</sup>

| Incision | Notes |
|---|---|
| **Single midline raphe** | Most commonly used; both testes delivered sequentially; single well-concealed scar; preferred when future vaginoplasty is planned because it minimizes scrotal-skin loss<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[6]](#ref6)</sup> |
| **Bilateral transverse hemiscrotal** | Alternative for additional exposure in selected cases |

## Anesthesia

The procedure can be performed under local anesthesia alone (spermatic cord anesthesia block, SCAB), MAC, or general anesthesia.

- A series of 132 bilateral simple orchiectomies under SCAB alone (Atlanta VA; mean age 75 years, none gender-affirming) reported a painless procedure (pain score = 0) in 77% of patients, with 92% rating satisfaction as "highly satisfactory."<sup>[[7]](#ref7)</sup>
- Typical block: equal-volume mixture of 1% lidocaine with epinephrine 1:100,000 and 0.25% bupivacaine, mean approximately 20 mL per case.<sup>[[7]](#ref7)</sup>
- Mean operative time excluding anesthesia was 33 minutes in the SCAB series and 37 minutes in the epididymal-sparing series, both in older men with prostate cancer.<sup>[[7]](#ref7)[[6]](#ref6)</sup>

## Step-by-Step Technique

1. **Incision and exposure.** Vertical midline incision through skin and dartos along the median raphe; the testis is identified within the tunica vaginalis.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[8]](#ref8)</sup>
2. **Delivery of the testis.** Blunt and sharp dissection through the spermatic-cord coverings (external spermatic fascia, cremasteric muscle, internal spermatic fascia); the tunica vaginalis is opened to expose the testis and epididymis.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup>
3. **Cord identification and isolation.** Vas deferens, testicular artery, pampiniform plexus, and cremasteric vessels are identified and isolated; the cord is clamped.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[8]](#ref8)</sup>
4. **Ligation and transection.** The cord is doubly suture-ligated and transected. Many surgeons ligate the vas deferens separately from the vascular pedicle for added security.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[6]](#ref6)</sup> In a published gender-affirming technique, Francis and colleagues ligate close to the external ring with heavy silk and/or suture ligatures and remove maximal cord length to avoid a palpable remnant.<sup>[[19]](#ref19)</sup>
5. **Hemostasis.** Surgical bed inspected; electrocautery applied as needed.
6. **Contralateral side.** Same steps, through the same midline raphe incision (preferred for scrotal-skin preservation) or a separate hemiscrotal incision.
7. **Closure.** Dartos and skin closed in layers; scrotal support / compression applied.

### Epididymal-sparing variant

In the epididymal-sparing technique, the testis is dissected free from the epididymis and the epididymis is folded on itself (epididymoplasty) and left in the scrotum, preserving scrotal bulk and esthetic appearance. In a series of 91 epididymal-sparing orchiectomies for prostate cancer, follow-up was available for 26 of 52 patients who had previously received LH-RH agonist therapy; 96.2% were satisfied with the result and 84.6% preferred the procedure over medical castration. The series did not include transgender patients.<sup>[[6]](#ref6)</sup>

## Tissue Preservation for Future Vaginoplasty

Scrotal-skin preservation at the time of orchiectomy is the main technical decision unique to gender-affirming orchiectomy, because the same tissue is used during penile-inversion vaginoplasty (PIV) to construct the labia majora and to supplement the vaginal canal.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[9]](#ref9)</sup>

- In a prospective cohort of 235 patients having PIV, prior orchiectomy was associated with 3 times greater odds of needing extragenital skin grafts, with less scrotal skin available. This is a preoperative counseling point.<sup>[[10]](#ref10)</sup>
- Stretched scrotal skin length &gt; 10 cm was associated with a low risk of needing additional graft, and stretched penile skin length &lt; 8 cm was associated with graft requirement.<sup>[[10]](#ref10)</sup>
- Duration of gender-affirming hormone exposure did not predict scrotal-skin availability (p = 0.8).<sup>[[10]](#ref10)</sup>
- **Hair removal before later vaginoplasty.** If a vaginal canal is planned, discuss permanent hair removal from penile-shaft and scrotal donor skin, and from any perineal skin selected for the lining, before vaginoplasty; hair-bearing neovaginal skin is a known complication that is difficult to revise. Standalone bridge orchiectomy can proceed while hair removal is incomplete.<sup>[[3]](#ref3)[[21]](#ref21)</sup>
- **Incision design.** A small midline raphe incision minimizes scrotal-skin loss. Excess scrotal skin is not excised at orchiectomy in patients who may pursue vaginoplasty.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup>

## Outcomes and Safety

| Domain | Data |
|---|---|
| **30-day complication rate** | 3.7% in NSQIP 2010–2020 (n = 246 gender-affirming orchiectomies); not significantly different from orchiectomy for torsion (4.4%) or testicular pain (5.9%)<sup>[[11]](#ref11)</sup> |
| **Longer clinical follow-up (separate cohort)** | In a retrospective two-center cohort of 119 patients (median clinical follow-up 2.3 years), 102/119 (86%) had no recorded complication; 5/119 (4.2%) had hemorrhage requiring reoperation, 7/119 (5.8%) developed an abscess, and 2/119 (1.7%) had operative-site pain lasting &gt;3 months.<sup>[[22]](#ref22)</sup> |
| **Setting** | Safely performed as outpatient across medically diverse patients<sup>[[11]](#ref11)</sup> |
| **Volume trend** | Transgender orchiectomy cases in NSQIP increased ~9.5 cases/year from 2015–2020<sup>[[11]](#ref11)</sup> |
| **Operative time** | 33–37 minutes excluding anesthesia (older men, prostate cancer series)<sup>[[7]](#ref7)[[6]](#ref6)</sup> |
| **Anesthesia satisfaction (SCAB)** | 77% painless, 92% highly satisfactory (n = 132, older men with prostate cancer)<sup>[[7]](#ref7)</sup> |

## Fertility Considerations

Bilateral orchiectomy irreversibly ends new sperm production, but sperm cryopreserved beforehand can preserve a route to genetic parenthood. Discuss sperm cryopreservation before surgery; estrogen or antiandrogen treatment may impair semen parameters, and spermatogenesis may recover after treatment cessation with variable timing. Fertility-preservation counseling was underdelivered in the cited 78-patient cohort.<sup>[[12]](#ref12)[[16]](#ref16)[[20]](#ref20)</sup>

- In a retrospective cohort of 78 patients, 22% reported that reproductive options were not discussed before surgical consultation.<sup>[[12]](#ref12)</sup>
- Among patients with pathology data, 40% had some spermatogenesis in the testicular tissue.<sup>[[12]](#ref12)</sup>
- Of the 85% who were not interested in fertility preservation, the reasons were lack of interest in biological children (74%), unwillingness to delay transition (47%) and cost (36%).<sup>[[12]](#ref12)</sup>

## Pathology

In a series of 45 orchiectomy specimens from 23 patients, only a subset showed changes of exogenous hormone therapy:<sup>[[13]](#ref13)</sup>

- Aspermatogenesis in 71% and hypospermatogenesis in 18%.<sup>[[13]](#ref13)</sup>
- Markedly reduced or absent Leydig cells in 85%.<sup>[[13]](#ref13)</sup>
- Nuclear cytomegaly in 25 of 45 specimens (56%) can mimic germ-cell neoplasia in situ (GCNIS); all 6 specimens tested for OCT4 were negative, a diagnostic pitfall for pathologists.<sup>[[13]](#ref13)</sup>

The authors propose a grossing protocol of two sections including rete testis / epididymis plus a third section of cord margin, which identified the relevant pathology in this population.<sup>[[13]](#ref13)</sup>

## Postoperative Care and Hormonal Management

General practice for outpatient scrotal surgery, not tied to a specific published series:

- Ice and scrotal support for 24–48 hours
- Activity restriction approximately 1–2 weeks
- Wound check at 1–2 weeks
- **Antiandrogen therapy.** Antiandrogens such as spironolactone can typically be discontinued or reduced after orchiectomy.<sup>[[14]](#ref14)</sup>
- **Post-gonadectomy hormone plan.** Coordinate sex-steroid management with the prescribing clinician before surgery, including for patients not taking hormones. Estradiol is typical after feminizing orchiectomy; SOC8 also discusses estrogen, testosterone, or both for nonbinary patients according to their goals. Monitor cardiovascular risk factors during hormone therapy and obtain bone-density testing when osteoporosis risk factors are present, particularly if sex steroids are stopped after gonadectomy.<sup>[[5]](#ref5)[[14]](#ref14)[[15]](#ref15)</sup>
- Submit specimens for histopathology; Cornejo and colleagues proposed a three-section grossing protocol based on 45 specimens from 23 patients.<sup>[[13]](#ref13)</sup>

## Key Principles

- Bilateral simple orchiectomy is a low-risk outpatient procedure (9/246 [3.7%] had 30-day complications in the NSQIP gender-affirming orchiectomy cohort) and has been performed by general urologists, general surgeons and urogynecologists.<sup>[[11]](#ref11)[[4]](#ref4)</sup>
- The scrotal approach is standard; the midline raphe incision is preferred when future vaginoplasty is contemplated.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup>
- **Scrotal-skin preservation** is the main technical decision. Prior orchiectomy was associated with 3 times greater odds of needing extragenital skin grafts at later vaginoplasty.<sup>[[10]](#ref10)</sup>
- SCAB-only anesthesia is feasible and was well tolerated in older men with prostate cancer (77% painless).<sup>[[7]](#ref7)</sup>
- The procedure is irreversible; fertility-preservation counseling should precede surgery and is often not delivered.<sup>[[12]](#ref12)</sup>
- Antiandrogens such as spironolactone can usually be stopped after orchiectomy; arrange ongoing sex-steroid management according to the patient's goals and bone-density testing when osteoporosis risk factors are present.<sup>[[5]](#ref5)[[14]](#ref14)[[15]](#ref15)[[16]](#ref16)</sup>
- Nuclear cytomegaly in these specimens can mimic GCNIS; the cases in the pathology series were OCT4-negative.<sup>[[13]](#ref13)</sup>
