# Gender-Affirming Hysterectomy ± Bilateral Salpingo-Oophorectomy (HBSO)

**Gender-affirming hysterectomy** is a masculinizing procedure for transmasculine and gender-diverse individuals with gender dysphoria. ACOG and WPATH recognize it as medically necessary care, and the Endocrine Society 2017 guideline advises clinicians to determine the medical necessity of total hysterectomy and oophorectomy in gender-affirming surgery.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[2]](#ref2)[[11]](#ref11)[[22]](#ref22)</sup> It is the second most desired masculinizing surgery after chest reconstruction, with 79% of transmasculine individuals reporting having undergone or wanting hysterectomy.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup> It is performed as a standalone procedure to relieve dysphoria, or as a prerequisite or first stage before vaginectomy and phalloplasty with urethral lengthening.<sup>[[3]](#ref3)[[4]](#ref4)</sup>

This is the dedicated atlas page. For the cohort-level framework, see [Masculinizing Gender-Affirming Surgery](/docs/surgical-techniques/04k-gender-affirming-surgery/masculinizing-surgery). For downstream stages, see vaginectomy and phalloplasty pages within that section.

---

## Indications

- **Primary.** Alleviation of gender dysphoria related to the presence of a uterus and/or menstruation.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[3]](#ref3)</sup>
- **Secondary.** Hysterectomy can eliminate persistent uterine bleeding and is usually coordinated with vaginectomy when phalloplasty with urethral lengthening is planned.<sup>[[2]](#ref2)[[5]](#ref5)</sup> Endometrial, ovarian and cervical cancers have been reported in transmasculine individuals on testosterone, but these case reports do not establish increased incidence.<sup>[[23]](#ref23)[[24]](#ref24)[[25]](#ref25)</sup> SOC 8 recommends against routine hysterectomy or oophorectomy solely to prevent uterine or ovarian cancer in average-risk patients on testosterone. Patients retaining a cervix follow cervical-screening guidance, while asymptomatic patients retaining a uterus do not need routine endometrial screening; abnormal bleeding warrants evaluation.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[22]](#ref22)</sup>
- **Standalone.** Hysterectomy may be performed independently of other genital surgeries, or combined with BSO, vaginectomy, metoidioplasty or phalloplasty.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[6]](#ref6)</sup>

## Eligibility Criteria (Endocrine Society 2017)

The criteria below are Table 16 of the Endocrine Society 2017 guideline, which reproduces the WPATH Standards of Care version 7 criteria for surgery that affects fertility.<sup>[[2]](#ref2)[[5]](#ref5)</sup> The guideline separately advises that clinicians determine the medical necessity of total hysterectomy and oophorectomy as part of gender-affirming surgery (ungraded good practice statement 4.7).<sup>[[2]](#ref2)</sup> WPATH SOC 8 (2022) is more current and differs on several points; see the note after the list.

1. Persistent, well-documented gender dysphoria.
2. Legal age of majority (≥ 18 yr).
3. ≥ 12 mo continuous gender-affirming hormone therapy (unless medically contraindicated).
4. ≥ 12 mo successful continuous full-time living in the affirmed gender role.
5. Well-controlled medical or mental-health conditions.
6. Demonstrable knowledge of all practical aspects of surgery.

SOC 8 suggests that surgeons consider gonadectomy, which includes oophorectomy, when the patient has tolerated at least 6 months of hormone therapy, unless hormones are not clinically indicated or not desired (statement 13.5). For other genital procedures it suggests considering surgery when the patient is stable on the current regimen, which may include at least 6 months of hormones or longer if needed for the surgical result (13.6). SOC 8 does not carry forward the 12-month real-life role requirement, recommends a single assessment opinion when documentation is required (5.5), and recommends confirming that reproductive options were discussed before gonadectomy (13.4).<sup>[[22]](#ref22)</sup> Many insurance carriers still require one or two mental-health letters before authorisation.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup>

## Preoperative Considerations

### Fertility counselling

Hysterectomy with BSO produces permanent infertility. Few transmasculine individuals carry a pregnancy or use their oocytes after gender-affirming treatment, but fertility preservation options, including oocyte cryopreservation, are discussed before surgery.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[3]](#ref3)</sup> Ovarian-tissue cryopreservation with in-vitro maturation of cumulus-oocyte complexes recovered at the time of hysterectomy is an emerging, experimental option available at selected centers; a prospective cohort of 40 trans men undergoing hysterectomy with BSO after prolonged testosterone treatment (mean 58 weeks) recovered 1,313 cumulus-oocyte complexes from the medulla of 35 patients, with most follicles primordial on histology. The study reports in-vitro maturation potential, not a live-birth outcome.<sup>[[32]](#ref32)</sup> Pregnancy screening before surgery is appropriate for transgender men of reproductive age, since ovulation may occur without testosterone and is not reliably suppressed by it.<sup>[[31]](#ref31)</sup>

### Cervical screening

Cervical screening is completed before hysterectomy when indicated. Female-to-male patients have a substantially higher rate of unsatisfactory Pap tests than cisgender women: in one center's records (233 FTM and 3,625 female patients), 10.8% of FTM Pap tests were inadequate versus 1.3% of female patients' tests, an adjusted odds ratio of 10.77 (95% CI 6.83–16.83) after adjustment for age, race and BMI.<sup>[[26]](#ref26)</sup> Patient-collected vaginal HPV swabs are an alternative to clinician-collected cytology for patients who find a speculum examination difficult; trans-masculine individuals report the self-collected swab as more acceptable than a provider-administered Pap test,<sup>[[27]](#ref27)</sup> and a 131-patient paired study found that self-collected vaginal swabs detected 15 of 21 clinician-positive high-risk HPV results (sensitivity 71.4%, specificity 98.2%).<sup>[[28]](#ref28)</sup> A self-swab is a patient-centered alternative for HPV-based screening; it does not replace cytology when cytology is specifically indicated, and standard age- and risk-based screening intervals apply.

### Testosterone-related surgical-anatomy challenges<sup>[[7]](#ref7)[[1]](#ref1)</sup>

- **Vaginal atrophy.** Testosterone is associated with mucosal thinning and introital narrowing, similar to postmenopausal changes.
- **Nulliparity.** Most patients are young and nulliparous, with a small uterus without descent.
- **Increased perineal-laceration risk.** In a retrospective cohort (n = 88 testosterone users, 242 controls), testosterone use was associated with intraoperative perineal lacerations requiring repair (RR 3.3, 95% CI 1.03–10.5).<sup>[[7]](#ref7)</sup>
- **Genital examination may worsen dysphoria.** Examination under anesthesia before the procedure may be appropriate.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup>
- **Vaginal estrogen.** Preoperative vaginal estrogen is considered to improve tissue quality. Postoperative vaginal estrogen, started at the postoperative visit, was used more often by testosterone users (8.0% vs 1.7%, p = 0.01).<sup>[[7]](#ref7)</sup>

## Surgical Approaches

Reviews recommend a minimally invasive approach.<sup>[[8]](#ref8)[[9]](#ref9)</sup>

| Approach | Frequency | Key considerations |
|---|---|---|
| **Total laparoscopic hysterectomy** | ~57% (most common) | Preferred; avoids vaginal manipulation that may worsen dysphoria; good visualization<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[2]](#ref2)</sup> |
| **Robotic-assisted laparoscopic** | Increasing | Similar outcomes to conventional laparoscopic; higher cost; ergonomic advantages<sup>[[10]](#ref10)</sup> |
| **Laparoscopic-assisted vaginal (LAVH)** | ~20% | Combines laparoscopic + vaginal components<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup> |
| **Vaginal** | Less common | No abdominal scarring; technically difficult — vaginal atrophy, narrow introitus, absent uterine descent<sup>[[4]](#ref4)</sup> |
| **Abdominal (open)** | ~15% | Reserved for complex cases; higher complication rates<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup> |
| **2-port laparoscopic (Marfori novel)** | Novel | Minimizes port-site scarring; good outcomes described<sup>[[11]](#ref11)</sup> |

In a comparison of hysterectomy with BSO followed by colpocleisis (n = 124: 92 transvaginal, 32 laparoscopic; mean 41-month follow-up), operative time was shorter with the vaginal route (51 vs 76 minutes, p &lt; 0.001), while complication rate (&lt;3% both groups), reoperation rate (0% both groups), blood loss and postoperative stay (4.3 vs 4.5 days) did not differ.<sup>[[30]](#ref30)</sup> This colpocleisis-specific population does not establish vaginal hysterectomy as preferable when genital reconstruction is planned later.

**Robotic setup.** One described robotic benign-hysterectomy workflow uses side-docking to keep the vagina accessible, with the operating table left undocked once the robot is docked unless the arms are disengaged. A reported port layout places the camera at an open transumbilical site, two robotic ports about 12 cm to either side at umbilical level, and a 10-mm assistant port between the umbilicus and the left robotic port, about 3 cm cranial; a fourth robotic port is placed symmetrically when used. During bladder dissection, a vaginal probe, cervical cup or manipulator identifies the cervicovaginal junction, with the bladder mobilized 1–2 cm beyond it.<sup>[[33]](#ref33)</sup> Use of a vaginal probe or manipulator should remain subject to the patient's preferences and dysphoria-related goals.

### Intraoperative technical considerations<sup>[[12]](#ref12)[[8]](#ref8)</sup>

- **Vaginal cuff.** Two-layer closure has been described as an option when tissue quality is a concern; its benefit for preventing dehiscence in transmasculine patients has not been established.<sup>[[8]](#ref8)</sup> One described laparoscopic closure sequence mobilizes the bladder caudally before colpotomy to permit adequate anterior tissue purchase, includes the uterosacral ligament in the initial angle bite, and inspects the cuff from both above and below for hemostasis.<sup>[[29]](#ref29)</sup> A reported robotic closure uses delayed-absorbable suture (for example 2-0 PDS), includes the uterosacral ligaments at each angle, and places bites about 5 mm from the edge and 5 mm apart.<sup>[[33]](#ref33)</sup> In a 2025 meta-analysis of benign minimally invasive hysterectomy, the pooled comparison did not establish lower cuff-dehiscence risk with double-layer versus single-layer closure (OR 0.39, 95% CI 0.08–1.99); a preferred closure method has not been established specifically for transmasculine patients.<sup>[[38]](#ref38)</sup> Energy-device colpotomy can leave a coagulated, devitalized vaginal edge that later sloughs; closure bites confined to that shallow, devitalized zone can cut through and reopen the cuff, so bites should pass through viable tissue beyond the thermally affected margin.<sup>[[34]](#ref34)</sup> When severe testosterone-associated vaginal atrophy raises concern for poor healing, a two-layer closure with delayed-absorbable suture and pelvic rest for up to 12 weeks has been recommended.<sup>[[31]](#ref31)</sup>
- **Oophorectomy.** When bilateral oophorectomy is planned, the infundibulopelvic ligament is divided with an adequate ovarian-tissue margin to reduce the risk of a symptomatic remnant. Ovarian remnant syndrome after hysterectomy can present with pelvic pain or a mass; it does not cause menstruation when the uterus has been removed.<sup>[[36]](#ref36)</sup>
- **Concurrent procedures.** Hysterectomy is often combined with BSO and may be staged with vaginectomy and genital reconstruction.<sup>[[6]](#ref6)</sup>
- **Postoperative catheter.** Foley removal at the end of surgery, when continued drainage is not otherwise indicated, avoids catheter management while awake, which may trigger dysphoria.<sup>[[31]](#ref31)</sup>

## The Oophorectomy Decision

Oophorectomy is decided by shared decision-making. No definitive evidence-based recommendation exists.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[13]](#ref13)</sup>

**Arguments for oophorectomy:**

- Oophorectomy eliminates endogenous estrogen production and ovarian-pathology risk.
- It removes a source of dysphoria for many patients.
- Rare ovarian-cancer cases have been reported in transmasculine individuals on testosterone.<sup>[[2]](#ref2)</sup>


**Arguments for ovarian preservation:**

- Ovarian preservation keeps fertility potential (oocyte retrieval remains possible).
- In cisgender women, premenopausal oophorectomy is associated with increased cardiovascular events and all-cause mortality; this has not been demonstrated in transmasculine individuals on testosterone.<sup>[[13]](#ref13)</sup>
- Testosterone has anabolic effects on cortical bone and, in adequate doses, may prevent bone demineralization.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup>
- Some patients may lose access to testosterone in the future (cost, access barriers), and endogenous estrogen then serves as a safety net.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup>

Long-term outcomes data are insufficient to make definitive oophorectomy recommendations in this population.<sup>[[13]](#ref13)</sup>

## Uterine Pathology on Testosterone

Despite amenorrhea in most testosterone-treated patients, histopathology reveals persistent endometrial activity:<sup>[[14]](#ref14)[[15]](#ref15)</sup>

- Proliferative endometrium was found in 33/81 specimens (40.7%) in Hawkins 2021 and 61/94 (64.9%) in Grimstad 2019; Grimstad reported active endometrium, including secretory cases, in 65/94 (69.1%).<sup>[[14]](#ref14)[[15]](#ref15)</sup>
- Atrophic endometrium was found in about 50% (Hawkins 2021).
- Endometrial polyps were found in about 11% (Hawkins 2021).
- No endometrial-cancer cases occurred in either specimen cohort. Case reports of endometrial cancer in testosterone-treated transmasculine individuals do exist in the broader literature, though they remain few, so this finding reflects these two series and not an absence of any reported case.<sup>[[23]](#ref23)</sup>
- One case of complex hyperplasia without atypia occurred in 94 patients (Grimstad 2019).<sup>[[14]](#ref14)</sup>
- The uterus is typically small with endometrial atrophy on gross examination.<sup>[[2]](#ref2)</sup>

The clinical significance of persistent endometrial activity despite clinical amenorrhea is uncertain.<sup>[[14]](#ref14)[[15]](#ref15)</sup>

## Outcomes and Safety

Comparative cohorts reported similar overall 30-day complication rates.<sup>[[9]](#ref9)[[21]](#ref21)</sup> In a separate retrospective benign-hysterectomy cohort with at least six months' follow-up, vaginal cuff dehiscence occurred in 5.8% of 154 gender-diverse testosterone users versus 2.4% of 21,046 cisgender women (adjusted OR 2.18, 95% CI 1.08–4.41); this association does not establish testosterone as the cause.<sup>[[35]](#ref35)</sup>

Bretschneider 2018 NSQIP analysis (521 gender-affirming hysterectomies among 159,736 hysterectomies, 2013–2016):<sup>[[9]](#ref9)</sup>

- Transgender male status not independently associated with postoperative complications (adjusted OR 1.11, 95% CI 0.56–2.10).
- The composite complication rate after propensity matching was 3.4% vs 3.3% in controls.
- Minimally invasive approaches were associated with lower complication rates than open surgery: laparoscopic adjusted OR 0.09; vaginal adjusted OR 0.04; laparoscopic-assisted vaginal adjusted OR 0.07.

Siringo 2023 (NSQIP 2009–2018, 526 gender-affirming hysterectomies among 40,742 hysterectomies):<sup>[[21]](#ref21)</sup>

- Complication rates were similar to those of hysterectomy for benign menstrual disorders, and return to the operating room did not differ significantly (adjusted OR 1.08, 95% CI 0.56–2.10).
- Gender-affirming patients were younger and healthier, had less diabetes, were more often treated as outpatients and had a shorter time to discharge.

Pando 2024 (retrospective cohort, 88 testosterone users vs 242 controls, minimally invasive hysterectomy):<sup>[[7]](#ref7)</sup>

- **Postoperative vaginal bleeding.** Testosterone users reported bleeding more often by message or phone call (RR 1.74, 95% CI 1.1–2.7). The reasons for emergency department visits did not differ.

## Psychological Outcomes

In a secondary analysis of the 2015 US Transgender Survey (n = 27,715 transgender adults), Almazan 2021 found that undergoing at least one gender-affirming surgery at least 2 years earlier, compared with wanting but not having surgery, was associated with:<sup>[[16]](#ref16)</sup>

- Lower past-month severe psychological distress (aOR 0.58).
- Lower past-year smoking (aOR 0.65).
- Lower past-year suicidal ideation (aOR 0.56).

In the Bakir 2025 qualitative study (20 transmasculine participants in Turkey), participants reported less distress and more happiness after hysterectomy, and one theme described it as "escaping the gender prison".<sup>[[17]](#ref17)</sup>

## Epidemiology and Disparities

- Approximately 0.3% of all hysterectomies in the US are performed for transmasculine individuals.<sup>[[12]](#ref12)[[9]](#ref9)</sup>
- Utilization of gender-affirming surgery overall increased more than 5-fold from 2012 to 2019 (0.9 to 5.0 per 100,000 inpatient records).<sup>[[18]](#ref18)</sup>
- Mean age at gender-affirming hysterectomy about 31 yr.<sup>[[19]](#ref19)</sup>
- Among transmasculine patients in one cohort, hysterectomy was the most desired surgery (61.4%), followed by oophorectomy (59.8%).<sup>[[20]](#ref20)</sup>
- **Insurance disparities.** Records with Medicaid (OR 0.27) or Medicare (OR 0.15) had lower odds of gender-affirming surgery than records with private insurance.<sup>[[18]](#ref18)</sup>

## Relationship to Other Masculinizing Genital Surgeries

Hysterectomy is often a prerequisite or concurrent procedure for subsequent genital reconstruction:<sup>[[4]](#ref4)[[6]](#ref6)</sup>

- **Phalloplasty with urethral lengthening.** Hysterectomy and vaginectomy typically required to allow urethral elongation and prevent a persistent vaginal cavity.<sup>[[4]](#ref4)</sup>
- **Metoidioplasty.** May be performed with or without prior hysterectomy and vaginectomy.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[4]](#ref4)</sup>
- **Staging:** HBSO commonly performed as a first stage, with genital reconstruction following after recovery.<sup>[[6]](#ref6)</sup>
- **Protecting donor tissue for planned urethral lengthening.** When urethral lengthening is planned, hysterectomy details are coordinated with the genital-reconstruction surgeon. An oversized cervical cup can require excision of vaginal tissue that may be useful later, and withdrawing a large uterus through a narrow introitus can tear vulvar epithelium intended for urethral reconstruction; gentle, lubricated vaginal manipulation is recommended, and a tear is managed with pressure for hemostasis and primary repair with absorbable suture on a non-cutting needle if needed.<sup>[[29]](#ref29)</sup>

## Evidence Limitations

- The Bretschneider 2018 (n = 521) and Siringo 2023 (n = 526) NSQIP analyses found similar 30-day complication rates to comparison groups.<sup>[[9]](#ref9)[[21]](#ref21)</sup> A 2026 matched NSQIP analysis included 975 gender-affirming hysterectomies (970 matched) and likewise found no significant difference in 30-day complications by approach; all three analyses are limited to perioperative outcomes.<sup>[[37]](#ref37)</sup>
- No randomized trials of approach or oophorectomy decision in this population have been identified.
- Long-term cardiovascular and bone outcomes of premenopausal oophorectomy in testosterone-treated transmasculine individuals are not well characterized; cisgender data may not apply.<sup>[[13]](#ref13)</sup>
- Persistent proliferative endometrium occurs in 40–69% of specimens on testosterone, with no cancer cases in these two series; its clinical significance is unclear, and rare endometrial-cancer case reports exist elsewhere in the literature.<sup>[[14]](#ref14)[[15]](#ref15)[[23]](#ref23)</sup>
- Validated PROMs specific to gender-affirming hysterectomy outcomes are lacking.
