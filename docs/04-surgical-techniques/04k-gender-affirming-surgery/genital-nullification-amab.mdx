# Genital Nullification — AMAB (Penectomy + Bilateral Orchiectomy + Scrotectomy + Perineal Urethrostomy)

**Genital nullification** (also termed nullo surgery or gender nullification) for AMAB individuals is the removal of penis, testes, and scrotum without creation of a neovagina, resulting in a smooth perineal contour with a perineal urethrostomy (PU) for urinary function.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup> It is an individually customized gender-affirming procedure that falls outside the binary surgical options (vaginoplasty or stand-alone orchiectomy). It is most commonly sought by nonbinary or gender-diverse individuals.

This is the dedicated atlas page. For the broader variant-GGAS framework and decision matrix, see [Non-Binary / Nullification Gender-Affirming Surgery](/docs/surgical-techniques/04k-gender-affirming-surgery/non-binary-nullification). For the perineal-urethrostomy component, see [Midline Perineal Urethrostomy](/docs/surgical-techniques/04a-urethral-reconstruction/seven-flap-perineal-urethrostomy) and [Blandy Perineal Urethrostomy](/docs/surgical-techniques/04a-urethral-reconstruction/blandy-perineal-urethrostomy). For the orchiectomy component, see [Bilateral Simple Orchiectomy](/docs/surgical-techniques/04k-gender-affirming-surgery/simple-orchiectomy). For the external vulvar alternative without a vaginal canal, see [Zero-Depth Vulvoplasty](/docs/surgical-techniques/04k-gender-affirming-surgery/zero-depth-vulvoplasty).

---

## Indication and Surgical Goal

The goal is absence of external genital structures with a smooth, aesthetically neutral perineum and seated-position voiding via a perineal urethrostomy.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup>

This is distinct from:

- Zero-depth or shallow-depth vulvoplasty, which creates external vulvar structures (labia, clitoral hood, neoclitoris) but no vaginal canal.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup>
- Full-depth vaginoplasty, which creates a neovaginal canal and vulvar structures.
- Stand-alone bilateral orchiectomy, in which the testes are removed but the penis and scrotum are retained.

Nullification can include complete glans removal, burial of a de-epithelialized glans remnant, or surface maturation of a glans remnant, depending on patient goals.<sup>[[46]](#ref46)</sup>

## Eligibility

WPATH SOC v8 (2022) recognizes that gender-affirming surgical goals are highly individual and that nonbinary individuals may seek procedures that do not conform to traditional binary options.<sup>[[2]](#ref2)[[3]](#ref3)</sup> SOC8 calls for an individualized assessment of gender incongruence, capacity for informed consent, and medical or mental-health concerns that could affect surgery. If written documentation is required, one opinion from a qualified assessor is sufficient. For genital surgery, stability on the current treatment regimen may include at least 6 months of hormone therapy when desired and clinically indicated; hormones are not required when undesired or contraindicated.<sup>[[3]](#ref3)[[43]](#ref43)</sup> For individually customized surgery, SOC8 also recommends that the surgeon consult a comprehensive multidisciplinary transgender-health team.<sup>[[43]](#ref43)</sup>

The following older criteria come from a 2016 Lancet review and should be read as historical, not as current SOC8 requirements:<sup>[[4]](#ref4)</sup>

- Persistent, well-documented gender dysphoria.
- Capacity for informed consent.
- Well-managed coexisting medical and mental-health conditions.
- Typically two independent mental-health-professional letters of recommendation.
- Older guidance described 12 months of hormone therapy before gonadectomy when appropriate to gender goals.<sup>[[4]](#ref4)</sup> WPATH SOC8 suggests considering at least 6 months when clinically indicated and consistent with the person’s goals; hormones need not precede gonadectomy when undesired or contraindicated.<sup>[[3]](#ref3)[[43]](#ref43)</sup>

The Pezzoli 2026 Delphi consensus on feminizing genital surgery in AMAB individuals (27 urological surgeons; applicability to nullification is extrapolated) identified BMI &gt; 24.9 kg/m², diabetes, smoking and cardiovascular disease as significant risk factors for postoperative complications. Advanced age alone was not considered a contraindication. Major psychiatric disorders and spinal cord injury were relative contraindications, and Charlson Comorbidity Index ≥ 3 was a relative contraindication for vaginoplasty only.<sup>[[5]](#ref5)</sup>

### Preoperative optimisation

- **Smoking cessation.** Advise cessation as soon as surgery is planned; abstinence for at least 4 weeks is associated with fewer pulmonary and wound complications, while shorter abstinence can still help. Consider delaying surgery case by case.<sup>[[21]](#ref21)</sup>
- **Genital hair plan.** Map any genital skin intended for retention or transposition. The feminizing-surgery Delphi consensus advises permanent hair removal or excision of hair-bearing genital tissue; routine epilation is not established when nullification removes that tissue.<sup>[[5]](#ref5)</sup>
- **Baseline function and goals.** Document urinary symptoms and sexual function, including desired sensation and orgasm, before selecting the urethrostomy and nerve-handling plan.<sup>[[46]](#ref46)</sup>
- **Glycemic optimization.** HbA1c screening and optimization in patients with diabetes or metabolic syndrome. In a general-surgery NSQIP cohort (n = 282,131), higher HbA1c was associated with more 30-day complications (Schaschinger 2026, *JAMA Surg*).<sup>[[22]](#ref22)</sup>
- **Fertility counseling.** Sperm cryopreservation is discussed before surgery because bilateral orchiectomy is irreversible.<sup>[[4]](#ref4)</sup>

## Surgical Technique — Step by Step

The steps below follow Ascha 2024 (a 16-patient series of individually customized genital procedures, the main GAS-specific reference for this operation):<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup>

### 1. Bilateral orchiectomy

Rahman and Ferrando 2026 describe gender-affirming orchiectomy as similar to vaginal gynecologic procedures and suggest that urogynecologists can incorporate it with minimal additional training (a technique video from a senior author with over 100 cases).<sup>[[23]](#ref23)</sup> Steps: midline raphe or bilateral scrotal incision; tunica vaginalis opened; testis delivered; spermatic-cord structures (vas deferens, testicular artery, pampiniform plexus, cremasteric vessels) individually identified, clamped, ligated, divided; gubernaculum divided; hemostasis confirmed; layered closure. Cross-link to [Bilateral Simple Orchiectomy](/docs/surgical-techniques/04k-gender-affirming-surgery/simple-orchiectomy) for full technique.

**Saltman 2023 NSQIP (n = 246 transgender orchiectomies).** The overall complication rate was 3.7%, similar to cisgender orchiectomy for testicular torsion (4.4%) or pain (5.9%). The authors concluded that stand-alone orchiectomy can be performed safely as an outpatient procedure.<sup>[[6]](#ref6)</sup>

### 2. Penectomy

Total penectomy removes the glans and penile shaft while retaining sufficient viable proximal spongiosal urethra to construct the perineal urethrostomy.<sup>[[47]](#ref47)</sup> The technique is adapted from the oncologic-penectomy literature:<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[9]](#ref9)</sup>

- **Circumferential skin incision** at the base of the penis.
- **Dorsal neurovascular bundle.** For en bloc excision, ligate the dorsal arteries, deep dorsal vein and dorsal nerves; a nerve-preserving variant instead retains the neurovascular bundle with a buried de-epithelialized glans remnant or a glans remnant matured at the surface to attempt sexual sensation.<sup>[[46]](#ref46)</sup> Breza 1989 identified grossly visible cavernous nerves traceable from the penile hilum to the prostate.<sup>[[25]](#ref25)</sup> Retained glans tissue may cause discomfort or infection, and comparative sensory outcomes are unavailable.<sup>[[46]](#ref46)</sup>
- Transect the corporal bodies at the planned proximal level and oversew the retained stumps. A noncrushing vascular clamp across the corpora before transection may reduce blood loss.<sup>[[26]](#ref26)[[47]](#ref47)</sup>
- **Urethra.** Mobilize the corpus spongiosum with the urethra away from the corpora cavernosa, preserving sufficient proximal urethral length for PU. Transect and ventrally spatulate the urethra for maturation at the perineal opening.<sup>[[47]](#ref47)</sup>
- **Suspensory ligament** divided.
- Corporal stumps oversewn with absorbable suture for hemostasis.

**Penectomy-specific outcomes.** In a retrospective NSQIP cohort of partial and total penectomies for penile cancer (Velazquez 2019, n = 304), the overall complication rate was 19.7%, with UTI in 3.0%, superficial SSI in 3.0% and bleeding requiring transfusion in 3.9%; the total-penectomy subgroup rate is not reported here.<sup>[[27]](#ref27)</sup> The penile-cancer cohort cannot estimate complication rates after gender-affirming nullification because patient characteristics, indications, and operative details differ.<sup>[[27]](#ref27)</sup> In a retrospective series of penile amputation with PU for invasive penile cancer (Falcone 2023, n = 10, median follow-up 16 months), operative time was 195 min (IQR 155–275), hospital stay 8 d (IQR 6–10) and postoperative complications 20% (1 surgical revision).<sup>[[9]](#ref9)</sup> At 3 months, median IPSS improved from 15 to 6 in this penile-cancer cohort.<sup>[[9]](#ref9)</sup>

### 3. Scrotectomy

Scrotectomy is excision of scrotal skin and dartos fascia after orchiectomy. In nullification, excise excess scrotal tissue for the planned contour while retaining well-perfused skin needed for urethrostomy maturation and tension-free perineal closure.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[24]](#ref24)[[46]](#ref46)</sup>

- Redundant scrotal skin excised; only enough preserved for tension-free closure.
- **Perineal body** carefully preserved to maintain pelvic-floor integrity.
- **For primary closure** (typical in nullification given smaller defects), redundant skin is approximated under appropriate tension.
- **For larger defects.** Thigh or abdominal-wall fasciocutaneous flaps have been described for large post-scrotectomy wounds. In Staniorski 2023, all 20 patients retained testes in thigh pouches; 20/20 wounds closed, 3/20 required split-thickness skin grafting, and 3/20 had complications at a median 9-month follow-up. These closure outcomes are not specific to orchiectomy with nullification.<sup>[[28]](#ref28)</sup>
- The oncologic-emasculation literature notes that a retained scrotum can hang in front of the neourethrostomy and become wet during micturition, causing urinary dermatitis; concurrent scrotectomy avoids this.<sup>[[24]](#ref24)</sup>

### 4. Perineal urethrostomy creation

The bulbar urethra is mobilized and brought to the perineum; meatus spatulated and matured to the perineal skin to create a widely patent stoma. Preserve perfusion and minimize tension when maturing the retained proximal urethra. Dorsal-plate preservation is described for stricture PU, whereas terminal urethrostomy after penectomy uses a transected urethral stump:<sup>[[7]](#ref7)[[8]](#ref8)[[47]](#ref47)</sup>

- **Myers 2011 dorsal-plate-preservation technique.** Primary success was 83% and secondary success 93% (n = 40, median follow-up 31 months).<sup>[[8]](#ref8)</sup>
- **Joshi 2024 algorithmic midline approach.** Success (freedom from re-intervention) was 95.1%, with 4.9% requiring re-intervention (n = 103).<sup>[[7]](#ref7)</sup>

The linked [Midline Perineal Urethrostomy](/docs/surgical-techniques/04a-urethral-reconstruction/seven-flap-perineal-urethrostomy) and [Blandy Perineal Urethrostomy](/docs/surgical-techniques/04a-urethral-reconstruction/blandy-perineal-urethrostomy) pages provide flap and stoma-maturation principles. After penectomy, select an inset suitable for a transected terminal urethra; the intact-urethra loop branch of a stricture-PU algorithm does not apply unchanged.<sup>[[47]](#ref47)</sup>

### 5. Closure

Remaining skin edges approximated to create a smooth, flat perineal contour. The goal is an aesthetically neutral genital area.

## Perioperative Management

Pezzoli 2026 proposed the following perioperative practices for feminizing genital surgery in AMAB individuals; their applicability to nullification requires procedure-specific assessment.<sup>[[5]](#ref5)</sup>

- **Preoperative.** Confirm which hair-bearing skin will be retained or removed. Counseling covers wound care, seated voiding, loss of genital structures, loss of penile penetrative intercourse and irreversibility, including loss of penile and scrotal tissue that may be needed for later vulvar or vaginal reconstruction; elicit sensory goals before choosing a nerve-preserving or nonpreserving approach.<sup>[[5]](#ref5)[[46]](#ref46)</sup>
- **Catheterization.** Plan removal after assessing the urethrostomy and perineal closure; a nullification-specific interval is not established. The feminizing-surgery Delphi panel proposed 3–5 days, whereas a 10-patient penile-cancer penectomy/PU series reported a median of 15 days (IQR 15–32). These are different operations and populations.<sup>[[5]](#ref5)[[9]](#ref9)</sup>
- **Hospital stay.** Stand-alone orchiectomy may be performed as an outpatient procedure.<sup>[[6]](#ref6)</sup> One 2025 AMAB nullification technique report describes outpatient discharge with a urethrostomy catheter for 72 hours; this is institutional practice without comparative outcomes.<sup>[[46]](#ref46)</sup> The 8-day median in Falcone 2023 came from older patients with penile cancer undergoing penectomy and PU.<sup>[[9]](#ref9)</sup>
- **Pelvic-floor PT.** The Delphi consensus recommends it before and after surgery.<sup>[[5]](#ref5)</sup>
- **No dilation.** Unlike vaginoplasty, nullification creates no neovaginal canal and requires no lifelong dilation.

### Perioperative hormone-therapy management

The Delphi consensus did not reach agreement on the timing of estrogen interruption or re-initiation.<sup>[[5]](#ref5)</sup> Retrospective and survey data indicate that many patients continue estrogen perioperatively:<sup>[[29]](#ref29)[[30]](#ref30)[[31]](#ref31)</sup>

- **Herndon 2024.** In a retrospective cohort of 183 transfeminine individuals managed with an individualized algorithm, 75.4% continued estradiol perioperatively. One VTE occurred in the continuation group; this retrospective, risk-stratified cohort detected no statistically significant difference in postoperative complications but cannot establish equivalence. The 25% who held estradiol for 2–6 weeks were older and had more cardiometabolic comorbidity and higher Caprini scores.<sup>[[30]](#ref30)</sup>
- Continuing estrogen perioperatively avoids withdrawal symptoms such as dysphoria and vasomotor symptoms; a summary of the WPATH guidance addresses this.<sup>[[3]](#ref3)</sup>
- **Elevated VTE risk** (older age, cardiometabolic comorbidities, higher Caprini score): individualized assessment is used, and temporary discontinuation may be appropriate.<sup>[[30]](#ref30)[[32]](#ref32)</sup>
- Spironolactone used solely for testicular testosterone suppression can usually be stopped after orchiectomy; persistent androgen-related hair growth may require separate treatment.<sup>[[33]](#ref33)[[36]](#ref36)</sup>
- In a Hung 2024 survey of WPATH surgeons (n = 51, 34% response), most did not stop hormone therapy before GAS and provided DVT prophylaxis to all patients for less than 1 week postoperatively.<sup>[[34]](#ref34)</sup>

### Thromboprophylaxis

The Delphi consensus recommends enoxaparin prophylaxis.<sup>[[5]](#ref5)</sup> Transfeminine patients on estrogen are at higher VTE risk than the general population, but absolute risk remains low.<sup>[[32]](#ref32)[[35]](#ref35)</sup> See Maharaj 2025 for an illustrative case study of postoperative-VTE prophylaxis on estrogen.<sup>[[29]](#ref29)</sup>

## Complications

Component-procedure complication data:

### Orchiectomy (Saltman NSQIP n = 246 transgender)<sup>[[6]](#ref6)</sup>

- The overall complication rate was 3.7%, not significantly different from cisgender orchiectomy for non-oncologic indications.
- Hematoma, wound infection, rare DVT.

### Perineal urethrostomy (aggregated)<sup>[[10]](#ref10)[[11]](#ref11)[[7]](#ref7)[[8]](#ref8)</sup>

| Complication | Incidence | Management |
|---|---|---|
| Stomal stenosis | 12–18% in non-nullification PU cohorts (penile cancer or stricture)<sup>[[10]](#ref10)[[11]](#ref11)[[8]](#ref8)</sup> | Dilation or surgical revision |
| Wound infection | 11% after penile-cancer PU (n = 299; 30 days)<sup>[[10]](#ref10)</sup> | Antibiotics, wound care |
| Wound dehiscence | 4.0% after penile-cancer PU (n = 299; 30 days)<sup>[[10]](#ref10)</sup> | Conservative or surgical |
| Urinary spraying / misdirection | Variable | Positional adjustment, revision |
| Urinary incontinence | Median ICIQ-UI SF score 0 (range 0–21) in a stricture-PU cohort; incidence not reported<sup>[[11]](#ref11)</sup> | Pelvic-floor PT |

In the largest multicenter series (de Vries 2021, retrospective, penile cancer, n = 299, median follow-up 19 months), stenosis occurred in 12%, 74% of stenoses needed surgical revision, and median time to revision was 6.1 months.<sup>[[10]](#ref10)</sup> Two stenoses were recorded more than two years after surgery in this penile-cancer cohort, but median follow-up was 19 months, limiting inference about late risk.<sup>[[10]](#ref10)</sup> Prior radiation therapy was the strongest stenosis risk factor in Myers 2011 (adjusted OR 11.2).<sup>[[8]](#ref8)</sup>

## Urinary Function After PU

Long-term urological outcomes come from cisgender PU series:<sup>[[11]](#ref11)[[12]](#ref12)[[7]](#ref7)[[13]](#ref13)</sup>

- **Voiding position.** Seated urination is described after perineal urethrostomy in a stricture cohort.<sup>[[7]](#ref7)</sup> In the Joshi series, 49/103 patients were reached by phone at a median 61 months; 31/41 (76%) were unbothered by sitting to urinate and 40/49 (82%) reported improvement in overall health.<sup>[[7]](#ref7)</sup>
- **Voiding function.** LUTS improved significantly. In Shinchi 2021 (56 patients, retrospective), Qmax rose from 3.8 to 17.6 mL/s and PVR fell from 77.6 to 21.3 mL among the 11 patients with paired measurements.<sup>[[13]](#ref13)</sup>
- **Continence.** Continence is generally preserved. Klemm 2024 (n = 76) reported a median ICIQ-UI of 0 (range 0–21) at a median follow-up of 55 months.<sup>[[11]](#ref11)</sup>
- **Retreatment-free survival.** Reported rates are 84–95% depending on technique and population.<sup>[[11]](#ref11)[[7]](#ref7)</sup>
- **Patient satisfaction.** In Joshi 2024 and Shinchi 2021, 42/49 (86%) and 33/39 (84.6%) survey respondents were satisfied or very satisfied, respectively, and Klemm 2024 reported very high satisfaction.<sup>[[11]](#ref11)[[7]](#ref7)[[13]](#ref13)</sup>

## Psychological and Quality-of-Life Outcomes

Nullification-specific PRO data are limited. The broader GAS literature includes:

- **Almazan and Keuroghlian, 2015 US Transgender Survey (27,715 total respondents; comparison groups: 3,559 with prior surgery and 16,401 wanting but not having surgery).** Having had at least one gender-affirming surgery at least 2 years earlier, compared with wanting but not having surgery, was associated with lower severe psychological distress (aOR 0.58, 95% CI 0.50–0.67), lower past-year suicidal ideation (aOR 0.56, 95% CI 0.50–0.64), lower smoking (aOR 0.65, 95% CI 0.57–0.75). Patients who underwent all desired surgeries had significantly lower odds of all adverse mental-health outcomes.<sup>[[14]](#ref14)</sup>
- **Yeo 2026 systematic review of longitudinal cohorts (14 studies, n = 3,023).** Psychological well-being and QoL improved initially within the first year after GAS, followed by a plateau or decline. Younger age, higher education and a supportive social environment were associated with positive outcomes, and higher pre-GAS psychopathology with poorer outcomes.<sup>[[39]](#ref39)</sup>
- **Cooney 2025 systematic review.** Among the surgical studies, all 4 QoL studies showed statistically significant improvement and all 5 gender-dysphoria studies showed improved gender congruence. Certainty across the review was moderate to very low.<sup>[[15]](#ref15)</sup>
- **Pletta 2025 (cross-sectional, n = 2,176).** Among the 946 participants with prior GAS, reproductive surgeries (including gonadectomy) were rated most satisfactory across gender-identity groups (mean 4.6/5 among recipients).<sup>[[17]](#ref17)</sup>
- **Ren 2024 systematic review (24 studies, n = 3,662).** Pooled regret prevalence was 1.94% (transfeminine 4.0%, transmasculine 0.8%).<sup>[[40]](#ref40)</sup>
- **Hung 2023 (VMP-G instrument, n = 207 preoperative and postoperative respondents).** Among postoperative respondents, 2.8% reported regret after GAS.<sup>[[16]](#ref16)</sup>

## Distinction from Related Procedures

| Procedure | Structures removed | Structures created | Dilation required | Voiding position |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Genital nullification** | Penis, testes, scrotum | Perineal urethrostomy only | No | Seated |
| [Penile inversion vaginoplasty](/docs/surgical-techniques/04k-gender-affirming-surgery/penile-inversion-vaginoplasty) | Penis, testes | Neovagina, neoclitoris, labia | Yes (lifelong) | Seated |
| [Zero-depth vulvoplasty](/docs/surgical-techniques/04k-gender-affirming-surgery/zero-depth-vulvoplasty) | Penis, testes | Vulvar structures (labia, hood, neoclitoris) — no canal | No | Seated |
| [Simple orchiectomy](/docs/surgical-techniques/04k-gender-affirming-surgery/simple-orchiectomy) | Testes only | None | No | Standing |
| Orchiectomy + scrotectomy (phallus preserved) | Testes; scrotal skin | None | No | Not reported in an eight-patient series<sup>[[51]](#ref51)</sup> |

One of eight patients required revision for wound dehiscence; the cohort's median follow-up was 97 days. Later vulvoplasty or vaginoplasty may be more difficult because less scrotal skin is available.<sup>[[51]](#ref51)</sup>

Nullification is the only procedure in this table with no constructed genital structures. The Claeys 2025 systematic review of variant GGAS (23 case series, all at high risk of bias) reports that patients mainly choose variant procedures out of personal desire, to avoid complication risk (for example lifelong dilation or urethral complications), or because they do not have dysphoria about certain parts of their genitalia.<sup>[[41]](#ref41)</sup>

## Lifelong Postoperative Care

After nullification:<sup>[[18]](#ref18)</sup>

### Hormone replacement after bilateral orchiectomy

Without sex-hormone replacement after orchiectomy, patients are at risk for vasomotor symptoms and bone loss, including osteoporosis.<sup>[[4]](#ref4)[[18]](#ref18)[[36]](#ref36)[[42]](#ref42)</sup>

- **Estrogen.** After orchiectomy, spironolactone used solely for testicular testosterone suppression can usually be stopped. Gooren 2011 described approximately 50 μg/day of transdermal estradiol as a usual post-gonadectomy dose and normal LH as a dosing marker in that review; current replacement is individualized to treatment goals and monitored with serum estradiol and testosterone under the 2017 Endocrine Society guideline.<sup>[[36]](#ref36)[[42]](#ref42)</sup>
- **Bone health.** In the Singh-Ospina 2017 meta-analysis of mostly observational studies, feminizing hormone therapy was associated with an increase in lumbar-spine BMD at 12 months (+0.04 g/cm²) and 24 months (+0.06 g/cm²); the effect on fracture risk is uncertain. In a cross-sectional study of 25 hormone-naive transfeminine adults (median age 30 years), 4/25 (16%) had baseline T-scores &lt; −2.5 before hormone therapy.<sup>[[49]](#ref49)</sup><sup>[[37]](#ref37)[[4]](#ref4)[[38]](#ref38)[[42]](#ref42)</sup>
- **DEXA screening.** The 2023 ISCD positions indicate baseline DXA after gonadectomy or gonadal suppression before hormone therapy, in hypogonadism when hormone therapy is not planned, and for standard indications such as fragility fracture. Planned sex-steroid discontinuation is also a follow-up DXA indication; age 40 is not a prerequisite.<sup>[[50]](#ref50)</sup> Appropriate sex reference for FRAX assessment remains debated; some recommend assessing risk with both male and female calculators and using an intermediate value.<sup>[[4]](#ref4)</sup> The Endocrine Society 2017 guideline recommends bone mineral density measurement when risk factors exist, specifically in those who stop hormone therapy after gonadectomy (recommendation 4.4, Strong), and suggests screening at age 60 in low-risk transgender females.<sup>[[42]](#ref42)</sup>
- **Calcium and vitamin D.** Adequate intake recommended for all patients post-gonadectomy.<sup>[[36]](#ref36)[[38]](#ref38)</sup>
- Patients who discontinue sex-hormone therapy after gonadectomy are at risk for bone loss and vasomotor symptoms; assess bone-health risk and the need for BMD testing.<sup>[[4]](#ref4)[[36]](#ref36)[[38]](#ref38)[[42]](#ref42)</sup>

### Prostate screening

The prostate remains in situ, and examination is anatomically distinct from examination after vaginoplasty.<sup>[[33]](#ref33)[[18]](#ref18)</sup> The Endocrine Society suggests individualized prostate-cancer screening according to personal risk; PSA and examination decisions should account for estrogen exposure and the absence of a neovagina.<sup>[[42]](#ref42)</sup> In a 2025 systematic review of four studies (290 participants), feminizing hormone therapy was associated with lower PSA values, but evidence was insufficient to establish a clinical PSA threshold; an orchiectomy-specific effect could not be isolated.<sup>[[48]](#ref48)</sup>

### Breast-cancer screening

Breast imaging depends on age, duration of estrogen exposure and individual risk.<sup>[[33]](#ref33)[[4]](#ref4)</sup> For average-risk transfeminine adults aged 40 years or older with at least 5 years of hormone use, the ACR rates mammography or tomosynthesis as "May Be Appropriate"; estrogen use alone does not set a screening schedule.<sup>[[45]](#ref45)</sup> For higher-than-average-risk adults aged 25–30 years or older with at least 5 years of hormone use, the ACR rates either test "Usually Appropriate."<sup>[[45]](#ref45)</sup>

### Other surveillance

- **PU surveillance.** Assess voiding symptoms and stomal caliber during postoperative healing, then individualize later follow-up<sup>[[10]](#ref10)</sup>
- **UTI assessment.** Evaluate symptomatic infection using usual clinical criteria.<sup>[[18]](#ref18)</sup> In one retrospective complex-stricture cohort, the study-defined recurrent-UTI endpoint (at least three UTIs/year, at least two in six months, or prophylactic UTI treatment) occurred in 14/67 PU patients and 14/66 urethroplasty patients (21% each; median follow-up 32 and 76 months); applicability to nullification is uncertain.<sup>[[44]](#ref44)</sup>
- **Urologic follow-up.** Assess the PU for stomal narrowing and new obstructive voiding symptoms, particularly during the first 18 months after surgery.<sup>[[10]](#ref10)</sup> The AAFP review recommends lifelong specialist follow-up after masculinizing genital surgery; that schedule is extrapolated for AMAB nullification.<sup>[[18]](#ref18)</sup>
- **Psychosocial support.** Offer support during adjustment. In a prospective vaginoplasty cohort of 48 participants, pre-existing mental-health diagnoses were associated with worse postoperative PROs; applicability to nullification is unknown.<sup>[[19]](#ref19)</sup>

## Access and Ethical Considerations

Only a small number of surgeons currently offer genital nullification, which falls outside the standard binary surgical menu. Ascha 2024 reported 16 patients undergoing individually customized procedures across all techniques at their institution.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup>

In Pletta 2025’s cross-sectional primary-care cohort, prior GAS was reported by 24/135 (17.8%) nonbinary AMAB patients, the lowest of four groups, versus 511/876 (58.3%) transgender men.<sup>[[17]](#ref17)</sup> The disparity may reflect both limited surgical availability and insurance barriers for nonbinary-specific procedures (many policies are structured around binary surgical pathways).<sup>[[20]](#ref20)[[1]](#ref1)</sup>

---

## Evidence Limitations

- Ascha 2024 (n = 16) is the main GAS-specific reference, but the cohort spans all customized procedures, not nullification specifically, and formal subgroup outcomes are not reported.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup>
- PU outcomes data are largely extrapolated from cisgender reconstructive-urology and penile-cancer cohorts (Joshi-Morey, Myers, de Vries, Klemm, Shinchi); stenosis risk in younger gender-diverse AMAB patients without radiation exposure has not been quantified.<sup>[[7]](#ref7)[[8]](#ref8)[[10]](#ref10)[[11]](#ref11)[[13]](#ref13)</sup>
- No nullification-specific PROMs have been validated in the nonbinary population.
- The Pezzoli 2026 Delphi consensus addresses feminizing genital surgery, not nullification specifically. Its perioperative recommendations are largely transferable but not formally validated for this indication.<sup>[[5]](#ref5)</sup>
