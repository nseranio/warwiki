# Genital Nullification — AMAB (Penectomy + Bilateral Orchiectomy + Scrotectomy + Perineal Urethrostomy)

**Genital nullification** (also termed nullo surgery or gender nullification) for AMAB individuals is the removal of penis, testes, and scrotum without creation of a neovagina, resulting in a smooth perineal contour with a perineal urethrostomy (PU) for urinary function.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup> It is an individually customized gender-affirming procedure that falls outside the binary surgical options (vaginoplasty or stand-alone orchiectomy). It is most commonly sought by nonbinary or gender-diverse individuals.

This is the dedicated atlas page. For the broader variant-GGAS framework and decision matrix, see [Non-Binary / Nullification Gender-Affirming Surgery](/docs/surgical-techniques/04k-gender-affirming-surgery/non-binary-nullification). For the perineal-urethrostomy component, see [Midline Perineal Urethrostomy](/docs/surgical-techniques/04a-urethral-reconstruction/seven-flap-perineal-urethrostomy) and [Blandy Perineal Urethrostomy](/docs/surgical-techniques/04a-urethral-reconstruction/blandy-perineal-urethrostomy). For the orchiectomy component, see [Bilateral Simple Orchiectomy](/docs/surgical-techniques/04k-gender-affirming-surgery/simple-orchiectomy). For the canal-creating alternative (external feminine appearance without vagina), see [Zero-Depth Vulvoplasty](/docs/surgical-techniques/04k-gender-affirming-surgery/zero-depth-vulvoplasty).

---

## Indication and Surgical Goal

The goal is absence of external genital structures with a smooth, aesthetically neutral perineum and seated-position voiding via a perineal urethrostomy.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup>

This is distinct from:

- Zero-depth or shallow-depth vulvoplasty, which creates external vulvar structures (labia, clitoral hood, neoclitoris) but no vaginal canal.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup>
- Full-depth vaginoplasty, which creates a neovaginal canal and vulvar structures.
- Stand-alone bilateral orchiectomy, in which the testes are removed but the penis and scrotum are retained.

Nullification specifically aims for the absence of any genital structures.

## Eligibility

WPATH SOC v8 (2022) recognizes that gender-affirming surgical goals are highly individual and that nonbinary individuals may seek procedures that do not conform to traditional binary options.<sup>[[2]](#ref2)[[3]](#ref3)</sup> General eligibility criteria for irreversible genital surgery, as summarized in a 2016 Lancet review that predates SOC v8, include:<sup>[[4]](#ref4)</sup>

- Persistent, well-documented gender dysphoria.
- Capacity for informed consent.
- Well-managed coexisting medical and mental-health conditions.
- Typically two independent mental-health-professional letters of recommendation.
- ≥ 12 mo hormone therapy generally recommended for gonadectomy, though individualized.<sup>[[4]](#ref4)[[3]](#ref3)</sup>

The Pezzoli 2026 Delphi consensus on feminizing genital surgery in AMAB individuals (27 urological surgeons; applicability to nullification is extrapolated) identified BMI &gt; 24.9 kg/m², diabetes, smoking and cardiovascular disease as significant risk factors for postoperative complications. Advanced age alone was not considered a contraindication. Major psychiatric disorders and spinal cord injury were relative contraindications, and Charlson Comorbidity Index ≥ 3 was a relative contraindication for vaginoplasty only.<sup>[[5]](#ref5)</sup>

### Preoperative optimisation

- **Smoking cessation.** Cessation at least 4 weeks before surgery is advised to reduce pulmonary and wound-healing complications (ACC 2018 expert consensus).<sup>[[21]](#ref21)</sup>
- **Genital hair removal.** The Delphi consensus recommends it.<sup>[[5]](#ref5)</sup>
- **Glycemic optimization.** HbA1c screening and optimization in patients with diabetes or metabolic syndrome. In a general-surgery NSQIP cohort (n = 282,131), higher HbA1c was associated with more 30-day complications (Schaschinger 2026, *JAMA Surg*).<sup>[[22]](#ref22)</sup>
- **Fertility counseling.** Sperm cryopreservation is discussed before surgery because bilateral orchiectomy is irreversible.<sup>[[4]](#ref4)</sup>

## Surgical Technique — Step by Step

The steps below follow Ascha 2024 (a 16-patient series of individually customized genital procedures, the main GAS-specific reference for this operation):<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup>

### 1. Bilateral orchiectomy

Rahman and Ferrando 2026 describe gender-affirming orchiectomy as similar to vaginal gynecologic procedures and suggest that urogynecologists can incorporate it with minimal additional training (a technique video from a senior author with over 100 cases).<sup>[[23]](#ref23)</sup> Steps: midline raphe or bilateral scrotal incision; tunica vaginalis opened; testis delivered; spermatic-cord structures (vas deferens, testicular artery, pampiniform plexus, cremasteric vessels) individually identified, clamped, ligated, divided; gubernaculum divided; hemostasis confirmed; layered closure. Cross-link to [Bilateral Simple Orchiectomy](/docs/surgical-techniques/04k-gender-affirming-surgery/simple-orchiectomy) for full technique.

**Saltman 2023 NSQIP (n = 246 transgender orchiectomies).** The overall complication rate was 3.7%, similar to cisgender orchiectomy for testicular torsion (4.4%) or pain (5.9%). The authors concluded that stand-alone orchiectomy can be performed safely as an outpatient procedure.<sup>[[6]](#ref6)</sup>

### 2. Penectomy

Total penectomy removes the entire penile shaft (glans, corpora cavernosa, corpus spongiosum). The technique is adapted from the oncologic-penectomy literature:<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[9]](#ref9)</sup>

- **Circumferential skin incision** at the base of the penis.
- **Dorsal neurovascular bundle ligated.** The dorsal arteries, deep dorsal vein and dorsal nerves are ligated. Breza 1989 mapped the detailed anatomy, in which the cavernous nerves are grossly identifiable structures traceable from the penile hilum to the prostate. Preservation is unnecessary in nullification because the goal is complete removal, unlike feminizing-vaginoplasty disassembly (no clitoroplasty target).<sup>[[25]](#ref25)</sup>
- The corpora cavernosa are dissected from their pubic-rami attachments (crura). In the Samm and Steiner technique, a noncrushing vascular clamp is placed across the corporal bodies before transection to reduce blood loss.<sup>[[26]](#ref26)</sup>
- **Urethra** carefully dissected free from the corpus spongiosum and preserved to the length needed for PU.
- **Suspensory ligament** divided.
- Corporal stumps oversewn with absorbable suture for hemostasis.

**Penectomy-specific outcomes.** In a retrospective NSQIP penectomy cohort for penile cancer (Velazquez 2019, n = 304), the overall complication rate was 19.7%, with UTI in 3.0%, superficial SSI in 3.0% and bleeding requiring transfusion in 3.9%.<sup>[[27]](#ref27)</sup> Patients with penile cancer are older and have cancer-related comorbidities, and complication rates in younger, healthier gender-affirming patients may be lower. In a retrospective series of penile amputation with PU for invasive penile cancer (Falcone 2023, n = 10, median follow-up 16 months), operative time was 195 min (IQR 155–275), hospital stay 8 d (IQR 6–10) and postoperative complications 20% (1 surgical revision). Median IPSS improved from 15 to 6.<sup>[[9]](#ref9)</sup>

### 3. Scrotectomy

Scrotectomy is excision of scrotal skin and dartos fascia after orchiectomy. In nullification the goal is complete removal of scrotal tissue to achieve a smooth perineal contour.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[24]](#ref24)</sup>

- Redundant scrotal skin excised; only enough preserved for tension-free closure.
- **Perineal body** carefully preserved to maintain pelvic-floor integrity.
- **For primary closure** (typical in nullification given smaller defects), redundant skin is approximated under appropriate tension.
- **For larger defects.** Fasciocutaneous flaps from the thigh or abdominal wall are used. In a retrospective series of 20 patients with large post-scrotectomy wounds (median area 443 cm²), Staniorski 2023 reported closure in all patients, 3 of whom needed split-thickness skin grafting, and complications in 15% (1 wound infection, 2 bleeding episodes).<sup>[[28]](#ref28)</sup>
- The oncologic-emasculation literature notes that a retained scrotum can hang in front of the neourethrostomy and become wet during micturition, causing urinary dermatitis; concurrent scrotectomy avoids this.<sup>[[24]](#ref24)</sup>

### 4. Perineal urethrostomy creation

The bulbar urethra is mobilized and brought to the perineum; meatus spatulated and matured to the perineal skin to create a widely patent stoma. Preservation of the dorsal urethral plate and longitudinal blood supply is needed to minimize stenosis risk:<sup>[[7]](#ref7)[[8]](#ref8)</sup>

- **Myers 2011 dorsal-plate-preservation technique.** Primary success was 83% and secondary success 93% (n = 40, median follow-up 31 months).<sup>[[8]](#ref8)</sup>
- **Joshi 2024 algorithmic midline approach.** Success (freedom from re-intervention) was 95.1%, with 4.9% requiring re-intervention (n = 103).<sup>[[7]](#ref7)</sup>

Technique selection follows the same algorithmic framework as cisgender PU; see the canonical [Midline Perineal Urethrostomy](/docs/surgical-techniques/04a-urethral-reconstruction/seven-flap-perineal-urethrostomy) (modern algorithmic midline approach; French, Hudak, and Morey 2011) and [Blandy Perineal Urethrostomy](/docs/surgical-techniques/04a-urethral-reconstruction/blandy-perineal-urethrostomy) (classical inverted-U flap; Blandy 1968) pages for technique detail.

### 5. Closure

Remaining skin edges approximated to create a smooth, flat perineal contour. The goal is an aesthetically neutral genital area.

## Perioperative Management

Per the Pezzoli 2026 Delphi consensus on feminizing genital surgery in AMAB individuals (largely applicable to nullification):<sup>[[5]](#ref5)</sup>

- **Preoperative.** Genital hair removal is recommended, and structured counseling covers wound care, voiding changes and irreversibility.
- **Catheterization.** The Delphi consensus (feminizing GAS) advises a urethral catheter for 3–5 d. In the Falcone penile-cancer PU series, median catheterization time was 15 d (IQR 15–32).<sup>[[5]](#ref5)[[9]](#ref9)</sup>
- **Hospital stay.** Stand-alone orchiectomy is an outpatient procedure. Combined nullification may require a short inpatient stay (median 8 d in the Falcone penectomy and PU series).<sup>[[6]](#ref6)[[9]](#ref9)</sup>
- **Pelvic-floor PT.** The Delphi consensus recommends it before and after surgery.<sup>[[5]](#ref5)</sup>
- **No dilation.** Unlike vaginoplasty, nullification creates no neovaginal canal and requires no lifelong dilation.

### Perioperative hormone-therapy management

The Delphi consensus did not reach agreement on the timing of estrogen interruption or re-initiation.<sup>[[5]](#ref5)</sup> Retrospective and survey data indicate that many patients continue estrogen perioperatively:<sup>[[29]](#ref29)[[30]](#ref30)[[31]](#ref31)</sup>

- **Herndon 2024.** In a retrospective cohort of 183 transfeminine individuals managed with an individualized algorithm, 75.4% continued estradiol perioperatively. One VTE occurred (in the continuation group), and postoperative complications did not differ between groups. The 25% who held estradiol for 2–6 weeks were older and had more cardiometabolic comorbidity and higher Caprini scores.<sup>[[30]](#ref30)</sup>
- Continuing estrogen perioperatively avoids withdrawal symptoms such as dysphoria and vasomotor symptoms; a summary of the WPATH guidance addresses this.<sup>[[3]](#ref3)</sup>
- **Elevated VTE risk** (older age, cardiometabolic comorbidities, higher Caprini score): individualized assessment is used, and temporary discontinuation may be appropriate.<sup>[[30]](#ref30)[[32]](#ref32)</sup>
- Spironolactone can be discontinued after orchiectomy; the endogenous testosterone source has been removed.<sup>[[33]](#ref33)</sup>
- In a Hung 2024 survey of WPATH surgeons (n = 51, 34% response), most did not stop hormone therapy before GAS and provided DVT prophylaxis to all patients for less than 1 week postoperatively.<sup>[[34]](#ref34)</sup>

### Thromboprophylaxis

The Delphi consensus recommends enoxaparin prophylaxis.<sup>[[5]](#ref5)</sup> Transfeminine patients on estrogen are at higher VTE risk than the general population, but absolute risk remains low.<sup>[[32]](#ref32)[[35]](#ref35)</sup> See Maharaj 2025 for an illustrative case study of postoperative-VTE prophylaxis on estrogen.<sup>[[29]](#ref29)</sup>

## Complications

Component-procedure complication data:

### Orchiectomy (Saltman NSQIP n = 246 transgender)<sup>[[6]](#ref6)</sup>

- The overall complication rate was 3.7%, not significantly different from cisgender orchiectomy for non-oncologic indications.
- Hematoma, wound infection, rare DVT.

### Perineal urethrostomy (aggregated)<sup>[[10]](#ref10)[[11]](#ref11)[[7]](#ref7)[[8]](#ref8)</sup>

| Complication | Incidence | Management |
|---|---|---|
| Stomal stenosis | 5–18% | Dilation or surgical revision |
| Wound infection | ~11% | Antibiotics, wound care |
| Wound dehiscence | ~4% | Conservative or surgical |
| Urinary spraying / misdirection | Variable | Positional adjustment, revision |
| Urinary incontinence | Rare (median ICIQ 0) | Pelvic-floor PT |

In the largest multicenter series (de Vries 2021, retrospective, penile cancer, n = 299, median follow-up 19 months), stenosis occurred in 12%, 74% of stenoses needed surgical revision, and median time to revision was 6.1 months. Only two stenoses were seen after 2 years of follow-up.<sup>[[10]](#ref10)</sup> Prior radiation therapy was the strongest stenosis risk factor in Myers 2011 (adjusted OR 11.2).<sup>[[8]](#ref8)</sup> Stenosis risk may be lower in the gender-affirming population, which is typically younger and without radiation history.

## Urinary Function After PU

Long-term urological outcomes come from cisgender PU series:<sup>[[11]](#ref11)[[12]](#ref12)[[7]](#ref7)[[13]](#ref13)</sup>

- **Voiding position.** All patients void seated. In the Joshi series, 76% of respondents were unbothered by the change and 82% reported improvement in overall health.<sup>[[7]](#ref7)</sup>
- **Voiding function.** LUTS improved significantly. In Shinchi 2021 (n = 56, retrospective), Qmax rose from 3.8 to 17.6 mL/s and PVR fell from 77.6 to 21.3 mL.<sup>[[13]](#ref13)</sup>
- **Continence.** Continence is generally preserved. Klemm 2024 (n = 76) reported a median ICIQ-UI of 0 (range 0–21) at a median follow-up of 55 months.<sup>[[11]](#ref11)</sup>
- **Retreatment-free survival.** Reported rates are 84–95% depending on technique and population.<sup>[[11]](#ref11)[[7]](#ref7)</sup>
- **Patient satisfaction.** In Joshi 2024 and Shinchi 2021, 86% and 84.6% of respondents were satisfied or very satisfied, and Klemm 2024 reported very high satisfaction.<sup>[[11]](#ref11)[[7]](#ref7)[[13]](#ref13)</sup>

## Psychological and Quality-of-Life Outcomes

Nullification-specific PRO data are limited. The broader GAS literature includes:

- **Almazan and Keuroghlian, 2015 US Transgender Survey (n = 27,715).** Having had at least one gender-affirming surgery at least 2 years earlier, compared with wanting but not having surgery, was associated with lower severe psychological distress (aOR 0.58, 95% CI 0.50–0.67), lower past-year suicidal ideation (aOR 0.56, 95% CI 0.50–0.64), lower smoking (aOR 0.65, 95% CI 0.57–0.75). Patients who underwent all desired surgeries had significantly lower odds of all adverse mental-health outcomes.<sup>[[14]](#ref14)</sup>
- **Yeo 2026 systematic review of longitudinal cohorts (14 studies, n = 3,023).** Psychological well-being and QoL improved initially within the first year after GAS, followed by a plateau or decline. Younger age, higher education and a supportive social environment were associated with positive outcomes, and higher pre-GAS psychopathology with poorer outcomes.<sup>[[39]](#ref39)</sup>
- **Cooney 2025 systematic review.** Among the surgical studies, all 4 QoL studies showed statistically significant improvement and all 5 gender-dysphoria studies showed improved gender congruence. Certainty across the review was moderate to very low.<sup>[[15]](#ref15)</sup>
- **Pletta 2025 (cross-sectional, n = 2,176).** Reproductive surgeries (including gonadectomy) were rated most satisfactory across all gender-identity groups (mean 4.6/5).<sup>[[17]](#ref17)</sup>
- **Ren 2024 systematic review (24 studies, n = 3,662).** Pooled regret prevalence was 1.94% (transfeminine 4.0%, transmasculine 0.8%).<sup>[[40]](#ref40)</sup>
- **Hung 2023 (VMP-G instrument, n = 207).** The regret rate across GAS was 2.8%.<sup>[[16]](#ref16)</sup>

## Distinction from Related Procedures

| Procedure | Structures removed | Structures created | Dilation required | Voiding position |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Genital nullification** | Penis, testes, scrotum | Perineal urethrostomy only | No | Seated |
| [Penile inversion vaginoplasty](/docs/surgical-techniques/04k-gender-affirming-surgery/penile-inversion-vaginoplasty) | Penis, testes | Neovagina, neoclitoris, labia | Yes (lifelong) | Seated |
| [Zero-depth vulvoplasty](/docs/surgical-techniques/04k-gender-affirming-surgery/zero-depth-vulvoplasty) | Penis, testes | Vulvar structures (labia, hood, neoclitoris) — no canal | No | Seated |
| [Simple orchiectomy](/docs/surgical-techniques/04k-gender-affirming-surgery/simple-orchiectomy) | Testes only | None | No | Standing |

Nullification is the only procedure in this table with no constructed genital structures. The Claeys 2025 systematic review of variant GGAS (23 case series, all at high risk of bias) reports that patients mainly choose variant procedures out of personal desire, to avoid complication risk (for example lifelong dilation or urethral complications), or because they do not have dysphoria about certain parts of their genitalia.<sup>[[41]](#ref41)</sup>

## Lifelong Postoperative Care

After nullification:<sup>[[18]](#ref18)</sup>

### Hormone replacement therapy (mandatory after BSO)

Without sex-hormone replacement, patients are at risk for osteoporosis, cardiovascular disease, metabolic syndrome and cognitive changes.<sup>[[4]](#ref4)[[18]](#ref18)[[36]](#ref36)</sup>

- **Estrogen.** For those on feminizing therapy, antiandrogens (spironolactone) can be discontinued post-orchiectomy. Estradiol dosing typically reduced to about 50 μg/d transdermal or equivalent; serum LH in the normal range is a reliable marker of adequate dosing.<sup>[[36]](#ref36)</sup>
- **Bone health.** In the Singh-Ospina 2017 meta-analysis of mostly observational studies, feminizing hormone therapy was associated with an increase in lumbar-spine BMD at 12 months (+0.04 g/cm²) and 24 months (+0.06 g/cm²); the effect on fracture risk is uncertain. Up to 16% of transgender females have T-scores &lt; −1 before starting HRT (baseline osteopenia).<sup>[[37]](#ref37)[[4]](#ref4)[[38]](#ref38)</sup>
- **DEXA screening.** Considered after age 40 if risk factors are present, or with personal fracture history. Appropriate sex reference for FRAX assessment remains debated; some recommend assessing risk with both male and female calculators and using an intermediate value.<sup>[[4]](#ref4)</sup>
- **Calcium and vitamin D.** Adequate intake recommended for all patients post-gonadectomy.<sup>[[36]](#ref36)[[38]](#ref38)</sup>
- Patients who discontinue HRT after gonadectomy are at significant risk for bone loss, vasomotor symptoms, CV disease, and metabolic syndrome.<sup>[[4]](#ref4)[[38]](#ref38)</sup>

### Prostate screening

Prostate is retained; screening follows standard age and risk guidelines. Digital rectal examination remains the standard approach (unlike post-vaginoplasty where vaginal palpation is used).<sup>[[33]](#ref33)[[18]](#ref18)</sup>

### Breast-cancer screening

Should follow guidelines for those designated female at birth if the patient has been on estrogen therapy.<sup>[[33]](#ref33)[[4]](#ref4)</sup>

### Other surveillance

- **PU surveillance.** Regular follow-up first 1–2 years for stenosis; most present within 18 mo. Annual or as-needed thereafter.<sup>[[10]](#ref10)</sup>
- **UTI surveillance.** Shortened urethra may increase susceptibility; standard treatment guidelines apply.<sup>[[18]](#ref18)</sup>
- **Lifelong urologic follow-up with a urologist familiar with GAS.** AAFP recommendation after masculinizing genital surgery, applicable equally to nullification.<sup>[[18]](#ref18)</sup>
- **Psychosocial support.** Particularly during adjustment; pre-existing mental-health diagnoses may predict worse PROs.<sup>[[19]](#ref19)</sup>

## Access and Ethical Considerations

Only a small number of surgeons currently offer genital nullification, which falls outside the standard binary surgical menu. Ascha 2024 reported 16 patients undergoing individually customized procedures across all techniques at their institution.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup>

Nonbinary AMAB individuals have the lowest rate of prior GAS of any gender-identity group; only 17.8% had received any form of GAS in Pletta 2025, versus 58.3% of transgender men.<sup>[[17]](#ref17)</sup> The disparity may reflect both limited surgical availability and insurance barriers for nonbinary-specific procedures (many policies are structured around binary surgical pathways).<sup>[[20]](#ref20)[[1]](#ref1)</sup>

---

## Evidence Limitations

- Ascha 2024 (n = 16) is the main GAS-specific reference, but the cohort spans all customized procedures, not nullification specifically, and formal subgroup outcomes are not reported.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup>
- PU outcomes data are largely extrapolated from cisgender reconstructive-urology and penile-cancer cohorts (Joshi-Morey, Myers, de Vries, Klemm, Shinchi); younger transmasculine or nonbinary patients without radiation history may have lower stenosis risk.<sup>[[7]](#ref7)[[8]](#ref8)[[10]](#ref10)[[11]](#ref11)[[13]](#ref13)</sup>
- No nullification-specific PROMs have been validated in the nonbinary population.
- The Pezzoli 2026 Delphi consensus addresses feminizing genital surgery, not nullification specifically. Its perioperative recommendations are largely transferable but not formally validated for this indication.<sup>[[5]](#ref5)</sup>
