# Neovaginal Granulation-Tissue Debridement

**Neovaginal granulation tissue** is one of the most common postoperative complications of penile inversion vaginoplasty, with a reported incidence of 2–26% depending on series and definition.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[2]](#ref2)[[3]](#ref3)</sup> It is a minor complication (Clavien–Dindo I–II). It is clinically meaningful because it is associated with revision surgery, can cause bleeding and dilation pain, and can progress to introital stenosis. Office serial silver-nitrate cauterization is the usual first-line treatment. In the Gharavi 2026 retrospective matched cohort (n = 32), adding betamethasone suppositories was associated with more resolution (p = 0.035, excluding patients who needed cauterization under anesthesia) and fewer median treatments.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup> Refractory cases escalate to cauterization under anesthesia, intralesional steroids, pulsed-dye laser or surgical excision.

This is the dedicated management-pathway page. For the broader postoperative complication framework, see [Neovaginal Stenosis Management](/docs/surgical-techniques/04k-gender-affirming-surgery/neovaginal-stenosis-management) (granulation tissue is the central precursor to stenosis). For the host operations, see [Penile Inversion Vaginoplasty](/docs/surgical-techniques/04k-gender-affirming-surgery/penile-inversion-vaginoplasty), [Peritoneal Pull-Through Vaginoplasty](/docs/surgical-techniques/04k-gender-affirming-surgery/peritoneal-pull-through-vaginoplasty), and [Intestinal Vaginoplasty](/docs/surgical-techniques/04k-gender-affirming-surgery/intestinal-vaginoplasty).

## Definition and Pathophysiology

**Hypergranulation tissue (HGT)**, also termed excess granulation tissue, proud flesh, or hypertrophic granulation, is abnormal granulation tissue raised above the level of surrounding intact skin or epithelium that impedes re-epithelialization.<sup>[[5]](#ref5)</sup> In the neovagina it represents a dysregulated proliferative phase of wound healing within the canal, at suture lines or at the introitus.

Normal granulation begins about 4 d after injury: macrophages, fibroblasts, and new capillaries invade the wound space; macrophages provide growth factors (PDGF, TGF-β1) that drive fibroplasia and angiogenesis; fibroblasts produce ECM scaffold, yielding the characteristic red, granular, vascular tissue.<sup>[[6]](#ref6)</sup> Hypergranulation occurs when this process becomes dysregulated with excessive PDGF and TGF-β activity, leading to overproduction that protrudes above the wound surface and prevents epithelial migration across the wound.<sup>[[7]](#ref7)[[5]](#ref5)</sup>

Neovaginal-context drivers:<sup>[[8]](#ref8)[[9]](#ref9)[[10]](#ref10)</sup>

- **Chronic mechanical irritation** from repeated dilation.
- **Moisture and occlusion.** Warm, moist, semi-occluded canal.
- **Suture material reaction** at skin-graft / native-tissue junctions.
- **Wound colonization or infection.** Wound infection associated with all tissue-healing complications (Dreher meta-analysis).<sup>[[10]](#ref10)</sup>
- **Skin-graft–recipient-bed interface.** Particularly at areas of incomplete graft take.
- **Wound separation / dehiscence** healing by secondary intention.

## Clinical Presentation and Diagnosis

### Symptoms<sup>[[12]](#ref12)[[8]](#ref8)[[15]](#ref15)[[16]](#ref16)</sup>

- **Vaginal bleeding.** Most common presentation; spotting or blood-tinged discharge after dilation or intercourse. ACOG Committee Opinion 823 notes patients may present with "bleeding or discharge consistent with granulation tissue".<sup>[[16]](#ref16)</sup>
- Blood-tinged discharge.
- Pain or discomfort with dilation: friable tissue bleeds on contact.
- Visible red, raised, friable tissue on speculum exam.
- Difficulty with dilation: physical obstruction, particularly if circumferential or introital.
- Malodor: if secondarily infected.

### Locations<sup>[[8]](#ref8)[[9]](#ref9)[[17]](#ref17)</sup>

- **Skin-graft junctions.** Penile-flap or scrotal-graft interfaces, or graft-to-peritoneal-flap interfaces.
- **Introitus.** Mucocutaneous junction (particularly common after intestinal vaginoplasty).
- **Neovaginal apex.** Graft-to-peritoneum or blind-end junctions.
- Suture lines.
- Areas of partial graft loss healing by secondary intention.

### Diagnosis

Clinical: direct visualization during speculum exam (Pederson speculum or anoscope).<sup>[[12]](#ref12)[[16]](#ref16)</sup> The Gharavi 2026 study classified hypergranulation as mild, moderate, or severe; no standardized grading system exists.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup>

**Differential:**

- **Intravaginal hair growth** (incomplete preoperative electrolysis)
- **Wound dehiscence** (partial graft / flap separation)
- **Neovaginal stenosis** (may coexist)
- **Infection / abscess**
- **Neoplasia.** Extremely rare; biopsy if atypical or refractory
- **Suture granuloma.** Foreign-body reaction; may require suture removal

## Clinical Significance — Not Just Cosmetic

Granulation tissue is a marker of impaired wound healing with downstream consequences.

- **Association with revision surgery.** In Boas 2019 (n = 117, retrospective), patients who needed revision labiaplasty or clitoroplasty were more likely to have had granulation tissue (p = 0.006) and intravaginal scarring (p &lt; 0.001).<sup>[[14]](#ref14)</sup>
- **Association with labial fat grafting.** In Patel and Morrison 2021, 6 of 182 patients underwent fat grafting; prolonged granulation &gt; 6 wk was present in 83% of them versus 32% of the others (p = 0.01).<sup>[[13]](#ref13)</sup>
- **Progression to introital stenosis.** Recurrent granulation can drive a cycle of granulation, cautery, scarring, and re-granulation; one Gupta case report described complete introital obliteration despite 2 surgical revisions, ultimately requiring UBM biological-graft reconstruction.<sup>[[17]](#ref17)</sup>
- **Dilation-compliance disruption.** Granulation causes pain and bleeding during dilation, leading to reduced dilation, stenosis, and more granulation (the vicious cycle).<sup>[[12]](#ref12)[[8]](#ref8)</sup>

## Stepwise Management Algorithm

### Step 1 — Silver-nitrate cauterisation (first-line)

Silver nitrate is the usual first-line modality.<sup>[[16]](#ref16)[[1]](#ref1)[[5]](#ref5)</sup>

- Speculum (Pederson / anoscope) access.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup>
- Silver-nitrate stick (75% AgNO₃ / 25% KNO₃) applied directly; chemical coagulation produces a gray-white eschar.
- Protect surrounding normal tissue (petroleum jelly on perimeter).
- Office setting; topical lidocaine for comfort.
- **Multiple treatments are required.** In Gharavi 2026, the median was 3.5 treatments for moderate and 5 for severe HGT.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup>

**Outcomes alone:**<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup>

- Moderate HGT resolution 60%.
- Severe HGT resolution 25%.
- Resolution rates were modest for moderate-to-severe disease, which is the rationale for adjunctive therapy.

### Step 2 — Silver nitrate + betamethasone suppositories (enhanced first-line)

**Gharavi 2026 retrospective matched cohort (n = 32):**<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup>

| Parameter | Silver nitrate alone (n = 16) | + Betamethasone (n = 16) | Significance |
|---|---|---|---|
| Moderate HGT resolution | 60% | 86% | Not reported |
| Severe HGT resolution | 25% | 50% | Not reported |
| Median treatments for moderate | 3.5 | 2 | Not reported |
| Median treatments for severe | 5 | 4 | Not reported |
| Overall resolution (excluding EUA cases) | Lower | Higher | p = 0.035 |
| Follow-up | 8.5 ± 5.5 mo | 7.8 ± 4.7 mo | NS |

Betamethasone suppositories are used between office silver-nitrate visits and deliver a topical corticosteroid that suppresses the inflammatory cascade driving HGT, complementing the chemical cautery. The cohort's authors conclude that this lessens treatment burden.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[5]](#ref5)</sup>

### Step 3 — Topical steroids (alternative or adjunct)

Evidence from general wound-healing literature:<sup>[[5]](#ref5)[[18]](#ref18)[[19]](#ref19)[[20]](#ref20)</sup>

- **Linneman 2022.** In an uncontrolled retrospective quality-improvement review of hypertrophic granulation wounds (18 treated with silver nitrate, 16 with 1% hydrocortisone cream), 1% hydrocortisone was associated with a greater reduction in wound length and width at 1 month (median 14 mm vs 5 mm, p &lt; 0.05).<sup>[[18]](#ref18)</sup>
- **Brødsgaard 2015.** In a randomized trial of umbilical granuloma in infants (n = 109), clobetasol propionate 0.05% cream applied at home had healing times and resolution rates identical to clinic silver nitrate.<sup>[[19]](#ref19)</sup>
- **Shoham 2025 burn-association survey.** 78.9% of European respondents routinely use topical steroids for hypergranulation (versus 33.3% North America); European respondents significantly more likely to consider them safe (100% versus 74.4%, p < .001).<sup>[[20]](#ref20)</sup>

**Options for the neovaginal context:**

- Betamethasone suppositories (Gharavi protocol)
- Clobetasol 0.05% cream: applied directly or on a dilator
- Triamcinolone acetonide cream: intermediate potency
- Hydrocortisone 1%: mild cases

### Step 4 — Intralesional corticosteroid injection

For refractory HGT:<sup>[[5]](#ref5)</sup>

- **Triamcinolone acetonide 10–40 mg/mL** into the base of granulation tissue.
- A dermatologic literature review identified 55 cases treated with intralesional steroids, with varying degrees of successful HGT reduction.<sup>[[5]](#ref5)</sup>
- Mechanism: direct suppression of fibroblast proliferation, collagen synthesis, angiogenesis.
- Office with local anesthesia; risk of local tissue atrophy with repeated injections.

### Step 5 — Cauterisation under anaesthesia (EUA)

For moderate-to-severe HGT refractory to office treatment:<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[8]](#ref8)</sup>

- OR with sedation or GA.
- Complete visualization with speculum / vaginoscope.
- **Electrocautery** (monopolar or bipolar) ablation: more thorough and controlled than silver nitrate.
- **Sharp debridement** with scissors or curette plus electrocautery to base.
- Concurrent assessment for other pathology (suture granuloma, retained suture, hair growth, stenosis).
- The Gharavi study excluded EUA-requiring patients from the primary analysis.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup>

### Step 6 — Surgical excision

For recurrent, extensive, or refractory HGT failing conservative measures:<sup>[[8]](#ref8)[[5]](#ref5)[[17]](#ref17)</sup>

- Formal excision with primary closure or tissue rearrangement.
- May combine with other revisions (labiaplasty, introitoplasty, stenosis management).
- **For introital HGT causing stenosis.** UBM biological-graft reconstruction described as a salvage technique after 2 failed surgical revisions (Gupta 2019 case report).<sup>[[17]](#ref17)</sup>

### Step 7 — Pulsed-dye laser (emerging / adjunctive)

For HGT refractory to silver nitrate plus steroids:<sup>[[5]](#ref5)[[21]](#ref21)</sup>

- A dermatologic literature review identified 13 cases of HGT treated with PDL.<sup>[[5]](#ref5)</sup>
- **Wang 2007 retrospective case review.** In 9 patients with hypergranulation after Mohs surgery (mostly scalp, forehead and temple), all improved after one treatment, and most achieved complete or near-complete re-epithelialization after 1–2 treatments. No local anesthesia was required and no postlaser complications were reported.<sup>[[21]](#ref21)</sup>
- Mechanism: selective photothermolysis of the vascular component (hemoglobin absorption).
- Settings: 595 nm, 7–10 mm spot, 4.0–8.0 J/cm², 0.45–1.5 ms pulse.<sup>[[21]](#ref21)</sup>
- Pulsed-dye laser has not been studied in the neovagina; the mechanism and the postsurgical-wound data are extrapolated.
- Accessibility and cost limit use.

## Comparison of Treatment Modalities

| Modality | Setting | Anesthesia | Efficacy | Best indication |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Silver nitrate alone | Office | Topical | 25–60% (moderate–severe) | First-line; mild–moderate<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup> |
| **Silver nitrate + betamethasone** | Office + home | Topical | **50–86%** | First-line enhanced — fewer treatments, better resolution<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup> |
| Topical steroids alone | Home | None | Comparable to AgNO₃ in non-neovaginal settings | Mild cases; home-based<sup>[[18]](#ref18)[[19]](#ref19)[[20]](#ref20)</sup> |
| Intralesional steroids | Office | Local | Varying success (55 cases in literature) | Refractory localised disease<sup>[[5]](#ref5)</sup> |
| EUA electrocautery | OR | Sedation / GA | High (thorough ablation) | Refractory to office; complete visualization needed<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup> |
| Surgical excision | OR | GA | High | Recurrent / extensive; revision setting<sup>[[8]](#ref8)[[17]](#ref17)</sup> |
| Pulsed-dye laser | Office | None | 9/9 improved (Mohs wounds, non-neovaginal) | Refractory; emerging<sup>[[21]](#ref21)</sup> |
| UBM biological graft | OR | GA | Case report | Salvage for introital obliteration after failed revisions<sup>[[17]](#ref17)</sup> |

## Special Considerations by Surgical Technique

- **PIV.** Granulation tissue is most commonly reported here, at skin-graft junctions and suture lines (Massie 26%; Gaither listed granulation among tracked complications).<sup>[[2]](#ref2)[[11]](#ref11)</sup>
- **Peritoneal-flap vaginoplasty.** Can occur at the peritoneal-flap or perineal-skin junction; the squamous-metaplasia transition zone may produce granulation during remodeling.<sup>[[8]](#ref8)</sup>
- **Sigmoid or intestinal vaginoplasty.** Granulation at the colovestibular anastomosis (sigmoid mucosa or perineal skin) is a recognized complication, and the Gupta case of complete introital obliteration originated here.<sup>[[17]](#ref17)</sup>
- **Vulvoplasty (zero-depth).** Granulation was the second most common complication (9.5%) in the Blickensderfer series, after UTI.<sup>[[3]](#ref3)</sup>

## Prevention

Strategies to reduce HGT formation at primary surgery:<sup>[[10]](#ref10)[[12]](#ref12)[[8]](#ref8)</sup>

- **Meticulous hemostasis.** Hematoma promotes granulation.
- **Tension-free closure.** Reduces dehiscence or secondary-intention healing.
- **Suture selection.** Rapidly absorbable sutures may reduce foreign-body reaction.
- **Preoperative electrolysis.** It prevents intravaginal hair, which can be a nidus for chronic inflammation or granulation.<sup>[[12]](#ref12)[[16]](#ref16)</sup>
- **Wound-infection prevention.** In the Dreher meta-analysis, wound infection was associated with an increased risk of all tissue-healing complications.<sup>[[10]](#ref10)</sup>
- **Gentle early dilation technique.** Avoid excessive trauma to healing surfaces.
- **Adequate graft fixation.** Complete graft take reduces secondary-intention healing.

## Surveillance and Patient Counselling

AAFP and ACOG recommendations:<sup>[[12]](#ref12)[[16]](#ref16)</sup>

- **Pelvic exam yearly or every other year** to monitor for granulation, stenosis, hair regrowth.
- **Speculum exam** with Pederson speculum or anoscope to visualise the entire canal.<sup>[[12]](#ref12)</sup>
- Counsel patients that granulation is a common, treatable, minor complication, and that bleeding, discharge, or dilation pain should be reported promptly.<sup>[[12]](#ref12)[[16]](#ref16)</sup>
- **Douching.** Regular douching with soapy water or dilute vinegar or betadine is advised for hygiene and to reduce chronic inflammation.<sup>[[12]](#ref12)</sup>

## Practical Algorithm

1. **Identify and grade** (mild / moderate / severe) on speculum exam.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup>
2. **Rule out concurrent pathology.** Suture granuloma (remove retained suture), infection (treat), hair (electrolysis), stenosis (address separately).
3. **Mild:** office silver nitrate every 2–4 weeks (interval based on practice; Gharavi did not study mild disease) ± betamethasone suppositories between visits.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[16]](#ref16)</sup>
4. **Moderate:** silver nitrate and betamethasone (median 2 treatments to resolution); if no improvement after 3–4, intralesional triamcinolone or escalate to EUA.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup>
5. **Severe:** silver nitrate and betamethasone (median 4 treatments); if refractory, EUA electrocautery ± sharp debridement.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup>
6. **Refractory or recurrent:** pulsed-dye laser, surgical excision, or biological-graft reconstruction if concurrent introital stenosis.<sup>[[5]](#ref5)[[17]](#ref17)[[21]](#ref21)</sup>
7. **Throughout.** Continue dilation as tolerated; optimize wound care (douching, hygiene); address modifiable factors (smoking, infection).

---

## Evidence Limitations

- No RCTs of any treatment modality for neovaginal granulation tissue have been identified.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup>
- Gharavi 2026 (n = 32) is the only comparative study specifically addressing HGT after PIV; all other evidence is extrapolated from general wound-healing, burn, and dermatologic literature.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup>
- No standardized grading system exists for neovaginal HGT severity.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup>
- No standardized treatment protocol exists, and practice varies by institution and surgeon.
- Pulsed-dye laser has not been studied specifically in the neovaginal context.<sup>[[21]](#ref21)</sup>
- The granulation–stenosis relationship is recognized but not well quantified; whether aggressive early HGT treatment prevents subsequent stenosis remains undefined.<sup>[[14]](#ref14)[[13]](#ref13)</sup>
- Long-term outcomes of HGT treatment are poorly characterized.
- Patient-reported outcomes (impact on dilation compliance, sexual function, QoL) are not well studied.
