# Testicular Implants — Gender-Affirming (Transmasculine Neoscrotal Augmentation)

**Testicular prosthesis placement** in transmasculine patients is performed as a staged, delayed component of masculinizing genital reconstruction, typically 6–12 months after scrotoplasty to allow tissue healing, neoscrotal expansion, and assessment of flap vascularity.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[2]](#ref2)[[3]](#ref3)</sup> Unlike penile prostheses, which enable penetrative intercourse, testicular implants serve a cosmetic and psychological function: scrotal fullness and male genital contour. In a retrospective Amsterdam series of 206 transgender men (Pigot 2019, mean follow-up 11.5 years), one or both prostheses were explanted in 20.8%; explantation occurred in 11.5% (6/52) with the Hoebeke scrotoplasty technique, now the most frequently performed.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup>

This page is the gender-affirming-specific atlas anchor. For the canonical cross-indication procedure hub (orchiectomy, congenital anorchia, torsion, pediatric, AMAB GA), see [Testicular Prosthesis](/docs/surgical-techniques/04e-genital-reconstruction/testicular-prosthesis) in the 04e genital-reconstruction section.

## Indication and Context

Testicular prostheses serve a cosmetic and psychological function in transmasculine individuals, providing scrotal fullness and natural male genital contour.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[4]](#ref4)</sup> Both metoidioplasty and phalloplasty procedures may involve scrotoplasty with staged placement of testicular implants.<sup>[[5]](#ref5)[[2]](#ref2)</sup> WPATH SOC and ACOG list testicular prosthesis implantation as a recognised masculinizing surgical procedure.<sup>[[2]](#ref2)</sup> In a cross-sectional survey of 2,176 transgender adults (Pletta 2025), reproductive surgeries were rated most satisfactory across gender-identity groups (mean 4.6/5) and genital surgeries lower (mean 4.0/5); testicular implants were not reported separately.<sup>[[6]](#ref6)</sup>

---

## Prerequisite: Scrotoplasty

A neoscrotum must first be constructed from native tissue. The labia majora are the embryologic homologs of the scrotum and serve as the ideal donor.<sup>[[7]](#ref7)[[3]](#ref3)</sup>

### Scrotoplasty techniques

- **Hoebeke / Ghent V-Y advancement with rotation.** This is the technique most frequently performed at the Amsterdam center. Superiorly based U-shaped labia-majora flaps are rotated 90° medially to bring the neoscrotum anterior to the legs. A pedicled horseshoe-shaped pubic flap and clitoral-hood tissue are incorporated, and pedicled labia-majora fat pads are relocated for bulk. Explantation in the Amsterdam series was 11.5% (6/52) with this technique.<sup>[[8]](#ref8)[[3]](#ref3)[[1]](#ref1)</sup>
- **Bilateral rotational advancement flaps.** Bilateral labia-majora elevation and rotation from posterior to anterior position. In a retrospective series of 147 scrotoplasties with phalloplasty (Miller 2021, mean follow-up 12.5 months), distal flap necrosis occurred in 4.1%, large perineoscrotal dehiscence in 4.7% and scrotal hematoma in 1.4%.<sup>[[7]](#ref7)</sup>
- **Tissue expanders.** Placed at initial scrotoplasty to stretch neoscrotal skin before definitive implant placement; they have been described as delayed implantation became more common.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[9]](#ref9)</sup>

---

## Timing of Implant Placement

ACOG Committee Opinion 823 describes placement approximately 6 months after scrotoplasty.<sup>[[2]](#ref2)</sup> The Amsterdam center has shifted from immediate placement at scrotal reconstruction to delayed implantation, which allows:<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup>

- Complete flap healing and maturation.
- Assessment of tissue adequacy and vascularity.
- Use of smaller initial implants with potential upsizing later.
- Reduced risk of infection and extrusion.

In staged phalloplasty, testicular implants are typically placed during the second or third stage, often concurrent with or prior to penile-prosthesis insertion (see [Penile Implant After Phalloplasty](/docs/surgical-techniques/04k-gender-affirming-surgery/penile-implant-after-phalloplasty)).<sup>[[5]](#ref5)</sup>

## Prosthesis Types

The devices are the same as those used in cisgender men after orchiectomy; no purpose-designed transmasculine implant exists. See the [04e canonical hub](/docs/surgical-techniques/04e-genital-reconstruction/testicular-prosthesis) for full materials history and device specifications.

| Type | Examples | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| **Silicone gel-filled** | Nagor, Sebbin, Polytech, Eurosilicone, Promedon | Nagor, Promedon and Sebbin devices were the most used in the Amsterdam series; multiple sizes<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup> |
| **Saline-filled** | Rigicon Testi10, Coloplast Torosa / Mentor | Saline-filled testicular prostheses are available in the US; volume adjustable at implantation; Testi10 survival from removal or revision was 99.8% at 54 months in a retrospective manufacturer-supported review of adults and adolescents (cisgender data)<sup>[[10]](#ref10)[[11]](#ref11)</sup> |
| **Silastic (custom)** | Silicone elastomer | Elliptical shapes; some Asian centres<sup>[[12]](#ref12)</sup> |

**Amsterdam transmasculine series (n = 206)** brands used: Nagor (n = 205), Promedon (n = 105), Sebbin (n = 44), Dow Corning (n = 22), Prometel (n = 22), Polytech (n = 10), Eurosilicone (n = 2).<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup>

**Size trend.** Earlier practice was to place large implants immediately at scrotoplasty; the Amsterdam series shows a trend toward smaller, lighter prostheses with delayed implantation.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup> In cisgender patients, the most common postoperative concerns involve positioning, size and weight.<sup>[[13]](#ref13)</sup>

## Surgical Technique

Differs from cisgender placement due to absence of native scrotal sac, dartos fascia, and tunica vaginalis:<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[13]](#ref13)[[7]](#ref7)</sup>

1. **Incision.** Typically scrotal or inguinal; small incision in neoscrotal skin.
2. **Pocket creation.** Blunt subcutaneous pocket sized to accommodate implant. A pocket that is too large leads to migration; one that is too small leads to pressure necrosis and extrusion.
3. **Implant placement.** Prosthesis inserted; bilateral implants placed through separate pockets.
4. **Closure.** Layered closure of neoscrotal tissue over implant.
5. **Fixation.** Some surgeons use suture fixation to prevent migration (no native gubernacular attachment exists in the neoscrotum).

## Outcomes — Amsterdam Largest Series (Pigot 2019, n = 206, 11.5-yr mean follow-up)<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup>

| Outcome | Rate |
|---|---|
| **Overall explantation** | **20.8%** (43/206) |
| **Explantation — modern Hoebeke technique** | **11.5%** (6/52) |
| Trend over time | Explantation rates **dropped over the last decade** with improved techniques and prosthesis selection |

### Complications

| Complication | Notes |
|---|---|
| Infection | Cause of explantation; smoking was a risk factor for infection and explantation |
| Extrusion | More common with larger / heavier implants and immediate placement |
| Discomfort | Size mismatch or malposition |
| Prosthesis leakage | Primarily saline-filled |
| Urethral problems | Related to neourethra proximity to implant pocket |

### Risk factors

- **Smoking.** A history of smoking was a risk factor for postoperative infection and explantation.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup>
- **Larger prosthesis size and immediate placement.** Earlier practice placed large prostheses immediately at scrotal reconstruction; the authors describe a trend toward smaller, lighter prostheses and delayed implantation.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup>

## Comparison vs Cisgender Testicular-Prosthesis Outcomes

The series below differ in design, outcome definition and follow-up, so the rates are not directly comparable. Explantation in transmasculine patients appears higher than revision in cisgender series, and reconstructed tissue rather than a native scrotal sac is a plausible contributor:

| Population | Adverse-event / explantation rate | Anchor |
|---|---|---|
| Cisgender saline-filled prosthesis (prospective, n = 149) | 19% adverse events (discomfort 3%, infection 1.3%, extrusion 2.6%, deflation 0.7%) | Turek 2004<sup>[[11]](#ref11)</sup> |
| Cisgender Rigicon Testi10 (retrospective, n = 427 adults and adolescents) | 99.8% Kaplan-Meier survival from removal or revision at 54 months | Atwater 2025<sup>[[10]](#ref10)</sup> |
| Cisgender satisfaction | 96.1% implant satisfaction ("too firm" 25.5%); 89.8% satisfied with appearance and 59.3% with feel in a separate testicular-cancer survey | Araújo 2024<sup>[[14]](#ref14)</sup>; Nichols 2019<sup>[[15]](#ref15)</sup> |
| Transmasculine (Amsterdam, retrospective, n = 206) | 20.8% overall explantation; 11.5% with Hoebeke scrotoplasty | Pigot 2019<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup> |

## Psychological Impact

Transmasculine-specific outcome data for testicular implants are lacking. In cisgender men, placement improved subjective testicular appearance (p < 0.001) in a prospective trial, and receiving a testicular prosthesis was associated with higher overall genital satisfaction (OR 3.29) in a survey of men after orchiectomy for germ-cell tumors.<sup>[[11]](#ref11)[[15]](#ref15)</sup>

In the Pletta 2025 survey, satisfaction was reported by surgery category, and testicular implants were not reported separately.<sup>[[6]](#ref6)</sup> A masculinizing-surgery review states that patient choices and preferred outcomes are highly individual, and that scrotal implants are one option.<sup>[[4]](#ref4)</sup>

## Special Considerations in the Gender-Affirming Context

What makes transmasculine placement unique versus cisgender:<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[7]](#ref7)</sup>

1. **Reconstructed tissue bed.** The neoscrotum from labia-majora flaps has different tissue characteristics (thinner, less redundant) than native scrotal skin.
2. **Absence of native anatomy.** No dartos muscle, tunica vaginalis, or gubernacular attachment, leading to increased migration and extrusion risk.
3. **Concurrent procedures.** Implants may be placed alongside or near a penile prosthesis, requiring spatial planning within the neoscrotum.
4. **Urethral proximity.** In patients who underwent urethral lengthening, the neourethra passes through or near the neoscrotum; urethral complications can necessitate implant removal.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup>
5. **Bilateral placement.** Transmasculine patients usually receive two implants, whereas most cisgender patients need one after unilateral orchiectomy.
6. **ZSI 475 FtM penile prosthesis.** This erectile prosthesis, designed for phalloplasty, has a testicle-shaped pump placed in the neoscrotum.<sup>[[16]](#ref16)</sup> Cross-link to [Penile Implant After Phalloplasty](/docs/surgical-techniques/04k-gender-affirming-surgery/penile-implant-after-phalloplasty).

## Staging Within Masculinizing Reconstruction

Typical staging:<sup>[[5]](#ref5)</sup>

1. **Stage 1.** Chest reconstruction, [hysterectomy ± BSO](/docs/surgical-techniques/04k-gender-affirming-surgery/gender-affirming-hysterectomy).
2. **Stage 2.** Vaginectomy, scrotoplasty (± tissue expanders), metoidioplasty or phalloplasty, urethroplasty.
3. **Stage 3 (approximately 6 mo later).** Testicular implant placement.
4. **Stage 4 (9–12 mo after phalloplasty).** [Penile prosthesis insertion](/docs/surgical-techniques/04k-gender-affirming-surgery/penile-implant-after-phalloplasty) if phalloplasty performed.

Individual timing varies by patient goals, comorbidities, and surgical history.

---

## Evidence Limitations

- Pigot 2019 (n = 206) is an Amsterdam single-center retrospective series covering 26 years (1992–2018). The difference between overall explantation (20.8%) and Hoebeke-technique explantation (11.5%) is not a controlled comparison.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup>
- **No comparative trials** between prosthesis types or brands in the transmasculine context.
- No purpose-designed transmasculine testicular implant exists; all devices are adapted from cisgender use.
- **Validated PROMs** specific to transmasculine testicular-implant outcomes are lacking; existing satisfaction data come from broader gender-affirming-surgery surveys.<sup>[[6]](#ref6)</sup>
- Emerging hormone-eluting and tissue-engineered devices (poly-HEMA, double-layer silastic, tissue-engineered cartilage), covered on the [04e canonical hub](/docs/surgical-techniques/04e-genital-reconstruction/testicular-prosthesis), have not been studied in the transmasculine context.
