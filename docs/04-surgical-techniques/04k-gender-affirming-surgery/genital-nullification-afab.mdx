# Genital Nullification — AFAB (HBSO + Colpectomy + Vulvectomy + Perineal Urethrostomy)

**Genital nullification for AFAB individuals** is an individually customized gender-affirming procedure combining hysterectomy with bilateral salpingo-oophorectomy (HBSO), colpectomy (vaginectomy), vulvectomy, and perineal urethrostomy (PU), resulting in a smooth perineal surface devoid of external or internal genital structures.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[2]](#ref2)</sup> It is most commonly sought by nonbinary or agender individuals who experience dysphoria related to all natal genital anatomy and do not desire masculinizing genital reconstruction (phalloplasty or metoidioplasty).<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[3]](#ref3)</sup>

This is the dedicated atlas page. For the broader variant-GGAS framework, see [Non-Binary / Nullification Gender-Affirming Surgery](/docs/surgical-techniques/04k-gender-affirming-surgery/non-binary-nullification). The four component procedures live on dedicated atlas pages: see [Gender-Affirming Hysterectomy](/docs/surgical-techniques/04k-gender-affirming-surgery/gender-affirming-hysterectomy), [Vaginectomy / Colpectomy](/docs/surgical-techniques/04k-gender-affirming-surgery/vaginectomy), and the [Midline Perineal Urethrostomy](/docs/surgical-techniques/04a-urethral-reconstruction/seven-flap-perineal-urethrostomy) / [Blandy Perineal Urethrostomy](/docs/surgical-techniques/04a-urethral-reconstruction/blandy-perineal-urethrostomy) canonical pages. For the AMAB counterpart, see [Genital Nullification — AMAB](/docs/surgical-techniques/04k-gender-affirming-surgery/genital-nullification-amab).

Published data on the complete AFAB nullification combination are limited, so most evidence comes from the individual component procedures. The Ascha 2024 series of individually customized genital procedures (n = 16, across phallus-preserving vaginoplasty, vagina-preserving phalloplasty and genital removal with perineal urethrostomy) is the main GAS-specific reference.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup>

---

## Surgical Overview and Staging

The procedure may be performed in one stage or several, depending on surgeon preference, patient factors and institutional protocols. Standard masculinizing-GAS staging is (1) HBSO ± chest surgery, (2) colpectomy with genital reconstruction, and (3) prosthetic implantation.<sup>[[4]](#ref4)</sup> Nullification omits phalloplasty and metoidioplasty, which simplifies the plan. In a retrospective cohort of 68 patients, Kim-Ortega 2025 found no significant difference in 30-day complications between concurrent hysterectomy and vaginectomy (n = 31) and staged vaginectomy after hysterectomy (n = 37). Median blood loss and operative time were higher with the concurrent approach, and no patient needed transfusion.<sup>[[5]](#ref5)</sup>

## Component 1 — HBSO

Full technique detail on the [Gender-Affirming Hysterectomy](/docs/surgical-techniques/04k-gender-affirming-surgery/gender-affirming-hysterectomy) atlas page.

**Technique considerations specific to this population:**<sup>[[6]](#ref6)[[7]](#ref7)[[8]](#ref8)[[9]](#ref9)</sup>

- **Vaginal atrophy.** Long-term testosterone is associated with mucosal atrophy and narrowing, and laparoscopic approaches give better visualization than vaginal hysterectomy.
- **Vaginal cuff.** Two-layer closure should be considered to reduce cuff complications and is preferred for patients with pelvic-organ dysphoria.
- **Infundibulopelvic-ligament margin.** An adequate margin minimizes the risk of ovarian-remnant syndrome. Persistent ovarian tissue that leads to menstruation is severely dysphoria-inducing.
- Preoperative pelvic examination may be omitted. In a retrospective cohort (Murphy 2023, n = 62), 53.2% had no examination within a year of surgery and 30-day outcomes did not differ, which reduces barriers to care.<sup>[[10]](#ref10)</sup>

**Outcomes.** In a NSQIP analysis (Siringo 2023; 526 gender-affirming hysterectomies among 40,742), complication rates were similar to those of hysterectomy for benign menstrual disorders.<sup>[[11]](#ref11)</sup> In Bretschneider 2018 (521 among 159,736 hysterectomies), transgender male status was not independently associated with complications (adjusted OR 1.11, 95% CI 0.56–2.10).<sup>[[30]](#ref30)</sup>

**Fertility counseling.** Oophorectomy is irreversible, and fertility-preservation consultation is offered before surgery.<sup>[[8]](#ref8)[[12]](#ref12)[[13]](#ref13)</sup>

## Component 2 — Colpectomy (Vaginectomy)

Colpectomy is the most technically demanding component and has the highest complication rate of the four. Full 4-variant technique detail (transperineal sharp excision, fulguration, RALV, laparoscopic vaginal-assisted) on the [Vaginectomy / Colpectomy](/docs/surgical-techniques/04k-gender-affirming-surgery/vaginectomy) atlas page.

**Nullification context.** Anterior vaginal wall flap preservation for urethral lengthening is not required (there is no phalloplasty or metoidioplasty target). Fulguration may be considered. In a retrospective single-institution comparison (Ho 2025; 70 patients, all with metoidioplasty), fulguration was associated with lower blood loss (88 vs 254 mL, p < 0.01) and shorter operative time (183 vs 290 min, p < 0.01) than epithelial excision in patients without hysterectomy. Vagino-cutaneous fistula rates were identical (10%), and vaginal remnant recurrence did not differ (5% vs 8%, p = 0.67).<sup>[[15]](#ref15)</sup> The Hougen 2020 technique paper describes the transperineal approach to sharp excision.<sup>[[16]](#ref16)</sup>

In a prospective cohort of robot-assisted laparoscopic colpectomy combined with robotic TLH-BSO as a single-step procedure (Groenman 2017, n = 36), median blood loss was 75 mL, median operative time 230 min and median discharge 3 d. One patient had a major complication (postoperative bleeding with readmission and transfusion).<sup>[[17]](#ref17)</sup>

### Colpectomy complications (Nikkels 2019 n = 143 — largest series)<sup>[[14]](#ref14)</sup>

| Complication | Incidence |
|---|---|
| Major perioperative (bowel / ureter / urethra / bladder injury, hemorrhage requiring transfusion or intervention, conversion to laparoscopy) | 10% |
| Major postoperative (hemorrhage, hematoma, fistula, wound infection, prolonged pain) | 12% |
| Minor postoperative (UTI, urinary retention, hemorrhage, hematoma) | 35% |
| Peritoneal entry (transperineal) | 44% (closed primarily without sequelae) |
| Vaginal remnant recurrence (Ho 2025)<sup>[[15]](#ref15)</sup> | 5–8% |
| Vagino-cutaneous fistula (Ho 2025; equal with excision and fulguration)<sup>[[15]](#ref15)</sup> | 10% |

Nikkels concluded that vaginal colpectomy has a "high complication rate, but its advantages seem to outweigh its disadvantages", with no long-term sequelae in all but one case.<sup>[[14]](#ref14)</sup>

## Component 3 — Vulvectomy

In the nullification context, vulvectomy is removal of the labia majora, labia minora, clitoral hood and clitoris to achieve a smooth perineal contour. It is the least well-described component in the GAS literature. Temporal-trend analyses of GAS list vulvectomy (ICD-9 71.62) as a gender-affirming procedure.<sup>[[2]](#ref2)</sup>

**Surgical considerations:**

- Clitorectomy permanently eliminates the primary source of erogenous sensation, and preoperative counseling covers the irreversibility of this loss.
- **Wound closure and perineal contouring.** After vulvar-structure removal, the remaining skin edges are approximated to leave a flat perineum. Techniques adapted from oncologic vulvar reconstruction, including V-Y advancement flaps, medial thigh fasciocutaneous flaps and gluteal-fold flaps, are used for larger defects or tension-free approximation.<sup>[[18]](#ref18)[[19]](#ref19)</sup>
- **Urethral-meatus management.** The native AFAB meatus sits within the vestibule; after vulvectomy removes the vestibular tissue surrounding the meatus, the urethra must be brought to the perineal skin surface as a PU (Component 4).

---

## Component 4 — Perineal Urethrostomy

In AFAB nullification, the PU differs from the AMAB context because the native AFAB urethra is already short (about 4 cm). After vulvectomy removes the vestibular tissue surrounding the native meatus, the urethra is matured to the new perineal skin surface.

**Technical principles** (adapted from reconstructive urology and oncologic surgery):<sup>[[20]](#ref20)[[21]](#ref21)</sup>

- The urethra is spatulated and sutured to perineal skin with interrupted absorbable sutures to create a widely patent stoma.
- The longitudinal urethral blood supply is preserved. In a series of 45 men, stenosis was uncommon when it was preserved and occurred mainly after radiation.<sup>[[21]](#ref21)</sup>
- The stoma is calibrated to at least 24–30 Fr to minimize stenosis risk.<sup>[[22]](#ref22)</sup>
- A urethral catheter or suprapubic tube is maintained postoperatively (typically 10–21 d).

See canonical PU pages for full technique:

- [Midline Perineal Urethrostomy](/docs/surgical-techniques/04a-urethral-reconstruction/seven-flap-perineal-urethrostomy): French / Hudak / Morey 2011 modern algorithmic midline approach.<sup>[[22]](#ref22)</sup>
- [Blandy Perineal Urethrostomy](/docs/surgical-techniques/04a-urethral-reconstruction/blandy-perineal-urethrostomy): Blandy 1968 classical inverted-U flap.

### PU outcomes (extrapolated from reconstructive-urology literature)<sup>[[20]](#ref20)[[21]](#ref21)</sup>

These figures come from cisgender men. In the Joshi 2024 midline series (n = 103), success (freedom from re-intervention) was 95.1% and complications (5.8%) were mild. Among 49 survey respondents at a median 61 months, 86% were satisfied or very satisfied and 76% of 41 respondents were unbothered by sitting to urinate.<sup>[[20]](#ref20)</sup> In Myers 2011 (n = 40 with follow-up, median 31 months), primary success was 83% and postoperative stenosis occurred in 18%, and radiation was the main risk factor (adjusted OR 11.2).<sup>[[21]](#ref21)</sup>

Absence of prior radiation, the strongest stenosis risk factor in Myers 2011, may confer a lower stenosis risk than in the cisgender male PU literature. The effect of the shorter native AFAB urethra on stenosis has not been reported.<sup>[[21]](#ref21)</sup>

## Urinary Function Considerations Specific to AFAB Anatomy

The AFAB urethra is anatomically shorter and lacks a sphincteric mechanism equivalent to the male external sphincter. After vulvectomy and PU:

- Continence depends on an intact bladder neck and pelvic-floor musculature. Pelvic-floor dysfunction affects up to 94.1% of transgender men, and a narrative review reports that pelvic-floor physical therapy before and after surgery can reduce dysfunction rates.<sup>[[23]](#ref23)</sup>
- Urethral complications in transmasculine surgery arise mainly from urethral lengthening: after phalloplasty, fistula in 15–60% and stricture in 25–58%. Nullification does not involve urethral lengthening, and these rates would be expected to be lower.<sup>[[23]](#ref23)</sup>
- UTI risk may be increased by altered anatomy and a shortened urethral path.<sup>[[24]](#ref24)</sup>

## Lifelong Postoperative Care

### Hormone replacement (mandatory after BSO)<sup>[[8]](#ref8)[[12]](#ref12)[[25]](#ref25)</sup>

- **Testosterone.** In patients previously on masculinizing therapy, adequate doses may prevent bone demineralization. No increased cardiovascular events have been reported in transmasculine individuals on testosterone.<sup>[[8]](#ref8)</sup>
- **Estrogen.** If not on testosterone, standard menopausal HRT protocols.
- **No hormone therapy.** Osteoporosis, cardiovascular and metabolic-syndrome risk is significant. The Endocrine Society recommends DEXA bone-mineral-density monitoring after gonadectomy if HRT discontinued.<sup>[[12]](#ref12)</sup>

ACOG notes that many patients do not plan to stop testosterone but some may do so owing to access issues. The risks and benefits of ovarian preservation are discussed even with patients who plan to continue testosterone.<sup>[[8]](#ref8)</sup>

### Cancer screening

After HBSO and colpectomy, cervical cancer screening is no longer needed (cervix removed). If vaginal-remnant tissue persists, surveillance may be warranted.<sup>[[24]](#ref24)</sup>

### PU surveillance

Follow-up is regular for the first 1–2 years to detect stenosis, which typically presents within 18 months.<sup>[[21]](#ref21)</sup>

### Pelvic-floor rehabilitation

Pre- and postoperative pelvic-floor physical therapy is used to optimize continence and voiding.<sup>[[23]](#ref23)</sup>

## Psychological Outcomes and Satisfaction

Nullification-specific data are lacking. Broader GAS evidence includes:

- **Almazan and Keuroghlian, 2015 US Transgender Survey.** Gender-affirming surgery at least 2 years earlier, compared with wanting but not having surgery, was associated with lower severe psychological distress (aOR 0.58), lower suicidal ideation (aOR 0.56) and lower smoking (aOR 0.65).<sup>[[26]](#ref26)</sup>
- **Pletta 2025.** Reproductive surgeries (including gonadectomy) consistently rated most satisfactory across all gender-identity groups (mean 4.6/5); nonbinary AFAB specifically 4.4/5.<sup>[[27]](#ref27)</sup>
- **Cooney 2025 systematic review.** Of the surgical studies, all 4 quality-of-life studies showed statistically significant improvement and all 5 dysphoria studies showed improved gender congruence. Certainty of evidence across the review was moderate to very low.<sup>[[28]](#ref28)</sup>
- Postoperative regret across GAS is reported at &lt;1–3%.<sup>[[29]](#ref29)</sup>

## Access and Insurance

AFAB genital nullification is rare and offered by few surgical centers. Only 33.9% of nonbinary AFAB individuals have received any form of GAS (versus 58.3% of transgender men).<sup>[[27]](#ref27)</sup> Insurance coverage for nonbinary-specific procedures is variable and often more challenging to obtain than for standard binary surgical pathways.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup>

| Component | OR time | EBL | Major complication rate | Key long-term risk |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| HBSO (laparoscopic) | 60–90 min | Minimal | ~ 3–4% (composite complications, Bretschneider)<sup>[[30]](#ref30)</sup> | Ovarian-remnant syndrome; need for HRT |
| **Colpectomy (excision)** | 112–132 min | 250–300 mL | 10% perioperative; 12% postoperative<sup>[[14]](#ref14)</sup> | Vaginal remnant 5–8%; fistula 10% |
| Colpectomy (fulguration) | ~ 183 min (combined) | 88 mL | Similar fistula (10%)<sup>[[15]](#ref15)</sup> | Vaginal remnant 5% |
| Vulvectomy | Variable | Variable | Wound dehiscence, infection | **Permanent loss of erogenous sensation (irreversible)** |
| Perineal urethrostomy | 30–60 min (est.) | Minimal | 5.8% (mild, Joshi)<sup>[[20]](#ref20)</sup> | Stomal stenosis 5–18%<sup>[[20]](#ref20)[[21]](#ref21)</sup> |

## Evidence Limitations

- No nullification-specific outcomes series in AFAB exists; all evidence is synthesized from component-procedure data.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup>
- Ascha 2024 (n = 16) is the main individually customized series but does not provide AFAB-nullification-specific subgroup outcomes.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup>
- PU outcomes in this population are extrapolated from cisgender reconstructive-urology and oncologic literature. Younger AFAB patients without radiation history may have a lower stenosis risk than that benchmark.<sup>[[20]](#ref20)[[21]](#ref21)</sup>
- Vulvectomy is the least-described component. No GAS-specific outcomes series exists, and technique principles are adapted from oncologic vulvar reconstruction.<sup>[[18]](#ref18)[[19]](#ref19)</sup>
- Validated nullification-specific PROMs are lacking in the nonbinary AFAB population. Oles 2022 documents the broader transgender-PROM gap.<sup>[[29]](#ref29)</sup>
