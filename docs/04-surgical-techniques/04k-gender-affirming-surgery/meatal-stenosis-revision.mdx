# Meatal Stenosis Revision After Feminizing Genital Surgery

**Meatal stenosis** is the most common urethral complication of feminizing vaginoplasty with an incidence of 4–40% across individual series, 5–16.3% in systematic reviews, and a Dreher 2018 meta-analytic rate of 14.4%.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[2]](#ref2)[[3]](#ref3)</sup> Management follows a stepwise approach: office-based dilation or meatotomy for first-time uncomplicated stenosis, followed by formal meatoplasty (Y-V, neovaginal advancement flap, vestibular flap) for recurrent cases and buccal mucosal graft (BMG) urethroplasty for refractory or lichen sclerosus–associated strictures.<sup>[[2]](#ref2)[[4]](#ref4)[[5]](#ref5)</sup>

This is the dedicated management-pathway page. For the primary urethroplasty component, see [Feminizing Urethroplasty](/docs/surgical-techniques/04k-gender-affirming-surgery/feminizing-urethroplasty). For the periurethral / periclitoral architecture that drives stenosis risk, see [Feminizing Labiaplasty](/docs/surgical-techniques/04k-gender-affirming-surgery/feminizing-labiaplasty) and [Clitoroplasty](/docs/surgical-techniques/04k-gender-affirming-surgery/clitoroplasty-preputial-flap).

---

## Definitions and Anatomy

**Meatal stenosis** is narrowing of the neourethral meatus (the surgically created opening in the vestibule between neoclitoris and introitus). It is distinct from, though may coexist with, urethral stricture, which is narrowing of the urethral lumen proximal to the meatus.<sup>[[2]](#ref2)[[6]](#ref6)</sup>

**Postoperative anatomy:** male urethra (about 18–20 cm) shortened to about 3–4 cm by excising the pendulous segment; prostatic, membranous and bulbar urethra preserved; distal stump spatulated and sutured to vestibular skin. The external urethral sphincter at the membranous urethra is preserved, maintaining continence. The neomeatus is therefore a surgically created mucocutaneous junction between native urethral mucosa (transitional or columnar) and surrounding keratinised perineal or vestibular skin, inherently prone to circumferential scar contracture, analogous to meatal stenosis after hypospadias repair or circumcision.<sup>[[7]](#ref7)[[2]](#ref2)[[8]](#ref8)</sup>

## Incidence

| Series | n | Rate | Notes |
|---|---|---|---|
| Dreher 2018 meta-analysis<sup>[[3]](#ref3)</sup> | 1,684 | **14.4%** | Most common complication overall |
| Nassiri 2020 SR<sup>[[2]](#ref2)</sup> | 3,463 (32 studies) | 4–40% | Meatal stenosis in the 10 MtF studies; 23 studies were FtM |
| Blasdel 2024 review<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup> | Multiple SRs | 5–16.3% | Mean across 8 SRs |
| Motiwala 2026 narrative review<sup>[[9]](#ref9)</sup> | Multiple | 5–10% | Transfeminine |
| Fakin 2021 single-stage vs two-stage PIV<sup>[[10]](#ref10)</sup> | 125 | Two-stage 14.5% urethral stenosis | Retrospective, single centre; single-stage rate not reported in the abstract |
| Levy 2019 high-volume single surgeon<sup>[[11]](#ref11)</sup> | 240 | 0.4% (1/240) reoperation for meatal stenosis | Median follow-up 87 days |
| Mañero 2023 cosmetic revisions<sup>[[12]](#ref12)</sup> | 354 | 9.38% of all cosmetic revisions | Urethral meatus revisions |

The 4–40% range probably reflects heterogeneous definitions, from any patient requiring a single office dilation to only formal revision. The Blasdel 2024 narrative review found higher rates of voiding dysfunction, incontinence and misdirected stream in non-surgeon-reported cohorts than in surgeon-reported cohorts (voiding dysfunction 47–66% versus 5.6–33%), so surgeon-reported rates may underestimate the burden.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup>

## Pathophysiology

### Circumferential scar contracture

Wound healing at the circumferential mucocutaneous suture line produces concentric scar contracture, the same mechanism that drives meatal stenosis after hypospadias repair and circumcision.<sup>[[2]](#ref2)[[8]](#ref8)</sup>

### Inadequate spatulation

Insufficient distal-urethral spatulation creates a small meatal opening from the outset that predisposes to stenosis. Adequate spatulation converts the circular lumen to a wider elliptical opening.<sup>[[2]](#ref2)</sup>

### Distal-stump ischemia

The distal urethral stump relies on the surrounding corpus spongiosum for blood supply. Penile-disassembly–related spongiosal devascularisation drives ischemic contracture.<sup>[[2]](#ref2)[[6]](#ref6)</sup>

### Periurethral tissue architecture

In a retrospective comparison of sequential cohorts (Raigosa 2020; 64 patients with labia minora and clitoral-hood creation versus 103 without), neomeatal stenosis occurred in 1.5% versus 15.5% (p = 0.003). This suggests that the tissue surrounding the neomeatus contributes to patency, in addition to the urethral shortening itself.<sup>[[13]](#ref13)</sup> Cross-link to the [Feminizing Labiaplasty page](/docs/surgical-techniques/04k-gender-affirming-surgery/feminizing-labiaplasty) for the full dataset.

### Residual corpus spongiosum

Bulky periurethral spongiosal tissue causes fullness and contributes to both stenosis and misdirected stream; debulking of residual spongiosum is part of some revision techniques.<sup>[[14]](#ref14)[[15]](#ref15)</sup>

### Lichen sclerosus (LS)

LS can affect the genital skin of transgender women and drives progressive fibrosis and stricture. In the Waterloos neovaginal-flap series, 1/5 patients had LS-associated urethral stricture requiring BMG augmentation in addition to the neovaginal flap.<sup>[[5]](#ref5)</sup> The AUA 2023 Urethral Stricture Disease Guideline Amendment, written for men, states that surgeons should not use genital skin for reconstruction in LS-proven urethral stricture (Strong Recommendation; Evidence Level: Grade B), citing very high long-term failure rates of genital skin flaps and grafts.<sup>[[4]](#ref4)</sup>

### Wound infection

Dreher meta-analysis: wound infection associated with increased risk of all tissue-healing complications.<sup>[[3]](#ref3)</sup>

## Clinical Presentation

| Symptom | Mechanism |
|---|---|
| Weak / diminished stream | Most common presentation |
| Straining | Compensatory effort |
| Hesitancy | Delayed initiation |
| Spraying / misdirected | Narrowed irregular meatal opening |
| Incomplete emptying | Elevated PVR |
| Frequency / urgency | Secondary to incomplete emptying |
| Recurrent UTI | Urinary stasis |
| Retention | Severe cases |
| Dysuria | Pain with voiding |

**Workup:**<sup>[[4]](#ref4)[[15]](#ref15)</sup>

- History: onset, progression, relationship to primary-surgery timing, prior interventions.
- **Direct visualisation** of the neomeatus: caliber, position, surrounding-tissue quality and LS features.
- **Uroflowmetry.** A Qmax below about 12 mL/s suggests obstruction (a threshold from male voiding assessment).
- Calibration with bougie-à-boules or paediatric sounds.
- **Cystoscopy** when stricture extends beyond the meatus.
- **Post-void residual** via bladder scan / catheterisation.
- **Biopsy** if LS suspected; male LS carries a 2–8.6% SCC rate.<sup>[[4]](#ref4)</sup>

## Stepwise Management

### Step 1 — Office-based dilation

For first-time uncomplicated stenosis without prior failed endoscopic manipulation, prior urethroplasty, or LS:<sup>[[4]](#ref4)</sup>

- Topical lidocaine; progressive urethral sounds / dilators.
- **High recurrence rate.** Pooled success only 49% for female urethral stricture versus > 85% for surgical reconstruction.<sup>[[16]](#ref16)</sup>
- AUA 2023 states that surgeons may initially treat meatal or fossa navicularis strictures with either dilation or meatotomy (Clinical Principle).<sup>[[4]](#ref4)</sup>

### Step 2 — Meatotomy

Standard first-line surgical intervention:<sup>[[2]](#ref2)[[4]](#ref4)[[17]](#ref17)</sup>

- **Dorsal meatotomy.** Simple incision through the dorsal aspect of the stenotic meatus to widen the opening; most commonly performed.<sup>[[2]](#ref2)</sup>
- Local anaesthesia (office) or sedation.
- Success was 87% after meatotomy in 73 men with distal urethral strictures (Meeks 2012; mean follow-up 61 months).<sup>[[18]](#ref18)</sup>
- In a large paediatric series (1,906 meatotomies), 0.58% needed reoperation and 3.6% required manual meatal spreading at the first postoperative visit; no child needed reoperation after meatoplasty (n = 305), and the authors favored meatoplasty.<sup>[[17]](#ref17)</sup>
- The AUA text notes some evidence that extended meatotomy with high-dose topical steroids may decrease recurrence compared with meatotomy alone in LS-associated strictures.<sup>[[4]](#ref4)</sup>
- Nassiri SR: meatal stenosis after MtF surgery usually requires meatotomy for repair.<sup>[[2]](#ref2)</sup>

### Step 3 — Formal Meatoplasty (recurrent stenosis)

AUA 2023 (written for men): surgeons should offer urethroplasty to patients with recurrent meatal or fossa navicularis strictures (Moderate Recommendation; Evidence Level: Grade C).<sup>[[4]](#ref4)</sup>

**A. Y-V meatoplasty (Sennert 2025).** Small inverted-V skin flap (3–5 mm limbs) elevated at meatal tip; wedge of narrow meatus excised; V tip advanced distally. n = 57, all with meatal stenosis after hypospadias repair (median age 53 months): 55 (96.5%) had symptom relief and 2 (3.5%) needed further surgery at median 4-yr follow-up (retrospective, single centre).<sup>[[8]](#ref8)</sup>

**B. Neovaginal advancement flap (Waterloos / Ghent).** This is the only dedicated series identified for transgender women. n = 5; no recurrence; no recurrence at median 37 mo (range 6–97):<sup>[[5]](#ref5)</sup>

- **7-flap** (2 patients): flap shaped like the number 7, raised from the anterior neovaginal wall adjacent to the meatus.
- **Inverted U-flap** (3 patients): U-shaped anterior-neovaginal flap rotated to augment the ventral urethral wall.
- **1 LS patient**: urethra additionally enlarged with buccal mucosa in addition to the neovaginal flap.
- Median stricture length 2.0 cm; median OR time 41 min; all satisfied; 1 low-grade complication (bladder spasms).
- The neovaginal wall (penile or scrotal skin) is the analogous tissue source to the native vaginal wall used in cisgender female urethral reconstruction; it is well-vascularised, pliable and adjacent.

**C. Jordan flap (ventral preputial flap).** Originally for cisgender male distal urethral strictures; uses a ventral preputial flap. In 21 men, the reconstruction was successful in all with no recurrence at mean 35 mo. It may be applicable in transgender women with residual preputial tissue (used for the clitoral hood).<sup>[[19]](#ref19)</sup>

**D. Vestibular flap meatoplasty (Romman 2015 cisgender analogue).** Broad-based vestibular flap raised from labia minora adjacent to the meatus; stenotic distal-urethral wedge excised; flap rotated inward to bridge the defect, converting the obstructing O-shape to a C-shape. In 28 evaluable women who had failed dilation, 19 (68%) met the success definition, and no new or worsened stress incontinence was reported. The flap is intended to keep the cut edges from scarring back together.<sup>[[20]](#ref20)</sup>

### Step 4 — Urethroplasty with Tissue Augmentation (refractory / extensive / LS)

**A. Buccal mucosal graft (BMG) urethroplasty.** BMG is a commonly used graft for recurrent meatal and fossa navicularis strictures and for LS:<sup>[[4]](#ref4)[[21]](#ref21)[[22]](#ref22)</sup>

- Harvested from inner cheek; dorsal onlay or ventral inlay augmentation.
- In men, pooled success was 56–100% (62 patients in five published series), and 69% at median 42 mo in the largest single-centre series (n = 32).<sup>[[22]](#ref22)</sup>
- **Dorsal-onlay BMG (DOBMGU).** 87.5% success (7/8) at median 12 mo versus 57.1% (4/7) for vaginal-flap urethroplasty in a 15-woman, single-centre, non-randomized comparison in which the flap was used mainly for distal strictures.<sup>[[21]](#ref21)</sup>
- **AUA 2023**: surgeons should not use genital skin for reconstruction in LS-proven stricture (Strong Recommendation; Evidence Level: Grade B).<sup>[[4]](#ref4)</sup>
- In the transgender context, Waterloos used BMG as an adjunct to neovaginal flap in 1 LS patient with successful outcome.<sup>[[5]](#ref5)</sup>

**B. Vaginal or neovaginal flap urethroplasty.** Anterior neovaginal wall (penile or scrotal skin) as pedicled flap; Romero-Maroto / Orandi lateral-based anterior vaginal wall flap: 100% success in 9 cisgender patients at mean 80.7 mo, no recurrence, no de novo SUI. In the transgender context, the Waterloos technique is the published adaptation.<sup>[[5]](#ref5)[[23]](#ref23)</sup>

## Comparison of Revision Techniques

| Technique | Success | Best indication |
|---|---|---|
| Office dilation | ~49% (pooled) | First-time mild stenosis<sup>[[16]](#ref16)</sup> |
| Dorsal meatotomy | 87% (men) | First-time uncomplicated stenosis<sup>[[2]](#ref2)[[4]](#ref4)[[18]](#ref18)</sup> |
| Y-V meatoplasty | 96.5% (n = 57) | Recurrent ring stenosis<sup>[[8]](#ref8)</sup> |
| **Neovaginal advancement flap (7 / U)** | **100% (5/5, 37 mo)** | Distal urethral stricture in transwomen — only GAS-specific technique<sup>[[5]](#ref5)</sup> |
| Vestibular flap | 68% (19/28, cisgender women) | Recurrent distal stenosis after failed dilation<sup>[[20]](#ref20)</sup> |
| BMG urethroplasty | 69–87.5% | Recurrent / LS-associated stricture<sup>[[21]](#ref21)[[22]](#ref22)</sup> |
| Jordan flap | 100% (n = 21) | Distal stricture with available preputial tissue<sup>[[19]](#ref19)</sup> |

## Concurrent Urethral Complaints

Meatal stenosis frequently coexists with other urethral complaints. NYU vulvar revision series (Dy 2022): urologic complaints in 48.6% (17/35) of revision patients, the third most common revision category after labial (77.1%) and clitoral (57.1%).<sup>[[15]](#ref15)</sup>

| Concurrent issue | Frequency | Mechanism / Management |
|---|---|---|
| Misdirected stream | 33–55% in non-surgeon-reported cohorts<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup> | Meatal position, periurethral architecture, residual spongiosum |
| Spraying | Common | Meatal shape; periurethral tissue |
| Periurethral fullness | — | Residual corpus spongiosum; may require debulking |
| Urethral diverticulum | Rare | Post-void dribbling |
| Urethrovaginal fistula | Rare | Continuous urinary leakage from neovagina |

These are often addressed concurrently in a single revision procedure that may also include labial, clitoral, and introital revisions.<sup>[[15]](#ref15)[[24]](#ref24)</sup>

## Lichen Sclerosus — Special Considerations

LS deserves special attention in the transgender context:<sup>[[4]](#ref4)[[5]](#ref5)[[25]](#ref25)</sup>

- **Affects genital skin** of transgender women: penile or scrotal-skin canal lining and periurethral tissue.
- **Progressive fibrosis** leading to meatal stenosis and urethral stricture.
- **Genital skin flaps and grafts should be avoided** for reconstruction; they remain prone to LS recurrence.<sup>[[4]](#ref4)</sup>
- **BMG is the graft usually chosen** for LS-associated strictures, because oral mucosa is not affected by LS.<sup>[[4]](#ref4)[[25]](#ref25)</sup>
- **Biopsy** for definitive diagnosis; male LS carries 2–8.6% SCC rate.<sup>[[4]](#ref4)</sup>
- **Topical clobetasol** is the medical-therapy mainstay; may prevent progression to extensive stricture.<sup>[[4]](#ref4)[[25]](#ref25)</sup>
- **Long-term surveillance** for SCC.<sup>[[4]](#ref4)</sup>

## Prevention at Primary Surgery

Strategies to reduce meatal stenosis:<sup>[[3]](#ref3)[[10]](#ref10)[[13]](#ref13)</sup>

- **Adequate ventral spatulation** of the distal urethra.
- **Periurethral tissue architecture.** Meatal stenosis was 1.5% with labia-minora and clitoral-hood creation versus 15.5% without in Raigosa 2020 (retrospective; p = 0.003).<sup>[[13]](#ref13)</sup>
- **Tension-free meatal suture line**.
- **Foley 14–16 Fr for 5–14 d** postoperatively (common practice).
- **Single-stage technique.** In Fakin 2021, urethral stenosis occurred in 14.5% of two-stage patients and was not among the complications reported for single-stage patients (retrospective).<sup>[[10]](#ref10)</sup>
- **Wound-infection prevention** (Dreher meta-analysis association).<sup>[[3]](#ref3)</sup>

## Postoperative Management After Meatal Revision

- **Foley 14–16 Fr** through the revised meatus for 5–14 d to maintain patency during healing.
- **Topical estrogen or steroid cream** (institution-dependent) to promote healing and reduce scar contracture.
- **Follow-up uroflowmetry** at 4–6 wk for objective flow-rate assessment.
- **Periodic surveillance** of meatal caliber and voiding symptoms; recurrence can occur months to years out.
- **Intermittent self-dilation** sometimes recommended after meatoplasty (analogous to neovaginal dilation).
- **LS screening.** Biopsy and topical steroids if suspected; long-term dermatologic surveillance.<sup>[[4]](#ref4)</sup>

## Voiding Dysfunction Beyond Meatal Stenosis

The Blasdel 2024 review compared surgeon-reported cohorts with other reports:<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup>

| Voiding parameter | Surgeon-reported | Other reports |
|---|---|---|
| Voiding dysfunction | 5.6–33% | **47–66%** |
| Misdirected stream | 9.5–33% | **33–55%** |
| Urinary incontinence | 4–19.3% | **23–33%** |
| Meatal stenosis | 5–16.3% | Higher; not well characterised |

A narrative review reports pelvic-floor dysfunction in up to 94.1% of transgender men and states that pelvic-floor physical therapy before and after surgery can reduce dysfunction rates.<sup>[[9]](#ref9)</sup> See [Neovaginal Stenosis Management](/docs/surgical-techniques/04k-gender-affirming-surgery/neovaginal-stenosis-management) for the broader PFPT framework (Jiang OHSU pre and post 28% versus 86%, FLOWER RCT no-routine-benefit signal).

## Evidence Limitations

- **No dedicated studies** focus on meatal-stenosis revision after feminizing vaginoplasty; the Waterloos neovaginal-flap series (n = 5) is the only published technique specifically designed for this population.<sup>[[5]](#ref5)</sup>
- **Heterogeneous definitions.** "meatal stenosis" variably defined; wide reported range (4–40%).<sup>[[2]](#ref2)</sup>
- **Surgeon-reported rates may underestimate** the true burden (voiding dysfunction 5.6–33% versus 47–66% in other reports).<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup>
- **No validated PROMs** for urinary function in transgender women.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[9]](#ref9)</sup>
- **No comparative trials** between revision techniques (meatotomy versus meatoplasty versus BMG) in the transgender context.
- **LS prevalence** in transgender women is unknown; no systematic evaluation of LS as a stenosis driver in this population.
- **Long-term outcomes** of meatal revision beyond 3–5 yr poorly characterised.
- Most management principles are extrapolated from cisgender male and female urethral-reconstruction literature with limited transgender-specific validation.<sup>[[4]](#ref4)[[26]](#ref26)</sup>
