# Gender-Affirming Vaginectomy / Colpectomy

**Vaginectomy** (also termed **colpectomy**) is the surgical removal or obliteration of the vaginal epithelium in transmasculine individuals. It is performed as a stand-alone procedure for dysphoria, alongside hysterectomy, or concurrently with metoidioplasty / phalloplasty.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[2]](#ref2)</sup> Vaginectomy is often performed with UL for standing voiding; in techniques that use a distal AVW flap, its mucosa is preserved to form the fixed neourethra.<sup>[[3]](#ref3)[[4]](#ref4)</sup> UL with vaginal preservation has also been reported. In a systematic review of 23 case series, urethral complications were more frequent when the vagina was preserved after phalloplasty or metoidioplasty, but all included studies had high risk of bias.<sup>[[21]](#ref21)</sup>

This page consolidates the four contemporary technique variants: transperineal sharp excision, electrocautery fulguration, robotic-assisted laparoscopic vaginectomy (RALV), and laparoscopic-assisted vaginectomy with concurrent hysterectomy. It also covers the AVW flap, which drives technique selection. For the cohort-level framework, see [Masculinizing Gender-Affirming Surgery](/docs/surgical-techniques/04k-gender-affirming-surgery/masculinizing-surgery). For the prerequisite procedure, see [Gender-Affirming Hysterectomy](/docs/surgical-techniques/04k-gender-affirming-surgery/gender-affirming-hysterectomy).

---

## Indications and Goals

- **Alleviation of gender dysphoria** related to the vaginal canal.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[2]](#ref2)</sup>
- **Reduction of vaginal discharge.**<sup>[[2]](#ref2)</sup>
- **Provision of AVW flap tissue for UL,** which is needed for standing micturition after metoidioplasty / phalloplasty.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[3]](#ref3)</sup>
- **Reduction of urethral-fistula risk** at the urethral-neourethral junction during subsequent phalloplasty / metoidioplasty.<sup>[[2]](#ref2)</sup>
- **Aim to reduce persistent vaginal-cavity complications,** which may still occur after masculinizing genital surgery.<sup>[[5]](#ref5)</sup>

---

## Eligibility and Preoperative Considerations

The Endocrine Society 2017 guideline recommends genital surgery only after the mental-health professional and the clinician responsible for endocrine treatment agree that it is medically necessary (recommendation 5.1) and advises approval only after at least 1 year of consistent hormone treatment, unless hormone therapy is not desired or is medically contraindicated (ungraded good practice statement 5.2).<sup>[[6]](#ref6)</sup> It suggests delaying gonadectomy and hysterectomy until the age of majority (recommendation 5.5).<sup>[[6]](#ref6)</sup> The WPATH Standards of Care, version 8 (2022), recommends one opinion from a competent assessor for eligible adults initiating gender-affirming medical or surgical treatment (statement 5.5).<sup>[[19]](#ref19)</sup> For gonadectomy, it suggests considering at least 6 months of tolerated hormone therapy when appropriate to the person’s goals, with explicit exceptions (statements 5.6 and 13.5). For genital surgery, it suggests stability on the current regimen, which may include hormone therapy when needed for the desired result (statement 13.6).<sup>[[18]](#ref18)[[19]](#ref19)</sup> When concurrent BSO is planned, WPATH statement 13.4 recommends that the surgeon confirm reproductive options have been discussed before gonadectomy.<sup>[[19]](#ref19)</sup>

Murphy 2023 was a retrospective single-institution cohort of 62 gender-affirming pelvic surgeries (hysterectomy alone, hysterectomy with vaginectomy, or vaginectomy alone). Outcomes at 30 days did not differ significantly between patients with and without a preoperative pelvic examination. The authors concluded that omitting the examination is probably safe and may reduce barriers to care.<sup>[[7]](#ref7)</sup>

In de Rooij and colleagues' centre, baseline voiding symptoms and emptying were assessed before colpectomy and reassessed before planned UL. In their retrospective cohort (n = 132), 6/132 (5%) did not proceed to desired UL because of postoperative voiding dysfunction; they used symptom scores, uroflowmetry and post-void residual measurements before and after surgery.<sup>[[22]](#ref22)</sup>

## Surgical Techniques — 4 Variants

### A. Transperineal Sharp Excision (Canonical)

This is the most widely described and commonly performed technique:<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[2]](#ref2)</sup>

1. **Positioning.** High lithotomy with Trendelenburg; Foley catheter; concurrent flap / donor sites prepped if staged with phalloplasty.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup>
2. **Circumferential mucosal incision** along the mucocutaneous junction: lateral / posterior first, then anterior sulci, then transverse anteriorly at bladder neck. Develop a plane between vaginal mucosa and underlying muscularis / adventitia.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup>
3. **Sharp mucosal excision** from introitus to apex in the paravaginal space, separating mucosa from rectovaginal septum posteriorly and vesicovaginal septum anteriorly. This is the most technically demanding portion. Complex vaginal fascial attachments and testosterone-induced mucosal atrophy make the plane harder to distinguish.<sup>[[3]](#ref3)</sup> Peritoneal entry occurred in approximately 44% of cases during apical dissection in Hougen's series and was closed primarily without sequelae.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup>
4. **AVW flap preservation.** Unlike in oncologic vaginectomy, the distal anterior vaginal wall mucosa is intentionally preserved as a pedicled flap based at the urethral meatus for UL (incorporated in approximately 83% of cases).<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup>
5. **Closure of paravaginal dead space.** Purse-string sutures from apex distally to prevent hematoma / seroma / persistent cavity.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[2]](#ref2)</sup>
6. **Cystoscopy and diversion in the illustrated UL case.** Hougen used cystoscopy to confirm ureteral patency and placed a suprapubic tube during second-stage phalloplasty with UL.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup>

**Operative metrics.** In the Hougen 2020 series (n = 40; 27 phalloplasties and 13 metoidioplasties), median OR time was 135 min and median EBL 250 mL.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup> In Nikkels 2019 (n = 143, retrospective; 109 colpectomy only), the colpectomy-only group had a mean OR time of 112 ± 40 min, median EBL 300 mL and mean LOS 3.2 ± 1.5 d.<sup>[[2]](#ref2)</sup>

### B. Electrocautery Fulguration (Ablative)

Fulguration destroys the epithelium in situ rather than excising it. In the single comparative series it was faster and had less blood loss:<sup>[[8]](#ref8)</sup>

**Ho 2025 retrospective comparison (n = 70; all patients had metoidioplasty; technique chosen by surgeon preference).** Blood loss and OR time were compared in patients without hysterectomy.

| Outcome | Excision | Fulguration | p |
|---|---|---|---|
| EBL | 254 mL | 88 mL | < 0.01 |
| OR time | 290 min | 183 min | &lt; 0.01 <sup>[[8]](#ref8)</sup> |
| Vagino-cutaneous fistula | 10% | 10% | NS |
| Vaginal-remnant recurrence | 8% | 5% | 0.67 |
| Repeat vaginectomy | 2% | 5% | 0.50 |

Fulguration does not preserve an AVW flap. When vaginal tissue is planned for UL, excision, or a hybrid that preserves the AVW and fulgurates the posterior and lateral walls, is the logical choice. The hybrid has not been compared in a published series. In a four-patient revision series after phalloplasty, concurrent distal urethroplasty used buccal mucosa, vaginal mucosa or skin grafts.<sup>[[11]](#ref11)</sup>

### C. Robotic-Assisted Laparoscopic Vaginectomy (RALV)

RALV is a minimally invasive transabdominal approach:<sup>[[9]](#ref9)[[10]](#ref10)</sup>

- **Groenman combined robotic approach.** After standard port placement, identify and lateralize both ureters to the ureterovesical junction before circumferential proximal vaginal dissection from above; protect their course during apex closure. Mark and preserve the distal AVW mucosa planned for UL.<sup>[[23]](#ref23)</sup>
- **Jun 2021 (n = 42, single surgeon, retrospective).** Median EBL was 200 mL, average OR time 299 min and median LOS 3 d. Complications within 30 days occurred in 36% (15/42); only 4 were vaginectomy-related and all resolved conservatively. A vaginal mucosa and gracilis flap was used in all 36 patients (86%) in whom a pars fixa was created.<sup>[[9]](#ref9)</sup>
- **Cohen 2020 split-gracilis technique (n = 16, level IV).** A pedicled gracilis muscle flap from the medial thigh is tunneled to the vaginal cavity and split longitudinally. The inferior half fills the vaginal dead space. The superior half wraps the AVW urethroplasty to buttress the suture line with well-vascularised tissue. No urinary fistula developed at the urethroplasty site at mean follow-up of 361 d.<sup>[[10]](#ref10)</sup>
- RALV has been described for revision vaginectomy (excision of vaginal remnants / urethral diverticula after prior vaginectomy). In a four-patient series, the transabdominal approach allowed excision without reopening the perineal closure.<sup>[[11]](#ref11)</sup>
- In a retrospective single-centre comparison (n = 310), robotic colpectomy with concurrent hysterectomy (n = 140; BSO in 129/140) was associated with lower median blood loss (100 vs 300 mL), shorter stay (2 vs 3 d), fewer intraoperative complications (0.7% vs 10.6%) and fewer grade IIIa or higher complications (10/140 vs 31/170; adjusted OR 0.41, 95% CI 0.17–0.98) than vaginal colpectomy after prior hysterectomy (n = 170).<sup>[[17]](#ref17)</sup> The procedures differed in hysterectomy status and extent, so these observations do not isolate the effect of surgical access.<sup>[[17]](#ref17)</sup> A later prospective pilot compared 31 robotic colpectomies after prior hysterectomy with 101 analyzed historical vaginal cases: median blood loss was 50 versus 300 mL, while a difference in severe complications was not established at three months.<sup>[[27]](#ref27)</sup> Historical controls and short follow-up limit inference.<sup>[[27]](#ref27)</sup>

### D. Laparoscopic Vaginal-Assisted Vaginectomy (with concurrent hysterectomy)

This is a combined laparoscopic-vaginal approach:<sup>[[12]](#ref12)</sup>

- Total laparoscopic hysterectomy and BSO are performed concurrently with vaginectomy.
- More than 50% of the vagina was removed laparoscopically in 20 of 23 patients (87%); the remainder was removed vaginally.
- Vaginal mucosa is conserved for subsequent neourethral reconstruction.
- **n = 23 (retrospective).** Average OR time was 155 ± 42 min (combined procedure). There were no intraoperative complications and 3 postoperative complications (hemoperitoneum, urinary retention, perineal hematoma). Discharge was within 72 h except in the patient with hemoperitoneum.<sup>[[12]](#ref12)</sup>

## The AVW Flap — Workhorse for Urethral Lengthening

When distal AVW mucosa is preserved, it can be tubularised to create the **pars fixa** (fixed perineal portion of the neourethra).<sup>[[3]](#ref3)[[1]](#ref1)</sup> Complete mucosal fulguration does not provide a pedicled AVW flap.<sup>[[8]](#ref8)</sup>

- **Flap design.** Distal AVW mucosa is preserved with the base at the native urethral meatus.
- **Tubularisation.** The flap is mobilised on its vascular pedicle and tubularised over a catheter to create a mucosa-lined conduit continuous with the native urethra.
- **Connection.** The pars fixa is connected to the **pars pendulans** (penile portion of the neourethra, constructed within the phalloplasty flap) during a subsequent stage.<sup>[[3]](#ref3)[[13]](#ref13)</sup>
- **Other reported UL tissue.** Metoidioplasty uses available flaps and grafts; a four-patient post-phalloplasty revision series used buccal, vaginal or skin grafts for concurrent distal urethroplasty.<sup>[[11]](#ref11)[[13]](#ref13)</sup>

## Timing and Staging

- **Concurrent with hysterectomy.** In the Kim-Ortega 2025 retrospective cohort (n = 68; 31 concurrent, 37 staged), 30-d perioperative complications did not differ significantly between transvaginal vaginectomy with concurrent hysterectomy and staged vaginectomy after prior hysterectomy. The concurrent group had higher median EBL and longer OR time; no transfusions occurred in either group.<sup>[[14]](#ref14)</sup>
- **At the time of phalloplasty / metoidioplasty.** This is common during staged genital reconstruction. The AVW flap was incorporated for UL in approximately 83% in Hougen's series.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup>
- **As a separate genital procedure.** Vaginectomy may be performed independently of phalloplasty or metoidioplasty for dysphoria relief or discharge reduction.<sup>[[2]](#ref2)</sup> Before vaginal obliteration, confirm whether the uterus and cervix have been removed and address any continuing cervical-screening indication; a retained cervix would no longer be accessible by the usual vaginal route.<sup>[[20]](#ref20)</sup>

Genital procedures for transmasculine patients are most often performed by a multidisciplinary surgical team (plastic, gynecologic, and urologic surgeons).<sup>[[4]](#ref4)</sup>

## Complications

**Nikkels 2019 (n = 143 colpectomies; retrospective; the first four rows below).**<sup>[[2]](#ref2)</sup> The peritoneal-entry row is from the Hougen series (n = 40)<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup>, and the last two rows are from Ho 2025 (n = 70, all with metoidioplasty).<sup>[[8]](#ref8)</sup>

| Category | Rate | Examples |
|---|---|---|
| Major perioperative | 10% (15/143)<sup>[[2]](#ref2)</sup> | Bowel, ureteral, urethral or bladder injury; hemorrhage requiring transfusion or intervention; conversion to laparoscopy |
| Minor perioperative | 0.7% | Hemorrhage |
| Major postoperative | 12% | Hemorrhage, hematoma, fistula, wound infection, prolonged pain |
| Minor postoperative | 35% | UTI, urinary retention, hemorrhage, hematoma |
| Peritoneal entry (transperineal) | 44% | Closed primarily without sequelae |
| Vagino-cutaneous fistula | 10% | Both excision and fulguration |
| Vaginal remnant recurrence | 5–8% | May require repeat vaginectomy |

**Separate staged cohort.** In 23 vaginectomies after prior metoidioplasty or groin-flap phalloplasty, full-thickness rectal injury occurred in 4/23 (all four after prior metoidioplasty); all were repaired intraoperatively without diversion or postoperative sequelae. At one year, 20/23 completed pelvic-floor questionnaires: overall symptom change was not significant, but the five prior groin-flap patients reported greater symptom distress. These small, staged subgroups do not provide a general vaginectomy complication rate.<sup>[[24]](#ref24)</sup>

Nikkels and colleagues concluded that vaginal colpectomy has a high complication rate, with no long-term sequelae in all but one patient, and that the high complication and reintervention rates should be discussed with patients considering the procedure.<sup>[[2]](#ref2)</sup>

## Postoperative Care and Long-Term Follow-Up

- Patients with phalloplasty and UL should have ongoing follow-up with a urologist familiar with GAS.<sup>[[5]](#ref5)</sup>
- Urinary retention, post-void dribbling, pelvic pain with fullness, and recurrent UTI warrant urologic referral and may indicate stricture, fistula, or persistent vaginal cavity.<sup>[[5]](#ref5)</sup>
- In a historical radial-forearm phalloplasty series, 127/316 (40%) developed fistula and/or stricture, including early fistulae; 54/316 required urethroplasty.<sup>[[4]](#ref4)[[25]](#ref25)</sup> A systematic review of UL with prior or concomitant vaginectomy found stricture or fistula in nearly one-third of radial-forearm and approximately one-quarter of anterolateral-thigh cohorts, with heterogeneous, low-quality evidence.<sup>[[26]](#ref26)</sup>
- **De Brouwer 2021 ENIGI multicentre European follow-up.** In 260 participants surveyed 5 years after first contact, the most frequently reported aftercare needs after gender-affirming surgery of any type were assistance in surgical recovery (47%), mental-health consultation (36%) and pelvic-floor physiotherapy (20%). Having undergone genital surgery was associated with needing recovery assistance (OR 2.55) and pelvic-floor therapy.<sup>[[15]](#ref15)</sup>

## Outcomes and Satisfaction

**Oles 2022 systematic review of all GAS literature.** The review found high patient satisfaction for genital procedures overall, but little concordance between study methods. Approximately 90% of patient-focused outcome metrics appeared only once or twice across the literature, and only 1% used metrics validated in the transgender population. Standardisation of outcome instruments remains an unmet need.<sup>[[16]](#ref16)</sup>

---

## Evidence Limitations

- Nikkels 2019 (n = 143) and the later Nikkels 2024 cohort (n = 310) are retrospective single-centre series.<sup>[[2]](#ref2)[[17]](#ref17)</sup>
- Ho 2025 (n = 70) compared excision and fulguration in one retrospective centre, with technique chosen by surgeon preference. Fulguration had lower blood loss and shorter operating time; vagino-cutaneous fistula occurred in 10% of each group, while vaginal-remnant recurrence was 4/50 (8%) after excision and 1/20 (5%) after fulguration. These data do not establish equivalent complication rates.<sup>[[8]](#ref8)</sup>
- The Cohen 2020 split-gracilis series (n = 16) reports no urinary fistula at mean 361 d and needs validation in larger cohorts.<sup>[[10]](#ref10)</sup>
- No randomized trials compare the four approaches.
- Validated PROMs specific to vaginectomy outcomes are lacking; Oles 2022 documents the broader transgender-PROM gap.<sup>[[16]](#ref16)</sup>
