# Skin Graft Vaginoplasty (FTSG / STSG Canal Augmentation)

**Skin graft vaginoplasty** uses a full-thickness (FTSG) or split-thickness (STSG) skin graft to augment or replace penile skin as neovaginal canal lining when there is insufficient genital skin to achieve adequate depth during penile inversion vaginoplasty (PIV).<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[2]](#ref2)[[3]](#ref3)</sup> It is a common adjunct to PIV: in the retrospective 15-year Ghent series (single surgeon, n = 384), 85.7% of patients needed a full-thickness graft to lengthen the vaginal vault toward the 14 cm depth target.<sup>[[4]](#ref4)</sup> It also serves as a standalone revision technique after failed primary vaginoplasty. In a retrospective comparison, perineal FTSG revision had 81% success, 12.5 cm depth and 19% rectal perforation.<sup>[[5]](#ref5)</sup>

This is the dedicated atlas page. For the host operation, see [Penile Inversion Vaginoplasty](/docs/surgical-techniques/04k-gender-affirming-surgery/penile-inversion-vaginoplasty). For the principal alternatives when donor tissue is insufficient, see [Peritoneal Pull-Through Vaginoplasty](/docs/surgical-techniques/04k-gender-affirming-surgery/peritoneal-pull-through-vaginoplasty) and [Intestinal Vaginoplasty](/docs/surgical-techniques/04k-gender-affirming-surgery/intestinal-vaginoplasty). For stenosis-management context, see [Neovaginal Stenosis Management](/docs/surgical-techniques/04k-gender-affirming-surgery/neovaginal-stenosis-management).

## Indications

Skin-graft augmentation is indicated when penile skin alone cannot provide adequate neovaginal depth. Risk factors for insufficient genital skin include:<sup>[[4]](#ref4)[[6]](#ref6)</sup>

- **Prior circumcision.** Circumcised patients often lack enough penile skin, and the Ghent series highlights this group.<sup>[[4]](#ref4)</sup>
- **Prior orchiectomy.** Associated with 3 times greater odds of needing an extragenital skin graft (Sineath 2022, prospective cohort of 235).<sup>[[6]](#ref6)</sup>
- **Stretched penile skin length &lt; 8 cm.**<sup>[[6]](#ref6)</sup>
- **Pubertal suppression.** Pubertal blockade can limit genital skin development.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup>
- **Lichen sclerosus** and other conditions can reduce genital-skin quality.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup>

**Decision threshold:** in the Amsterdam series, the option to add a graft was offered when penile skin length fell between 7 and 12 cm.<sup>[[7]](#ref7)</sup> In the Ghent series, 85.7% received an FTSG.<sup>[[4]](#ref4)</sup>

## Graft Types and Donor Sites

| Graft type | Donor site | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| **Full-thickness skin graft (FTSG)** — most commonly used | **Scrotal skin** (preferred — hairless after electrolysis, readily available); abdominal / lower-abdomen / groin | Scrotal FTSG gives a hairless canal after electrolysis<sup>[[4]](#ref4)[[8]](#ref8)</sup> |
| **Split-thickness skin graft (STSG)** | Thigh, buttock | More common in modified Abbe–McIndoe technique for vaginal agenesis; also applicable in transgender vaginoplasty<sup>[[9]](#ref9)[[10]](#ref10)</sup> |
| **Extragenital FTSG** | Flank, iliac crest, thigh | When scrotal skin is unavailable / insufficient<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[11]](#ref11)</sup> |

Preoperative electrolysis of the donor site (especially scrotum) prevents intravaginal hair growth.<sup>[[12]](#ref12)</sup>

---

## Surgical Technique

1. **Donor-site harvest.** Scrotal-skin FTSG is most common; epilation is completed ≥ 6 weeks preoperatively.
2. **Graft preparation.** Defat (FTSG); fenestrate as needed.
3. **Anastomosis to inverted penile skin tube.** The graft is sutured to the distal end of the inverted penile skin tube, extending the canal lining.
4. **Inversion into the canal.** The composite tube (penile skin and graft) is inverted into the dissected rectoprostatic space.
5. **Stent / mold** placed to maintain canal during initial healing.<sup>[[2]](#ref2)[[13]](#ref13)</sup>

## Outcomes

| Outcome | Skin-graft vaginoplasty | Comparator |
|---|---|---|
| Intraoperative neovaginal depth | mean 13.8 ± 1.4 cm (Amsterdam series, with or without graft) | — |
| 1-year depth | mean 11.5 ± 2.5 cm (15% decline, mostly in first 3 weeks) | — |
| Revision FTSG depth | 12.5 ± 2.8 cm | Intestinal revision 15.9 ± 1.4 cm (p < 0.01)<sup>[[5]](#ref5)</sup> |
| Penetrative intercourse, self-reported (Ghent, all patients) | 97.2% | — |
| Orgasm, self-reported (Ghent, all patients) | 83.4% | — |
| Aesthetic and functional satisfaction | No significant difference with or without graft (Buncamper 2017) | — |

Anchors: Buncamper 2017<sup>[[7]](#ref7)</sup>, Opsomer Ghent 15-yr<sup>[[4]](#ref4)</sup>, van der Sluis 2016 revision comparison<sup>[[5]](#ref5)</sup>.

## Complications

A 2021 systematic review (eight case series and one cohort study; 1,622 patients) reported the following outcomes when skin grafts were used as adjuncts to PIV:<sup>[[3]](#ref3)</sup>

| Complication | Incidence | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| Neovaginal stenosis | 1.2–12% | Managed with intensified dilation or revision |
| Neovaginal necrosis (partial / complete) | 0–22.8% | Range across graft series<sup>[[3]](#ref3)</sup> |
| Lubrication satisfaction | Low in select studies | Skin-lined canals do not self-lubricate<sup>[[3]](#ref3)[[12]](#ref12)</sup> |
| Rectal perforation, primary | 1.6% (6/384) | Ghent series<sup>[[4]](#ref4)</sup> |
| Rectal perforation, revision FTSG | 19% | Compared with 10% for intestinal revision<sup>[[5]](#ref5)</sup> |
| Donor-site morbidity | Minimal | Scarring possible<sup>[[3]](#ref3)</sup> |
| Late revision surgery | 37.1% | Diabetes was the only independent risk factor (Ghent)<sup>[[4]](#ref4)</sup> |

## Graft vs No Graft — The Buncamper 2017 Controversy

A prospective, non-randomized Amsterdam study (n = 100; 32 with and 68 without additional FTSG) reported:<sup>[[7]](#ref7)</sup>

- No significant difference in neovaginal shrinkage, aesthetic outcome, sexual function or genital self-image.
- No evidence that the graft worsens outcomes through contraction or insensitivity.
- No clear improvement in aesthetic results from preserving more penile skin for vulvar construction.
- The authors could confirm neither the argument for nor the argument against routine graft use.<sup>[[7]](#ref7)</sup>

## Skin Graft vs Alternative Revision Techniques

A retrospective chart review of revision vaginoplasty (n = 53; median follow-up 3.2 years):<sup>[[5]](#ref5)</sup>

| Outcome | Perineal FTSG revision (n = 32) | Laparoscopic intestinal revision (n = 21) |
|---|---|---|
| Success | 81% | 91% |
| Depth | 12.5 ± 2.8 cm | 15.9 ± 1.4 cm (p < 0.01) |
| OR time | 131 ± 35 min (p < 0.01) | 191 ± 45 min |
| Rectal perforation | 19% | 10% |

FTSG revision had the shorter OR time, a shallower neovagina and more rectal perforations; the authors reported that overall complications did not differ significantly. For revision technique selection see the [Neovaginal Stenosis Management](/docs/surgical-techniques/04k-gender-affirming-surgery/neovaginal-stenosis-management) page (Step 3 framework: peritoneal-flap first-line, sigmoid for complete obliteration, FTSG when shortest OR is the priority).

## Postoperative Care

- **Lifelong vaginal dilation.** Up to 3 times daily initially, tapering over months. Failure to dilate leads to stenosis and depth loss.<sup>[[12]](#ref12)</sup>
- **External lubricant always required.** The skin-lined canal does not self-lubricate.<sup>[[12]](#ref12)</sup>
- **Pelvic-floor PT** for persistent pain or dilation difficulty.<sup>[[12]](#ref12)</sup>

See the [PIV page](/docs/surgical-techniques/04k-gender-affirming-surgery/penile-inversion-vaginoplasty) dilation-protocol table for the full schedule and the [Neovaginal Stenosis Management](/docs/surgical-techniques/04k-gender-affirming-surgery/neovaginal-stenosis-management) page for the PFPT framework (Jiang OHSU pre and post 28% versus 86%, FLOWER RCT no-routine-benefit signal, Shamamian dilation-difficulty predictors).

---

## Evidence Limitations

- The Salibian 2021 review pooled case series and one cohort study (1,622 patients); the authors called for direct comparative data on complications, depth and lubrication.<sup>[[3]](#ref3)</sup>
- Buncamper 2017 (n = 100) is a prospective, non-randomized comparison of graft versus no graft and found no significant difference in outcomes.<sup>[[7]](#ref7)</sup>
- van der Sluis 2016 (n = 53) is a retrospective comparison of FTSG versus intestinal revision.<sup>[[5]](#ref5)</sup>
- The 0–22.8% range for neovaginal necrosis probably reflects differences in definitions and technique across series.<sup>[[3]](#ref3)</sup>
- Long-term outcomes beyond 5 years are poorly characterized across most graft series.
