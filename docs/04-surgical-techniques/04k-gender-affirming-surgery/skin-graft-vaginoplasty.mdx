# Skin Graft Vaginoplasty (FTSG / STSG Canal Augmentation)

**Skin graft vaginoplasty** uses a full-thickness (FTSG) or split-thickness (STSG) skin graft to augment or replace penile skin as neovaginal canal lining when there is insufficient genital skin to achieve adequate depth during penile inversion vaginoplasty (PIV).<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[2]](#ref2)[[3]](#ref3)</sup> It is a common adjunct to PIV: in the retrospective 15-year Ghent series (single surgeon, n = 384), 85.7% of patients needed a full-thickness graft to lengthen the vaginal vault toward the 14 cm depth target.<sup>[[4]](#ref4)</sup> It also serves as a standalone revision technique after failed primary vaginoplasty. A 2023 single-patient technical report described a primary graft-only variant: inner scrotal FTSG lined the entire canal, while penile skin was reserved for external vulvar construction; comparative outcomes remain unknown.<sup>[[18]](#ref18)</sup> In a retrospective revision cohort of 50 transgender and three cisgender women, perineal FTSG achieved vaginal reconstruction in 26/32 procedures (81%), mean intraoperative depth of 12.5 ± 2.8 cm, and rectal perforation in 6/32 (19%).<sup>[[5]](#ref5)</sup>

This is the dedicated atlas page. For the host operation, see [Penile Inversion Vaginoplasty](/docs/surgical-techniques/04k-gender-affirming-surgery/penile-inversion-vaginoplasty). For the principal alternatives when donor tissue is insufficient, see [Peritoneal Pull-Through Vaginoplasty](/docs/surgical-techniques/04k-gender-affirming-surgery/peritoneal-pull-through-vaginoplasty) and [Intestinal Vaginoplasty](/docs/surgical-techniques/04k-gender-affirming-surgery/intestinal-vaginoplasty). For stenosis-management context, see [Neovaginal Stenosis Management](/docs/surgical-techniques/04k-gender-affirming-surgery/neovaginal-stenosis-management).

## Indications

Skin-graft augmentation is indicated when penile skin alone cannot provide adequate neovaginal depth. A 2026 international surgeon Delphi panel considered prior pelvic surgery or radiotherapy and Charlson Comorbidity Index ≥3 relative contraindications to vaginoplasty; these factors warrant individualized preoperative assessment.<sup>[[14]](#ref14)</sup> Risk factors for insufficient genital skin include:<sup>[[4]](#ref4)[[6]](#ref6)</sup>

- **Prior circumcision.** Circumcised patients often lack enough penile skin, and the Ghent series highlights this group.<sup>[[4]](#ref4)</sup>
- **Prior orchiectomy.** Associated with 3 times greater odds of needing an extragenital skin graft (Sineath 2022, prospective cohort of 235).<sup>[[6]](#ref6)</sup>
- **Preoperative skin lengths.** In Sineath’s 235-patient cohort, stretched penile skin length &lt; 8 cm was associated with extragenital graft need; scrotal skin length &gt; 10 cm was associated with low likelihood of needing an extragenital graft.<sup>[[6]](#ref6)</sup>
- **Pubertal suppression.** Pubertal blockade can limit genital skin development.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup>
- **Lichen sclerosus** and other conditions can reduce genital-skin quality; in one 52-patient peritoneal pull-through series, lichen sclerosus was the indication in 3 patients with genital-skin insufficiency.<sup>[[20]](#ref20)</sup>

**Decision threshold:** in the Amsterdam series, the option to add a graft was offered when penile skin length fell between 7 and 12 cm.<sup>[[7]](#ref7)</sup> In the Ghent series, 85.7% received an FTSG.<sup>[[4]](#ref4)</sup>

## Graft Types and Donor Sites

| Graft type | Donor site | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| **Full-thickness skin graft (FTSG)** — most commonly used | **Scrotal skin** (preferred; remove hair and assess for regrowth before harvest); abdominal / lower-abdomen / groin | Scrotal FTSG can extend canal lining after preoperative hair removal; confirm clearance after a full hair-growth cycle before surgery.<sup>[[4]](#ref4)[[8]](#ref8)[[17]](#ref17)</sup> |
| **Split-thickness skin graft (STSG)** | Thigh, buttock | More common in modified Abbe–McIndoe vaginal reconstruction<sup>[[9]](#ref9)[[10]](#ref10)</sup>; may also augment gender-affirming canal lining. Bene et al. describe a general preference for extragenital FTSG to limit secondary contracture and stenosis risk.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup> |
| **Extragenital FTSG** | Groin, then lower abdomen (Bene et al. preference); flank, iliac crest, hip, thigh and gluteal sites also described | When scrotal skin is unavailable / insufficient; Bene et al. avoid hip and thigh sites because of visible scars<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[11]](#ref11)</sup> |

Preoperative hair removal of canal-lining donor skin, especially scrotal skin, aims to reduce intravaginal hair-related complications. Laser hair removal and electrolysis are both used.<sup>[[12]](#ref12)[[15]](#ref15)</sup>

## Surgical Technique

1. **Donor-site harvest.** Scrotal-skin FTSG is most common. Assess canal-lining donor skin for hair regrowth before surgery; University of Iowa Health Care advises waiting one full hair cycle (6+ weeks) after hair removal to confirm clearance.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[17]](#ref17)</sup>
2. **Graft preparation.** Defat (FTSG); fenestrate as needed.
3. **Anastomosis to inverted penile skin tube.** The graft is sutured to the distal end of the inverted penile skin tube, extending the canal lining.
4. **Inversion into the canal.** The composite tube (penile skin and graft) is inverted into the dissected rectoprostatic space.
5. **Vaginal packing or stent / mold** is generally placed to maintain the canal and promote graft adherence during initial healing.<sup>[[2]](#ref2)[[13]](#ref13)[[19]](#ref19)</sup>

**Revision FTSG (Amsterdam series).** Incise the residual canal lining bilaterally and reopen the scarred vesicorectal tunnel with rectal palpation or a probe to localize the rectum and a urinary catheter to localize the urethra. Obtain meticulous hemostasis before suturing the defatted graft to the residual lining.<sup>[[5]](#ref5)</sup> In the Amsterdam perineal skin-graft approach, the peritoneal fold remains closed; if an abdominal approach opens it, the authors use peritoneal flaps or intestinal tissue rather than graft-only lining.<sup>[[2]](#ref2)</sup>

## Outcomes

| Outcome | Reported cohort (graft-specific only where stated) | Comparator |
|---|---|---|
| Intraoperative neovaginal depth | mean 13.8 ± 1.4 cm (Amsterdam series, with or without graft) <sup>[[7]](#ref7)</sup> | — |
| 1-year depth | mean 11.5 ± 2.5 cm (Buncamper cohort, with or without graft); the largest decline (15%) occurred in the first 3 postoperative weeks <sup>[[7]](#ref7)</sup> | — |
| Intraoperative revision depth | 12.5 ± 2.8 cm (FTSG) | 15.9 ± 1.4 cm (intestinal; p &lt; 0.01)<sup>[[5]](#ref5)</sup> |
| Penetrative intercourse, self-reported (Ghent patients reporting outcome) | 97.2% <sup>[[4]](#ref4)</sup> | — |
| Orgasm, self-reported (Ghent patients reporting outcome) | 83.4% <sup>[[4]](#ref4)</sup> | — |
| Aesthetic and functional satisfaction | No significant difference with or without graft (Buncamper 2017) | — |

Anchors: Buncamper 2017<sup>[[7]](#ref7)</sup>, Opsomer Ghent 15-yr<sup>[[4]](#ref4)</sup>, van der Sluis 2016 revision comparison<sup>[[5]](#ref5)</sup>.

## Complications

A 2021 systematic review identified eight case series and one cohort study (reported total 1,622 PIV patients) in which adjunct skin grafts were used in some or all patients. Some study populations overlapped. The following complication rates describe those cohorts with varying graft exposure.<sup>[[3]](#ref3)</sup>

| Complication | Incidence | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| Neovaginal stenosis after primary PIV in reviewed cohorts | 1.2–12% <sup>[[3]](#ref3)</sup> | Managed with intensified dilation or revision; rates are not necessarily graft-specific. |
| Complete stenosis after revision FTSG | 5/30 (17%) | Amsterdam patients with ≥1-year follow-up; 3 later had intestinal and 2 repeat FTSG revision.<sup>[[5]](#ref5)</sup> |
| Neovaginal necrosis (partial / complete) | 0–22.8% | Range across mixed PIV cohorts that used grafts in some cases; definitions and graft exposure varied, so this is not a graft-specific rate.<sup>[[3]](#ref3)</sup> |
| Partial skin-graft loss | No pooled graft-specific rate | Limited loss may heal with local wound care; extensive loss or delayed healing may require debridement, closure, or resurfacing.<sup>[[3]](#ref3)</sup> |
| Lubrication satisfaction | Low in select studies | Skin-lined canals do not self-lubricate<sup>[[3]](#ref3)[[12]](#ref12)</sup> |
| Rectal perforation, primary | 1.6% (6/384) | Ghent series<sup>[[4]](#ref4)</sup> |
| Rectal perforation, revision FTSG | 19% | Compared with 10% for intestinal revision<sup>[[5]](#ref5)</sup> |
| Donor-site morbidity | Site-dependent | Scrotal harvest may add little morbidity when skin would otherwise be discarded; extragenital harvest can leave visible scars.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[3]](#ref3)</sup> |
| Late revision surgery | 37.1% of all 384 PIV cases | Ghent cohort; most received FTSG, but this is not a graft-specific rate. Diabetes was the only independent risk factor.<sup>[[4]](#ref4)</sup> |

## Graft vs No Graft — The Buncamper 2017 Controversy

A prospective, non-randomized Amsterdam study (n = 100; 32 with and 68 without additional FTSG) reported:<sup>[[7]](#ref7)</sup>

- No significant difference in neovaginal shrinkage, aesthetic outcome, sexual function or genital self-image.
- In this 32-graft versus 68-no-graft cohort, no statistically significant difference in shrinkage or reported function was detected; the study does not exclude smaller or later differences.<sup>[[7]](#ref7)</sup>
- No clear improvement in aesthetic results from preserving more penile skin for vulvar construction.
- The authors could confirm neither the argument for nor the argument against routine graft use.<sup>[[7]](#ref7)</sup>

## Skin Graft vs Alternative Revision Techniques

A retrospective chart review of revision vaginoplasty (n = 53; median follow-up 3.2 years):<sup>[[5]](#ref5)</sup>

| Outcome | Perineal FTSG revision (n = 32) | Laparoscopic intestinal revision (n = 21) |
|---|---|---|
| Successful reconstruction; adequate depth for penetration among patients with ≥1-year follow-up | 26/32 (81%); 24/30 (80%) | 19/21 (91%); 18/20 (90%, p = 0.45 for depth)<sup>[[5]](#ref5)</sup> |
| Intraoperative depth | 12.5 ± 2.8 cm | 15.9 ± 1.4 cm (p &lt; 0.01)<sup>[[5]](#ref5)</sup> |
| OR time | 131 ± 35 min (p < 0.01) | 191 ± 45 min |
| Rectal perforation | 19% | 10% |

In this retrospective comparison with different case mixes, FTSG revision had shorter operative time and shallower intraoperative depth. Rectal perforation occurred in 6/32 FTSG and 2/21 intestinal procedures (p = 0.46); overall complication rates did not differ significantly.<sup>[[5]](#ref5)</sup> For revision technique selection see the [Neovaginal Stenosis Management](/docs/surgical-techniques/04k-gender-affirming-surgery/neovaginal-stenosis-management) page (Step 3 framework: FTSG or peritoneal-flap revision for moderate stenosis; intestinal revision for severe stenosis or complete obliteration).

## Postoperative Care

- **Vaginal dilation.** Up to three times daily may be needed during recovery to maintain canal depth and width; thereafter, dilation remains important for months to years and for most patients may be a lifelong commitment. Inadequate dilation can contribute to stenosis and depth loss.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[12]](#ref12)</sup>
- **For dilation difficulty, increase external lubricant and consider a smaller dilator before gradually increasing size.** The skin-lined canal does not lubricate naturally.<sup>[[12]](#ref12)</sup>
- **Pelvic-floor PT** for persistent pain or dilation difficulty.<sup>[[12]](#ref12)</sup>

See the [PIV page](/docs/surgical-techniques/04k-gender-affirming-surgery/penile-inversion-vaginoplasty) dilation-protocol table for the full schedule and the [Neovaginal Stenosis Management](/docs/surgical-techniques/04k-gender-affirming-surgery/neovaginal-stenosis-management) page for the PFPT framework (Jiang OHSU postoperative pelvic-floor dysfunction: 28% with pre- and postoperative PT versus 86% with postoperative-only PT<sup>[[16]](#ref16)</sup>, FLOWER RCT no-routine-benefit signal, Shamamian dilation-difficulty predictors).

## Evidence Limitations

- The Salibian 2021 systematic review summarized eight case series and one cohort (reported total 1,622 patients), including mixed graft/no-graft cohorts and overlapping patient populations; it called for direct comparative data on complications, depth and lubrication.<sup>[[3]](#ref3)</sup>
- Buncamper 2017 (n = 100) is a prospective, non-randomized comparison of graft versus no graft and found no significant difference in outcomes.<sup>[[7]](#ref7)</sup>
- van der Sluis 2016 (n = 53) is a retrospective comparison of FTSG versus intestinal revision.<sup>[[5]](#ref5)</sup>
- The 0–22.8% range for neovaginal necrosis probably reflects differences in definitions and technique across series.<sup>[[3]](#ref3)</sup>
- Long-term outcomes beyond 5 years are poorly characterized across most graft series.
