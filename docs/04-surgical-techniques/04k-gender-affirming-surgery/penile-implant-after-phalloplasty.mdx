# Erectile Prosthesis (Penile Implant) After Gender-Affirming Phalloplasty

**Penile implant placement after gender-affirming phalloplasty** is a complex, high-risk procedure that gives transmasculine individuals a rigid neophallus for penetrative intercourse. When a patient chooses an implant, placement is commonly a later stage of masculinizing genital reconstruction.<sup>[[2]](#ref2)</sup> Programs commonly wait 9–12 months after phalloplasty, until the flap has healed and vascular pedicle anatomy and reliable micturition have been confirmed.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[2]](#ref2)[[5]](#ref5)</sup> In Rooker’s 2019 review, 22 of 23 included retrospective reports were tabulated; among those selected reports, 792/1,056 (75.0%) phalloplasty patients received a prosthesis. This within-review fraction does not estimate implant uptake among all phalloplasty patients.<sup>[[3]](#ref3)</sup> Of these, 36.2% (287/792) had a prosthesis complication requiring additional surgery; 60.0% (305/508) had the original implant at follow-up (mean 2.6 years among 433 patients in retention studies reporting mean follow-up), and 83.9% (193/230) achieved penetration in studies reporting that outcome.<sup>[[3]](#ref3)</sup>

This is the dedicated atlas page covering all prosthesis types and variants. For the cohort-level framework, see [Masculinizing Gender-Affirming Surgery](/docs/surgical-techniques/04k-gender-affirming-surgery/masculinizing-surgery). For prior reconstruction, see the phalloplasty pages in 04k. [Gender-Affirming Hysterectomy](/docs/surgical-techniques/04k-gender-affirming-surgery/gender-affirming-hysterectomy) is pathway-dependent rather than a prerequisite for every penile implant.<sup>[[35]](#ref35)</sup>

---

## Indication and Context

The neophallus constructed during phalloplasty lacks corpora cavernosa and therefore has no intrinsic erectile capacity. A penile prosthesis can provide rigidity sufficient for penetrative intercourse without an external device.<sup>[[3]](#ref3)[[4]](#ref4)</sup>

---

## Timing and Prerequisites

A Delphi-derived consensus protocol (Levy 2026; 21 surgeons agreed to participate, 16 voted in the second round) addresses pre-, peri- and postoperative care. Insertion is typically delayed for 9–12 months after phalloplasty to allow:<sup>[[5]](#ref5)[[2]](#ref2)[[6]](#ref6)</sup>

- Complete flap healing and maturation.
- Verification of vascular pedicle anatomy (Doppler ultrasound or angiography).
- Establishment of reliable micturition (if urethral lengthening was performed).
- Adequate neophallus sensation: some surgeons advocate waiting until protective sensation returns.<sup>[[7]](#ref7)</sup> A 2023 Asia-Pacific Society of Sexual Medicine consensus advises waiting until the neourethra has healed and reasonable genital sensitivity is present.<sup>[[36]](#ref36)</sup>
- Resolve any urethral complications (fistula, stricture). At one center, preimplant urinary evaluation includes uroflowmetry and postvoid residual; cystoscopy with retrograde urethrography is used when symptoms or urinary studies indicate it.<sup>[[30]](#ref30)</sup>
- **Active infection:** Defer AMS 700 implantation for active urogenital or operative-site skin infection. Titan labeling excludes active infection anywhere, particularly urinary or genital infection.<sup>[[27]](#ref27)[[28]](#ref28)</sup>
- **Other device-specific contraindications:** For InhibiZone-coated AMS 700, exclude rifampin or minocycline/other tetracycline sensitivity and systemic lupus erythematosus. Titan labeling also excludes silicone/polyurethane hypersensitivity, unresolved urinary problems such as elevated residual volume from obstruction or neurogenic bladder, and unwillingness to undergo revision surgery. Check the selected device’s full labeling.<sup>[[27]](#ref27)[[28]](#ref28)</sup>

A 2017 surgical review describes prosthesis placement as an optional third stage around 9–12 months after phalloplasty; a 2024 textbook describes waiting at least 6 months after the final phalloplasty operation. These sources describe clinical intervals, not a universal timing threshold.<sup>[[2]](#ref2)[[21]](#ref21)</sup>

## Prosthesis Types

| Type | Examples | Cylinder configuration | Key features |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Three-piece inflatable (IPP)** | AMS 700, Coloplast Titan | Single or dual | Inflatable implants overall accounted for 83.6% (689/824) of reported implants in Rooker 2019; the three-piece share was not reported; inflatable cycling; requires reservoir + pump<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[3]](#ref3)</sup> |
| **Two-piece inflatable** | AMS Ambicor | Two cylinders in the labeled configuration | Preconnected pump and cylinders with proximal fluid reservoirs; no separate abdominal reservoir. Deflation requires bending the phallus, which may increase tissue-injury or migration risk in a neophallus; comparative rates are unknown.<sup>[[33]](#ref33)[[34]](#ref34)</sup> |
| **Malleable (semirigid)** | Coloplast Genesis, AMS Spectra | Single or dual | Always semirigid; simpler surgery; no mechanical parts; permanently firm<sup>[[2]](#ref2)[[3]](#ref3)</sup> |
| **ZSI 475 FtM (hydraulic)** | ZSI 475 FtM | Single | Purpose-designed for neophallus; large base for pubic fixation; testicle-shaped pump; realistic hard glans<sup>[[8]](#ref8)[[5]](#ref5)</sup> |
| **ZSI 100 FtM (malleable)** | ZSI 100 FtM | Single | Purpose-designed malleable for neophallus; specific anchoring<sup>[[8]](#ref8)[[9]](#ref9)</sup> |

**Single versus dual cylinder:** In nine reports that specified cylinder number, 314/515 (61.0%) implant operations used one cylinder and 201/515 (39.0%) used two.<sup>[[3]](#ref3)</sup> Available girth and the absence of a native septum influence the choice for an individual neophallus.<sup>[[3]](#ref3)[[10]](#ref10)</sup> At one center, when a paired inflatable implant is adapted for single-cylinder placement, both cylinders are filled, one is cut away, and the tubing end is capped.<sup>[[30]](#ref30)</sup>

## Surgical Technique

Implantation in a neophallus differs fundamentally from native penile-prosthesis surgery due to the absence of corpora cavernosa, tunica albuginea, and native crura. Give perioperative antimicrobial prophylaxis according to the center's implant protocol and selected device labeling; InhibiZone coating does not replace standard prophylaxis.<sup>[[27]](#ref27)[[30]](#ref30)</sup>

Three technical steps:<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[4]](#ref4)</sup>

### 1. Cylinder-channel creation

A space is carefully dissected within the neophallus by blunt and sharp dissection through flap tissue. In a reported malleable-implant technique, distal dilation stops with ample tissue coverage remaining at the tip.<sup>[[34]](#ref34)</sup> Protect the vascular pedicle and neourethra during dissection. If catheterization is chosen to delineate a lengthened neourethra, plan wire-guided or flexible cystoscopic rescue after failed passage rather than repeated blind attempts. In the 20-patient SAFE trial during IPP insertion or revision, initial standard Foley placement succeeded in 3/10 allocated patients; this small trial does not establish a universal catheter strategy.<sup>[[30]](#ref30)[[31]](#ref31)</sup> If the neourethra is injured during dissection, implantation should be abandoned so the urethra can be repaired, with the prosthesis reattempted about 4–6 months later.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup>

### 2. Proximal fixation to the pubic bone

With no native crura for rear-tip anchoring, proximal fixation is generally obtained from pubic bone or periosteum; the specific site and method depend on the patient’s flap and pedicle anatomy.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup> Techniques:<sup>[[11]](#ref11)[[10]](#ref10)</sup>

- **Bone anchoring.** Corticotomy in the pubic symphysis with bone drill; rear-tip extender seated with anchoring sutures through bone.<sup>[[11]](#ref11)</sup>
- **Infrapubic approach.** Horizontal incision anterior to the pubic symphysis allows direct neophallus-tract dissection and anchor-site creation.<sup>[[12]](#ref12)[[6]](#ref6)</sup>
- **Neoscrotal approach.** Advocated by some surgeons for better access.<sup>[[10]](#ref10)</sup>

A described periosteal-suture arrangement uses three heavy, nonabsorbable sutures placed in a triangle on the anterior pubis (unilateral for a single cylinder, bilateral for two), with adequate vertical distance between the upper and lower sutures: the lower lateral stitch influences erect-phallus angle and stability, and the upper lateral and medial stitches set implant position relative to the phallus. Incorporating the adductor tendon is avoided because of chronic pain.<sup>[[22]](#ref22)</sup> An alternative preference uses Smith & Nephew TwinFix-type bone anchors placed directly into the pubic ramus, with the preloaded permanent suture secured to the rear-tip extender; this has been reported to make anchor removal easier at later revision, which is relevant in a population with frequent prosthesis revisions.<sup>[[25]](#ref25)</sup> Comparative evidence that any of these fixation preferences improves outcomes has not been reported.

**Reservoir placement and the vascular pedicle.** Incision and reservoir route can be selected around the flap's vascular pedicle: a cephalad infrapubic approach has been described when the pedicle enters inferolaterally (for example, a pedicled anterolateral-thigh flap, or a free flap connected to the superficial femoral artery), and a groin-crease approach when it enters superolaterally from the inferior epigastric artery. Ipsilateral space-of-Retzius placement is avoided when the pedicle arises from the femoral or inferior epigastric system, and blind ectopic placement behind the rectus sheath can also injure the pedicle; a described alternative is an anterior extraperitoneal route to the iliac fossa or paracolic gutter, with tubing kept deep to rectus and exiting lateral to the superficial inguinal ring to avoid the pedicle and ilioinguinal nerve. Dissection caudal to the pubic symphysis is avoided because of the venous plexus, clitoral nerve, and native urethra. These route preferences should be tailored to the individual's documented flap anatomy.<sup>[[22]](#ref22)</sup>

### 3. Cylinder wrapping (neotunica construction)

To provide proximal support and distal cushioning where native tunica is absent, surgeons have described a proximal graft sock, a distal cap, or a full-cylinder sleeve; graft configuration varies:<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[13]](#ref13)[[14]](#ref14)[[30]](#ref30)[[34]](#ref34)</sup>

- **Gore-Tex (PTFE) sleeve.** In Zuckerman’s uncontrolled 31-patient mixed-indication series (48% gender dysphoria), 81% were sexually active after implantation at a mean 59.7 months of follow-up; five prostheses were explanted for infection or erosion.<sup>[[13]](#ref13)</sup>
- **Dacron vascular graft.** Falcone / London series; fitted around proximal and distal cylinder to anchor the device and prevent apical protrusion.<sup>[[10]](#ref10)[[14]](#ref14)[[15]](#ref15)</sup>
- **Allografts** (acellular dermal matrix): alternative biologic wrapping.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup> An acellular-matrix cap sutured to the cylinder tip has also been described to add distal cushioning and fixation through scarring.<sup>[[25]](#ref25)</sup>

A distal "sock" or cap is placed over the cylinder tip to distribute pressure and reduce distal-erosion risk through thin neophallus skin.<sup>[[4]](#ref4)</sup> One described fabrication cuts about 6 cm of Dacron or Gore-Tex vascular graft for a proximal sock, folding and suturing its base with a small anteromedial opening for tubing; a distal cap of a 3–4 cm piece of vascular graft, attributed to Jordan et al., has its distal corners rounded and its tip closed with a continuous suture, with a traction stitch passed through the cap and the cylinder tip's pre-made holes and brought back through the inner surface to the outside for loading on a Keith needle. The phallus is dilated one Hegar size larger than the cap (for example, an 18 mm dilator for a 16 mm Dacron cap).<sup>[[22]](#ref22)[[23]](#ref23)</sup> Dacron and Gore-Tex sock materials for this purpose have also been described separately.<sup>[[24]](#ref24)</sup> A full sheath, rather than a sock or cap alone, has been reported as necessary with a malleable implant to prevent displacement.<sup>[[22]](#ref22)</sup> For inflatable prostheses, the reservoir is placed extraperitoneally and the pump in the neoscrotum; Falcone’s series used two stages for component placement.<sup>[[15]](#ref15)</sup>

### Staged vs single-stage approach

Some centres use a two-stage approach for inflatable prostheses:<sup>[[15]](#ref15)</sup>

- **Stage 1.** Extraperitoneal reservoir placement, glans sculpture and testicular implant insertion.
- **Stage 2.** About three months later, place the cylinder(s) and exchange the temporary testicular implant for the pump in Falcone's described protocol.<sup>[[38]](#ref38)</sup>

Staging separates reservoir insertion from cylinder and pump placement and may shorten each operative session; Falcone’s 247-patient retrospective series did not compare operative time or complications with single-stage implantation.<sup>[[15]](#ref15)</sup>

### Postoperative use

At one center, inflatable devices are left partially inflated for 7–10 days, first cycled at 4–6 weeks, and cleared for penetrative sexual activity at three months.<sup>[[30]](#ref30)</sup>

## Outcomes

### Functional

- **Penetrative intercourse:** 77% used the device for intercourse (Falcone 2018), 83.9% (193/230 in Rooker studies reporting this outcome), 85.7% reported regular penetrative intercourse (12/14 questionnaire respondents, ZSI 475, Neuville; series mean follow-up 8.9 months), and 93% (13/14 with the implant in place, ZSI 100, Pigot; mean follow-up 6.3 months).<sup>[[3]](#ref3)[[15]](#ref15)[[8]](#ref8)[[9]](#ref9)</sup>
- **Patient satisfaction:** 88% (Falcone 2018) and 92.8% of 14 respondents (Neuville 2019).<sup>[[15]](#ref15)[[8]](#ref8)</sup>
- **IIEF-5:** Mean 20.2/25 among 14/20 questionnaire respondents in the ZSI 475 FtM series (series mean follow-up 8.9 months).<sup>[[8]](#ref8)</sup>
- **Self-Esteem and Relationship score:** mean 84.5/100 among 14/20 questionnaire respondents (series mean follow-up 8.9 months).<sup>[[8]](#ref8)</sup>

### Device survival

| Series | Device | Follow-up | Survival / retention |
|---|---|---|---|
| Falcone 2018 (n = 247)<sup>[[15]](#ref15)</sup> | Inflatable (Dacron-modified) | 20 mo mean | **5-year Kaplan–Meier device-survival estimate: 78%** |
| Kocjancic review<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup> | IPP (various) | 5 yr | 42–78% retention |
| Rooker SR (n = 792)<sup>[[3]](#ref3)</sup> | Mixed | 2.6 yr mean among 433 in retention studies reporting mean follow-up | 60% (305/508) original implant present at follow-up |
| ZSI FtM (Amsterdam)<sup>[[17]](#ref17)</sup> | Hydraulic + malleable | 26 mo hydraulic / 32 mo malleable median | 3-yr explantation-free survival after first implantation: 39% hydraulic / 31% malleable |
| ZSI 475 FtM (Lyon; Marchand 2025, 89 patients, 130 implantations)<sup>[[16]](#ref16)</sup> | Hydraulic | 3.9 yr median | Median survival **5.4 yr**; 4-yr **74%** |
| Chen infrapubic (n = 107)<sup>[[6]](#ref6)</sup> | Modified commercial | 79.8 wk | 16% revision rate |

In a separate single-center retrospective cohort of 473 first-time three-piece inflatable implants with at least five years of follow-up, 240 (50.7%) underwent no further surgery within five years. This is freedom from reoperation, not mechanical device survival or a comparison of device types.<sup>[[26]](#ref26)</sup>

## Complications

Complication rates are higher than in cisgender penile-prosthesis surgery. Authors attribute this to the absence of native corporal tissue, thin flap coverage and the need for pubic fixation.<sup>[[3]](#ref3)[[18]](#ref18)</sup> Vascular pedicle injury can also cause flap loss: one arterial pedicle lesion in a 50-patient ZSI cohort led to necrosis and partial phallic amputation despite repair.<sup>[[17]](#ref17)</sup>

**Fraiman 2024 meta-analysis:**<sup>[[18]](#ref18)</sup>

- **Inflatable**: 38% (95% CI 21–59%).
- **Malleable**: 37% (95% CI 18–62%).
- **No significant difference** between types.

| Complication | Inflatable | Malleable |
|---|---|---|
| Infection | 8.5% (Falcone 2018) to 22.4% (15/67 inflatable implants, Briles 2022); 14.5% pooled | 7.7% (1/13, Briles) to 11.2% pooled<sup>[[15]](#ref15)[[18]](#ref18)[[19]](#ref19)</sup> |
| Mechanical dysfunction | 12.9% pooled; 40.0% (52/130 implantations, ZSI 475, Marchand) | 9.1% pooled<sup>[[16]](#ref16)[[18]](#ref18)</sup> |
| Dislocation / detachment | 5.7% pooled | 14.9% pooled<sup>[[18]](#ref18)</sup> |
| Erosion / extrusion | 1.5% (2/130 implantations, ZSI 475, Marchand) | 7.6% pooled<sup>[[16]](#ref16)[[18]](#ref18)</sup> |
| Leakage / malposition | 5.4% leakage pooled (Fraiman); 5.4% malposition (7/130 implantations, ZSI 475, Marchand) | Not estimated<sup>[[16]](#ref16)[[18]](#ref18)</sup> |
| **Explantation rate** | **19%** (95% CI 9–38%) | **13%** (95% CI 4–33%)<sup>[[18]](#ref18)</sup> |
| **Revision rate** | 43% (Falcone 2018, n = 247) | 40% (12/30 with follow-up; 40 enrolled, Sun 2023)<sup>[[15]](#ref15)[[12]](#ref12)</sup> |

### Risk factors for complications

- **Second implantation:** OR 3.5 (p = 0.03) for late complications requiring surgical intervention versus first implantation.<sup>[[17]](#ref17)</sup>
- **Urethral lengthening:** among first implantations, HR 2.6 (95% CI 1.3–5.0, p = 0.01) for time to late complication.<sup>[[17]](#ref17)</sup>
- **Phalloplasty flap type.** Significantly affects infection risk (p = 0.013).<sup>[[15]](#ref15)</sup>
- **Center experience signal.** In one 80-patient center, infection was less likely later in its chronological implant series (p = 0.03); this observational association does not establish a surgeon-specific volume threshold.<sup>[[19]](#ref19)</sup>

## Purpose-Designed (ZSI FtM) vs Modified Cisgender Prostheses

A debate concerns whether purpose-designed FtM prostheses (ZSI 475 FtM, ZSI 100 FtM) outperform modified cisgender devices (AMS 700, Coloplast Titan / Genesis with Dacron wrapping).<sup>[[14]](#ref14)[[8]](#ref8)</sup>

- The ZSI 475 FtM (manufacturer design) has a large base for pubic bone fixation, a realistically shaped hard glans, and a testicle-shaped pump.<sup>[[8]](#ref8)</sup> Zephyr's FAQ directs US patients to implantation centers abroad, citing the cost and uncertainty of FDA authorization.<sup>[[37]](#ref37)</sup>
- **Falcone 2026 comparative study.** In a single-center prospective series (n = 47; 18 ZSI 475 FtM, 29 Dacron-modified cis-male devices), operative time was shorter with ZSI FtM (mean 85 versus 105 min, p = 0.001). Infection (5.6% versus 24.1%) and mechanical failure (22.2% versus 31%) did not differ significantly. Explantation-free survival was higher at 12 months (100% versus 79.3%, p = 0.039) but not at 24 months (94.4% versus 72.4%, p = 0.062).<sup>[[14]](#ref14)</sup>
- In a 50-patient retrospective cohort, three-year complication-free survival after primary ZSI implantation was 20% for hydraulic and 31% for malleable implants; three-year explantation-free survival was 39% and 31%, respectively. Across 85 primary and repeat implantations, late complications requiring surgery occurred in 56% of hydraulic and 58% of malleable implants.<sup>[[17]](#ref17)</sup>
- **No device has proven general superiority.** The field continues to evolve.<sup>[[3]](#ref3)[[14]](#ref14)</sup> In a single-center retrospective cohort of 694 first-time three-piece implants after gender-affirming phalloplasty, the five-year cumulative incidence of revision for mechanical failure was 17.6% for AMS 700 CX/CXR and 8.3% for Titan Zero Degree.<sup>[[29]](#ref29)</sup> Brand use shifted over time, and phalloplasty flap and cylinder mixes differed; this association does not establish a causal device advantage.<sup>[[29]](#ref29)</sup>

## Penile Implants in AMAB Individuals After Phalloplasty

Penile prostheses are also implanted in AMAB individuals undergoing phalloplasty for penile inadequacy (bladder exstrophy–epispadias complex, penile cancer, trauma, micropenis). Pang 2025 SR (n = 184): complication rates up to 64.3%, explantation up to 40% for erosion, but 80–100% satisfaction.<sup>[[20]](#ref20)</sup>

---

## Emerging Approaches

- **Osteointegrated penile epithesis.** External prosthetic phallus anchored to the pubic bone via osseointegrated implants; alternative to traditional phalloplasty and internal prosthesis.<sup>[[2]](#ref2)</sup>
- Continued refinement of purpose-designed devices with improved anchoring and materials.
- **Standardised consensus protocols** for perioperative care (Levy 2026 Delphi).<sup>[[5]](#ref5)</sup>

---

## Key Counselling Points

Patients must be thoroughly counselled that:<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[3]](#ref3)[[15]](#ref15)</sup>

1. **Further surgery is common.** Revision occurred in 43% of the largest single-center inflatable series (n = 247). A 2026 international Delphi panel considered reimplantation acceptable after one implant failure but discouraged it after multiple complications; this is expert consensus, not a validated cutoff.<sup>[[15]](#ref15)[[32]](#ref32)</sup>
2. Complication rates are significantly higher than cisgender penile prosthesis surgery.
3. Reported satisfaction is high (88–93% in single-center series) and most patients use the device for intercourse.
4. **Inflatable versus malleable trade-offs.** Inflatable devices cycle between rigid and flaccid states but require a pump and reservoir; assess whether the patient has the dexterity and strength to operate the pump. Malleable devices remain semirigid. Pooled observational series reported device dysfunction in 12.9% and 9.1%, respectively; these descriptive estimates do not establish a device-type advantage.<sup>[[18]](#ref18)[[27]](#ref27)</sup>
5. **No ideal prosthesis** specifically designed for the neophallus exists yet; purpose-built devices are improving.

---

## Evidence Limitations

- **Rooker 2019 SR (1,056 phalloplasty patients overall, including 1,020 transmasculine patients; 792 patients with prosthesis)** is the largest aggregate; component series heterogeneous in device, technique, follow-up.<sup>[[3]](#ref3)</sup>
- **Fraiman 2024 meta-analysis** (9 studies) found no significant difference in complication or explantation rates between inflatable and malleable prostheses; the underlying studies are small and retrospective.<sup>[[18]](#ref18)</sup>
- **Levy 2026 Delphi consensus** offers a consensus-based protocol; the authors note that no universally accepted guideline exists.<sup>[[5]](#ref5)</sup>
- **No RCTs** comparing device types or anchoring techniques.
- **Long-term outcomes:** In a single-center retrospective cohort of 694 first-time three-piece implants, the 15-year cumulative incidence of surgery for mechanical failure was 30.6%, while the Kaplan–Meier probability of any revision was 65.1%. These surgery-based estimates do not directly measure every device’s function, and the cohort partly overlaps earlier London reports.<sup>[[29]](#ref29)</sup>
- **Validated PROMs** for transmasculine implant outcomes are lacking; IIEF-5 and Self-Esteem-and-Relationship scores adapted from cisgender populations.<sup>[[8]](#ref8)</sup>
