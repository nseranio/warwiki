# Endoureterotomy

**Endoureterotomy** is an endoscopic treatment for ureteral stricture in which the narrowed ureter is incised full-thickness from within the lumen and then stented while the ureter heals in an expanded configuration. It is an endoscopic option for selected benign strictures; balloon dilation is another, and a 2026 expert algorithm favors balloon dilation as the initial endoscopic treatment for many simple strictures. Endoscopic durability is most favorable for short, nonocclusive, well-vascularized lesions; long, obliterative, ischemic, radiation-related, or recurrent strictures generally favor reconstruction.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[2]](#ref2)[[3]](#ref3)[[4]](#ref4)[[40]](#ref40)</sup>

This page covers endoscopic incision of ureteral strictures. For reconstructive alternatives, see [Ureteroureterostomy](/docs/surgical-techniques/04d-upper-tract-reconstruction/ureteroureterostomy), [Ureteral Reimplantation](/docs/surgical-techniques/04d-upper-tract-reconstruction/ureteral-reimplantation), [Buccal Mucosa Graft Ureteroplasty](/docs/surgical-techniques/04d-upper-tract-reconstruction/bmg-onlay-ureter), and [Upper Tract Reconstruction Principles](/docs/surgical-techniques/04d-upper-tract-reconstruction/upper-tract-reconstruction-principles). Endoscopic paclitaxel dilation is covered separately under [Drug-Coated Balloon Therapy](/docs/surgical-techniques/drug-coated-balloon).

## Core Principle

The operation releases the scar ring by making a longitudinal full-thickness incision through mucosa, muscularis, and adventitia until periureteral fat is seen.<sup>[[5]](#ref5)[[6]](#ref6)[[7]](#ref7)</sup> A ureteral stent then splints the incised segment for roughly 4-8 weeks, allowing the lumen to re-epithelialize open rather than recontracting immediately.

The analogy is direct visual internal urethrotomy: both procedures are attractive because they are low morbidity, and both are vulnerable to recurrence when the stricture is long, ischemic, dense, or repeatedly treated.

**Endopyelotomy** is the same concept applied at the ureteropelvic junction (UPJ). It has a narrower role now that minimally invasive [pyeloplasty](/docs/surgical-techniques/04d-upper-tract-reconstruction/pyeloplasty) is widely available.

## Historical Development

| Era | Development | Relevance |
|---|---|---|
| Davis intubated ureterotomy | Open intubated ureterotomy concept | Established the principle that an incised ureter can heal over a splint |
| 1980s | Antegrade percutaneous endopyelotomy | Moved UPJ incision into the endoscopic era |
| 1992 | Meretyk, Clayman, and colleagues reported a dedicated endoureterotomy series | Early 62% success and the beginning of stricture-specific endoscopic series<sup>[[5]](#ref5)</sup> |
| 1993 | Acucise cutting balloon introduced | Fluoroscopy-guided incision without direct visualization<sup>[[8]](#ref8)</sup> |
| 1997 | Ho:YAG laser endoureterotomy series reported | Helped establish laser incision as the dominant modern technique<sup>[[9]](#ref9)</sup> |
| Modern era | Laser incision plus selective balloon dilation, steroid injection, dual stents, or metallic stents | Attempts to improve an operation whose core limitation is restenosis biology<sup>[[10]](#ref10)[[11]](#ref11)[[12]](#ref12)[[13]](#ref13)</sup> |

## Approaches

| Approach | How It Is Done | Best Fit | Limitations |
|---|---|---|---|
| Retrograde ureteroscopic | Semirigid or flexible ureteroscope passed from bladder to stricture; incision under direct vision | Most native ureteral strictures; outpatient endoscopic workflow | Can be difficult across tight, proximal, or ureteroenteric strictures |
| Antegrade percutaneous | Nephrostomy access with antegrade ureteroscope/nephroscope | Proximal ureter, UPJ, transplant, or ureteroenteric strictures where retrograde access is poor | Requires percutaneous access and its morbidity |
| Combined antegrade-retrograde | Rendezvous access from both ends | Near-obliterative strictures where a wire cannot be passed safely | More complex; often a signal that reconstruction may be more durable |
| Acucise cutting balloon | Fluoroscopic balloon with electrocautery wire incises while inflated | Historical option for UPJ or ureteral strictures | No direct visualization, no biopsy, less control of incision depth; now largely replaced by laser<sup>[[8]](#ref8)[[14]](#ref14)[[15]](#ref15)</sup> |

## Retrograde Laser Technique

1. **Cystoscopy and retrograde pyelography** define stricture location, length, severity, and upstream dilation.
2. **Safety wire placement** across the stricture is attempted. A completely impassable segment should prompt antegrade access, rendezvous strategy, or reconstruction rather than blind force.
3. **Ureteroscopy** confirms the stricture endoscopically. Suspicious lesions should be biopsied before incision; malignant strictures are not endoureterotomy disease.
4. **Incision planning** uses the ureteral level, cross-sectional vascular anatomy and endoscopic orientation to select a line away from adjacent vessels and bowel. The incision line varies with ureteral level and nearby vessels and bowel.
5. **Laser incision** is made with a small Ho:YAG fiber, commonly 200-365 microns, through the full scar thickness until periureteral fat is visible.<sup>[[9]](#ref9)[[16]](#ref16)</sup>
6. **Balloon dilation** may be added after incision, typically to open the released scar ring and confirm full expansion.<sup>[[11]](#ref11)[[16]](#ref16)</sup>
7. **Adjunctive triamcinolone** has been used off-label in selected protocols. Its incremental benefit and optimal dose are not established by the uncontrolled combination-treatment series; it is not a routine requirement.<sup>[[2]](#ref2)[[12]](#ref12)</sup>
8. **Stenting** splints the incision. Standard double-J stents are common; larger-caliber endoureterotomy stents or dual ipsilateral stents have been used to increase luminal scaffolding.<sup>[[2]](#ref2)[[13]](#ref13)[[17]](#ref17)</sup>
9. **Drainage and follow-up** depend on approach and extravasation. Stents are usually removed after 4-8 weeks.

### Incision Direction

At the UPJ, Sampaio and Favorito's vascular anatomy work supports avoiding deep anterior incision because prominent anterior vessels were common, while the posterior surface was often vessel-free only within a limited segment. For UPJ endopyelotomy, Sampaio and Favorito advise a lateral deep incision on anatomic grounds.<sup>[[18]](#ref18)</sup>

For upper ureteral strictures, a posterolateral incision is commonly described; for distal strictures, an anteromedial line is described to avoid nearby vessels. These are orientation guides, not universally safe planes. At the iliac crossing, in a transplant, after diversion or after prior reconstruction, establish the actual vascular and bowel relationships before a full-thickness incision.<sup>[[32]](#ref32)</sup> One anatomic teaching rule, based on the ureter's segmental arterial supply running medially in the proximal ureter, posteriorly in the mid-ureter and laterally in the distal ureter, pairs each level with an incision on the opposite side of the vessel course: lateral proximally, anterior in the mid-ureter, and medial distally. This mid-ureter anterior line is not otherwise covered above; treat it, like the other directional guides, as a starting orientation to be confirmed against the anatomy actually encountered.<sup>[[35]](#ref35)</sup>

## Patient Selection

Endoureterotomy is a selection-sensitive treatment.

| Feature | Favors Endoureterotomy | Favors Reconstruction |
|---|---|---|
| Length | Usually &lt;2 cm and nonocclusive; individualize near the boundary<sup>[[40]](#ref40)</sup> | ≥2 cm, especially &gt;3 cm; weigh anatomy and stricture biology |
| Etiology | Nonischemic, stone-related, short iatrogenic narrowing | Radiation, devascularization, ureteroenteric ischemia, dense fibrosis |
| Lumen | Partial stricture; wire can pass | Obliterated or near-obliterated segment |
| Renal function | Adequate function and potentially recoverable drainage | Poor function warrants individualized assessment of reconstruction, drainage or nephrectomy; no split-function cutoff alone selects the operation |
| Prior treatment | First endoscopic attempt | Failed prior endoureterotomy or repeated dilation |
| Hydronephrosis | Mild or moderate | Severe chronic dilation |
| Patient priority | Lowest morbidity and accepts recurrence risk | Maximal durable patency |

Classic series repeatedly found worse outcomes with long strictures, ischemic strictures, compromised ipsilateral renal function, and ureteroenteric strictures.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[2]](#ref2)[[3]](#ref3)[[4]](#ref4)</sup>

The classic prognostic-factor framework for endopyelotomy names stricture length, hydronephrosis grade, ipsilateral renal function and a crossing vessel (especially combined with a higher hydronephrosis grade) as the recognized predictors of outcome; teaching materials have proposed specific numeric cutoffs for candidacy (for example, function above roughly 25-30% and a stricture under about 1.5-2 cm) that vary between sources and are not a single agreed threshold, which is why no split-function cutoff alone selects the operation on this page.<sup>[[33]](#ref33)</sup>

## Outcomes: Endoscopic Ureteral Stricture and UPJ Series

| Series | N | Technique | Success | Key Lesson |
|---|---:|---|---:|---|
| Meretyk et al. | 13 | Endoureterotomy | 62% | Early dedicated series; steroid injection appeared promising<sup>[[5]](#ref5)</sup> |
| Singal et al. | 22 treated (21 assessed; mixed native and ureteroenteric strictures) | Ho:YAG laser | 16/21 (76%) clinically well without recurrence | Length and etiology drove results; three patients had only 3–6 months of follow-up<sup>[[9]](#ref9)</sup> |
| Wolf et al. | 38 benign strictures with ipsilateral function ≥25% | Mixed endoureterotomy | 80% 3-year success | None of 9 procedures with ipsilateral function &lt;25% succeeded; ureteroenteric strictures performed worse<sup>[[2]](#ref2)</sup> |
| Richter et al. | 19 strictures with compromised vascular supply | Pooled endopyelotomy/ureterotomy with stenting | 17/19 (89.5%) for the pooled subgroup | Native-ureter-only success was not reported separately. Balloon dilation succeeded in 33/37 (89.2%) short strictures with intact vascularity, with poorer results in long or poorly vascularized strictures.<sup>[[19]](#ref19)</sup> |
| Razdan et al. | 50 patients (mixed benign and malignant; native and ureteroenteric) | Ureteroscopic endoureterotomy | 74% overall in the mixed cohort | All seven strictures &gt;2 cm failed; dual stenting succeeded in 8/10 recurrent cases. The pooled rate is not native benign stricture success.<sup>[[3]](#ref3)</sup> |
| Gnessin et al. | 35 | Ho:YAG laser | Symptom-free 29/35 (82%); radiographically unobstructed 26/33 (78.7%) at median 27 months | Nonischemic success 100% versus 64.7% for ischemic strictures<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup> |
| Corcoran et al. | 34 | Balloon dilation and/or Ho:YAG endoureterotomy | 85% (29/34) | Mean stricture length 1.6 cm; mean 1.7 procedures per patient<sup>[[4]](#ref4)</sup> |
| Ou et al. | 95 patients receiving endourologic treatment | Mixed endoscopic | 51.6% with symptomatic improvement and radiographic blockage alleviation | 95.7% by the same success definition in a separate 47-patient reconstruction group<sup>[[10]](#ref10)</sup> |

In a retrospective series of 35 benign strictures, 29/35 (82%) were symptom-free and 26/33 (78.7%) were free of radiographic obstruction at median 27 months; outcomes were better for nonischemic than ischemic strictures.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup> Broader cohorts report lower success when long, complex, ischemic, or recurrent strictures are included.

## Ureteroenteric Anastomotic Strictures

Ureteroenteric anastomotic strictures are harder endoscopic targets because ischemia, scarring, prior diversion, and altered anatomy are common. For a short, accessible ureteroenteric stricture, an endoscopic first attempt may be considered after counseling about recurrence and repeat intervention. In a 2026 systematic review, open revision had higher primary patency than endoscopic treatment in seven nonrandomized comparative cohorts (368 renal units; RR 2.31, 95% CI 1.54–3.47), while endoscopic laser results were better for strictures &lt;1 cm; definitions and cohorts varied.<sup>[[38]](#ref38)</sup>

| Series | Technique | Success | Note |
|---|---|---:|---|
| Wolf et al. | Mixed endoureterotomy | 32% 3-year success | Markedly worse than benign native strictures<sup>[[2]](#ref2)</sup> |
| Lin et al. | Acucise | 30% (3/10 benign strictures after excluding malignancy; patent at 18 and 30 months) | Limited durability for ureteroenteric disease<sup>[[20]](#ref20)</sup> |
| Touiti et al. | Acucise | 50% ureteroileal; 83% ureterovesical | Site-specific results in six strictures per group; median follow-up 16 months<sup>[[15]](#ref15)</sup> |
| Laven et al. | Endoureterotomy vs open revision | 8/16 renal units (50%) vs 12/15 (80%); median follow-up 35 vs 34 months | Higher reported success after open revision in this cohort; success meant radiologic improvement or full activity without pain, infection, stent, or nephrostomy<sup>[[21]](#ref21)</sup> |
| Gomez et al. | First-line endourologic management | 20/28 strictures (71.4%); median follow-up 25 months | Among 27 patients, three grade II and five grade III complications were reported; selection and risk require counseling<sup>[[22]](#ref22)</sup> |
| Katims et al. | Laser incision, triamcinolone, balloon dilation, then parallel stents or upside-down nephrostomy tubes | 20/24 strictures (83.3%) in 21 patients; median follow-up 30 months | Uncontrolled combination protocol; does not isolate steroid benefit<sup>[[12]](#ref12)</sup> |

## Transplant and UPJ Settings

### Transplant Ureteral Strictures

Transplant strictures are anatomically and biologically distinct. Gdor et al.’s 12-patient transplant-kidney cohort included nine ureterovesical anastomotic strictures and three UPJ obstructions; among eight lesions ≤10 mm across these sites, success (stable creatinine without hydronephrosis) occurred in 5/5 after combined balloon dilation and laser endoureterotomy versus 1/3 after balloon dilation alone (mean follow-up 52 months for the eight).<sup>[[23]](#ref23)</sup> These are small numbers, but they are consistent with the general pattern that very short strictures are the best endoscopic candidates.

### Endopyelotomy for UPJ Obstruction

Endopyelotomy has lower durability than pyeloplasty. A comparative effectiveness study found higher failure after endopyelotomy than open pyeloplasty, while minimally invasive pyeloplasty was more durable than endopyelotomy.<sup>[[24]](#ref24)</sup> Contemporary review data place retrograde endopyelotomy success below pyeloplasty, with best results in short stenoses, modest hydronephrosis, preserved renal function, and no major crossing-vessel concern.<sup>[[25]](#ref25)[[26]](#ref26)</sup>

Endopyelotomy is now a selective option for favorable adult UPJ obstruction, failed patients who cannot tolerate reconstruction, or those prioritizing the lowest morbidity after counseling. [Pyeloplasty](/docs/surgical-techniques/04d-upper-tract-reconstruction/pyeloplasty) remains the durability standard. For recurrent UPJ obstruction, a 2026 meta-analysis of 12 comparative studies (549 adults and children) associated redo pyeloplasty with higher study-defined success than endopyelotomy or balloon dilation (OR 6.32, 95% CI 3.25–12.32); variable outcomes and high-to-serious risk of bias limit certainty.<sup>[[42]](#ref42)</sup>

Endoluminal ultrasonography, using a small probe (approximately 6 Fr)<sup>[[43]](#ref43)</sup> passed over a guidewire and oriented against the wire and fluoroscopic catheter position, has been described to map a crossing vessel and the length and position of a high-insertion septum before incision, with visible pulsation through the ureteroscope alone considered an unreliable way to localize a crossing vessel; CT angiography can aid preoperative planning when the probe is unavailable, but a small 1999 prospective comparison found fewer crossing vessels with CT than with endoluminal ultrasonography; a negative CT angiogram alone does not establish a vessel-free incision path.<sup>[[41]](#ref41)</sup> In one series using this technique, sonographic findings excluded 5 of 37 patients from the procedure, and among the 32 who were treated, 87.5% (28/32) had pain-free resolution of obstruction on diuretic renal scan at a mean 10-month follow-up.<sup>[[36]](#ref36)</sup> This older, single-center series did not test routine probe use or compare it with CT angiography.<sup>[[36]](#ref36)</sup>

A Cleveland Clinic variant of the antegrade approach places the stent before incising the UPJ, then incises over the stent with a Bugbee electrode; proponents describe the pre-placed stent as marking the intended incision line and guiding marsupialization into the pelvis, and argue that it avoids passing a larger tapered endopyelotomy stent through a freshly incised UPJ, which they believe may reduce avulsion risk. A tapered stent and nephrostomy tube follow. No comparative outcome data show this sequence is safer or more effective than incising first and stenting afterward.<sup>[[37]](#ref37)</sup>

## Adjunctive Measures

| Adjunct | Rationale | Evidence Signal |
|---|---|---|
| Balloon dilation after incision | Expands the released scar ring and confirms lumen caliber | Often combined with Ho:YAG incision; a 19-series meta-analysis of balloon dilation reported 54% ± 14% success (mean ± standard error) at 6–12 months, without establishing later durability.<sup>[[11]](#ref11)[[27]](#ref27)</sup> |
| Triamcinolone injection | Reduces fibroblast proliferation and collagen deposition in the incised bed | Off-label; observational association and a small combination-treatment series<sup>[[2]](#ref2)[[12]](#ref12)</sup> |
| Larger stents | Splint the incision more aggressively | Wolf et al. associated &gt;=12F stenting with better results in strictures &gt;1 cm<sup>[[2]](#ref2)</sup> |
| Dual ipsilateral stents | Increase scaffold diameter without a custom endoureterotomy stent | In a randomized bilharzial-stricture study, two 7F stents achieved 83.9% success versus 53.1% with one 7F stent among 63 patients completing follow-up longer than 18 months; generalizability to other etiologies is uncertain.<sup>[[13]](#ref13)</sup> |
| Covered metallic stents | Longer-term internal scaffold for patients unfit for reconstruction or after endoscopic release | Allium and other covered stents show improving but still selective evidence<sup>[[28]](#ref28)[[29]](#ref29)[[30]](#ref30)</sup> |

Metal stents are maintenance or salvage tools. They do not replace reconstruction in healthy surgical candidates. The 2025 meta-analysis pooled different devices and cohorts. A prospective Allium series of 310 patients with 330 ureteral strictures reported declining surgical success over 3 years.<sup>[[28]](#ref28)[[30]](#ref30)</sup>

## Complications

| Complication | Comment |
|---|---|
| Extravasation / urinoma | Expected risk after full-thickness incision; usually managed with stenting, drainage, or nephrostomy if needed |
| Bleeding | Hemorrhage risk varies by technique. Conlin and Bagley reported no significant hemorrhage in 21 ureteroscopic UPJ incisions.<sup>[[34]](#ref34)</sup> In a 52-procedure balloon-cautery endopyelotomy series, Schwartz and Stoller reported four vascular injuries, including three renal-artery injuries treated with embolization; one presented 12 days later.<sup>[[39]](#ref39)</sup> |
| UTI / fever | Treat with culture-directed antibiotics and ensure drainage. Infectious complications are likely underreported; one series recorded them in about 5% of patients. Follow standard antimicrobial-prophylaxis guidance for the procedure.<sup>[[34]](#ref34)</sup> |
| Ureteral perforation | Usually stented; risk rises with tight strictures and difficult access |
| Vascular injury | Incision direction matters, especially near the UPJ and iliac vessels. In a historical 52-procedure retrograde balloon-cautery endopyelotomy series, four vascular injuries (7.7%) occurred, including an ovarian-vein laceration requiring nephrectomy and vein ligation.<sup>[[39]](#ref39)</sup> This technique-specific frequency does not estimate risk with direct-vision laser incision. |
| Recurrent stricture | Main long-term failure mode; recurrence risk depends on selection |
| Need for secondary procedure | Repeat endoscopy, chronic stent, nephrostomy, or definitive reconstruction may be required; covered metal stents can also fail through migration, occlusion, encrustation, or infection.<sup>[[30]](#ref30)</sup> |

## Timing of Failure and Follow-Up

Failure timing varies by cohort. In Gnessin et al., all but one failure occurred before 9 months; Wolf et al. observed no benign-stricture failure after 11 months, with 25 patients still at risk. In a broader mixed endourologic cohort, Ou et al. reported a median recurrence time of 51 months.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[2]](#ref2)[[10]](#ref10)</sup> Surveillance is close during the first postoperative year and continues thereafter.

Typical follow-up includes:

- stent removal at 4-8 weeks,
- renal ultrasound or CT urogram after stent removal,
- functional drainage study when symptoms, renal function, or imaging are equivocal,
- low threshold for earlier imaging if flank pain, fever, rising creatinine, or recurrent hydronephrosis develops,
- continued longer-term surveillance for ureteroenteric, ischemic, transplant, or metal-stent cases.

## Endoureterotomy vs Reconstruction

Endoureterotomy offers shorter operative time, less blood loss, faster recovery, and outpatient feasibility. Its cost is durability. Ou et al. found overall success of 51.6% for endourologic treatment compared with 95.7% for surgical reconstruction in benign ureteral strictures, in a retrospective, predominantly stone-related cohort.<sup>[[10]](#ref10)</sup> Zi et al. similarly found that balloon dilation could approximate reconstructive approaches early but lost ground at 12 and 24 months.<sup>[[31]](#ref31)</sup>

| Scenario | Usually Better Choice |
|---|---|
| Short (&lt;2 cm), benign, partial, nonischemic stricture | Initial balloon dilation or endoureterotomy may be considered after discussing recurrence risk; the 2026 expert algorithm favors balloon dilation for many simple strictures.<sup>[[40]](#ref40)</sup> |
| Long (&gt;2 cm) proximal/mid stricture | [BMG ureteroplasty](/docs/surgical-techniques/04d-upper-tract-reconstruction/bmg-onlay-ureter), augmented anastomotic repair, appendix, or ileal substitution depending anatomy |
| Upper/mid complete transection or short excisable stricture | [Ureteroureterostomy](/docs/surgical-techniques/04d-upper-tract-reconstruction/ureteroureterostomy) |
| Distal stricture | [Ureteral reimplantation](/docs/surgical-techniques/04d-upper-tract-reconstruction/ureteral-reimplantation), psoas hitch, Boari flap, or selective non-transecting reimplant |
| Radiation-induced, ischemic, recurrent, or obliterated stricture | Reconstruction favored |
| Ureteroenteric anastomotic stricture | Endoscopic attempt is reasonable in selected cases; revision remains the durability standard |
| Poor operative candidate | Endoureterotomy, balloon dilation, chronic stent, nephrostomy, or metallic stent may be pragmatic |

## Operative Pearls

- Confirm benign disease before incision; biopsy suspicious strictures.
- Do not force a wire through an obliterated segment; use antegrade or rendezvous access or reconstruct.
- A therapeutic incision releases the full scar thickness, but only along a planned safe line; avoid uncontrolled extension into adjacent structures.
- Match incision direction to the ureteral level and actual vessels; a lateral or posterolateral rule is unsafe for every distal stricture.
- Counsel patients that low morbidity is paired with lower durability than reconstruction.
- Recurrent narrowing after a technically adequate endoureterotomy should usually prompt consideration of definitive reconstruction rather than serial incision, except when endoscopic treatment is palliative.<sup>[[40]](#ref40)</sup>
- For ureteroenteric strictures, counsel on recurrence and revision options. Steroid-containing combination protocols remain incompletely validated.
- Preserve future reconstruction options by avoiding devascularizing or traumatic repeated instrumentation.

## Summary

Endoureterotomy is an option for short, benign, partial, nonischemic ureteral strictures with preserved renal function. Ho:YAG laser incision under direct vision is the most commonly reported modern technique, combined with stenting and, selectively, balloon dilation. Steroid injection is an off-label adjunct with uncertain incremental benefit. Wolf et al. reported 80% Kaplan–Meier success at 3 years for 38 benign ureteral-stricture treatments with ipsilateral renal function at least 25%.<sup>[[2]](#ref2)</sup> In Ou et al.'s nonrandomized broader cohort, endourologic success was 51.6% versus 95.7% after reconstruction; the groups were selected rather than matched.<sup>[[10]](#ref10)</sup> Its role is selective: endoureterotomy may be considered as an initial endoscopic attempt for selected short, nonocclusive native strictures or ureteroenteric anastomotic strictures, and as a palliative or temporizing option for poor reconstructive candidates.<sup>[[2]](#ref2)[[23]](#ref23)</sup>
