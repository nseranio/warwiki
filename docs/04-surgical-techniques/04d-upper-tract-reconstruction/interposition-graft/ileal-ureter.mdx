# Ileal Ureter

**Ileal ureter replacement** interposes a vascularized ileal segment between the proximal ureter or renal pelvis and the bladder to reconstruct long-segment ureteral loss. It is a salvage operation for defects that cannot be solved by ureteroureterostomy, [ureteral reimplantation](/docs/surgical-techniques/04d-upper-tract-reconstruction/ureteral-reimplantation), [Boari flap / psoas hitch](/docs/surgical-techniques/04d-upper-tract-reconstruction/boari-flap-psoas-hitch), or oral-mucosa onlay reconstruction.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[2]](#ref2)[[3]](#ref3)</sup>

Ileum is mobile, vascular, and tubular. Bowel mucosa in the urinary tract produces mucus, absorbs ammonium chloride, secretes bicarbonate, and can cause metabolic acidosis, stones, infection, and long-term nutritional problems. Ileal ureter is therefore a salvage option for the native-tissue defects above and is not an early reconstructive step.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[4]](#ref4)[[5]](#ref5)</sup>

For the location-based ladder, see [Principles of Upper Tract Reconstruction](/docs/surgical-techniques/04d-upper-tract-reconstruction/upper-tract-reconstruction-principles). For the disease workup, see [Ureteral Stricture](/docs/clinical-conditions/03e-upper-tract/ureteral-stricture). For bowel physiology, see [Bowel Anatomy](/docs/foundations/anatomy-physiology/pelvis-support/bowel-anatomy), [Mucus Management](/docs/foundations/pharmacology/urinary-diversion-specific/mucus-management), [Vitamin B12 Supplementation](/docs/foundations/pharmacology/urinary-diversion-specific/vitamin-b12-supplementation), and [Urinary Acidifiers & Alkalinizers](/docs/foundations/pharmacology/urinary-diversion-specific/urinary-acidifiers).

## Indications

Ileal ureter is reserved for complex ureteral defects where a durable native repair would be under tension, ischemic, too narrow, or impossible.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[2]](#ref2)[[6]](#ref6)</sup>

Common indications:

- long-segment or panureteral benign stricture,
- radiation-induced ureteral stricture,
- extensive iatrogenic ureteral injury,
- retroperitoneal fibrosis,
- ureteral loss after oncologic resection; NCCN includes ileal replacement after complete ureterectomy for proximal or mid-ureteral tumors,<sup>[[27]](#ref27)</sup>
- failed prior pyeloplasty, reimplantation, Boari flap, or endoscopic management,
- bilateral extensive ureteral strictures,
- solitary kidney with a long ureteral stricture when salvage is worth the bowel burden,
- endemic infectious strictures such as tuberculosis or schistosomiasis where relevant.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[2]](#ref2)[[3]](#ref3)[[7]](#ref7)</sup>

### The threshold question

Before choosing ileal ureter, ask:

1. Can the kidney be drained with a simpler native repair?
2. Is renal function adequate enough to tolerate bowel in the urinary tract?
3. Is usable, non-radiated ileum available?
4. Is the bladder a safe low-pressure recipient?
5. Would renal autotransplantation or diversion be better for this patient?

## Contraindications and Caution Zones

| Problem | Why It Matters |
|---|---|
| Advanced renal insufficiency | Acidosis risk rises when renal acid excretion reserve is poor |
| Short bowel syndrome or major prior ileal resection | Nutritional and diarrheal morbidity may be unacceptable |
| Active inflammatory bowel disease involving ileum | Diseased bowel should not be used as urinary conduit |
| Radiation enteritis or irradiated ileum | High stricture, fistula, and leak risk |
| Uncontrolled UTI or urosepsis | Drain first; reconstruct electively after infection control |
| Small-capacity or high-pressure bladder | Distal implantation into hostile storage threatens the kidney |
| Severe hepatic dysfunction | Ammonium reabsorption can rarely contribute to encephalopathy |
| Poor surgical fitness for bowel anastomosis | Ileal ureter is a major abdominal reconstruction |

Renal insufficiency is a relative rather than absolute contraindication in many series; the authors of a 36-patient Yang-Monti series considered eGFR &lt;40 mL/min/1.73 m² a relative contraindication.<sup>[[8]](#ref8)</sup> The lower the eGFR and serum bicarbonate reserve, the stronger the case for shorter bowel length, a Yang-Monti configuration, renal autotransplantation, diversion, or nephrectomy when renal salvage is not worthwhile.<sup>[[4]](#ref4)[[8]](#ref8)</sup> One chapter's more conservative selection guidance uses an overall GFR above 60 mL/min and, after nephrostomy decompression, an affected-kidney contribution of at least 20% of overall renal function as thresholds for choosing salvage over nephrectomy; these are one author group's selection thresholds, not validated cutoffs, and the individualized reserve-based approach above remains the broader framework.<sup>[[25]](#ref25)</sup>

## Preoperative Planning

Preoperative planning defines the defect, the kidney, the bladder, and the bowel.

| Domain | Evaluation | Purpose |
|---|---|---|
| Renal function | Serum creatinine, eGFR, electrolytes, bicarbonate, MAG3 split function | Determines salvage value and metabolic reserve |
| Anatomy | CT urogram, MR urogram, antegrade/retrograde pyelography | Defines stricture length, laterality, renal pelvis target, and bladder reach |
| Drainage status | Nephrostomy, stent history, urine culture | Controls infection and lets inflammation settle before reconstruction |
| Bladder | Cystoscopy, capacity assessment, urodynamics selectively | Confirms safe distal reservoir |
| Bowel | Prior resections, radiation fields, IBD history, nutritional risk | Confirms usable non-radiated ileum |
| Metabolic baseline | Bicarbonate, chloride, potassium, B12 when relevant | Creates postoperative comparison |

Preoperative CT can estimate required ileal segment length, but final segment choice is made intraoperatively after the ureter is debrided to healthy tissue and the bladder and kidney are mobilized.<sup>[[9]](#ref9)</sup>

## Standard Ileal Ureter Technique

The standard operation uses a non-reconfigured ileal segment, usually 15-25 cm for unilateral reconstruction and longer for bilateral or panureteral defects.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[2]](#ref2)[[5]](#ref5)</sup>

### Step sequence

1. **Expose and stage the ureter.** Identify the diseased segment, preserve any usable periureteral blood supply, and debride to healthy ureter or renal pelvis.
2. **Prepare the proximal target.** Spatulate the proximal ureter or open the renal pelvis for a wide ileoureteral or ileopyelostomy anastomosis.
3. **Select ileum.** Choose a mobile, well-vascularized ileal segment while preserving the terminal ileum when possible to protect bile-salt and B12 absorption.
4. **Harvest on mesentery.** Isolate the segment on an intact mesenteric pedicle and confirm reach without twisting.
5. **Restore bowel continuity.** Perform stapled or hand-sewn ileoileostomy and close the mesenteric defect. Some surgeons instead leave the mesenteric defect open as a matter of practice, including with the Yang-Monti segment; no comparative internal-hernia outcome has been reported for either approach, so closing the defect remains the safer default absent such data.<sup>[[25]](#ref25)</sup>
6. **Orient isoperistaltically.** Place the oral end proximally and the aboral end distally so intestinal peristalsis supports antegrade urine transport.
7. **Perform proximal anastomosis.** Sew ileum to renal pelvis or proximal ureter with absorbable suture over a stent. An intraoperative leak check has been described: fill the renal pelvis with saline through the nephrostomy after completing the proximal anastomosis to look for extravasation.<sup>[[25]](#ref25)</sup>
8. **Perform distal anastomosis.** Implant the distal ileum into the bladder, usually refluxing unless antireflux reconstruction is specifically indicated. An analogous distal check fills the bladder with saline through the urethral or suprapubic catheter to check the distal anastomosis for leak.<sup>[[25]](#ref25)</sup>
9. **Stent and drain.** Provide intraluminal urinary drainage and Foley drainage; use an intra-abdominal drain according to the repair. No consensus device-removal schedule applies across reported series. In one robotic case report, the Foley catheter was removed on postoperative day 10 after a normal cystogram and a single intraluminal 15 Ch drain was removed endoscopically at four weeks; no intra-abdominal drain was placed.<sup>[[24]](#ref24)</sup>
10. **Retroperitonealize when possible.** Route the ileal ureter along the natural ureteral course while avoiding mesenteric tension, kinking, and internal hernia.

### Positioning and Room Setup

Ileal ureter substitution exposes the retroperitoneal kidney/ureter, the bowel, and the bladder in the same operation, so more than one intraoperative position is often needed rather than a single universal setup. One robotic case report of complete ureteral avulsion needed a change from the initial lateral decubitus position to supine and back to lateral decubitus to complete the renal, bowel, and bladder portions of the reconstruction (operative time approximately 300 minutes).<sup>[[24]](#ref24)</sup> A separate robotic series used preoperative CT measurement of the required ileal segment length specifically to plan the operation so that only a single intraoperative position change was needed, illustrating that repositioning (not a fixed single position) is the default expectation for this operation unless planned around.<sup>[[9]](#ref9)</sup> No ileal-ureter-specific VTE recommendation exists, but this is a major abdominal reconstruction with a bowel anastomosis and often a longer operative time than native-tissue ureteral repair; apply the general risk framework (age, obesity, prior VTE, malignancy, prolonged operation or immobility) on the [VTE Prophylaxis](/docs/foundations/pharmacology/perioperative-eras/vte-prophylaxis) page, weighted toward the higher end of that assessment for this operation.

### Key technical rules

- **Use the shortest segment that reaches without tension.** Bowel length is metabolic surface area.
- **Preserve terminal ileum when possible.** This protects B12 and bile-salt absorption.
- **Avoid mesenteric twist.** A perfect anastomosis fails if the ileal segment is ischemic or kinked.
- **Spatulate generously.** Anastomotic stricture usually starts with ischemia, tension, or caliber mismatch.
- **Control dead space.** Urine leak near bowel and retroperitoneum can become abscess or fistula.

## Yang-Monti Ileal Ureter

The **Yang-Monti** modification uses a short ileal segment that is divided, detubularized, and retubularized transversely into a longer narrow tube. It reduces bowel length and mucosal surface area while producing a caliber closer to ureter.<sup>[[8]](#ref8)[[10]](#ref10)</sup>

### Concept

- Isolate 5-7.5 cm of ileum.
- Divide into two or three short segments.
- Incise each segment along the antimesenteric or paramesenteric border.
- Retubularize the opened plates into a longer narrow tube over a catheter.
- Interpose the reconstructed tube as a neo-ureter.
- As an approximate planning guide, one 2-3 cm bowel segment yields roughly 8-10 cm of reconfigured tube, with additional segments joined in series when more length is needed; this is an approximate author-reported yield, not a fixed result for every patient.<sup>[[25]](#ref25)</sup>

### Advantages

- far less bowel used than standard ileal ureter,
- less mucus surface area,
- lower expected metabolic burden,
- suitable caliber for submucosal tunneling,
- useful in solitary kidney or mild-moderate CKD when defect length permits.<sup>[[8]](#ref8)[[10]](#ref10)</sup>

In the long-term Yang-Monti series, renal function parameters improved overall and metabolic complications were not observed, but patients with more advanced CKD (stage 3 in four cases) still deteriorated, so renal reserve limits candidacy.<sup>[[8]](#ref8)</sup>

## Other Modifications

### Tapered ileal ureter

Tapering reduces the diameter of a standard ileal segment to reduce urinary stasis and make antireflux implantation easier. The trade-off is more bowel manipulation, a longer staple or suture line, and potential ischemia.

### Ileal onlay flap

When a ureteral plate remains usable, detubularized ileum can be used as an onlay rather than circumferential replacement. This preserves native ureteral blood supply and reduces the amount of bowel mucosa exposed to urine. In a reconfigured intestinal-segment series, renal function was preserved in most evaluable renal units with only minor mucus symptoms.<sup>[[11]](#ref11)</sup>

### Combined Boari / ileal ureter

For full-length defects, a Boari flap with psoas hitch can bridge the distal gap while a shorter ileal segment bridges the proximal gap. This reduces the total ileal length needed and can preserve bladder-based distal reconstruction.<sup>[[12]](#ref12)</sup>

### Downward nephropexy

Downward nephropexy mobilizes the kidney caudally to reduce the remaining gap. It is useful when an ileal or Boari-based reconstruction almost reaches but would otherwise be under tension.<sup>[[13]](#ref13)[[14]](#ref14)</sup>

## Antireflux Strategy

Whether the distal ileovesical anastomosis needs an antireflux mechanism is debated.<sup>[[5]](#ref5)[[15]](#ref15)[[16]](#ref16)</sup>

### Refluxing anastomosis

Arguments for refluxing implantation:

- technically simpler,
- lower distal obstruction risk,
- acceptable when implanting into a normal low-pressure native bladder,
- long ileal segments may damp visible reflux through bowel peristalsis and capacitance.<sup>[[5]](#ref5)[[16]](#ref16)</sup>

The largest single-center series concluded that reflux prevention is of minor importance when the native bladder is the recipient, but more important when implanting into an intestinal reservoir.<sup>[[5]](#ref5)</sup>

### Antireflux anastomosis

Arguments for antireflux implantation:

- recurrent pyelonephritis concern,
- an intestinal reservoir recipient after confirming safe storage pressures,
- solitary kidney or vulnerable renal unit,
- pediatric or young patient with long expected exposure.

An antireflux mechanism does not make a high-pressure bladder safe, so storage dysfunction is addressed before distal implantation. Distal antireflux designs include submucosal tunneling and a nipple valve; Bonfig et al. described serous-lined extramural tunneling or distal intussusception into an intestinal reservoir.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[15]](#ref15)</sup> Xu et al. described a separate proximal iliopsoas tunnel at the ureter-to-ileum junction, with the distal ileum joined end-to-side to the bladder.<sup>[[17]](#ref17)</sup> Antireflux designs carry a risk of obstruction.

## Outcomes

| Study | N | Follow-up | Key Findings |
|---|---:|---|---|
| Chung 2006 | 56 | Mean 6 yr | Long-term bowel-replacement series; renal function and reintervention require continued surveillance<sup>[[3]](#ref3)</sup> |
| Armatys 2009 | 91 | Mean 36 mo | 74.7% stable/decreased creatinine; 3.3% anastomotic stricture and 6.6% fistula<sup>[[18]](#ref18)</sup> |
| Kocot 2017 | 157 | Mean 54.1 mo | 25-year single-center experience; creatinine stable or improved in most patients<sup>[[5]](#ref5)</sup> |
| Roth 2017 | 108 | Median 51 mo | 82.4% stable/improved renal function; bilateral repair increased renal-deterioration risk<sup>[[6]](#ref6)</sup> |
| Launer 2021 | 46 | Mean 4.4 yr | 83% success (no further open intervention); 24% major complication rate<sup>[[2]](#ref2)</sup> |
| Ali-El-Dein 2021 | 36 | Median 68 mo | Yang-Monti: improved eGFR overall, no metabolic complications reported<sup>[[8]](#ref8)</sup> |
| Yang 2023 | 15 | Median 14 mo | Totally intracorporeal robotic unilateral/bilateral ileal ureter feasible; functional success 86.7%<sup>[[19]](#ref19)</sup> |
| Ji 2026 | 48 operated; 36 with ≥6 mo follow-up | Median 24 mo in evaluable cohort | Success 33/36 (91.7%); major 30-day complications 9/48 (18.8%)<sup>[[20]](#ref20)</sup> |
| Wang 2026 | 82 | Median 48 mo clinical; SF-36 at 12 mo | Prospective single-arm cohort: among patients with poor baseline quality of life, 45.5–79.2% improved by ≥10 SF-36 points at 12 mo, depending on domain; 1 radiographic reobstruction and 8 grade III–IV complications<sup>[[29]](#ref29)</sup> |

The series measure different endpoints: creatinine was stable or decreased in 68/91 at mean 36 months, 38/46 required no further open surgery at mean 4.4 years, and 33/36 evaluable patients met a symptom, obstruction, and intervention composite at median 24 months.<sup>[[18]](#ref18)[[2]](#ref2)[[20]](#ref20)</sup> Major complications occurred in 11/46 (24%) in the Launer series.<sup>[[2]](#ref2)</sup> Ileal ureter is not a low-morbidity substitute for Boari flap or buccal graft ureteroplasty.

## Robotic Ileal Ureter Replacement

Robotic intracorporeal ileal ureter replacement has been reported in single-center and multi-institutional series from high-volume reconstructive centers.<sup>[[19]](#ref19)[[20]](#ref20)[[21]](#ref21)</sup>

### Totally intracorporeal sequence

1. Position to access kidney and ureter, bowel, and bladder; port strategy depends on unilateral versus bilateral reconstruction.
2. Identify diseased ureter and debride to healthy proximal target.
3. Measure the defect after mobilization and adjunctive bladder and kidney maneuvers.
4. Harvest ileum intracorporeally.
5. Restore bowel continuity with intracorporeal stapled or hand-sewn anastomosis.
6. Orient the ileal segment isoperistaltically.
7. Complete proximal ileopyelostomy or ileoureterostomy.
8. Complete distal ileovesicostomy.
9. Place stents, Foley, and drains.

### Extracorporeal segment preparation

Some robotic cases use a small extraction incision for ileal harvest and preparation, then return the segment for robotic urinary anastomoses. This can reduce intracorporeal bowel time while preserving minimally invasive urinary reconstruction.<sup>[[22]](#ref22)</sup>

### Bilateral configurations

Bilateral reconstructions may use:

- one long U-shaped ileal segment,
- reverse-7 or asymmetric configuration,
- separate ileal segments,
- a "cat-tail" configuration: a single ileal segment (described as approximately 15 cm, and elsewhere in the same source as 15-20 cm) with its proximal end passed through the sigmoid mesentery to the left renal pelvis and the right ureter joined end-to-side roughly 8-10 cm distal to that anastomosis, with the distal ileum joined to the bladder,<sup>[[26]](#ref26)</sup>
- ileal segment plus Boari and psoas adjuncts depending on bladder and defect geometry.<sup>[[7]](#ref7)[[19]](#ref19)[[22]](#ref22)</sup>

The cat-tail configuration is an author-described option for simultaneous bilateral long-segment replacement without a reported comparison against the other bilateral layouts above.<sup>[[26]](#ref26)</sup>

In the 2026 multi-institutional robotic series, 48 patients underwent robotic intracorporeal ileal ureter replacement across three institutions; 35.4% were bilateral and 41.7% had prior radiation. Major 30-day complications occurred in 9/48 (18.8%). The success estimate was 33/36 (91.7%) among patients with at least six months of follow-up, not 91.7% of all 48 operations. All three observed failures occurred in the radiation subgroup; this observational association informs counseling but does not determine an individual result.<sup>[[20]](#ref20)</sup>

## Complications

| Complication | Mechanism | Prevention / Management |
|---|---|---|
| Hyperchloremic metabolic acidosis | Ileum absorbs ammonium chloride and secretes bicarbonate | Use shortest bowel length, monitor bicarbonate/chloride, oral alkali |
| Mucus obstruction | Ileal mucosa continues mucus secretion | Hydration, irrigation when needed, endoscopic clearance rarely |
| Anastomotic stricture | Ischemia, tension, caliber mismatch, radiation | Wide spatulation, healthy margins, tension-free routing |
| Urinary leak / fistula | Anastomotic leak, distal obstruction, infected field | Stents, Foley drainage, drains, delayed reconstruction after sepsis |
| UTI / pyelonephritis | Reflux, stasis, mucus, colonization | Treat symptomatic infection; evaluate obstruction or reflux if recurrent |
| Small bowel obstruction | Adhesions or internal hernia | Close mesenteric defects; manage conservatively or surgically |
| Worsening renal function | Baseline CKD, bilateral repair, infection, obstruction, acidosis | Careful selection, lifelong monitoring, early obstruction workup |
| Stones | Mucus, infection, urinary stasis | Surveillance imaging, treat infection/stasis |
| B12 deficiency / bile-salt diarrhea | Terminal ileal loss | Preserve terminal ileum; monitor and supplement |

Metabolic acidosis is the signature long-term problem. Chronic acidosis can worsen bone health and potassium balance, and patients with reduced renal function have less reserve to compensate.<sup>[[4]](#ref4)</sup>

## Alternatives

| Option | Best Fit | Why It May Beat Ileal Ureter |
|---|---|---|
| Psoas hitch | Lower/mid ureteral defect | No bowel in urine; lower metabolic burden |
| Boari flap | Mid/upper defect with good bladder | Uses bladder tissue instead of bowel |
| Buccal mucosa graft ureteroplasty | Long nonobliterative stricture with ureteral plate | Avoids bowel anastomosis and metabolic complications |
| Appendiceal interposition/onlay | Right-sided moderate defect, normal appendix | No bowel anastomosis; small caliber |
| Transureteroureterostomy | Bladder unavailable, contralateral ureter healthy | Avoids bowel but risks contralateral renal unit |
| Renal autotransplantation | Long proximal/panureteral disease with bowel contraindication | Avoids bowel mucosa; adds transplant-level vascular risk |
| Diversion or nephrectomy | Poor bladder, poor renal function, unsafe reconstruction | May be safer than heroic salvage |

Recent robotic comparisons increasingly frame ileal ureter and renal autotransplantation as competing salvage tools for long strictures; choice depends on bowel suitability, vascular anatomy, renal function, prior radiation, and center expertise.<sup>[[23]](#ref23)</sup>

## Surveillance

Ileal ureter patients need long-term follow-up.

| Surveillance | Typical Interval | Why |
|---|---|---|
| Serum creatinine / eGFR | Every 6-12 mo | Detect obstruction, infection-related injury, CKD progression |
| Electrolytes, bicarbonate, chloride, potassium | Every 6-12 mo | Detect hyperchloremic metabolic acidosis |
| Renal ultrasound or CT/MR urography | At least annually early; individualized long term | Detect hydronephrosis, stones, mucus obstruction |
| MAG3 renography | Symptoms, worsening imaging, solitary kidney, uncertain drainage | Functional obstruction assessment |
| Vitamin B12 | Annually if terminal ileum or longer ileal length used | Detect delayed deficiency |
| Bone health | Consider with chronic acidosis | Acidosis can worsen bone buffering and mineral loss |
| Urine culture | Symptoms or before mucosa-breaching instrumentation | Avoid routine treatment of asymptomatic bacteriuria outside established exceptions |

---

## Operative Pearls

- **Use ileal ureter late.** Tension-free native, bladder-based, and onlay options come first.
- **Do not use radiated bowel.** Radiation strictures often require bowel replacement, but the segment should come from outside the radiation field.
- **Keep the segment short.** Every extra centimeter is more mucus and metabolic surface area.
- **Preserve terminal ileum.** B12 and bile-salt absorption matter years later.
- **Orient isoperistaltically.** Peristalsis then assists drainage.
- **Bilateral repairs carry more renal risk.** Bilateral repair was associated with worsening renal function in one series (HR 3.7); monitor more closely.
- **Radiation.** All three failures in the 2026 multi-institutional series occurred after radiation; counsel accordingly.
- **Antireflux is context-dependent.** In Kocot et al.’s 157-patient series (79 native bladders, 78 intestinal reservoirs), the authors considered reflux prevention of minor importance with native-bladder implantation and recommended antireflux implantation for intestinal reservoirs.<sup>[[5]](#ref5)</sup>
- **Metabolic surveillance is part of the operation.** Follow-up continues after the stent is removed.
