# Pyelovesicostomy

**Pyelovesicostomy** (also vesicopyelostomy or pyelo-vesicotomy) is a direct anastomosis between the renal pelvis and the bladder, completely bypassing the ureter. It is feasible only when the kidney lies close to the bladder, as with a renal allograft in the iliac fossa or a pelvic ectopic kidney, or when the bladder can be mobilized sufficiently (typically with a Boari flap) to reach the pelvis. Small selected series report successful drainage, including 2 transplant patients followed for 20 years. Overall success and late-complication rates are unknown from these small selected series.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[2]](#ref2)[[4]](#ref4)[[5]](#ref5)[[14]](#ref14)</sup>

The anastomosis must reach the dependent renal pelvis without tension. A compliant bladder without outlet obstruction is also required.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[7]](#ref7)[[14]](#ref14)</sup>

---

## Lineage

- **1973.** Kennelly, Konnak, and Herwig used vesicopyelostomy in 2 transplant patients with ureteral loss; both allografts were functioning at more than 20 years.<sup>[[5]](#ref5)</sup>
- **1985.** Carini et al. reported pyelovesicostomy as an alternative to UPJ-plasty in pelvic ectopic kidneys.<sup>[[2]](#ref2)</sup>
- **1986.** Rajfer et al. reported 8 transplant patients (follow-up 2 months to 11 years), with no deterioration in renal function attributed to anastomotic obstruction or free pyelovesical reflux.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup>
- **1998.** del Pizzo et al., direct pyelovesicostomy in 5/20 transplant patients requiring total ureteral reconstruction, with 100% success.<sup>[[6]](#ref6)</sup>
- **2005.** Davis and Wolf, first laparoscopic pyelovesicostomy for UPJO in a pelvic kidney.<sup>[[3]](#ref3)</sup>
- **2009.** Kumar and Panigrahy, five laparoscopic pyelovesicostomies for complex UPJO in pelvic kidneys or giant hydronephrosis.<sup>[[7]](#ref7)</sup>
- **2020.** Kim et al. reported robot-assisted repair of transplant ureteral strictures, including 3 pyelovesicostomies.<sup>[[8]](#ref8)</sup>
- **2023.** Navratil et al. reported 5 transplant pyelovesicostomies over 23 years (mean follow-up 7 years), all with functioning grafts at last follow-up (one patient died with a functioning graft 20 years after surgery).<sup>[[14]](#ref14)</sup>

## Indications

### Renal transplantation

- **Complete ureteral necrosis.** Entire donor ureter absent, necrotic, or diseased.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[6]](#ref6)</sup>
- **Complex transplant urinary leak.** Navratil et al. used pyelovesicostomy in 3/5 patients with urinary leakage, including one with ureteral necrosis.<sup>[[14]](#ref14)</sup>
- **Long or multisegment ureteral strictures** refractory to endoscopic management.<sup>[[9]](#ref9)[[6]](#ref6)</sup>
- **Failed prior ureteral reconstruction** (ureteroureterostomy, ureteroneocystostomy, pyeloureterostomy).<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup>
- **Absent or unusable recipient native ureter** that would otherwise allow pyeloureterostomy.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup>

### Pelvic ectopic kidneys

- **UPJ obstruction** in a congenital pelvic kidney where a short, tortuous ureter makes pyeloplasty technically difficult.<sup>[[2]](#ref2)[[7]](#ref7)[[10]](#ref10)</sup>
- **Failed prior pyeloplasty** in a pelvic kidney (especially solitary kidney).<sup>[[2]](#ref2)</sup>
- **Giant hydronephrosis** with a dependent pelvis lying adjacent to the bladder.<sup>[[7]](#ref7)</sup>

### Other

- Crossed fused ectopia with UPJO.<sup>[[7]](#ref7)</sup>
- Salvage when all other reconstructive options have been exhausted.<sup>[[4]](#ref4)[[1]](#ref1)</sup>

## Surgical Technique

Open, laparoscopic, or robotic.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[3]](#ref3)[[8]](#ref8)[[7]](#ref7)</sup>

### Open (transplant setting)

1. **Exposure.** Use the original transplant incision or an iliac-fossa approach; identify and excise the necrotic or strictured ureter.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[6]](#ref6)</sup>
2. **Bladder mobilization.** Mobilize the bladder superiorly and laterally toward the renal pelvis. The contralateral superior vesical pedicle may be divided to gain additional reach.<sup>[[6]](#ref6)</sup>
3. **Pelvic preparation.** Open the renal pelvis at its most dependent point and spatulate the opening for a wide-mouthed anastomosis.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup>
4. **Anastomosis.** Tension-free, mucosa-to-mucosa, single- or two-layer closure with absorbable suture (4-0 or 5-0 polyglycolic acid).<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[6]](#ref6)</sup>
5. **Stenting and bladder drainage.** Stenting varies by technique and series. Del Pizzo et al. reported mean stenting of 27 days in the Boari-flap subgroup; Navratil et al. used neither an internal stent nor a nephrostomy in five direct repairs and removed the bladder catheter after 5–7 days.<sup>[[6]](#ref6)[[14]](#ref14)</sup>
6. **Drainage.** Closed-suction drain in the perivesical space.<sup>[[6]](#ref6)</sup>

### Pyelovesicostomy with Boari flap (transplant setting)

When the renal pelvis cannot reach the bladder directly:<sup>[[9]](#ref9)</sup>

1. Full-thickness rectangular or trapezoidal flap from the anterior bladder wall.
2. Swing cranially and tubularize to reach the renal pelvis.
3. Check that the tubularized flap reaches the renal pelvis without tension or redundant kinking, then complete a watertight anastomosis. McGregor et al. reported a day-4 revision for a redundant, kinked Boari flap among 12 transplant reconstructions with a flap.<sup>[[17]](#ref17)</sup>
4. Kroczak et al. (n = 10) reported success in all 10, with graft function salvaged in every case and no stricture recurrence at a mean follow-up of 18 months.<sup>[[9]](#ref9)</sup>

### Laparoscopic (pelvic kidney)

Kumar and Panigrahy described this approach:<sup>[[7]](#ref7)</sup>

1. Four-port technique: two 10-mm (umbilicus and lateral border of ipsilateral rectus) and two 5-mm.
2. Identify pelvic kidney, dissect renal pelvis free, mobilize bladder dome.
3. Anastomose the most dependent part of the renal pelvis to the bladder dome with intracorporeal suturing.
4. Suprapubic Foley as an across-anastomosis stent.
5. Mean OR time 140 min and mean blood loss 50 mL describe the whole 9-patient mixed-procedure cohort, including 5 pyelovesicostomies; no intraoperative complications were reported.<sup>[[7]](#ref7)</sup>

### Robotic (transplant setting)

Kim n = 5 (3 pyelovesicostomy + 2 ureteroneocystostomy):<sup>[[8]](#ref8)</sup>

- Mean LOS 2.2 d.
- All 5 successful with no strictures or delayed leaks at mean 97 d.
- This uncontrolled 5-patient cohort shows robotic feasibility without comparative tissue trauma or morbidity data.

## The Reflux Question

The anastomosis is inherently refluxing, with no antireflux mechanism between the bladder and the renal pelvis. Small selected series reported stable function despite reflux:<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[6]](#ref6)[[5]](#ref5)</sup>

- Rajfer n = 8 at up to 11 yr, with no deterioration attributable to reflux or anastomotic obstruction.
- Kennelly (n = 2) reported both allografts functioning at more than 20 years after a refluxing vesicopyelostomy.
- In del Pizzo, reflux into the transplant pelvis occurred in 6/20 (Boari and direct combined). Two patients had reversible deterioration in renal function from rejection, and none was attributed to reflux.

Bladder storage pressure, emptying, recurrent infection and graft function are assessed, not assumed normal. Kumar and Panigrahy advised double voiding in their series; emptying management is tailored to the patient, with long-term renal and drainage surveillance.<sup>[[7]](#ref7)[[11]](#ref11)</sup>

## Complications

- **UTI.** In Navratil et al.'s five-patient transplant cohort, 2/5 were readmitted within 30 days for UTI; the authors attributed both infections to native polycystic kidneys.<sup>[[4]](#ref4)[[7]](#ref7)[[14]](#ref14)</sup>
- **Persistent hydronephrosis.** Mild residual collecting-system dilatation may persist without obstruction on diuretic renography.<sup>[[2]](#ref2)[[6]](#ref6)</sup>
- **Anastomotic obstruction.** Monitor even when early drainage is satisfactory; absence in small reports does not exclude late risk.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[6]](#ref6)[[5]](#ref5)</sup>
- **Prolonged drain output.** In the Boari-flap subgroup, prolonged high-volume drain output (mean 22 d) was not uncommon.<sup>[[6]](#ref6)</sup>
- **Graft dysfunction or loss.** Distinguish obstruction and infection from rejection and other transplant causes; reconstruction does not eliminate these risks.<sup>[[4]](#ref4)[[1]](#ref1)</sup>

## Subcutaneous Pyelovesical Bypass Graft (SPBG) — Salvage Alternative

For transplant patients with extended strictures not eligible for open ureteral reconstruction, a percutaneous prosthetic stent tunneled subcutaneously from renal pelvis to bladder offers a last-resort salvage:<sup>[[12]](#ref12)[[13]](#ref13)</sup>

- Muller 15-year experience (n = 7) reported no encrustation or dislodgment at a mean follow-up of 6 years. One patient died of septic shock after fungal prosthesis colonization, and two others developed asymptomatic UTI (three patients with infection-related outcomes).<sup>[[12]](#ref12)</sup>
- Azhar n = 8, with 7/8 grafts functioning (mean GFR 58.5 mL/min/1.73 m²) at mean 19.4 mo, but 1 graft lost to persistent SPBG infection.<sup>[[13]](#ref13)</sup>

SPBG is reserved for patients in whom conventional pyelovesicostomy or Boari-flap reconstruction is not feasible.<sup>[[13]](#ref13)</sup>

## Choosing Among Reconstructive Options

Direct pyelovesicostomy requires the bladder to reach a suitable dependent renal pelvis. A Boari flap may bridge an additional gap if bladder capacity, compliance and perfusion are adequate. Both avoid bowel harvest but may still be complex reoperations around transplant vessels.<sup>[[6]](#ref6)[[8]](#ref8)[[9]](#ref9)</sup>

[Ileal ureter](/docs/surgical-techniques/04d-upper-tract-reconstruction/ileal-ureter) and [renal autotransplantation](/docs/surgical-techniques/04d-upper-tract-reconstruction/renal-autotransplantation) have different bowel, vascular and renal prerequisites. Cross-series success estimates are not comparable across these anatomically different options.

---

## Pyelovesicostomy vs Calicovesicostomy

When the renal pelvis is intrarenal (small or absent extrarenal pelvis), direct pyelovesicostomy may be infeasible. **Calicovesicostomy**, anastomosis of a lower-pole calyx directly to the bladder, is an alternative. Kumar and Panigrahy performed calicovesicostomy in 1 patient, with patency confirmed on postoperative nephrostogram. The authors advised double voiding and long-term surveillance; emptying management is tailored to the individual bladder.<sup>[[7]](#ref7)</sup>
