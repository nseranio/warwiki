# Balloon Dilation of the Upper Tract

**Non-drug-coated balloon dilation** is a minimally invasive endourologic technique for managing benign upper urinary tract strictures, including ureteral strictures, ureteropelvic junction (UPJ) obstruction, and ureteroenteric anastomotic strictures. A 2019 meta-analysis of 19 uncontrolled series in benign ureteral strictures reported pooled technical success of about 89% and long-term success of about 54% at 6–12 months. Success was significantly higher for strictures ≤2 cm; onset within 3 months showed a higher success rate that was not statistically significant. Compromised vascular supply predicts failure.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[2]](#ref2)</sup>

For drug-coated (paclitaxel) balloons, see [Drug-Coated Balloon Therapy](/docs/surgical-techniques/drug-coated-balloon). For sharp endoscopic incision, see [Endoureterotomy](/docs/surgical-techniques/04d-upper-tract-reconstruction/endoureterotomy).

---

## Indications

| Scenario | Notes |
|---|---|
| **Benign ureteral strictures** | Iatrogenic injury, ureteroscopy, radiation, or inflammatory.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[3]](#ref3)</sup> |
| **UPJ obstruction** | Primary or recurrent after failed pyeloplasty.<sup>[[4]](#ref4)[[5]](#ref5)</sup> |
| **Ureteroenteric anastomotic strictures** | After radical cystectomy with ileal conduit, neobladder, or continent pouch.<sup>[[6]](#ref6)[[7]](#ref7)</sup> |
| **Post-surgical anastomotic strictures** | After pyeloplasty, ureteral reimplantation, or ureteroneocystostomy.<sup>[[8]](#ref8)[[3]](#ref3)</sup> |

---

## Technique

Performed via either a **retrograde** (cystoscopic or ureteroscopic) or **antegrade** (percutaneous nephrostomy) approach under fluoroscopic guidance.<sup>[[6]](#ref6)[[3]](#ref3)</sup>

| Parameter | Typical setting |
|---|---|
| **Balloon type** | Semicompliant or high-pressure; profiles 5–7 mm in pediatric cases, larger in adults.<sup>[[9]](#ref9)</sup> |
| **Inflation pressure** | 10–20 atm, sustained for 5–15 min.<sup>[[6]](#ref6)</sup> |
| **Post-dilation drainage** | Double-J ureteral stent or nephrostomy for 4–8 weeks on average; some protocols extend to several months.<sup>[[3]](#ref3)[[6]](#ref6)</sup> |
| **Standardization** | No consensus on optimal balloon type, pressure, number of inflations, or stent retention time.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup> |

---

## Efficacy by Scenario

| Population / study | Result | Interpretation |
|---|---|---|
| Benign ureteral strictures, retrospective cohort (114 patients, at least 2 years of follow-up) | Balloon dilation succeeded in 33/37 (89.2%) short strictures with intact vascular supply, versus 3/8 long strictures and 2/5 short strictures with compromised supply | Selected retrospective cohort; the authors favored endoureterotomy for long strictures and compromised supply.<sup>[[2]](#ref2)</sup> |
| Benign ureteral strictures, 2019 meta-analysis of 19 series | About 60% success at 3 months and 54% at 6–12 months | Heterogeneous uncontrolled series; technical passage is not durable patency.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup> |
| UPJ obstruction shorter than 2 cm, retrospective comparison | Balloon dilation: 78.9% at 12 months and 71.0% at 24 months; laparoscopic pyeloplasty: 95.5% at both timepoints | Patient-selected treatment groups; this was not a randomized trial.<sup>[[4]](#ref4)</sup> |
| Ureteroenteric strictures, 27-year institutional experience | 60-month patency: 27% after mixed endourologic procedures versus 69% after open revision | The endourologic estimate includes several modalities and is not balloon-specific.<sup>[[7]](#ref7)</sup> |

Pediatric UPJ, benign native-ureter and ureteroenteric studies should not be combined into a single expected success rate.

## Predictors of Outcome

**Favorable factors:**<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[3]](#ref3)[[2]](#ref2)</sup>

- Stricture length ≤2 cm.
- Onset within 3 months (a higher success rate in the meta-analysis, not statistically significant).
- Intact vascular supply (no prior radiation, no devascularizing dissection).
- Primary rather than recurrent stricture.

**Failure risk factors:**<sup>[[10]](#ref10)[[2]](#ref2)</sup>

- Multiple strictures (independent predictor on multivariate analysis).
- Compromised vascular supply (post-radiation, ischemic).
- Long strictures.
- Prior failed repair.
- Smaller balloon circumference (independent predictor in the same retrospective cohort).

---

## Comparison With Surgical Reconstruction

Reconstruction generally offers greater durability at the cost of a larger intervention. Operative time, admission and cost depend on the specific repair, era and local pathway; a mixed benign-ureter reconstruction series should not be labeled as a pyeloplasty comparison. Use the population-specific outcomes above for counseling.<sup>[[4]](#ref4)[[7]](#ref7)[[11]](#ref11)</sup>

---

## Adjunctive Techniques

- **Balloon dilation and endoureterotomy.** Reported success was higher than with dilation alone in one retrospective cohort (87% versus 78% at 1 year for lower-ureteral strictures).<sup>[[10]](#ref10)</sup>
- **Laser incision and triamcinolone injection followed by balloon dilation.** 20 of 24 ureteroenteric strictures (83.3%; 21 patients) had no recurrence at a median 30 months in a retrospective series.<sup>[[12]](#ref12)</sup>
- **Acucise balloon catheter.** Combined electrocautery incision and dilation, with success in 8 of 12 strictures (75%) after reimplantation or uretero-intestinal anastomosis at median 16 months (83% ureterovesical, 50% uretero-ileal).<sup>[[13]](#ref13)</sup>

---

## Clinical Role

Balloon dilation is used as a first-line minimally invasive option for selected patients (short, primary strictures with intact vascularity) and as a temporizing or alternative approach in patients unfit for open reconstruction. Re-dilation is feasible for recurrence. In one retrospective series (43 patients), all 6 recurrences occurred within 36 months and 4 within the first year, and 3 were successfully re-dilated; late recurrences arise, and the authors advise long-term follow-up.<sup>[[7]](#ref7)[[14]](#ref14)</sup>
