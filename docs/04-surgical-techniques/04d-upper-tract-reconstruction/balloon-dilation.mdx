# Balloon Dilation of the Upper Tract

**Non-drug-coated balloon dilation** is a minimally invasive endourologic technique for managing benign upper urinary tract strictures, including ureteral strictures, ureteropelvic junction (UPJ) obstruction, and ureteroenteric anastomotic strictures. A 2019 meta-analysis of 19 uncontrolled series in benign ureteral strictures reported pooled technical success of about 89% and long-term success of about 54% at 6–12 months.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup> Success was significantly higher for strictures ≤2 cm; onset within 3 months showed a higher success rate that was not statistically significant. Compromised vascular supply predicts failure.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[2]](#ref2)</sup>

For drug-coated (paclitaxel) balloons, see [Drug-Coated Balloon Therapy](/docs/surgical-techniques/drug-coated-balloon). For sharp endoscopic incision, see [Endoureterotomy](/docs/surgical-techniques/04d-upper-tract-reconstruction/endoureterotomy).

---

## Indications

| Scenario | Notes |
|---|---|
| **Benign ureteral strictures** | Iatrogenic injury, ureteroscopy, radiation, or inflammatory.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[3]](#ref3)</sup> |
| **UPJ obstruction** | Primary or recurrent after failed pyeloplasty.<sup>[[4]](#ref4)[[5]](#ref5)</sup> |
| **Ureteroenteric anastomotic strictures** | After radical cystectomy with ileal conduit, neobladder, or continent pouch. If cystectomy was for cancer, evaluate for tumor recurrence before assuming the narrowing is benign; the cited comparative series included urine cytology in that evaluation.<sup>[[6]](#ref6)[[7]](#ref7)</sup> |
| **Post-surgical anastomotic strictures** | After pyeloplasty, ureteral reimplantation, or ureteroneocystostomy.<sup>[[8]](#ref8)[[3]](#ref3)</sup> |

---

## Preprocedure Assessment

Before elective endoscopic dilation, obtain a urine culture.<sup>[[18]](#ref18)</sup> The 2026 EAU Urological Infections guideline recommends screening for and treating asymptomatic bacteriuria before urologic procedures breaching the mucosa (Strong).<sup>[[18]](#ref18)</sup>

## Technique

Use retrograde (cystoscopic or ureteroscopic) or antegrade (nephrostomy) access, usually with fluoroscopic contrast guidance. Pass a guidewire across the stricture, center the balloon on the narrowed segment, dilate, and check the result with antegrade or retrograde pyelography before temporary drainage.<sup>[[6]](#ref6)[[3]](#ref3)[[14]](#ref14)</sup> A 76-patient retrospective series also described selected ureteroscope-guided dilation without fluoroscopy.<sup>[[19]](#ref19)</sup> Positioning follows the access route: the retrograde approach uses dorsal lithotomy, as for cystoscopy or ureteroscopy, while the antegrade approach uses the prone, prone-oblique, or supine-oblique position needed for percutaneous nephrostomy or nephrostomy-tract access. No dilation-specific VTE recommendation exists; this is a brief endoscopic procedure, so it falls toward the low end of the general urologic-surgery risk framework on the [VTE Prophylaxis](/docs/foundations/pharmacology/perioperative-eras/vte-prophylaxis) page, but individual risk factors (age, obesity, prior VTE, malignancy) still apply.

| Parameter | Typical setting |
|---|---|
| **Balloon type** | A 50-infant UPJ series used 5–7 mm semicompliant high-pressure balloons; adult dimensions were not studied.<sup>[[9]](#ref9)</sup> |
| **Inflation pressure** | One 20-patient series used 10–20 atm for 5–15 min; pressure and inflation duration are not standardized.<sup>[[6]](#ref6)[[1]](#ref1)[[10]](#ref10)</sup> |
| **Post-dilation drainage** | Ureteral stenting for 4–8 weeks in one series; another reported a mean of 2.1 months (range 1–5 months).<sup>[[3]](#ref3)[[6]](#ref6)</sup> |
| **Standardization** | No consensus on optimal balloon type, pressure, number of inflations, or stent retention time.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup> |

## Efficacy by Scenario

| Population / study | Result | Interpretation |
|---|---|---|
| Benign ureteral strictures, retrospective cohort (114 patients total; 81 received balloon dilation; at least 2 years of follow-up) | Balloon dilation succeeded in 33/37 (89.2%) short strictures with intact vascular supply, versus 3/8 long strictures and 2/5 short strictures with compromised supply | Selected retrospective cohort; the authors favored endoureterotomy for long strictures and compromised supply.<sup>[[2]](#ref2)</sup> |
| Benign ureteral strictures, 2019 meta-analysis of 19 series | About 60% success at 3 months and 54% at 6–12 months | Heterogeneous uncontrolled series; technical passage is not durable patency.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup> |
| UPJ obstruction shorter than 2 cm, retrospective comparison | Balloon dilation: 78.9% at 12 months and 71.0% at 24 months; laparoscopic pyeloplasty: 95.5% at both timepoints | Patient-selected treatment groups; this was not a randomized trial.<sup>[[4]](#ref4)</sup> |
| Ureteroenteric strictures | A 27-year series reported 60-month patency of 27% after mixed endourologic procedures versus 69% after open revision; a separate meta-analysis reported 35% pooled success after balloon dilation | The 27% estimate is not balloon-specific. Endoscopic results were more favorable for strictures ≤1 cm, but the balloon studies and follow-up were heterogeneous.<sup>[[7]](#ref7)[[15]](#ref15)</sup> |

Success estimates are setting-specific across pediatric UPJ, benign native-ureter and ureteroenteric studies.

## Predictors of Outcome

**Favorable factors:**<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[3]](#ref3)[[2]](#ref2)</sup>

- Stricture length ≤2 cm.
- Onset within 3 months (a higher success rate in the meta-analysis, not statistically significant).
- Intact vascular supply (no prior radiation, no devascularizing dissection).
- Primary rather than recurrent stricture.

**Failure risk factors:**<sup>[[10]](#ref10)[[2]](#ref2)</sup>

- Multiple strictures (independent predictor on multivariate analysis).
- Compromised vascular supply (post-radiation, ischemic).
- Long strictures.
- Prior failed repair.
- Larger balloon circumference was associated with failure in one retrospective cohort; the study did not establish an optimal balloon size.<sup>[[10]](#ref10)</sup>

---

## Complications

Guidewire passage may fail; in one mixed-treatment series, 13/59 attempts required invasive surgery because the wire could not traverse the stricture.<sup>[[14]](#ref14)</sup> Hematuria, mucosal injury, and ureteral perforation are recognized procedural harms.<sup>[[20]](#ref20)</sup> In a selected 42-patient benign lower-ureter series using balloon dilation with internal-external drainage, hematuria in the drainage bag cleared in 1–4 days and no perforations were reported.<sup>[[20]](#ref20)</sup>

## Comparison With Surgical Reconstruction

Reconstruction generally offers greater durability at the cost of a larger intervention. Operative time, admission and cost depend on the specific repair, era and local pathway; a mixed benign-ureter reconstruction series should not be labeled as a pyeloplasty comparison. For ureteroenteric strictures, a 2026 review of seven comparative cohorts (368 renal units) found higher primary patency after open revision than after mixed endoscopic management (RR 2.31, 95% CI 1.54–3.47), with more minor complications after open repair; success definitions varied.<sup>[[16]](#ref16)</sup> Use the population-specific outcomes above for counseling.<sup>[[4]](#ref4)[[7]](#ref7)[[11]](#ref11)</sup>

## Adjunctive Techniques

- **Balloon dilation and endoureterotomy.** One retrospective lower-ureter cohort reported 1-year success of 87% with combined treatment (30 patients) versus 78% with dilation alone (37 patients); the groups differed in age and stricture length, so the comparison does not establish added benefit.<sup>[[10]](#ref10)</sup>
- **Laser incision and triamcinolone injection followed by balloon dilation.** 20 of 24 ureteroenteric strictures (83.3%; 21 patients) had no recurrence at a median 30 months in a retrospective series.<sup>[[12]](#ref12)</sup>
- **Acucise balloon catheter.** Combined electrocautery incision and dilation was reported successful in eight patients among 11 patients with 12 treated strictures at median 16 months; reported subgroup success was 83% for ureterovesical and 50% for uretero-ileal strictures.<sup>[[13]](#ref13)</sup>

---

## Clinical Role

Balloon dilation is used as a first-line minimally invasive option for selected patients (short, primary strictures with intact vascularity) and as a temporizing or alternative approach in patients unfit for open reconstruction. For recurrent UPJ obstruction after pyeloplasty, a 2026 review of 12 mixed-age comparative studies (549 patients) found higher odds of success after redo pyeloplasty than after pooled endoscopic treatments, including balloon dilation and endopyelotomy (OR 6.32, 95% CI 3.25–12.32); risk of bias was high to serious.<sup>[[17]](#ref17)</sup> Re-dilation remains feasible for selected recurrent ureteral strictures. In a retrospective mixed-treatment cohort, 43/59 attempted patients were analyzed after 13 required invasive surgery for failed guidewire access and three were followed elsewhere; 32 received balloon dilation, 10 catheter dilation, and one laser incision.<sup>[[14]](#ref14)</sup> Among 34 initially successful patients, six recurred within 36 months (four within 12 months), all after malignancy treatment. Three were re-dilated; two met success criteria at 36 months and one died earlier of progressive malignancy.<sup>[[14]](#ref14)</sup> The authors advise long-term follow-up.<sup>[[7]](#ref7)[[14]](#ref14)</sup>

---

## Follow-Up

Follow-up after stent removal assesses symptoms, renal function, and upper-tract drainage.<sup>[[14]](#ref14)</sup> One retrospective mixed-treatment cohort checked drainage two weeks after stent removal; stent- and nephrostomy-free patients then had CT urography or MAG3 scintigraphy plus creatinine/eGFR at 3, 6, 12, 24, and 36 months.<sup>[[14]](#ref14)</sup>
