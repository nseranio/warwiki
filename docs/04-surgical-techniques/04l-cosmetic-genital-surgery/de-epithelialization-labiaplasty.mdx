# De-Epithelialization Labiaplasty

**De-epithelialization labiaplasty** was first described by Choi and Kim in 2000 in six patients. It reduces labial width by removing only the epithelium from a defined area of the medial labial surface and folding the raw surfaces together. The native free edge keeps its color, contour and neurovascular supply.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[2]](#ref2)</sup> In one meta-analysis, de-epithelialization had the highest pooled satisfaction among labiaplasty techniques (97%, 95% CI 85–99%, five studies), although the intervals overlap those of other techniques.<sup>[[3]](#ref3)</sup> Pooled dehiscence was highest for wedge resection and laser, and de-epithelialization was not among the highest.<sup>[[3]](#ref3)[[8]](#ref8)</sup> For positioning versus other female cosmetic options see [Cosmetic Genital Surgery — Female](/docs/surgical-techniques/04l-cosmetic-genital-surgery/female-cosmetic).

**Society positioning.** The ACOG 2020 Committee Opinion No. 795 states that female genital cosmetic procedures are not medically indicated in patients without structural or functional abnormalities, that safety and effectiveness have not been established, and that patients should be counseled on the lack of high-quality evidence and the risk of pain, bleeding, infection, scarring, adhesions, altered sensation, dyspareunia, and reoperation.<sup>[[4]](#ref4)</sup> The FIGO 2025 Statement holds that it is ethically inappropriate for obstetrician-gynecologists to recommend, perform or refer patients for cosmetic genital procedures.<sup>[[10]](#ref10)</sup> Screening for body dysmorphic disorder (BDD) and counseling on normal anatomic variation come first in every consultation. ACOG Committee Opinion No. 686 limits adolescent labiaplasty to significant congenital malformation or persistent symptoms directly caused by labial anatomy.<sup>[[5]](#ref5)</sup>

---

## Concept and rationale

Trim resection amputates the protruding labial margin and removes its natural contour and pigmentation. Wedge resection removes a full-thickness V and carries risks of dehiscence and flap necrosis. De-epithelialization reduces width without sacrificing the free edge or crossing full thickness. Only the superficial epithelium is excised within a marked elliptical or rectangular area on the medial labial surface, and the underlying submucosal tissue, vasculature and innervation are left intact. The raw surfaces are then folded inward and sutured, which shortens labial projection while the free edge stays in continuity.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[6]](#ref6)</sup> The native edge and its sensory supply are preserved, and reduction capacity is limited in severe hypertrophy.<sup>[[2]](#ref2)[[6]](#ref6)</sup>

---

## Indications

- **Mild to moderate labial hypertrophy** where excess is primarily in width / projection rather than edge irregularity.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[6]](#ref6)</sup>
- **Patient explicitly desires preservation of the natural labial edge**, color, and contour.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[2]](#ref2)</sup>
- **Edge is aesthetically acceptable** and the patient does not want it altered.<sup>[[2]](#ref2)</sup>
- Sensory preservation is a stated priority; the technique avoids transecting the labial neurovascular bundle.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[7]](#ref7)</sup>
- Patients who want to limit dehiscence risk: pooled dehiscence was highest for wedge resection (8%, 95% CI 5–13%, in one meta-analysis) and laser, and de-epithelialization was not among the highest.<sup>[[8]](#ref8)[[3]](#ref3)</sup>

Severe hypertrophy with substantial protruding tissue is generally a poor fit, because de-epithelialization cannot remove enough tissue.<sup>[[2]](#ref2)[[6]](#ref6)</sup>

## Surgical technique

| Step | Detail |
|---|---|
| **1. Marking** | Lithotomy position. The area of excess is identified on the central and / or posterior labial surface. An elliptical or rectangular zone is marked on the medial (mucosal) side corresponding to the planned reduction.<sup>[[6]](#ref6)[[1]](#ref1)</sup> |
| **2. Anesthesia** | Local with epinephrine, with or without sedation, or general.<sup>[[2]](#ref2)</sup> |
| **3. De-epithelialization** | The epithelium within the marked area is carefully removed using a scalpel, fine scissors, or low-power electrocautery. Only the superficial epithelium is excised, and the underlying submucosa, vasculature and innervation are preserved.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[6]](#ref6)</sup> |
| **4. Folding and reapproximation** | The de-epithelialized raw surfaces are folded inward and approximated with absorbable sutures in a layered fashion, shortening labial projection while leaving the free edge intact.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[6]](#ref6)</sup> |
| **5. Closure** | Fine absorbable suture (4-0 or 5-0 polyglactin or chromic gut), commonly running.<sup>[[6]](#ref6)</sup> |
| **6. Contralateral side** | Repeat with deliberate intraoperative comparison for symmetry. |

Cao 2012 focused the de-epithelialization on the central and posterior portions of the labium minus. In this retrospective review of 167 patients, one had a minor dehiscence that healed without treatment, two felt the reduction was incomplete, and all were satisfied with the final appearance.<sup>[[6]](#ref6)</sup>

## Modifications and hybrid techniques

| Variant | Description | Outcomes |
|---|---|---|
| **Choi-Kim 2000 (original)** | Central de-epithelialization and reapproximation, preserving the neurovascular supply to the edge | n = 6; founding description<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup> |
| **Cao 2012 central and posterior** | De-epithelialization focused on middle and posterior labial segments | n = 167 (retrospective); one minor dehiscence; all satisfied with appearance<sup>[[6]](#ref6)</sup> |
| **Jiang 2021 medial wedge de-epithelialization and lateral edge resection** | Combined approach: wedge de-epithelialization on the medial side with edge resection on the lateral side | n = 51 (retrospective, 26 wedge controls); satisfaction 94.1% vs 96.2% with wedge (not significant); FSFI improved significantly only in the study group, with no significant between-group difference<sup>[[7]](#ref7)</sup> |
| **Wang 2025 modified mucosal advancement and de-epithelialized flap** | Combined mucosal advancement flap and de-epithelialized mucosal flap | n = 25; all satisfied; no hematoma or other adverse complications at 3–12 months<sup>[[9]](#ref9)</sup> |

The combined approaches are intended to overcome the limited reduction capacity of pure de-epithelialization.

---

## Outcomes

- **Satisfaction.** Géczi 2024 pooled 97% (95% CI 85–99%) for de-epithelialization (five studies), against 94% overall.<sup>[[3]](#ref3)</sup>
- **Dehiscence.** In Escandón 2022 the highest pooled dehiscence rates were for laser (5%, 95% CI 2–8%) and wedge resection (3%, 1–5%). Géczi 2024 reported 8% (5–13%) for wedge resection. De-epithelialization was not among the highest in either analysis.<sup>[[8]](#ref8)[[3]](#ref3)</sup>
- **Sensory preservation.** The technique avoids transecting the labial edge, so its sensory supply is left structurally intact.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup>
- **Sexual function.** In Jiang 2021, FSFI improved significantly after combined medial wedge de-epithelialization and lateral edge resection but not after wedge resection. The between-group difference was not significant.<sup>[[7]](#ref7)</sup>
- **Transient pain or discomfort.** Escandón 2022 found the highest pooled incidence for de-epithelialization and W-shape resection (each 2%; 95% CI for de-epithelialization &lt;1–23%).<sup>[[8]](#ref8)</sup>

## Complications

| Complication | Notes |
|---|---|
| **Wound dehiscence** | Not among the highest pooled rates (those were laser and wedge resection); one minor dehiscence in 167 patients in the Cao series.<sup>[[8]](#ref8)[[6]](#ref6)</sup> |
| **Hematoma** | Rare. |
| **Infection** | Rare. |
| **Flap necrosis** | Not attributed to de-epithelialization in the meta-analysis (three cases overall, two after wedge resection).<sup>[[8]](#ref8)</sup> |
| **Transient pain / dysesthesia** | About 2% pooled incidence (95% CI &lt;1–23%).<sup>[[8]](#ref8)</sup> |
| **Incomplete reduction / persistent hypertrophy** | Reflects underestimation of the degree of hypertrophy. Revision by trim or wedge is possible. |
| **Inability to address edge irregularities** | Inherent to the technique, not strictly a complication; consent should state that hyperpigmented or rugose edges will not be improved.<sup>[[2]](#ref2)</sup> |

## De-epithelialization vs trim vs wedge

| Parameter | De-epithelialization | Wedge | Trim |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Native edge** | Preserved | Preserved | Removed |
| **Sensory supply** | Preserved (no transection) | Largely preserved | Edge sensation removed with edge |
| **Pooled satisfaction (Géczi 2024)** | 97% (5 studies; CI 85–99%) | 96% (15 studies) | 96% (edge resection, 13 studies) |
| **Pooled dehiscence** | Not among the highest | 3% (1–5%, Escandón 2022); 8% (5–13%, Géczi 2024) | Not among the highest |
| **Flap necrosis** | Not attributed to the technique | Reported (rare) | Not applicable |
| **Reduction capacity** | Limited (mild–moderate) | Substantial (focal central) | Substantial (diffuse) |
| **Best for** | Mild–moderate width excess; edge-preservation goal; sensory priority | Focal central protrusion with good edge | Hyperpigmented / irregular edge; diffuse protrusion; smokers |
| **Surgeon experience needed** | Moderate | Higher | Lower |
| **Smokers / cocaine users** | Acceptable | Relative contraindication | Preferred |

## Postoperative management

- **Activity restriction.** Avoid intercourse, tampon use, and strenuous exercise for 4–6 weeks.
- **Wound care.** Ice / cold compresses 48–72 h; sitz baths from 24–48 h; topical antibiotic ointment; loose-fitting clothing.
- **Suture management.** Absorbable suture eliminates the need for removal.
- **Follow-up.** 1 week (wound check), 6 weeks (clearance), 3–6 months (final outcome assessment).
- **PRO assessment.** FSFI and FGSIS at baseline and ≥ 6 months postoperatively.
- **Counseling on reduction limits.** The technique reduces width and does not alter the edge, so hyperpigmentation or irregularity of the edge will not change.
