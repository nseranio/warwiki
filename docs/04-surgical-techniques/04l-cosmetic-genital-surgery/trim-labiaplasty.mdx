# Trim (Edge / Linear) Resection Labiaplasty

The **trim labiaplasty**, also called edge resection or linear labiaplasty, is a direct technique for labia minora reduction. It involves a linear excision of the redundant free edge of the labium along its length, with closure that creates a new suture-line edge. Together with the central wedge, it is one of the two most commonly used labiaplasty techniques according to Zahedi and colleagues.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup> Hunter describes edge excision, wedge excision and central de-epithelialization as the three basic techniques.<sup>[[2]](#ref2)</sup> For positioning versus other female cosmetic options see [Cosmetic Genital Surgery: Female](/docs/surgical-techniques/04l-cosmetic-genital-surgery/female-cosmetic).

**Society positioning.** ACOG Committee Opinion No. 795 (2020) states that procedures to alter sexual appearance or function, excluding those for clinical indications, are not medically indicated, pose substantial risk, and have no established safety or effectiveness. It advises counseling on the lack of high-quality data and on the risks of pain, bleeding, infection, scarring, adhesions, altered sensation, dyspareunia and reoperation, with assessment for body dysmorphic disorder (BDD) when indicated.<sup>[[3]](#ref3)</sup> For surgery sought solely to alter appearance in women without structural or functional abnormalities, the FIGO 2025 statement calls it ethically inappropriate for obstetrician-gynecologists to recommend, perform, or refer patients.<sup>[[13]](#ref13)</sup> For patients younger than 18, ACOG advises education about normal variation, BDD screening, and assessment of physical and emotional readiness, with mental-health referral when BDD is suspected.<sup>[[4]](#ref4)</sup> It advises considering labiaplasty only for significant congenital malformation or persistent symptoms the clinician believes are caused directly by labial anatomy, and warns that non-medically indicated labial surgery in this age group violates U.S. federal criminal law.<sup>[[4]](#ref4)</sup> Counseling on normal anatomic variation belongs at the front of the consultation.

---

## Definition and concept

The trim technique excises the protruding labial edge along the entire length of the labium, typically as a curvilinear excision that follows the contour the patient and surgeon plan as the new free margin. Closure creates a single suture line that becomes the new labial edge, replacing the native rugose or pigmented margin.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[2]](#ref2)</sup>

The main difference from the central wedge is edge handling. The trim removes the native edge entirely (along with any hyperpigmentation, irregular contour, or rugosity), whereas the wedge preserves the native edge by removing a full-thickness central V or W and reapproximating two preserved edge segments.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[5]](#ref5)</sup>

---

## Indications

The trim technique is particularly well-suited when the patient's complaint or anatomy fits one of the following patterns<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[2]](#ref2)</sup>:

- Darkened or hyperpigmented labial edge the patient explicitly wants removed. The trim directly excises the native free edge, whereas the central wedge preserves it.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup>
- **Irregular, rugose, or poor-quality labial edge** where preserving the native margin would be aesthetically undesirable.
- Diffuse, generalized protrusion along the entire length of the labium, rather than a focal central bulge.
- **Significant asymmetry** requiring tailored resection along the full labial length.
- **Patient prefers greater reduction or a "tucked-in" contour.** Counsel that trim removes the native free margin and that tissue retraction can make over-resection difficult to correct; plan a conservative residual labial cuff.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup>
- **Nicotine or cocaine exposure when surgery is otherwise appropriate.** In Zahedi's expert algorithm, exposure favors trim over a vascularly dependent wedge; exposure itself does not establish an indication for surgery or safety during active use.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[6]](#ref6)</sup>

The Zahedi 2023 trim-versus-wedge algorithm summarizes selection: trim when the patient desires edge or pigment removal, has poor edge quality, has diffuse protrusion, or uses nicotine or cocaine; wedge when the patient has good edge quality, focal central protrusion, and wants the natural edge preserved. The authors emphasize that "the best technique is always the one the surgeon performs comfortably and safely."<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup>

## Surgical technique

| Step | Detail |
|---|---|
| **1. Anesthesia plan** | Choose local anesthesia alone, local with sedation, or general anesthesia. After completing Step 2 markings, infiltrate the labia minora with lidocaine plus epinephrine; injection before marking can distort anatomy.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup> |
| **2. Marking** | Planned resection line marked along the free edge, accounting for desired final labial width and side-to-side symmetry. Particular attention to the anterior extent (clitoral hood / frenular folds) and posterior extent (fourchette) to avoid distortion. |
| **3. Traction and exposure** | The labium is placed on gentle traction — typically with Allis clamps or stay sutures — to expose the tissue and allow accurate excision along the marked line. |
| **4. Excision** | Excise full thickness along the premarked line with a scalpel, scissors, electrocautery, or radiofrequency device. Some surgeons use clamp-and-cut for hemostasis; Zahedi cautions that a rushed clamp-cut-cautery-running-suture sequence can compromise contour or function if tissue retraction, frenular anatomy, and suture tension are neglected.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup> |
| **5. Hemostasis** | Bipolar electrocautery; suture ligation as needed. |
| **6. Closure** | Approximate the central lamina and mucosal edges with absorbable suture (typically 4-0 or 5-0 chromic gut, polyglactin, or poliglecaprone); running or interrupted closure may be used. Zahedi describes a running 5-0 chromic weave in the central lamina and a running subcuticular mucosal closure. Avoid tight over-and-over edge sutures, which can scallop or over-reduce the new free margin.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup> |
| **7. Contralateral side** | The procedure is repeated on the opposite labium with deliberate intraoperative comparison for symmetry of width, length, and edge contour. |

**Technical pearls.** Resection should be conservative; over-resection cannot be reversed without labial reconstruction. Mark and cut without stretching the labium to reduce over-resection risk; after marking, gentle Allis countertraction can stabilize tissue during excision.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup> Do not encroach on or divide the clitoral frenulum, including when a hood reduction is planned; mark any hood excision according to its anatomy.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup> Preserve the fourchette; Zahedi et al. advise keeping the two posterior trim markings at least 1 cm apart to prevent webbing. They separately warn that linear closure of redundant posterior fourchette mucosa can cause scar contracture and pain during intercourse.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup>

## Outcomes

### Aesthetic and patient-reported outcomes

Meta-analyses report pooled satisfaction across labiaplasty techniques of 94% (Géczi 2024, 53 studies with quantitative data) and 99% (Escandón 2022, 46 studies), with heterogeneous, mostly non-randomized source studies.<sup>[[7]](#ref7)[[8]](#ref8)</sup> A 2026 review of 11 female genital cosmetic surgery studies found an association with improved sexual-function scores in six pooled studies (SMD 0.52, 95% CI 0.38–0.65), but rated certainty low and found no clear superiority of one technique.<sup>[[14]](#ref14)</sup> Series and trials that include trim labiaplasty:

- **Sorice-Virk 2020 prospective n = 62** (82% trim, 18% wedge). Patients reported a mean 6.5 of 11 surveyed physical and appearance-related complaints before surgery; 93.5% reported none of those 11 at mean 13.3-month follow-up (range 6–24 months).<sup>[[9]](#ref9)</sup>
- **Goodman 2010 multicenter retrospective cross-sectional survey, 258 respondents of 473 patients contacted** (341 procedures, including vaginoplasty and perineoplasty). Among survey respondents across the mixed procedure groups, overall satisfaction was 91.6% at 6–42 months. Subjective sexual enhancement (p = 0.0078) was most marked after vaginal tightening and perineal support procedures.<sup>[[10]](#ref10)</sup>
- **Ucar 2025 retrospective comparison, n = 40.** Trim showed greater improvement in genital self-image (FGSIS) than wedge in this single-center comparison, and wedge showed greater improvement in some sexual-function domains.<sup>[[6]](#ref6)</sup>
- **Minikowski 2025 RCT n = 48.** No statistically significant difference between wedge and trim was detected for genital self-image improvement or BDD-symptom reduction at 6 months; no statistically significant association between technique and complications was detected in this sample.<sup>[[5]](#ref5)</sup>

### Body-image and psychological outcomes

- **Sharp 2016 prospective controlled study (29 labiaplasty patients, 22 comparison women).** Genital appearance dissatisfaction fell (p &lt; 0.001), but general psychological well-being and relationship quality did not change. Higher preoperative psychological distress (p = 0.001) and having a romantic partner (p = 0.016) were associated with lower satisfaction with the result.<sup>[[11]](#ref11)</sup>
- **Minikowski 2025.** Genital self-image improved and BDD-symptom scores fell in both the wedge and trim groups (P &lt; 0.001), with no difference between groups.<sup>[[5]](#ref5)</sup>

## Complications

| Complication | Notes |
|---|---|
| **Bleeding / hematoma** | Generally uncommon. |
| **Infection** | Rare; standard wound care + selective antibiotics. |
| **Wound dehiscence** | Escandón 2022 meta-analysis: the highest pooled dehiscence was with laser-assisted labiaplasty (5%; 95% CI 2–8%) and wedge resection (3%; 95% CI 1–5%).<sup>[[7]](#ref7)</sup> Géczi 2024 pooled wedge dehiscence at 8% (95% CI 5–13%).<sup>[[8]](#ref8)</sup> In the Ucar comparison, all three early dehiscences occurred in wedge patients who were active smokers.<sup>[[6]](#ref6)</sup> |
| **Flap necrosis** | **Not applicable** — trim is not a flap technique. Escandón reported 3 cases of flap necrosis among 3,804 patients; 2 followed wedge resection and 1 was unspecified.<sup>[[7]](#ref7)</sup> |
| **Loss of native edge** | Inherent to the technique, not strictly a complication; patients must be counseled and consented for it. |
| **Visible edge scar** | The suture line becomes the new free margin and is sometimes visible as a fine line; uncommon source of dissatisfaction. |
| **Over-resection / "amputated" appearance** | Potentially irreversible trim-specific harm. Tissue retraction after excision can leave too little labium and cause chronic discomfort, tightness, or dyspareunia. Reconstruction may require local advancement, a flap, or a graft. Zahedi cautions that local tissue may be insufficient, making correction impossible. Mark and cut without stretching and preserve a conservative residual cuff.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup> |
| **Painful scarring or dyspareunia** | Recognized risks after frenular injury, over-resection, or posterior fourchette contracture; their trim-specific frequency and persistence are uncertain.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[3]](#ref3)</sup> |
| **Altered sensation** | A potential complication; its frequency and persistence after trim specifically are not established.<sup>[[3]](#ref3)</sup> |
| **Revision** | In Escandón 2022, the most common reasons for revision (169 cases) were dehiscence and aesthetic concerns.<sup>[[7]](#ref7)</sup> |

## Trim vs wedge — comparative summary

| Parameter | Trim (edge) resection | Central wedge resection |
|---|---|---|
| **Native edge** | Removed | Preserved |
| **Best for** | Hyperpigmented or irregular edge; diffuse protrusion | Good-quality edge; focal central protrusion |
| **Genital self-image (FGSIS)** | Higher in the Ucar 2025 retrospective comparison; no statistically significant between-group difference detected in the Minikowski 2025 RCT at 6 months<sup>[[5]](#ref5)[[6]](#ref6)</sup> | No statistically significant between-group difference detected in the 48-person RCT at 6 months<sup>[[5]](#ref5)</sup> |
| **Genital self-image and BDD-symptom reduction** | Improved from baseline; no statistically significant difference from wedge detected at 6 months<sup>[[5]](#ref5)</sup> | Improved from baseline; no statistically significant difference from trim detected at 6 months<sup>[[5]](#ref5)</sup> |
| **Dehiscence** | Not separately pooled in the abstracts cited | Pooled 3%, 95% CI 1–5% (Escandón); 8%, 95% CI 5–13% (Géczi)<sup>[[7]](#ref7)[[8]](#ref8)</sup> |
| **Flap necrosis** | Not applicable | Reported (rare)<sup>[[7]](#ref7)</sup> |
| **Nicotine / cocaine exposure** | Zahedi's algorithm relatively favors trim if surgery proceeds; exposure itself is not an indication<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup> | Wedge requires robust perfusion; Zahedi advises avoiding wedge during active exposure, and Ucar reported three early wedge dehiscences in smokers<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[6]](#ref6)</sup> |
| **Surgeon experience needed** | Precise marking and frenular preservation are required; apparent simplicity can be deceptive<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup> | Flap design and vascular preservation are required<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup> |
| **Decision rule (Zahedi 2023)** | Edge / pigment removal, poor edge quality, diffuse protrusion, smokers | Good edge quality, focal central protrusion, edge-preservation goal |

## Postoperative management

- **Activity restriction.** One published postoperative protocol advises avoiding tampons and intercourse for 4 weeks after trim, high-impact exercise until 6 weeks, and saddle activities such as cycling for at least 8 weeks, adjusted for healing and comfort.<sup>[[15]](#ref15)</sup>
- **Wound care.** Sitz baths starting 24–48 h postoperatively, topical antibiotic ointment, loose-fitting clothing.
- **Edema control.** For the first 48 hours, place cold packs over underpants rather than directly on the skin or mucosa, 20 minutes on and 20 minutes off; use intermittent pelvic elevation when feasible.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup>
- **Follow-up cadence.** 1 week (wound check), 6 weeks (clearance for activity), 3–6 months (final outcome assessment).
- **Patient-reported outcome (PRO) assessment.** FSFI and FGSIS at baseline and ≥ 6 months postoperatively.
- **Counseling on edema and final result.** Patients should be told that the labia will look more swollen for several weeks and that the final aesthetic result is not assessable until about 3–6 months, when edema fully resolves.

## Pitfalls and prevention

- **Over-resection.** Over-resection is the main irreversible error. Mark conservatively; re-examine without traction; err on the side of leaving a small visible labial cuff.
- **Asymmetry.** Most often from inattention to side-to-side comparison during marking. Mark both sides before excising either; compare under identical traction.
- **Anterior over-extension.** Resection carried too anteriorly can create distortion at the clitoral hood and frenular folds. If significant hood redundancy coexists, plan a combined hoodoplasty rather than extending the trim line.<sup>[[12]](#ref12)</sup>
- **Posterior over-extension.** Resection carried too posteriorly can pull the fourchette and narrow the introitus.
- **Patient selection.** Assess expectations and screen for BDD and other psychological concerns; refer for evaluation before considering surgery when such concerns are suspected.<sup>[[3]](#ref3)</sup> Higher preoperative distress was associated with lower satisfaction at 6 months in a 29-patient prospective labiaplasty cohort, but the study did not establish distress alone as an automatic exclusion criterion.<sup>[[11]](#ref11)</sup>
