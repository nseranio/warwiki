# Laser-Assisted Labiaplasty

**Laser-assisted labiaplasty** uses a laser, most commonly a CO₂ laser at 10,600 nm, as the cutting or excision instrument in place of a scalpel or scissors during a standard labiaplasty. Some reviews list laser labiaplasty as its own technique,<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[2]](#ref2)</sup> but the laser is a cutting tool that can be used with any resection pattern (trim, wedge, de-epithelialization, hood-extending hockey stick). A 2024 meta-analysis found significantly less bleeding, swelling and hematoma with laser than with scalpel.<sup>[[4]](#ref4)</sup> A 2022 meta-analysis found the highest pooled dehiscence among techniques for laser-assisted labiaplasty (5%, 95% CI 2–8%);<sup>[[3]](#ref3)</sup> the lateral zone of thermal coagulation at the wound edge is a proposed explanation, and the 2024 meta-analysis pooled wedge resection dehiscence higher (8%, 95% CI 5–13%).<sup>[[4]](#ref4)</sup> For positioning versus other female cosmetic options see [Cosmetic Genital Surgery — Female](/docs/surgical-techniques/04l-cosmetic-genital-surgery/female-cosmetic).

**Society positioning and FDA distinction.** The FDA 2018 Safety Communication warned against the use of energy-based devices (lasers, RF) for vaginal "rejuvenation" or cosmetic vaginal procedures, citing serious adverse events including vaginal burns, scarring, pain during intercourse and recurring or chronic pain. The FDA had not cleared or approved any energy-based device for these indications.<sup>[[19]](#ref19)</sup> ACOG 2020 takes a similar position.<sup>[[5]](#ref5)</sup> The FIGO 2025 statement holds that it is ethically inappropriate for obstetrician-gynecologists to recommend, perform or refer patients for cosmetic genital surgery and notes insufficient data on the safety and effectiveness of laser devices for cosmetic purposes.<sup>[[20]](#ref20)</sup> The 2018 warning targets non-surgical, non-excisional applications (fractional laser to "tighten" the vagina or treat "laxity") and does not address the use of a laser as a surgical cutting instrument during labiaplasty itself.<sup>[[6]](#ref6)</sup> The remainder of this article covers surgical laser labiaplasty.

---

## Distinguishing surgical laser labiaplasty from non-surgical "rejuvenation"

| Use | What the laser does | Tissue removal | FDA clearance |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Surgical laser labiaplasty** | Replaces scalpel as cutting tool during excisional labiaplasty | Yes — full-thickness or epithelial excision | Standard surgical procedure |
| **Non-surgical vulvovaginal "rejuvenation"** | Fractional CO₂ or Er:YAG at lower, non-excisional settings to induce collagen remodeling | No | **Not FDA-cleared** for cosmetic / "rejuvenation" indications<sup>[[5]](#ref5)[[6]](#ref6)</sup> |

The two uses are easily conflated in counseling and marketing.

## Laser types

| Laser | Wavelength | Role |
|---|---|---|
| **CO₂** | 10,600 nm | Most commonly reported for surgical labiaplasty. High water absorption gives precise vaporization with simultaneous hemostasis. Typical settings are 10–30 W, continuous or superpulsed.<sup>[[7]](#ref7)[[8]](#ref8)</sup> |
| **Diode** | 810–980 nm | Deeper thermal increase than CO₂ or Er:YAG in an ex vivo bovine tongue model (5 W diode). Less commonly used for labiaplasty.<sup>[[9]](#ref9)</sup> |
| **Er:YAG** | 2,940 nm | Smallest thermal increase of the lasers compared in the same ex vivo model;<sup>[[9]](#ref9)</sup> hemostasis is generally weaker than with CO₂. More commonly used for non-ablative vaginal applications than surgical excision.<sup>[[10]](#ref10)</sup> |
| **Radiofrequency (not a laser)** | Not applicable | Some series group RF cutting (e.g., Ellman Surgitron) with energy-assisted labiaplasty. Curvilinear labiaplasty using RF has been described by its authors as precise and safe.<sup>[[11]](#ref11)</sup> |

## Surgical technique (CO₂ laser labiaplasty, after Pardo 2006)

The laser substitutes for the scalpel during excision; the rest of the procedure follows whatever resection pattern is planned (trim, wedge, de-epithelialization, hockey stick).<sup>[[12]](#ref12)</sup>

| Step | Detail |
|---|---|
| **1. Position and anesthesia** | Lithotomy. Local with epinephrine, with or without sedation, or general. |
| **2. Marking** | The chosen resection pattern is marked with a surgical pen — linear, V, modified, or hood-extending. |
| **3. Laser excision** | CO₂ laser in **focused mode** at 10–30 W is used to excise tissue along the marked lines. The laser cuts and coagulates small vessels simultaneously, producing a relatively bloodless field.<sup>[[12]](#ref12)[[8]](#ref8)</sup> |
| **4. Hemostasis** | The laser's coagulative properties seal small vessels during cutting. Larger vessels still require selective bipolar electrocautery or suture ligation.<sup>[[8]](#ref8)</sup> |
| **5. Closure** | Approximation with fine absorbable suture (4-0 or 5-0 polyglactin or chromic gut), as in conventional labiaplasty.<sup>[[12]](#ref12)</sup> |
| **6. Specimen handling** | If the excised tissue is sent for histopathology, note that laser excision creates a thermal-artifact zone at the margins that can complicate margin interpretation.<sup>[[16]](#ref16)</sup> |

**Safety requirements.** Laser use calls for eye protection for all OR personnel, smoke and plume evacuation (laser plume is a recognized occupational hazard), and a designated laser safety officer per institutional laser-safety policy.

## Outcomes vs scalpel — the Géczi 2024 meta head-to-head

The Géczi 2024 systematic review and meta-analysis (53 studies with quantitative data) compared cutting tools across labiaplasty techniques. Scalpel had a significantly higher incidence than laser of bleeding, swelling and hematoma:<sup>[[4]](#ref4)</sup>

| Parameter | Laser | Scalpel | Significance |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Intraoperative bleeding** | Lower | Higher | Significant (p &lt; 0.05) |
| **Postoperative swelling** | Lower | Higher | Significant (p &lt; 0.05) |
| **Hematoma** | Lower | Higher | Significant (p &lt; 0.05) |

Other properties, drawn from tissue-level and non-labiaplasty studies rather than the Géczi meta-analysis:

| Parameter | Laser | Scalpel |
|---|---|---|
| **Hemostasis during surgery** | Simultaneous coagulation<sup>[[8]](#ref8)</sup> | Requires separate cautery |
| **Healing time** | Reduced in dermatologic CO₂ laser experience<sup>[[8]](#ref8)</sup> | Standard |
| **Lateral thermal damage** | Present (100–500 µm zone)<sup>[[15]](#ref15)[[16]](#ref16)</sup> | Minimal |

Pardo 2006, a single-center CO₂ laser series, reported that 91% of 55 patients were very satisfied and 9% satisfied (none dissatisfied) at 60 days. Four of 55 (7%) had minimal early-postoperative suture dehiscence, all of which healed without intervention.<sup>[[12]](#ref12)</sup>

## The dehiscence trade-off

The Escandón 2022 systematic review and meta-analysis of labia minoraplasty reported the highest pooled dehiscence for laser-assisted labiaplasty at 5% (95% CI 2–8%), followed by wedge resection at 3% (1–5%).<sup>[[3]](#ref3)</sup> The Géczi 2024 meta-analysis pooled wedge resection dehiscence at 8% (5–13%).<sup>[[4]](#ref4)</sup> Rates therefore vary by pooling method, and laser is not consistently the highest.

**Proposed mechanism.** CO₂ laser excision creates a lateral zone of thermal coagulation, described as 100–500 µm at the wound edge. In non-genital models, compared with cold-steel incisions, this zone shows:<sup>[[14]](#ref14)[[15]](#ref15)[[16]](#ref16)</sup>

- Delayed early-phase re-epithelialization
- Reduced early collagen deposition
- Lower early tensile strength

These differences are described as equalizing over time. They may raise dehiscence risk in the first 1–2 weeks, when activity restriction is hardest to enforce; this has not been shown directly in labiaplasty.

---

## Advantages

- **Bleeding.** Less intraoperative bleeding, through simultaneous vaporization and coagulation of small vessels.<sup>[[4]](#ref4)[[8]](#ref8)</sup>
- **Swelling.** Less postoperative swelling and hematoma in the Géczi 2024 meta-analysis.<sup>[[4]](#ref4)</sup>
- **Precision.** Tissue is removed with little mechanical trauma, which helps in anatomically constrained access.<sup>[[8]](#ref8)</sup>
- **Setting.** Office-based CO₂ laser surgery under local anesthesia has been described for benign lower genital tract conditions.<sup>[[13]](#ref13)</sup>
- **Combination.** Laser labiaplasty can be combined with concurrent gynecologic procedures (hood reduction, hymenoplasty) without increased complications in the Pardo 2006 series.<sup>[[12]](#ref12)</sup>

---

## Disadvantages

- **Dehiscence.** Highest pooled dehiscence among techniques in the Escandón 2022 meta-analysis (5%; 2–8%).<sup>[[3]](#ref3)</sup>
- **Thermal damage.** A lateral zone of 100–500 µm may delay early wound healing and complicates margin histology.<sup>[[15]](#ref15)[[16]](#ref16)</sup>
- **Equipment cost.** CO₂ systems require capital investment and maintenance.<sup>[[8]](#ref8)</sup>
- **Safety overhead.** Eye protection, plume evacuation and a laser safety officer are needed.
- **Learning curve.** Laser physics, tissue interaction and safety training are needed.
- **Histologic artifact.** Thermal artifact appears at specimen margins.<sup>[[16]](#ref16)</sup>
- **Regulatory confusion.** Surgical laser labiaplasty is easily conflated with the FDA-warned non-surgical "vaginal rejuvenation" use of energy-based devices.<sup>[[19]](#ref19)</sup>

## Tool comparison — laser vs scalpel vs RF / electrocautery

| Feature | CO₂ laser | Scalpel | Electrocautery / RF |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Hemostasis** | Simultaneous with cutting | Requires separate cautery | Good |
| **Lateral thermal damage** | Moderate | Minimal | Moderate–high |
| **Intraoperative bleeding** | Lower | Higher | Lower |
| **Dehiscence (pooled)** | 5% (highest by technique, Escandón 2022)<sup>[[3]](#ref3)</sup> | Not pooled by tool; wedge 8% in Géczi 2024<sup>[[4]](#ref4)</sup> | Not pooled |
| **Early wound healing** | Slightly delayed; normalizes | Fastest early | Delayed |
| **Cost** | High | Low | Moderate |
| **Safety overhead** | Extensive (eye protection, plume) | Minimal | Moderate |

## Long-term outcomes context

McGrattan 2025 long-term review: across all labiaplasty techniques, the most common long-term complications are postoperative asymmetry (approximately 6%), revisional surgery (approximately 5.6%), and scarring (approximately 1.9%). Tool-level data distinguishing laser from scalpel at long-term follow-up are limited.<sup>[[17]](#ref17)</sup>

Surgical laser labiaplasty is sometimes combined with non-surgical fractional CO₂ for vulvovaginal rejuvenation in "genital beautification" packages (Toplu 2021, n = 37); each component is counseled separately because the FDA status differs.<sup>[[18]](#ref18)</sup>

---

## Postoperative management

- **Activity restriction.** Avoid intercourse, tampon use, and strenuous exercise for 4–6 weeks, with particular attention to the first 1–2 weeks, when dehiscence is most likely.
- **Wound care.** Ice / cold compresses 48–72 h; sitz baths from 24–48 h; topical antibiotic ointment; loose-fitting clothing.
- **Smoking cessation.** Advised preoperatively when feasible.
- **Follow-up.** 1 week (active dehiscence surveillance), 2 weeks, 6 weeks (clearance), 3–6 months (final outcome).
- **PRO assessment.** FSFI and FGSIS at baseline and ≥ 6 months postoperatively.
