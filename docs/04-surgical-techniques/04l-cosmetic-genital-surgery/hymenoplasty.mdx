# Hymenoplasty (Hymen Reconstruction)

**Hymenoplasty** (also called **hymenorrhaphy** or **hymen reconstruction**) restores or reconstructs the hymen to narrow the vaginal introitus and produce vaginal bleeding upon subsequent sexual intercourse.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[2]](#ref2)</sup> It is one of the least described vulvovaginal procedures in plastic surgery, with no surgical standard.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup> In the Lahlali 2021 retrospective series from one Moroccan center (n = 529), no hymenorrhaphy failure was reported, and every patient who had intercourse within 15 days of hymenorrhaphy bled.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup> For positioning versus other female cosmetic options see [Cosmetic Genital Surgery — Female](/docs/surgical-techniques/04l-cosmetic-genital-surgery/female-cosmetic).

**Society positioning and ethical context.** ACOG Committee Opinion No. 795 (2020) classifies hymenoplasty as elective female genital cosmetic surgery. It states that such procedures are not medically indicated in the absence of a clinical indication, that women should be told high-quality data are lacking, and that patients should be assessed for body dysmorphic disorder (BDD) if indicated.<sup>[[3]](#ref3)</sup> The FIGO 2025 statement goes further and asserts that it is ethically inappropriate for obstetrician-gynecologists to recommend, perform or refer patients for cosmetic genital surgery.<sup>[[12]](#ref12)</sup> Hymenoplasty is generally lawful in most jurisdictions,<sup>[[4]](#ref4)</sup> but it is a criminal offence in England and Wales, where the Health and Care Act 2022 prohibited it from 1 July 2022. Local law applies. Hymenoplasty is distinct from female genital cutting or mutilation, which is illegal in most jurisdictions.

---

## Definition and terminology

| Procedure | Description |
|---|---|
| **Hymenorrhaphy** | Direct suturing of existing hymen remnants together<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup> |
| **Hymenal-flap hymenoplasty** | Reconstruction using the patient's own hymen remnant tissue as flaps<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup> |
| **Vaginal-flap hymenoplasty** | Creation of a neo-hymen from vaginal mucosal flaps when remnants are insufficient<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup> |

The procedure is classified by ACOG under elective female genital cosmetic surgery.<sup>[[3]](#ref3)</sup>

---

## Indications and motivations

Hymenoplasty is not medically indicated in the traditional sense. Requests are driven primarily by sociocultural factors in communities where premarital virginity is expected and bleeding at first intercourse is considered proof of virginity.<sup>[[2]](#ref2)[[5]](#ref5)</sup> Reported motivations include<sup>[[5]](#ref5)[[6]](#ref6)</sup>:

- Fear of social consequences: humiliation, ostracism, divorce, and in extreme cases "honor" violence or killing.
- Desire to marry and become a mother in cultures where perceived virginity is a prerequisite.
- Recovery from past sexual trauma, including rape or coercion.
- Psychological well-being and a sense of bodily autonomy.

The clinician's role includes non-judgmental counseling about the lack of high-quality data, the sociocultural pressures driving the request, assessment for BDD when indicated, and informed-consent documentation.

## Surgical techniques

Choice of technique depends primarily on the presence and quality of hymen remnants.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup>

### 1. Hymenorrhaphy (temporary / simple repair)

The edges of existing hymen remnants are approximated and sutured with absorbable suture (typically 5-0 polyglactin), leaving a small central opening. The simplest technique. It is considered temporary because absorbable sutures dissolve over weeks. In Lahlali 2021 (n = 529), 42% underwent hymenorrhaphy. There was no reported failure, and all patients who had intercourse within 15 days bled.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup>

### 2. Hymenal-flap hymenoplasty

When hymen remnants are present but insufficient for simple suturing, the remnant tissue is mobilized as flaps and sutured to create a more durable reconstruction. In the same Moroccan series, 39% of patients underwent this technique.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup>

### 3. Vaginal-flap hymenoplasty

When hymen remnants are absent or severely deficient, vaginal mucosal flaps are raised from the introital walls and sutured together to create a neo-hymen, a more involved procedure. In Lahlali 2021, 19% underwent this with only 1 failure of 99.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup>

### 4. Vestibulo-introital tightening technique (Eserdağ 2021)

A diamond-shaped incision is made in the vestibulum with the base at the posterior midline and the superior corner 2–3 cm above the hymen. The submucosal layer is closed from the apex downward, incorporating vaginal mucosa. This reduces tension on the hymen alone and adds introital tightening. In this retrospective series of 145 procedures, satisfaction was 99.3% and no adverse events were observed.<sup>[[7]](#ref7)</sup>

### 5. STSI (Suture Three Stratums around the Introitus)

Three tissue layers around the introitus are sutured to create a durable reconstruction. In this retrospective series (n = 125), healing was recorded in 91.9% (91/99 who returned at 1 month). Among the 51 patients who had intercourse afterward, 92.2% (47/51) were satisfied and 54.9% (28/51) reported bleeding at first intercourse.<sup>[[8]](#ref8)</sup>

### 6. Introital fascial approach — temporary vs permanent (Dogan 2024)

A retrospective comparative study of 246 patients (2015–2023) used an introital fascial approach for temporary and permanent techniques. Bleeding satisfaction refers to satisfaction with the duration of bleeding:<sup>[[9]](#ref9)</sup>

| Endpoint | Temporary | Permanent |
|---|---|---|
| **Bleeding satisfaction** | 100% | 78.6% (110/140) |
| **Pain at first intercourse (VAS)** | 7.0 (IQR 6–7) | 4.0 (IQR 2–5) |
| **Median age** | 27 yr | 24 yr |
| **Durability** | Days–weeks | Months–years |

## Temporary vs permanent hymenoplasty

| Feature | Temporary | Permanent |
|---|---|---|
| **Timing before intercourse** | Days to ~ 2 weeks | Months to years |
| **Mechanism of bleeding** | Suture disruption + raw mucosal surfaces | Tissue disruption of the reconstructed membrane |
| **Bleeding satisfaction** | 100% | 78.6% |
| **Pain at first intercourse (VAS)** | 7.0 | 4.0 |
| **Typical age** | Older (median 27 yr) | Younger (median 24 yr) |
| **Durability** | Days–weeks | Months–years |

## Anesthesia and perioperative management

- **Anesthesia.** Typically local with epinephrine as an outpatient procedure; sedation or general anesthesia per preference and technique complexity.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[7]](#ref7)</sup>
- **Operative time.** Approximately 30–60 min.
- **Postoperative care** (typical practice, not from a comparative study).
  - Topical emollients (petroleum jelly, zinc-based cream) to the surgical site.
  - Pain management with NSAIDs or topical anesthetic jelly (typically minimal).
  - Avoidance of tampon use and sexual activity until healing is complete.
  - Keep the introitus clean and dry.

## Complications

The series report few, minor complications.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[7]](#ref7)[[8]](#ref8)</sup> ACOG lists pain, bleeding, infection, scarring, adhesions, altered sensation, dyspareunia and need for reoperation as potential complications of female genital cosmetic surgery.<sup>[[3]](#ref3)</sup>

| Complication | Notes |
|---|---|
| Pain | Pain at first intercourse was higher after temporary than permanent hymenoplasty (median VAS 7.0 vs 4.0).<sup>[[9]](#ref9)</sup> |
| Bleeding | Rare. One uncontrolled postoperative bleed in 125 patients (Wei 2015).<sup>[[8]](#ref8)</sup> |
| Infection | Not quantified in the cited series. |
| Scarring / adhesions | Not quantified in the cited series. |
| Altered sensation | Not quantified in the cited series. |
| Dyspareunia | No persistent dyspareunia, menstruation changes, or long-term health problems reported in the published series.<sup>[[8]](#ref8)</sup> |
| Stenosis | Not reported in the cited series. ACOG Committee Opinion 780 (hymenal variants, a different indication) describes hymenal excision as rarely associated with long-term sequelae.<sup>[[10]](#ref10)</sup> |
| Reoperation | Not quantified in the cited series. |

## Outcomes

Retrospective case series report satisfaction of 92.2% (STSI), 99.3% (vestibulo-introital technique) and 78.6% for bleeding duration after permanent hymenoplasty, with few complications.<sup>[[7]](#ref7)[[8]](#ref8)[[9]](#ref9)</sup> Bleeding at first intercourse occurred in 54.9% (28/51) after STSI.<sup>[[8]](#ref8)</sup> ACOG states that the safety and effectiveness of female genital cosmetic procedures have not been established.<sup>[[3]](#ref3)</sup>

## Ethical, legal, and professional considerations

| Domain | Key points |
|---|---|
| **In favor (clinician-side arguments)** | Patient autonomy and bodily self-determination; protection from violence, ostracism, or death; mental and social well-being as components of health; pragmatic empowerment in some patients' own framing.<sup>[[5]](#ref5)[[6]](#ref6)</sup> |
| **Against (clinician-side arguments)** | Inherently deceptive in some framings; reinforces patriarchal norms and gender inequality; not medically necessary; perpetuates the myth that an "intact hymen" equates to virginity.<sup>[[5]](#ref5)[[6]](#ref6)</sup> |
| **ACOG 2020** | Not medically indicated; counsel about lack of high-quality data and potential complications; assess for BDD if indicated, and refer for evaluation before surgery when psychological concerns are suspected.<sup>[[3]](#ref3)</sup> |
| **FIGO 2025** | Ethically inappropriate for obstetrician-gynecologists to recommend, perform or refer for cosmetic genital surgery; counsel that procedures are not medically indicated and carry potential risks.<sup>[[12]](#ref12)</sup> |
| **Legal status** | Usually lawful and distinguishable from female genital cutting / mutilation.<sup>[[4]](#ref4)</sup> England and Wales prohibited hymenoplasty in 2022 (Health and Care Act 2022, in force 1 July 2022). Check local law before proceeding. |
| **Islamic ethico-legal perspectives** | Two opinions: always impermissible; or generally impermissible but licit when risks of not having post-coital bleeding are sufficiently great (e.g., threat of violence).<sup>[[11]](#ref11)</sup> |
| **Practice variability** | No standard of practice; institutional consultation approaches vary widely (pedagogical / counseling-first vs more practical), producing significantly different surgical rates.<sup>[[2]](#ref2)[[6]](#ref6)</sup> |

---

## Key takeaways

- Hymenoplasty is technically straightforward but ethically complex. The cited evidence is retrospective single-center case series.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[3]](#ref3)</sup>
- Series report few complications and satisfaction of 78.6–99.3%, but the absence of a standard technique, validated outcome measures and comparative data limits inference.
- Obtain informed consent, assess for BDD when indicated, and take the sociocultural context of the request into account.
- **Check local law.** England and Wales prohibit the procedure.

---

## Postoperative management

- **Activity restriction.** Avoid intercourse, tampon use, and strenuous exercise per the chosen technique's timing window, usually 2 weeks for temporary techniques (with intercourse to follow within approximately 15 days for hymenorrhaphy) and 4–6 weeks for permanent techniques.
- **Wound care.** Topical emollient; sitz baths; loose-fitting clothing.
- **Counseling.** Bleeding at first intercourse is not guaranteed even with successful reconstruction (54.9% after STSI; 78.6% satisfied with bleeding duration after permanent hymenoplasty). The procedure does not "restore virginity" in any biological or universal sense.
