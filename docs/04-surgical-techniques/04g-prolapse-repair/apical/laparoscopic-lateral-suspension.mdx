# Laparoscopic Lateral Suspension (LLS)

**Laparoscopic lateral suspension (LLS)** is a **minimally invasive mesh-based technique** for apical and anterior pelvic organ prolapse repair that suspends the vaginal vault or cervix to the **lateral abdominal wall** via **retroperitoneal tunneling of mesh arms**, avoiding presacral dissection entirely. First described by **Jean-Bernard Dubuisson** in the early 2000s, LLS has emerged as a prominent alternative to laparoscopic sacrocolpopexy, particularly for **anterior-apical prolapse** and **uterine-preserving** surgery.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[2]](#ref2)[[3]](#ref3)[[4]](#ref4)[[5]](#ref5)</sup>

For the sacral-fixation alternative, see [Sacrocolpopexy](/docs/surgical-techniques/04g-prolapse-repair/sacrocolpopexy). For the Cooper's-ligament alternative, see [Pectopexy](/docs/surgical-techniques/04g-prolapse-repair/pectopexy). For the broader prolapse decision framework, see [Prolapse Repair](/docs/surgical-techniques/04g-prolapse-repair).

---

## Definition and Rationale

LLS routes the lateral arms of a **T-shaped mesh** through **retroperitoneal tunnels** to the **lateral abdominal wall**, where they are anchored to the **anterior abdominal wall fascia** at the level of the lateral trocar sites — bypassing the sacral promontory entirely.<sup>[[2]](#ref2)[[3]](#ref3)[[6]](#ref6)</sup>

| Theoretical advantage | Detail |
|---|---|
| **No presacral dissection** | Eliminates presacral venous plexus / middle sacral / hypogastric nerve risks |
| **Limited surgical field** | Vesicovaginal space + retroperitoneal tunnels — no deep pelvic dissection |
| **Shorter operative time** | Consistently reproduced across all comparative studies |
| **Technically simpler** | Shorter learning curve, more accessible field |
| **Suited to uterine preservation** | Mesh fixed to cervical isthmus without requiring hysterectomy<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[2]](#ref2)[[3]](#ref3)</sup> |

---

## Indications and Patient Selection

POP-Q stage ≥ 2 anterior and / or apical prolapse.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[7]](#ref7)[[8]](#ref8)</sup> Particularly advantageous in:

- **Anterior-apical prolapse** — LLS appears optimal for combined anterior + apical repair.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup>
- **Uterine preservation** — mesh fixed to cervical isthmus.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[3]](#ref3)[[6]](#ref6)</sup>
- **Obese patients** — anterior pelvic field is more accessible than the sacral promontory.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup>
- **Difficult promontory access** — prior pelvic radiation, sacral anomalies, deep pelvis, severe adhesions.<sup>[[2]](#ref2)</sup>
- **Post-hysterectomy vault prolapse** — mesh fixed to vaginal cuff.<sup>[[4]](#ref4)</sup>

**Less effective for** severe posterior prolapse — LLS primarily addresses anterior and apical compartments; posterior defects typically require concomitant native-tissue posterior colporrhaphy.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[9]](#ref9)</sup>

## Surgical Technique — Standard Dubuisson LLS

### Positioning and ports<sup>[[2]](#ref2)[[6]](#ref6)</sup>

- Dorsal lithotomy, steep Trendelenburg.
- One **10-mm umbilical optical** trocar, one **10-mm suprapubic** trocar, two **5-mm lateral** trocars placed ~5 cm above the ASIS bilaterally.
- The lateral trocars serve dual purpose: operative access **and** exit points for the mesh arms.

### Step-by-step

1. **Vesicovaginal dissection.** Incise the uterovesical peritoneal fold transversely. Mobilize the bladder off the anterior vaginal wall to ~**2 cm below the bladder neck**.<sup>[[2]](#ref2)[[6]](#ref6)</sup>
   - **Tip (Di Michele 2026)**: a segment of Foley catheter can be used to suspend the bladder to the anterior abdominal wall for atraumatic retraction and to measure the anterior vaginal wall length for mesh tailoring.<sup>[[2]](#ref2)</sup>
2. **Mesh preparation.** **T-shaped mesh** with central (vertical) arm fixed to anterior vaginal wall + cervical isthmus / cuff, and two lateral (horizontal) arms tunneled retroperitoneally. Central arm 4–6 cm × 3–4 cm; lateral arms 15–20 cm × 1.5–2 cm.<sup>[[2]](#ref2)[[6]](#ref6)</sup>
3. **Mesh material — critical.** Use **Type 1 macroporous monofilamentous polypropylene** (standard or titanized). **Avoid Type 3 mesh** (macroporous + multifilamentous / microporous components) — adjusted **OR 13.0** (p = 0.02) for mesh erosion.<sup>[[10]](#ref10)</sup>
4. **Central-arm fixation.** Suture central arm to the **vesicovaginal fascia** with **absorbable sutures** (e.g., Vicryl 2-0). First stitch at the most distal point (near bladder neck); additional sutures proximally. Proximal end fixed to **cervical isthmus** (uterine-preserving) or **vaginal cuff** (post-hysterectomy) — non-absorbable sutures at the cervical stroma.<sup>[[2]](#ref2)[[6]](#ref6)[[9]](#ref9)</sup>
5. **Retroperitoneal tunneling — the defining LLS step.** A laparoscopic grasper introduced through each lateral trocar is advanced **retroperitoneally**:
   - Stay **anterior to the broad ligament** (avoids uterine vessels and ureter)
   - Stay **lateral to the obliterated umbilical artery** (medial umbilical ligament)
   - Avoid the iliac vessels, ureter, and obturator nerve.<sup>[[2]](#ref2)[[6]](#ref6)</sup>

   The grasper creates a tunnel from the lateral abdominal wall to the vesicovaginal space; each lateral mesh arm is grasped and pulled retroperitoneally to exit at the lateral trocar site.
6. **Tension-free adjustment.** Pull lateral arms through the abdominal wall until the apex / cervix is elevated to anatomic position. **Tension-free** — excessive traction causes pain, dyspareunia, and increases mesh erosion. Fix lateral arms to the **anterior abdominal wall fascia** (rectus sheath or external oblique aponeurosis) at the lateral trocar sites with non-absorbable sutures or tacks. Trim excess mesh.<sup>[[2]](#ref2)[[6]](#ref6)</sup>
7. **Peritonization.** Close peritoneum of the uterovesical fold over the mesh with running absorbable suture; **round-ligament plication** can be incorporated for additional support.<sup>[[6]](#ref6)</sup>
8. **Concomitants as needed.** Posterior colporrhaphy (vaginally, native tissue — **avoid posterior mesh**); Burch / midurethral sling for SUI; supracervical hysterectomy when indicated.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[9]](#ref9)[[10]](#ref10)</sup>

### Mesh configurations

| Configuration | Use |
|---|---|
| **T-shaped mesh** (standard Dubuisson) | Standard anterior-apical prolapse<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[2]](#ref2)</sup> |
| Anterior-only mesh | Predominant cystocele<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup> |
| Anterior + posterior mesh | Combined anterior + posterior prolapse — **higher erosion risk**<sup>[[3]](#ref3)</sup> |
| Titanized polypropylene | Standard at some centers<sup>[[4]](#ref4)</sup> |

## Robot-Assisted LLS

Identical surgical steps with the da Vinci platform — 8-mm camera, two lateral robotic trocars 5 cm above ASIS, 10-mm assistant. **Retroperitoneal tunneling is performed with a standard laparoscopic forceps** through the lateral trocars (not robotic arms). Advantages: 3D visualization, enhanced suturing maneuverability.<sup>[[6]](#ref6)</sup>

---

## Modifications

**LLS combined with uterosacral ligament folding and shortening (LLS-ULFS, Xiong 2025)**: adds USL plication to standard LLS. In a 445-patient retrospective cohort: apical anatomical success **94.84%** vs **96.98%** for sacrocolpopexy (NS), with shorter operative time (85 vs 105 min, p < 0.05).<sup>[[11]](#ref11)</sup>

## Outcomes (pooled meta-analyses 2021–2025)

| Endpoint | Result |
|---|---|
| Apical anatomical success | **92.9%** (95% CI 89.8–95.1)<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup> |
| Anterior anatomical success | **86.9%** (95% CI 81.4–90.9)<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup> |
| Posterior anatomical success | 86.55%<sup>[[2]](#ref2)</sup> |
| Subjective success (PGI-I) | **88.9%** (95% CI 85.3–91.7)<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup> |
| Recurrence | 9.6% (95% CI 7.0–13.2)<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup> |
| Reoperation | 6.2% (95% CI 4.3–8.9)<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup> |
| Patient satisfaction | > 90%<sup>[[2]](#ref2)</sup> |
| OR time (LLS alone) | 78–160 min<sup>[[2]](#ref2)[[3]](#ref3)[[4]](#ref4)</sup> |
| OR time (with concomitants) | 123–254 min<sup>[[2]](#ref2)[[4]](#ref4)</sup> |

## vs Laparoscopic Sacrocolpopexy

| Parameter | LLS | LSC | Significance |
|---|---|---|---|
| Apical correction | Comparable | — | OR 1.24, p = 0.55 (NS)<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup> |
| Anterior correction | Comparable | — | OR 0.78, p = 0.39 (NS)<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup> |
| Subjective success | Comparable | — | NS<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup> |
| **Operative time** | **43 min shorter** | — | p = 0.001<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup> |
| Blood loss | Less or comparable | — | NS to p < 0.001<sup>[[2]](#ref2)[[3]](#ref3)</sup> |
| Intraoperative / postoperative complications | Comparable | — | NS<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup> |
| De novo SUI | Comparable | — | NS<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup> |
| Sexual function | Comparable | — | NS<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup> |
| Bowel dysfunction | Comparable | — | NS<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup> |
| Total vaginal length | Shorter | Longer | p < 0.05<sup>[[4]](#ref4)</sup> |

**Three-way comparison** (sacropexy vs pectopexy vs LLS, n = 180): LLS took **less than half the OR time** of sacropexy, with no increase in surgical failure (Sánchez-Ferrer 2025).<sup>[[5]](#ref5)</sup>

A multicenter non-inferiority RCT (preliminary, n = 106 operated): no differences in POP-Q, symptom scales, or body image; only significant difference was **shorter OR time for LLS**.<sup>[[14]](#ref14)</sup>

## Complications

| Complication | Rate |
|---|---|
| Overall complication rate | **5.7%** (95% CI 3.4–9.4)<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup> |
| Mesh-related complications | **1.9%** (95% CI 1.0–3.8)<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup> |
| Clavien-Dindo ≥ III | 1.9% (16/848)<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup> |
| Mesh erosion | 0–10% (pooled 3.8% with mean 82-mo follow-up)<sup>[[2]](#ref2)[[3]](#ref3)</sup> |
| Intraoperative bladder injury | Rare<sup>[[2]](#ref2)[[4]](#ref4)</sup> |
| Conversion to laparotomy | Rare (3/93 in one RCT)<sup>[[5]](#ref5)</sup> |

### Risk factors for mesh erosion (Dällenbach 2016, n = 480, mean follow-up 82 mo)<sup>[[10]](#ref10)</sup>

| Factor | Adjusted OR |
|---|---|
| **Type 3 mesh** (macroporous + multifilamentous / microporous) | **13.0** (p = 0.02) |
| **Smoking** | **10.4** (p < 0.05) |
| **Posterior mesh placement** | **5-fold increase** (p < 0.05) |

**Counsel smokers preoperatively** and use only Type 1 monofilament polypropylene; **avoid posterior mesh** — treat posterior compartment with native-tissue colporrhaphy.

## Advantages and Limitations

| Advantage | Limitation |
|---|---|
| **Shorter OR time** (43 min less; &lt;half in some series)<sup>[[13]](#ref13)[[5]](#ref5)</sup> | Less effective for severe posterior prolapse — needs concomitant native-tissue posterior repair<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[9]](#ref9)</sup> |
| Technically simpler — shorter learning curve<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[3]](#ref3)</sup> | Mesh-dependent — inherent erosion / exposure risks (low with Type 1 monofilament)<sup>[[12]](#ref12)[[10]](#ref10)</sup> |
| Excellent for uterine preservation<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[2]](#ref2)[[6]](#ref6)</sup> | **Limited long-term data** (~ 20 mo mean follow-up) — durability beyond 5 yr less established than sacrocolpopexy<sup>[[12]](#ref12)[[13]](#ref13)</sup> |
| Particularly suited to obese patients<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup> | Lack of standardization across mesh / configuration / technique<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[3]](#ref3)</sup> |
| Comparable anatomical and functional outcomes to sacrocolpopexy<sup>[[13]](#ref13)[[15]](#ref15)[[14]](#ref14)</sup> | Shorter total vaginal length than sacrocolpopexy<sup>[[5]](#ref5)</sup> |
| | **Not yet declared an equally effective alternative** to sacrocolpopexy for advanced (stage III–IV) prolapse — limited subgroup data<sup>[[13]](#ref13)</sup> |

---

## Key Principles

- LLS suspends the vault / cervix to the **lateral abdominal wall** via retroperitoneal mesh tunneling — avoiding presacral dissection entirely.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[2]](#ref2)</sup>
- **Type 1 monofilament polypropylene mesh** is mandatory — Type 3 mesh carries a **13× erosion risk**.<sup>[[10]](#ref10)</sup>
- The retroperitoneal tunnel must remain **anterior to the broad ligament** and **lateral to the obliterated umbilical artery**.<sup>[[2]](#ref2)[[6]](#ref6)</sup>
- **Tension-free placement** — apex elevated to anatomic position without traction; over-tension causes pain and erosion.<sup>[[2]](#ref2)</sup>
- **Avoid posterior mesh** — 5× erosion risk; treat posterior compartment with native-tissue colporrhaphy.<sup>[[10]](#ref10)</sup>
- **Counsel smokers** about the 10× erosion risk; recommend smoking cessation preoperatively.<sup>[[10]](#ref10)</sup>
- **Comparable anatomical and functional outcomes vs sacrocolpopexy** with **significantly shorter OR time** (~43 min less).<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[13]](#ref13)</sup>
- **Best fit**: anterior-apical prolapse with uterine preservation, obese patients, or difficult promontory access.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[3]](#ref3)</sup>
- **Long-term data > 5 yr remain limited** — sacrocolpopexy retains gold-standard status for stage III–IV prolapse.<sup>[[13]](#ref13)</sup>
