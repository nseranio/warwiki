# Laparoscopic Lateral Suspension (LLS)

**Laparoscopic lateral suspension (LLS)** is a mesh-based abdominal approach to selected apical and anterior prolapse in which mesh arms are brought laterally through extraperitoneal/retroperitoneal paths rather than attached at the sacral promontory. Published variants differ in the target attachment, uterine status, mesh, concomitant repair, and platform; “LLS” is not one fully standardized operation.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[2]](#ref2)</sup>

For the sacral fixation alternative, see [Sacrocolpopexy](/docs/surgical-techniques/04g-prolapse-repair/sacrocolpopexy). For the iliopectineal-ligament alternative, see [Pectopexy](/docs/surgical-techniques/04g-prolapse-repair/pectopexy). For the broader decision framework, see [Prolapse Repair](/docs/surgical-techniques/04g-prolapse-repair).

## What the operation changes

LLS avoids **sacral-promontory dissection**, but it does not eliminate individualized risks from abdominal access, dissection, mesh, urinary tract or bowel injury, bleeding, infection, thrombosis, anesthesia, or later reoperation. Avoiding one dissection field is not evidence of a lower overall complication rate for every patient.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[2]](#ref2)</sup>

The lateral approach is principally described for anterior/apical defects. Whether a particular compartment pattern, body habitus, prior surgery, uterine-preservation goal, or difficulty reaching the promontory makes it the preferred operation cannot be inferred from the current evidence alone.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[2]](#ref2)</sup>

## Selection and shared decision-making

When an abdominal mesh repair is under consideration, selection should be individualized around symptoms, compartments involved, uterine/cervical pathology, prior operations, mesh-related considerations, anesthetic risk, surgeon training, and the patient’s priorities. Counseling should compare conservative care, vaginal native-tissue repair, sacrocolpopexy/sacrohysteropexy where appropriate, and the limited comparative follow-up for LLS.

The 2025 systematic review included 12 prospective or randomized reports but acknowledges heterogeneity, different outcome definitions, and incomplete long-term evidence. Its pooled percentages should not be applied as a patient-specific success, complication, or erosion prediction.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup>

## Evidence boundaries

| Question | What the accessible evidence supports | What it does not support |
|---|---|---|
| Short-term comparison | A 2024 single-center randomized study enrolled 93 women, with 89 completing 12-month follow-up; it found no statistically significant between-group difference in the reported anatomic/symptom measures. | Equivalence, noninferiority, or long-term safety/durability. The sample, follow-up, exclusions, and operation-specific protocol limit generalization. |
| Ongoing multicenter comparison | A 2025 preliminary randomized report analyzed 106 operated participants with 1–12 months of follow-up and no observed examination failures in either group. The authors state completion is necessary for relevant conclusions. | A completed noninferiority finding, a definitive functional advantage, or a universal shorter learning curve. |
| Systematic-review outcomes | Prospective and randomized reports suggest LLS is feasible and can improve anatomic and symptom measures in selected cohorts. | Exact universal rates, superiority over sacrocolpopexy, lower mesh morbidity, or a preferred mesh configuration. |
| Technical details | Individual study protocols describe specific meshes, tunnel paths, sutures, and concomitant procedures. | A universal port map, tunnel boundary, mesh, fixation, tensioning, or posterior-repair rule. |

The broader apical-prolapse Cochrane synthesis groups lateral-suspension/round-ligament procedures with pectopexy in relevant comparisons. Those pooled studies do not represent one standardized LLS procedure and are too sparse and imprecise for strong long-term comparative claims.<sup>[[3]](#ref3)</sup>

## Operative safety

This is not a step-by-step operative protocol. Mesh choice, dissection, tunneling, fixation, tensioning, peritoneal closure, urinary-tract assessment, thromboprophylaxis, antibiotic prophylaxis, and postoperative care should follow supervised training, current institutional protocols, and applicable instructions for use.

The absence of a sacral dissection does not make lateral tunneling or mesh use risk-free. Specific mesh-erosion associations reported in older observational cohorts are not a mandate for a universal mesh type or a substitute for discussion of patient, mesh, and procedural factors.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup>

## Follow-up

Follow-up should be symptom- and examination-directed, including assessment for recurrent bulge symptoms, mesh exposure or pain, urinary and bowel symptoms, sexual function, and recovery goals. New bleeding/discharge, pelvic pain, dyspareunia, voiding or defecatory difficulty, or suspected mesh complication warrants evaluation; no fixed surveillance interval can be prescribed from the reviewed evidence.

## Key principles

- LLS uses lateral extraperitoneal/retroperitoneal mesh-arm routes instead of sacral-promontory attachment; technique labels conceal important variation.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[2]](#ref2)</sup>
- Avoiding promontory dissection does not demonstrate lower overall risk or superiority for all patients.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[2]](#ref2)</sup>
- The current randomized evidence is short term, and the multicenter study is preliminary; it does not establish durable equivalence or noninferiority.<sup>[[2]](#ref2)[[4]](#ref4)</sup>
- No universal mesh, tunnel, fixation, tensioning, patient-selection, or follow-up rule is validated by the sources reviewed here.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[3]](#ref3)</sup>
