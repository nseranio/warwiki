# Laparoscopic Lateral Suspension (LLS)

**Laparoscopic lateral suspension (LLS)** is a minimally invasive mesh-based technique for apical and anterior pelvic organ prolapse repair that suspends the vaginal vault or cervix toward the lateral abdominal wall via retroperitoneal tunneling of mesh arms, avoiding presacral dissection. The open lateral-suspension concept dates to Kapandji in 1967; Jean-Bernard Dubuisson adapted it for laparoscopy in 1997. LLS is an option particularly studied for anterior-apical prolapse and uterine-preserving surgery, but the chosen route does not remove all vascular, nerve or mesh risks.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[2]](#ref2)[[3]](#ref3)[[4]](#ref4)[[5]](#ref5)[[16]](#ref16)</sup>

For the sacral-fixation alternative, see [Sacrocolpopexy](/docs/surgical-techniques/04g-prolapse-repair/sacrocolpopexy). For the Cooper's-ligament alternative, see [Pectopexy](/docs/surgical-techniques/04g-prolapse-repair/pectopexy). For the broader prolapse decision framework, see [Prolapse Repair](/docs/surgical-techniques/04g-prolapse-repair).

---

## Definition and Rationale

LLS routes the lateral arms of a T-shaped mesh through retroperitoneal tunnels toward the lateral abdominal wall, bypassing the sacral promontory. Final arm placement or fixation varies: the original laparoscopic description uses lateral aponeurotic support, while a published robotic variant fixes the arms to abdominal-wall peritoneum with absorbable tacks; not all experts routinely attach the arms to muscle fascia.<sup>[[2]](#ref2)[[3]](#ref3)[[6]](#ref6)[[16]](#ref16)</sup>

| Theoretical advantage | Detail |
|---|---|
| **No presacral dissection** | Avoids exposure of the sacral promontory and its presacral structures; other operative risks remain |
| **Different operative field** | Vesicovaginal dissection and lateral retroperitoneal tunnels replace presacral fixation; pelvic dissection and its risks remain |
| **Shorter operative time** | Reported in several comparisons, but affected by case mix, concomitant hysterectomy and high between-study heterogeneity<sup>[[7]](#ref7)[[13]](#ref13)</sup> |
| **Anterior operative field** | May simplify access in selected anatomy; a universal shorter learning curve is not established |
| **Suited to uterine preservation** | Mesh fixed to cervical isthmus without requiring hysterectomy<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[2]](#ref2)[[3]](#ref3)</sup> |

---

## Indications and Patient Selection

LLS has been studied for POP-Q stage ≥ 2 anterior and / or apical prolapse.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[7]](#ref7)[[8]](#ref8)</sup> Situations in which it may be considered include:

- **Anterior-apical prolapse.** The T-mesh route addresses both compartments; comparative superiority is not established.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[16]](#ref16)</sup>
- **Uterine preservation.** Mesh fixed to cervical isthmus.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[3]](#ref3)[[6]](#ref6)</sup>
- **Obese patients.** The non-sacral field may be useful, but an obesity-specific outcome advantage is not established.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[16]](#ref16)</sup>
- **Difficult promontory access.** Selected altered anatomy or adhesions may favor considering a non-sacral route, but prior radiation or complex scarring requires individualized risk assessment.<sup>[[2]](#ref2)[[16]](#ref16)</sup>
- **Post-hysterectomy vault prolapse.** Mesh fixed to vaginal cuff.<sup>[[4]](#ref4)</sup>

**Posterior prolapse needs separate assessment.** LLS primarily targets anterior and apical support; significant posterior defects may need an additional repair. Do not infer an obligatory posterior colporrhaphy or a procedure-level failure rate from mixed cohorts.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[9]](#ref9)[[12]](#ref12)</sup>

## Surgical Technique — Illustrative Dubuisson-Style LLS

The details below combine published laparoscopic and robotic descriptions; trocar layout, mesh dimensions, tissue fixation and lateral-arm anchoring vary by approach and product. They are not one universal operative sequence.<sup>[[2]](#ref2)[[6]](#ref6)[[9]](#ref9)[[16]](#ref16)</sup>

### Positioning and ports<sup>[[2]](#ref2)[[6]](#ref6)</sup>

- Dorsal lithotomy, steep Trendelenburg.
- Trocar plans differ. One published robotic case uses an 8-mm umbilical camera, two lateral 8-mm robotic ports about 5 cm above the ASIS and a 10-mm assistant port; a laparoscopic setup may use a suprapubic port and smaller lateral ports. Choose access and arm exits for the patient's anatomy and the actual technique.<sup>[[2]](#ref2)[[6]](#ref6)</sup>

### Step-by-step

1. **Vesicovaginal dissection.** Incise the uterovesical peritoneal fold transversely. In the Lange robotic example, dissection extends to approximately 2 cm above the bladder neck, not 2 cm below it; identify the neck and individual tissue plane before proceeding.<sup>[[2]](#ref2)[[6]](#ref6)</sup>
   - **Tip (Di Michele 2026 technical video abstract)**: a segment of Foley catheter is described for bladder retraction and anterior-vaginal-wall measurement for mesh tailoring; the complete operative video was not reviewed here.<sup>[[2]](#ref2)</sup>
2. **Mesh preparation.** A T-shaped mesh has a central portion for the anterior vaginal wall and cervical isthmus or cuff, with two lateral arms tunneled retroperitoneally. Mesh dimensions are product- and patient-specific: the Lange robotic example uses a preformed 9 × 41.5-cm titanized mesh, not a universal central-arm or lateral-arm measurement.<sup>[[6]](#ref6)[[9]](#ref9)</sup>
3. **Mesh material.** Published variants use macroporous monofilament polypropylene (including titanized products). In one older 480-woman observational cohort, Type 3 mesh (macroporous with multifilamentous or microporous components) was associated with higher erosion odds than Type 1 mesh (adjusted OR 13.0; 95% CI 1.5–110.6). That association supports material caution, not a universal 13-fold individual risk or an IFU substitute.<sup>[[6]](#ref6)[[9]](#ref9)[[10]](#ref10)</sup>
4. **Central-arm fixation.** Suture central arm to the vesicovaginal fascia with absorbable sutures (e.g., Vicryl 2-0). First stitch at the most distal point (near bladder neck); additional sutures proximally. Proximal end fixed to cervical isthmus (uterine-preserving) or vaginal cuff (post-hysterectomy), with non-absorbable sutures at the cervical stroma.<sup>[[2]](#ref2)[[6]](#ref6)[[9]](#ref9)</sup>
5. **Retroperitoneal tunneling (the defining LLS step).** A laparoscopic grasper introduced through each lateral trocar or skin exit is advanced retroperitoneally toward the round ligament. The published robotic example keeps the forceps visible through the peritoneum and warns against external iliac-vessel injury; tunnel direction and exit are variant-specific. Identify and protect the iliac vessels, ureter and other nearby structures rather than treating a single memorized boundary as sufficient.<sup>[[2]](#ref2)[[6]](#ref6)[[16]](#ref16)</sup>

   The grasper creates a tunnel from the lateral abdominal wall to the vesicovaginal space; each lateral mesh arm is grasped and pulled retroperitoneally to exit at the lateral trocar site.
6. **Tension adjustment and lateral support.** Bring the arms laterally without excessive traction. The historical laparoscopic aponeurotic support and the Lange robotic abdominal-wall peritoneal fixation with absorbable tacks are different techniques; do not turn either into a universal fascia-suture or tack instruction. Confirm the chosen mesh or device instructions and operative anatomy.<sup>[[2]](#ref2)[[6]](#ref6)[[16]](#ref16)</sup>
7. **Peritonization.** Close peritoneum of the uterovesical fold over the mesh with running absorbable suture; round-ligament plication can be incorporated for additional support.<sup>[[6]](#ref6)</sup>
8. **Concomitants as needed.** Posterior colporrhaphy (vaginally, native tissue) for a symptomatic posterior defect; Burch / midurethral sling for selected SUI; supracervical hysterectomy when indicated. The observed posterior-mesh erosion association in one cohort informs selection but is not proof that every posterior mesh configuration or patient has the same risk.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[9]](#ref9)[[10]](#ref10)</sup>

### Mesh configurations

| Configuration | Use |
|---|---|
| **T-shaped mesh** (standard Dubuisson) | Standard anterior-apical prolapse<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[2]](#ref2)</sup> |
| Anterior-only mesh | Predominant cystocele<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup> |
| Anterior + posterior mesh | An option in selected combined defects; posterior mesh placement was associated with higher erosion odds in one older observational cohort, so weigh this against native-tissue posterior repair.<sup>[[10]](#ref10)</sup> |
| Titanized polypropylene | Standard at some centers<sup>[[4]](#ref4)</sup> |

## Robot-Assisted LLS

One published da Vinci case or video variant uses an 8-mm umbilical camera, two lateral 8-mm robotic trocars 5 cm above the ASIS and a 10-mm assistant trocar, with a standard laparoscopic forceps through the lateral trocars for retroperitoneal tunneling. The report fixes lateral arms to abdominal-wall peritoneum using absorbable tacks and illustrates 3D visualization and suturing ergonomics; it does not establish robotic superiority or make its fixation method universal.<sup>[[6]](#ref6)</sup>

## Modifications

LLS combined with uterosacral ligament folding and shortening (LLS-ULFS, Xiong 2025) adds USL plication to LLS and is not LLS alone. In a retrospective cohort of 213 LLS-ULFS and 232 sacrocolpopexy patients, two-year apical anatomical success was 94.84% versus 96.98% (no detected difference); operative-time medians were 85 versus 105 min (p &lt; 0.001). Baseline age and BMI and concomitant procedures differed.<sup>[[11]](#ref11)</sup>

## Outcomes (heterogeneous cohorts and reviews)

The Wang 2025 single-arm meta-analysis pooled 18 studies and 1,430 patients (mean follow-up 20.27 months). Its rates are not directly comparable with another operation's rates and do not establish long-term durability; outcome definitions and between-study heterogeneity vary.<sup>[[12]](#ref12)</sup>

| Endpoint | Result |
|---|---|
| Apical anatomical success, Wang pooled | **92.9%** (95% CI 89.8–95.1)<sup>[[12]](#ref12)</sup> |
| Anterior anatomical success, Wang pooled | **86.9%** (95% CI 81.4–90.9); high heterogeneity<sup>[[12]](#ref12)</sup> |
| Subjective success, Wang pooled | **88.9%** (95% CI 85.3–91.7); not a uniform PGI-I endpoint<sup>[[12]](#ref12)</sup> |
| Recurrence, Wang pooled | **9.6%** (95% CI 7.0–13.2)<sup>[[12]](#ref12)</sup> |
| Reoperation, Wang pooled | **6.2%** (95% CI 4.3–8.9)<sup>[[12]](#ref12)</sup> |
| Anterior / apical / posterior success, Plotti study medians | **91.15% / 94.95% / 86.55%** across 12 prospective or randomized reports (1,261 patients); **medians of study rates**, not pooled patient-level estimates<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup> |
| Operative time, Plotti included studies | Reported range **71.13–254 min** across heterogeneous procedures and concomitants; not a procedure-specific expected time<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup> |

## vs Laparoscopic Sacrocolpopexy

The Lombisani 2025 comparative review included six studies and 632 women with median follow-up about 14 months. Included studies excluded posterior defects; prior prolapse surgery was excluded, and concomitant hysterectomy was imbalanced between groups. The 2024 randomized study enrolled 93 women, with 89 completing 12 months and concomitant supracervical hysterectomy; another randomized comparison used concurrent total laparoscopic hysterectomy, a distinct operative context. Differences in mesh, fixation and outcomes further limit transportability. A nonsignificant result is not proof of equivalence.<sup>[[7]](#ref7)[[13]](#ref13)[[15]](#ref15)</sup>

| Parameter | LLS | LSC | Significance |
|---|---|---|---|
| Apical correction | No detected difference | — | OR 1.24, p = 0.55; equivalence not shown<sup>[[13]](#ref13)</sup> |
| Anterior correction | No detected difference | — | OR 0.78, p = 0.39; equivalence not shown<sup>[[13]](#ref13)</sup> |
| Subjective success | No detected difference | — | Definitions varied across studies<sup>[[13]](#ref13)</sup> |
| **Operative time** | Mean **43.1 min shorter** | — | Very high heterogeneity (I² = 97%); concomitant hysterectomy was more frequent in the sacrocolpopexy group<sup>[[13]](#ref13)</sup> |
| Blood loss | No robust comparative advantage established | — | Only two studies contributed to this review outcome<sup>[[13]](#ref13)</sup> |
| Complications and mesh events | No detected difference | — | Events were uncommon; no durable safety equivalence established<sup>[[13]](#ref13)</sup> |
| De novo SUI, sexual and bowel function | Procedure-specific differences remain uncertain | — | Short follow-up, varying definitions and limited event counts<sup>[[7]](#ref7)[[13]](#ref13)</sup> |
| Total vaginal length | May differ by technique | — | Do not generalize a pooled direction from the available comparative set<sup>[[7]](#ref7)[[13]](#ref13)</sup> |

Three-way comparison (sacropexy versus pectopexy versus LLS, n = 180): this observational cohort reported shorter operative time for LLS and no detected difference in its reported failure outcome. It does not establish equivalence, nor make the time ratio a general expectation.<sup>[[5]](#ref5)</sup>

The multicenter non-inferiority trial reported preliminary results for 106 operated patients, with 182 planned and available follow-up of 1–12 months. It found no detected differences in the listed clinical scales and shorter operative time for LLS, but did not yet establish non-inferiority or long-term equivalence.<sup>[[14]](#ref14)</sup>

## Complications

| Complication | Rate |
|---|---|
| Overall complication rate, Wang pooled | **5.7%** (95% CI 3.4–9.4)<sup>[[12]](#ref12)</sup> |
| Mesh-related complications, Wang pooled | **1.9%** (95% CI 1.0–3.8)<sup>[[12]](#ref12)</sup> |
| Clavien-Dindo ≥ III, Wang review | **16/848 (1.9%)** among studies reporting severity<sup>[[12]](#ref12)</sup> |
| Mesh erosion, Dällenbach cohort | **18/480 (3.8%)**, mean follow-up **82.3 months**; this is one cohort, not a pooled long-term rate<sup>[[10]](#ref10)</sup> |
| Bladder injury / conversion | Reported but infrequent in the cited series; rates depend on case mix and reporting<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[4]](#ref4)[[7]](#ref7)</sup> |

### Risk factors for mesh erosion (Dällenbach 2016, n = 480, mean follow-up 82 mo)<sup>[[10]](#ref10)</sup>

| Factor | Adjusted OR |
|---|---|
| **Type 3 mesh** (macroporous + multifilamentous / microporous) | **13.0** (95% CI 1.5–110.6) |
| **Smoking** | **10.4** (95% CI 2.3–46.5) |
| **Posterior mesh placement** | **5.1** (95% CI 1.2–21.8) |

These are associations from one nested case-control analysis within the cohort, not absolute-risk multipliers or proof of causation. Counsel smokers about mesh exposure or erosion and cessation. Consider mesh characteristics, product instructions and native-tissue posterior options in shared decision-making; this study alone does not prescribe one mesh or prohibit every posterior-mesh configuration.<sup>[[10]](#ref10)</sup>

## Advantages and Limitations

| Advantage | Limitation |
|---|---|
| Presacral dissection is avoided, and shorter operative time was observed in the comparative meta-analysis<sup>[[13]](#ref13)</sup> | The time estimate has **I² = 97%** and imbalanced concomitant hysterectomy; it is not a guaranteed saving.<sup>[[13]](#ref13)</sup> |
| Anterior/lateral access can be useful when promontory access is difficult; this is a selection consideration, not proven procedural superiority.<sup>[[2]](#ref2)[[16]](#ref16)</sup> | The lateral tunnels still require vascular and nerve awareness; a shorter learning curve has not been established.<sup>[[6]](#ref6)[[16]](#ref16)</sup> |
| Allows uterine preservation with cervical fixation in suitable patients<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[6]](#ref6)</sup> | Mesh exposure/erosion remains possible; material and patient factors matter.<sup>[[10]](#ref10)[[12]](#ref12)</sup> |
| Short-term anatomical and subjective outcomes are encouraging across cohorts.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[12]](#ref12)</sup> | Follow-up in the Wang pooled analysis averaged about **20 months**; comparative long-term durability and advanced-stage subgroups remain uncertain.<sup>[[12]](#ref12)[[13]](#ref13)</sup> |
| Can be combined with compartment-specific native-tissue repairs when indicated.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[9]](#ref9)</sup> | LLS primarily addresses anterior/apical support; posterior defects require separate assessment and mesh/fixation techniques vary.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[10]](#ref10)[[16]](#ref16)</sup> |

## Key Principles

- LLS suspends the vault or cervix to the lateral abdominal wall via retroperitoneal mesh tunneling, avoiding presacral dissection entirely.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[2]](#ref2)</sup>
- The older observational mesh cohort associated Type 3 mesh with higher erosion odds (adjusted OR 13.0); this is not a universal 13-fold absolute-risk prediction.<sup>[[10]](#ref10)</sup>
- Plan retroperitoneal tunnels according to the chosen technique and visible anatomy; protect the iliac vessels, ureter and nearby structures rather than relying on one unverified boundary rule.<sup>[[6]](#ref6)[[16]](#ref16)</sup>
- Adjust the lateral arms to restore apical and anterior support without excessive traction; published fixation approaches vary, and a universal tension or anchoring rule is not established here.<sup>[[6]](#ref6)[[16]](#ref16)</sup>
- Assess posterior prolapse separately. In one older cohort, posterior mesh placement was associated with higher erosion odds (adjusted OR 5.1; 95% CI 1.2–21.8); native-tissue repair is an option, not a mandatory substitute for every patient.<sup>[[10]](#ref10)</sup>
- **Counsel smokers** about the observed association with mesh erosion (adjusted OR 10.4 in the cited cohort) and recommend preoperative smoking cessation.<sup>[[10]](#ref10)</sup>
- Comparative studies found no detected difference in several short-term endpoints and reported shorter operative time, but durable equivalence or non-inferiority should not be assumed.<sup>[[7]](#ref7)[[13]](#ref13)[[14]](#ref14)</sup>
- Consider LLS in selected anterior-apical or uterine-preserving cases, or when sacral access is difficult. Obesity alone is not an evidence-backed indication for superiority.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[16]](#ref16)</sup>
- Longer-term comparative evidence, especially for advanced stage III–IV prolapse, remains limited; discuss alternatives including sacrocolpopexy in the broader shared decision.<sup>[[13]](#ref13)</sup>
