# Vaginal Hysterectomy

**Vaginal hysterectomy (VH)** removes the uterus and cervix through the vagina. For benign disease, ACOG recommends the vaginal route when feasible; laparoscopic hysterectomy is an alternative when vaginal access is unsuitable. Route selection also depends on the planned operation, anatomy, surgeon experience and the informed patient's preferences.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup>

For uterine prolapse, hysterectomy must be accompanied by an apical-support repair. Removing the uterus does not correct the support defect. Uterine preservation remains an option to offer surgical candidates without contraindications, and hysterectomy is not an obligatory component of prolapse treatment.<sup>[[2]](#ref2)[[20]](#ref20)</sup> Counseling should include the cancer detection and prevention benefit of hysterectomy, weighed against the patient's own risk and values.<sup>[[20]](#ref20)</sup>

For treatment selection, see [Prolapse Repair](/docs/surgical-techniques/04g-prolapse-repair). Operative companions include [McCall Culdoplasty](/docs/surgical-techniques/04g-prolapse-repair/mccall-culdoplasty), [Uterosacral Ligament Suspension](/docs/surgical-techniques/04g-prolapse-repair/uterosacral-ligament-suspension), [Sacrospinous Ligament Fixation](/docs/surgical-techniques/04g-prolapse-repair/sacrospinous-ligament-fixation), and the uterus-preserving [Sacrospinous Hysteropexy](/docs/surgical-techniques/04g-prolapse-repair/sacrospinous-hysteropexy) and [Manchester-Fothergill Procedure](/docs/surgical-techniques/04g-prolapse-repair/manchester-fothergill) pages.

## Indications and route selection

Establish the indication for hysterectomy before choosing its route. Common benign settings include symptomatic prolapse when hysterectomy is selected, persistent abnormal uterine bleeding, symptomatic fibroids and adenomyosis after discussion of appropriate alternatives. A diagnosis of cervical dysplasia or endometrial hyperplasia alone is not an automatic indication for this operation; premalignant or malignant disease requires its own diagnostic and treatment pathway.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[3]](#ref3)</sup>

| Assessment | Implication for planning |
|---|---|
| **Uterine size, shape, mobility and descent** | Assess exposure and safe extraction; size alone does not supply a universal cutoff |
| **Vaginal access** | Evaluate working space, not simply parity or a history of vaginal delivery |
| **Prior cesarean or pelvic surgery** | Neither is an absolute contraindication; distorted bladder planes and adhesions can change the operative plan |
| **Extrauterine disease or adnexal pathology** | Endometriosis, adhesions and required concurrent procedures may favor laparoscopic assistance or another route |
| **Prolapse and patient goals** | Agree on uterine preservation versus removal, the apical repair, and any additional compartment or continence procedure |
| **Expertise and resources** | Choose an approach the team can perform safely, with a plan for additional exposure or conversion if necessary |

These factors are considered together; feasibility in selected expert-center series does not establish that every patient is a vaginal candidate.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[2]](#ref2)[[4]](#ref4)</sup>

## Operative framework

The sequence changes with anatomy and the planned suspension. These are the core stages of conventional VH, with detailed apical reconstruction on the linked technique pages.<sup>[[4]](#ref4)[[5]](#ref5)[[6]](#ref6)</sup>

1. **Exposure and preparation.** Position in supported lithotomy, drain the bladder, perform vaginal antisepsis (chlorhexidine or povidone-iodine is appropriate) and establish adequate lighting and retraction. Confirm the planned adnexal and prolapse procedures.
2. **Cervicovaginal incision and dissection.** Incise at the cervicovaginal junction and develop the vesicocervical plane with controlled dissection. Prior cesarean delivery may distort this plane.
3. **Peritoneal entry.** Identify and enter the anterior and posterior reflections under controlled exposure. Anterior-first entry is not mandatory. When access is difficult, posterior-first entry or staged extraperitoneal pedicle control may help; do not force entry through an uncertain plane. Linder describes bladder illumination as an additional troubleshooting aid.<sup>[[6]](#ref6)</sup>
4. **Pedicle control.** Secure, divide and confirm hemostasis of the uterosacral and cardinal attachments, uterine vessels and upper pedicles as exposure permits. Keep the bladder, ureters and bowel clear of the clamp, needle and active energy jaws. The upper pedicle differs when the ovaries are preserved versus removed.
5. **Specimen delivery.** Remove the uterus intact when feasible. If vaginal debulking or manual fragmentation is contemplated, establish an appropriate preoperative malignancy assessment, extraction plan and consent; it is not a mandatory percentage of cases or a generic substitute for oncologic planning.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup>
6. **Hemostasis, suspension and closure.** Reassess all pedicles, complete the chosen apical repair and close the cuff without compromising the planned vaginal length or support. Cuff orientation alone does not establish better sexual function. The 2017 SGS review reported about 1 cm greater vaginal length with vertical than with horizontal cuff closure, and either peritoneum or vaginal epithelium can be used to close the colpotomy. Evidence for many individual steps was limited.<sup>[[2]](#ref2)[[4]](#ref4)</sup>
7. **Urinary-tract assessment.** For vaginal uterosacral suspension, perform intraoperative cystoscopy to assess the bladder and bilateral ureteral patency after suspension. Follow the specific repair's technique and injury-evaluation pathway.<sup>[[2]](#ref2)</sup>

Injected vasopressin decreased blood loss by about 130 mL in the 2017 SGS review.<sup>[[4]](#ref4)</sup> Routine postoperative vaginal packing is not supported by the same review. Selective packing for a defined clinical reason requires an explicit removal plan.<sup>[[4]](#ref4)</sup>

## Vessel sealing vs suture ligation

Clamp-cut-ligate and bipolar vessel sealing are alternative methods of pedicle control. A vessel sealer can shorten this part of the operation, but does not replace apical reconstruction or establish protection from ureteral, bladder or bowel injury.

The 2024 Society of Gynecologic Surgeons systematic review included 19 comparative pedicle-sealing studies. Its supplemental evidence table reports approximately 16 minutes shorter surgery (mean difference −15.9; 95% CI −23.3 to −8.5) and 37 mL less blood loss (−36.9; −56.9 to −17.0). Stay and pain differences were modest and heterogeneous; pooled opioid use did not show a clear reduction. These averages do not prove fewer major complications or the same benefit in every difficult case.<sup>[[7]](#ref7)</sup>

The randomized evidence before and after that review points the same way for bipolar sealers.

- **Kroft and Selk meta-analysis** (7 randomized trials, 662 women). Energy-based sealing shortened operative time by a mean of 17.2 minutes (95% CI 7.5–27.0) and blood loss by 47.7 mL (95% CI 15.5–79.9), with no increase in complications. The authors noted that no study examined mortality or quality of life.<sup>[[9]](#ref9)</sup>
- **Bonavina network meta-analysis** (10 randomized and 7 observational studies, 1,577 women). LigaSure and BiClamp both lowered blood loss compared with conventional suturing; operative time, postoperative hemoglobin change and pain were reduced only in the LigaSure group. Intraoperative complication risk was comparable across techniques.<sup>[[10]](#ref10)</sup>
- **Cronjé randomized trial** (68 women). Bipolar sealing used a median of 1 ligature versus 7 with suturing, and median operating time was 32 versus 40 minutes.<sup>[[11]](#ref11)</sup>
- **Elhao randomized trial** (103 women). LigaSure shortened operative time and reduced blood loss without a significant change in complication rates, and the blood-loss advantage was more pronounced in the more difficult procedures.<sup>[[12]](#ref12)</sup>
- **Ultrasonic shears.** In a randomized trial of 40 women, the Harmonic scalpel reduced blood loss (mean 62.6 versus 136.1 mL) but did not shorten operative time or reduce analgesic use; the evidence is weaker than for bipolar sealers.<sup>[[13]](#ref13)</sup>
- **Hefni randomized trial** (116 women). LigaSure shortened operating time. Perioperative hemorrhagic complications occurred in none of the LigaSure group and in 6.8% of the suture group (P = 0.057); no LigaSure patient had bleeding from an unsecured pedicle.<sup>[[14]](#ref14)</sup>

Use the selected device within its instructions, under direct exposure, and avoid incorporating adjacent structures. Inadequate sealing (tissue too thick, wet or poorly grasped) and unintended thermal injury to the ureter, bladder or bowel are distinct hazards; suture hemostasis must remain available, and suture is still needed for cuff closure and apical suspension. The reduction in ligatures reported in one randomized trial does not by itself show a cost advantage. The device evidence applies to vaginal operations, not automatically to abdominal hysterectomy or other reconstruction.<sup>[[7]](#ref7)[[8]](#ref8)</sup>

## Concurrent procedures

- **Apical suspension.** It is part of hysterectomy for uterine prolapse. Choose the specific repair according to support defects and patient goals.<sup>[[2]](#ref2)</sup>
- **Other compartment and continence procedures.** Plan them from symptoms, examination and shared decisions. They are not automatic additions to every hysterectomy.
- **Opportunistic salpingectomy.** Discuss it when appropriate. It should not by itself change the intended hysterectomy route.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup>
- **Ovarian conservation or oophorectomy.** Decide separately according to indication, age, ovarian pathology, cancer risk and preferences. Technical ability to remove the ovaries is not itself an indication for bilateral oophorectomy.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup>

## Comparative outcomes

| Evidence | Clinically useful interpretation |
|---|---|
| **Cochrane 2023: 63 RCTs, 6,811 participants across all route comparisons** | VH versus abdominal hysterectomy probably permits earlier return to normal activities (about 11 days, four trials/274 women) and fewer wound infections. Differences in operating time, transfusion and uncommon visceral injuries remain uncertain. Recovery averages from older trials are not fixed postoperative restrictions.<sup>[[3]](#ref3)</sup> |
| **Meyer 2026: NSQIP 2012–2022 observational analysis** | Among 83,436 matched patients, 30-day complications were 8.2% after VH versus 6.4% after laparoscopic/robotic hysterectomy (adjusted OR 1.23, 95% CI 1.15–1.31). This is an association after matching, not randomized proof of a superior route; case selection, surgeon factors and concurrent surgery matter.<sup>[[15]](#ref15)</sup> |
| **Cassani 2026: 63-RCT urinary-injury network review** | The review supports a ureter-injury signal for laparoscopic versus abdominal hysterectomy, but rare events, wide intervals, overlapping older trials and inconsistencies between text and supplements limit route rankings. It does not establish a universally safest vaginal, robotic or vNOTES approach.<sup>[[16]](#ref16)</sup> |
| **Settnes 2020: Danish Hysterectomy Database, 2004–2015** | 51,141 benign hysterectomies; major (Clavien-Dindo III–V) 30-day complications were 4.9% after VH for non-prolapse, 2.3% after VH for prolapse, 4.1% after laparoscopic and 10.3% after abdominal hysterectomy. | Adjusted risk of major complications was lower after laparoscopic hysterectomy than after VH for non-prolapse indications (RR 0.78, 95% CI 0.68–0.90), and the laparoscopic advantage grew from 2004–2009 (RR 0.96) to 2010–2015 (RR 0.72). VH for prolapse had the lowest risk (RR 0.55). This is registry data with confounding by indication.<sup>[[22]](#ref22)</sup> |
| **LAVA 2025 randomized trial** | Compared laparoscopic with abdominal hysterectomy, not VH. Only 75 of 3,250 planned participants were randomized and 62 underwent surgery; the planned noninferiority conclusion could not be made. Similar observed recovery is not proof of equivalence.<sup>[[17]](#ref17)</sup> |

### Conventional VH and vNOTES

The 2026 VANH randomized trial analyzed 113 patients at two Dutch centers. Same-day discharge occurred in 87.3% after vaginal-assisted NOTES versus 71.4% after conventional VH (OR 2.76; 95% CI 1.04–7.25). Reported complications and readmissions did not differ significantly, but the sample cannot establish equivalent rare-event safety. This is short-term evidence about conventional endoscopic assistance, not a demonstration of robotic benefit or long-term prolapse durability.<sup>[[18]](#ref18)</sup> See [vNOTES and robotic platforms](/docs/foundations/tools/technology/robotics/vnotes) for the distinctions between techniques.

## Complications and perioperative care

Counsel about bleeding, infection, bladder, ureter or bowel injury, retention, VTE, vaginal healing and the outcomes of any concomitant prolapse repair. Rates depend on indication, route definitions, follow-up and patient risk, and a historical percentage is not a personal prediction.

| Complication | Reported estimate |
|---|---|
| Infection | 13.0% after vaginal hysterectomy, 10.5% after abdominal and 9.0% after laparoscopic hysterectomy (narrative review)<sup>[[23]](#ref23)</sup> |
| Estimated blood loss | Median range 215–287 mL for vaginal hysterectomy; transfusion was more likely after laparoscopic than vaginal hysterectomy (OR 2.07, 95% CI 1.12–3.81)<sup>[[23]](#ref23)</sup> |
| Vaginal cuff dehiscence | 0.08% after total vaginal hysterectomy, compared with 0.15% after total abdominal, 0.28% after laparoscopic-assisted vaginal and 1.35% after total laparoscopic hysterectomy<sup>[[23]](#ref23)</sup> |
| Neuropathy | 0.2–2% after major pelvic surgery<sup>[[23]](#ref23)</sup> |
| Ureteral and bladder injury | In 296,130 California benign hysterectomies (all routes), 1.0% had ureteral and 0.7% bladder injury; an exclusively vaginal approach had lower odds of genitourinary injury than an abdominal approach (OR 0.56, 95% CI 0.53–0.64). Concomitant prolapse repair (OR 1.44) and an incontinence procedure (OR 1.40) were associated with higher odds<sup>[[24]](#ref24)</sup> |
| Fistula after injury | Subsequent genitourinary fistula was less frequent when the injury was identified immediately than when it was delayed (ureteral 0.7% vs 3.4%; bladder 2.5% vs 6.5%)<sup>[[24]](#ref24)</sup> |
| Major and total complications | 2.6% and 11.7% after vaginal hysterectomy in the Finnish FINHYST cohort<sup>[[19]](#ref19)</sup> |

In FINHYST, prior cesarean delivery and uterine weight at least 500 g were associated with bladder injury (OR 4.01 and 2.88 respectively). These were adjusted associations across a mixed-route cohort, not VH-specific absolute risks or contraindications. Urinary injury can be recognized after surgery; new flank pain, urinary leakage, fever or unexpected recovery warrants evaluation.<sup>[[19]](#ref19)</sup>

| Element | Practical approach |
|---|---|
| Infection prevention | Use hysterectomy-appropriate prophylaxis, with agent, allergy, weight and redosing decisions in [Perioperative Antibiotic Prophylaxis](/docs/foundations/pharmacology/infection-prophylaxis/perioperative-antibiotic-prophylaxis) |
| Analgesia | Individualized multimodal treatment; acetaminophen/NSAIDs when suitable, selected regional techniques and rescue opioids. Routine new gabapentin is not a default. See [Analgesia](/docs/foundations/perioperative-care/intraoperative-care/analgesia) |
| VTE prevention | Early mobilization and mechanical/pharmacologic measures matched to thrombotic and bleeding risk; see [VTE Prophylaxis](/docs/foundations/pharmacology/perioperative-eras/vte-prophylaxis) |
| Discharge and recovery | Confirm pain/nausea control, mobility and a voiding or catheter plan. Individualize graded activity and vaginal-healing advice to the complete operation, especially concurrent prolapse repair. The 2025 AUGS consensus statement recommends that surgeons allow patients to resume activity liberally after urogynecologic surgery, because the evidence does not support routine lifting or activity restrictions.<sup>[[21]](#ref21)</sup> Vaginal intercourse after cuff closure: many surgeons advise 6–12 weeks of pelvic rest, but dehiscence triggered by intercourse occurs outside that window, and no study shows that a longer delay lowers risk; AUGS cites a 2021 meta-analysis of laparoscopic and robotic hysterectomy with mean dehiscence at 68.5 days and intercourse the trigger in 64%.<sup>[[21]](#ref21)</sup> |
