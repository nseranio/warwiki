# Vaginal Hysterectomy

**Vaginal hysterectomy (VH)** is the **preferred minimally invasive route for hysterectomy whenever feasible** (ACOG Committee Opinion 701). The uterus is removed entirely through the vagina without an abdominal incision; recovery is faster, hospital stay shorter, and wound infections fewer than with abdominal hysterectomy. In the prolapse setting, VH is the standard surgical treatment for symptomatic uterine prolapse when the patient does not desire uterine preservation, and **must be combined with a concomitant apical support repair** (McCall culdoplasty, USLS, or SSLF) — hysterectomy without apical suspension is inadequate (Level B).<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[2]](#ref2)</sup>

For uterus-preserving alternatives, see [Sacrospinous Hysteropexy](/docs/surgical-techniques/04g-prolapse-repair/sacrospinous-hysteropexy) and [Manchester-Fothergill Procedure](/docs/surgical-techniques/04g-prolapse-repair/manchester-fothergill). For the apical adjuncts performed at the same setting, see [McCall Culdoplasty](/docs/surgical-techniques/04g-prolapse-repair/mccall-culdoplasty), [Uterosacral Ligament Suspension](/docs/surgical-techniques/04g-prolapse-repair/uterosacral-ligament-suspension), and [Sacrospinous Ligament Fixation](/docs/surgical-techniques/04g-prolapse-repair/sacrospinous-ligament-fixation). For the broader prolapse decision framework, see [Prolapse Repair](/docs/surgical-techniques/04g-prolapse-repair).

## Indications

VH is most commonly performed for benign disease. Approximately **590,000 hysterectomies are performed annually** in the United States; the most common indications are abnormal uterine bleeding, prolapse, and fibroids.<sup>[[2]](#ref2)</sup>

- **Symptomatic uterine prolapse** when uterine preservation is not desired — the classic, most straightforward indication. **Always pair with an apical suspension** (Level B).<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup>
- **Abnormal uterine bleeding** refractory to medical management.
- **Uterine leiomyomata**, particularly when uterine size is not prohibitively large.
- **Cervical dysplasia** (selected cases).
- **Endometrial hyperplasia** without atypia.

## Patient Selection and Feasibility

Key factors determining candidacy:<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup>

| Factor | Consideration |
|---|---|
| **Uterine size and mobility** | Adequate descent or sufficient mobility after dividing uterosacral and cardinal ligaments |
| **Vaginal accessibility** | Adequate caliber for instrumentation |
| **Pathology confined to uterus** | Absence of significant adnexal pathology, severe endometriosis, or dense adhesions |
| **Prior surgery** | Prior cesarean delivery and nulliparity are **not absolute contraindications**; **92%** of VH planned in women with no prior vaginal deliveries were successfully completed vaginally<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup> |

A systematic review found **no patient characteristics that absolutely preclude a vaginal approach**.<sup>[[3]](#ref3)</sup> Significant extrauterine disease (severe endometriosis, dense adhesions, markedly enlarged uterus) may preclude VH; **laparoscopic assistance** can extend feasibility.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup>

## Surgical Technique

1. **Positioning and prep.** Dorsal lithotomy; bladder drainage; vaginal antisepsis with chlorhexidine or povidone-iodine.<sup>[[3]](#ref3)</sup>
2. **Circumferential cervicovaginal incision.**<sup>[[4]](#ref4)</sup>
3. **Anterior cul-de-sac entry.** Sharp dissection into the vesicocervical space; identification and sharp entry of the peritoneal reflection. Confirm correct entry by visualizing small bowel or omental fat. In **difficult cases** (procidentia, cervical elongation, prior cesarean section), useful adjuncts include **cystoscopic bladder illumination**, **posterior entry first**, or **extraperitoneal pedicle ligation** before completing anterior entry.<sup>[[4]](#ref4)</sup>
4. **Posterior cul-de-sac entry.** Sharp entry into the pouch of Douglas.
5. **Sequential pedicle ligation.** Uterosacral ligaments → cardinal ligaments → uterine vessels → upper pedicles (round ligaments and utero-ovarian or infundibulopelvic ligaments).
6. **Uterine removal.** Delivered through the vagina. **Morcellation** (bisection, wedging, coring, or intramyometrial techniques) is required in approximately **11%** of cases for larger uteri.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup>
7. **Vaginal cuff closure.** **Vertical cuff closure** results in approximately 1 cm greater vaginal length than horizontal closure. Either peritoneum or vaginal epithelium can be used for colpotomy closure.<sup>[[3]](#ref3)</sup>
8. **Apical support — mandatory for prolapse cases.** McCall culdoplasty, USLS, or SSLF performed at the same setting; hysterectomy alone for prolapse is inadequate.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup>

### Intraoperative adjuncts<sup>[[3]](#ref3)</sup>

- **Vasopressin** injection — decreases blood loss by ~130 mL.
- **Tissue-sealing devices** (LigaSure, Enseal) — decrease blood loss by ~44 mL and operative time by ~15 min.
- **Routine vaginal packing is not advised** postoperatively.

## Concurrent Procedures

- **Opportunistic salpingectomy** can usually be safely accomplished at VH and **should not alter the intended route**.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[2]](#ref2)</sup>
- **Bilateral salpingo-oophorectomy (BSO)** completion at VH ranges 65–97.5%.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup>
- **Pelvic-floor repair** — anterior / posterior colporrhaphy and apical suspension are commonly performed concurrently in prolapse cases.

## Outcomes vs Other Routes

| Outcome | VH vs Abdominal | VH vs Laparoscopic | Source |
|---|---|---|---|
| Return to normal activities | ~12 days shorter with VH | No significant difference | <sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup> |
| Hospital stay | ~1–1.6 days shorter with VH | Similar / shorter with VH | <sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[2]](#ref2)</sup> |
| Operative time | Shorter with VH | Shorter with VH (~110 vs 137 min) | <sup>[[2]](#ref2)[[3]](#ref3)</sup> |
| Wound / abdominal-wall infection | **1% vs 6%** — significantly lower with VH | N/A (no abdominal incision) | <sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup> |
| Urinary tract injury | No clear difference | No clear difference (~1% each) | <sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup> |
| Vaginal cuff dehiscence | **0.08% (lowest of all routes)** | Higher with laparoscopic (1.35%) | <sup>[[6]](#ref6)</sup> |
| Blood transfusion | Lower with VH | Higher with VH in some large cohorts | <sup>[[3]](#ref3)[[5]](#ref5)</sup> |

### Contemporary re-evaluation

A large 2026 NSQIP analysis of **83,436 propensity-matched patients** found low overall complication rates for both VH and laparoscopic hysterectomy (8.2% vs 6.4%). VH carried slightly higher rates of transfusion, UTI, and reoperation; laparoscopic hysterectomy carried higher rates of wound dehiscence and pulmonary embolism. The authors concluded **VH is not clearly superior to laparoscopic hysterectomy** in contemporary practice — the traditional preference may warrant re-evaluation.<sup>[[5]](#ref5)</sup>

The Danish Hysterectomy Database (n = 51,141) similarly found that **laparoscopic hysterectomy had fewer major complications than VH for non-prolapse indications** (RR 0.78), particularly in 2010–2015 as laparoscopic expertise matured.<sup>[[7]](#ref7)</sup>

## Complications

Overall VH complication rates are among the lowest of all hysterectomy routes:<sup>[[8]](#ref8)</sup>

| Domain | Rate |
|---|---|
| Major complications | 2.6–4.9% (lower for prolapse indications)<sup>[[7]](#ref7)[[8]](#ref8)</sup> |
| Total complications | ~11.7%<sup>[[8]](#ref8)</sup> |
| Estimated blood loss | 215–287 mL; transfusion uncommon<sup>[[6]](#ref6)</sup> |
| **Bladder injury** | ~1%; risk **prior cesarean OR 4.01**, **uterus ≥ 500 g OR 2.88**<sup>[[8]](#ref8)[[9]](#ref9)</sup> |
| Ureteral injury | ~0.3%; VH has the **lowest GU-injury rate** vs abdominal (OR 0.56)<sup>[[9]](#ref9)</sup> |
| UTI | Most common VH-specific complication<sup>[[2]](#ref2)</sup> |
| Vaginal cuff infection | Slightly more common than abdominal hysterectomy (limited data)<sup>[[2]](#ref2)</sup> |
| Vaginal cuff dehiscence | **0.08%** — lowest of all routes<sup>[[6]](#ref6)</sup> |
| Neuropathy | 0.2–2% after major pelvic surgery<sup>[[6]](#ref6)</sup> |
| VTE | Less common after VH than after laparoscopic hysterectomy<sup>[[5]](#ref5)</sup> |

## Perioperative Care

| Element | Recommendation |
|---|---|
| **Antibiotic prophylaxis** | Single-dose **cefazolin** for all hysterectomy routes (>30 RCTs, 4 meta-analyses)<sup>[[10]](#ref10)</sup> |
| **ERAS analgesia** | Multimodal — scheduled acetaminophen, NSAIDs, gabapentin to minimize opioids; **paracervical nerve block** or intrathecal morphine specifically for VH<sup>[[11]](#ref11)</sup> |
| **VTE prophylaxis** | Early ambulation, intermittent pneumatic compression, compression stockings; pharmacologic prophylaxis based on VTE risk stratification<sup>[[11]](#ref11)</sup> |
| **Recovery** | Return to normal activities **24–38 days** after VH vs ~42 days after abdominal hysterectomy; multidisciplinary consensus recommends **graded resumption** of activities<sup>[[2]](#ref2)[[12]](#ref12)</sup> |

## ACOG Guidance (Committee Opinion 701)<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup>

- **VH is the approach of choice whenever feasible.**
- **Laparoscopic hysterectomy** is the preferred alternative when VH is not feasible.
- Nulliparity and prior cesarean delivery are **not contraindications**.
- **Opportunistic salpingectomy should not change** the planned route.
- Surgeon training, experience, and case volume should factor into route selection.

---

## Key Principles

- **VH is the preferred minimally invasive route for hysterectomy whenever feasible** (ACOG CO 701).<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup>
- **For prolapse, VH alone is inadequate** — pair with apical suspension (McCall culdoplasty, USLS, or SSLF).<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup>
- **No patient characteristics absolutely preclude a vaginal approach**; nulliparity and prior cesarean are not contraindications (92% completion in no-prior-vaginal-delivery cohorts).<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[3]](#ref3)</sup>
- **Vasopressin** and **tissue-sealing devices** reduce blood loss and operative time.<sup>[[3]](#ref3)</sup>
- **Vertical cuff closure** preserves ~1 cm more vaginal length than horizontal.<sup>[[3]](#ref3)</sup>
- **Bladder injury rises with prior cesarean (OR 4.01) and uterus ≥ 500 g (OR 2.88)** — counsel and prepare accordingly.<sup>[[9]](#ref9)</sup>
- VH has the **lowest cuff dehiscence rate** of all routes (0.08%).<sup>[[6]](#ref6)</sup>
- **Contemporary data** (NSQIP 2026, Danish Database) suggest laparoscopic hysterectomy may match or exceed VH in non-prolapse indications — surgeon experience and case mix matter.<sup>[[5]](#ref5)[[7]](#ref7)</sup>
- **Opportunistic salpingectomy should not change route**; perform at VH when feasible.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[2]](#ref2)</sup>
