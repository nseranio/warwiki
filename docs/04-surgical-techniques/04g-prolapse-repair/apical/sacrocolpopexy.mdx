# Sacrocolpopexy

**Sacrocolpopexy** restores vaginal apical support by attaching mesh to the vagina and securing it to the anterior longitudinal ligament over the sacrum. It is an established option for symptomatic vaginal vault prolapse, particularly when durable support and preservation of vaginal length are important. Choice among abdominal mesh repair, vaginal native-tissue suspension and other treatments depends on symptoms, anatomy, operative risk and the patient's priorities.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[2]](#ref2)[[3]](#ref3)</sup>

For the overall decision framework, see [Prolapse Repair Principles](/docs/surgical-techniques/04g-prolapse-repair/prolapse-repair-principles). Related operations have separate pages: [Supracervical Hysterectomy at Sacrocolpopexy](/docs/surgical-techniques/04g-prolapse-repair/supracervical-hysterectomy), [Sacrohysteropexy](/docs/surgical-techniques/04g-prolapse-repair/sacrohysteropexy) and [Pectopexy](/docs/surgical-techniques/04g-prolapse-repair/pectopexy). [Cervicosacropexy/vaginosacropexy (CESA/VASA)](/docs/surgical-techniques/04g-prolapse-repair/cesa-vasa) is a less-established bilateral sacral-fixation alternative developed specifically for apical prolapse with coexisting urgency or mixed incontinence. For the first abdominal sacrocolpopexy and its later evolution, see the [History of Prolapse Surgery & Urogynecology](/docs/roots/history/prolapse-urogynecology#first-abdominal-sacrocolpopexy-1962-and-the-minimally-invasive-era).

## Patient Selection and Planning

Consider sacrocolpopexy for bothersome apical prolapse when the patient chooses reconstructive surgery after discussion of nonsurgical and surgical options. Recurrent prolapse, substantial anterior or apical descent, prior surgery and vaginal length help shape that discussion; a single age or BMI threshold does not determine the operation.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[2]](#ref2)</sup>

Before surgery, document:

- **Symptoms and support:** bulge bother, POP-Q, vaginal length, compartment defects, bowel symptoms and sexual goals.
- **Urinary function:** stress and urgency leakage, emptying symptoms and prolapse-reduction stress testing when relevant to continence planning.
- **Previous treatment:** operative reports, prior mesh or graft, hysterectomy status and complications.
- **Operative suitability:** abdominal access, ability to tolerate the planned positioning and anesthesia, healing risks, and the feasibility of a vaginal alternative.
- **Patient preferences:** permanent mesh, uterine or cervical preservation, recovery priorities and willingness to accept possible future treatment.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[2]](#ref2)</sup>

Counsel about recurrent prolapse, mesh exposure, urinary or bowel dysfunction, pain or dyspareunia, organ or vascular injury and possible further surgery. Mesh implanted abdominally for sacrocolpopexy is a different procedure from transvaginal mesh implantation for prolapse.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[3]](#ref3)[[8]](#ref8)</sup>

## Mesh and Fixation

| Component | Practical interpretation |
|---|---|
| **Mesh** | Macroporous monofilament polypropylene is the usual synthetic material. Mesh weight, porosity and configuration matter; a product's favorable short-term results do not establish lifelong safety.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[7]](#ref7)[[13]](#ref13)</sup> Retrospective comparisons of mesh weight give mixed signals, not a proven preference. One robotic cohort found roughly twice the hazard of overall anatomic failure and about five times the hazard of anterior-compartment failure with ultralightweight versus heavier mesh, with less mesh exposure in the ultralightweight group (1.6% vs. 6.0%) but shorter follow-up in that arm.<sup>[[28]](#ref28)</sup> A second cohort found no significant difference in composite failure between ultralightweight and lightweight mesh (incidence rate ratio 1.47, 95% CI 0.83–2.52), with repeat surgery for apical failure or mesh complications in 4% and 2% of cases, respectively.<sup>[[29]](#ref29)</sup> A third, 1,272-patient cohort likewise found no difference in composite anatomic recurrence (89/1,272, 7%) between mesh weights, though lightweight mesh had significantly more mesh complications than ultralightweight mesh (5.8% vs. 1.3%) despite shorter ultralightweight follow-up.<sup>[[30]](#ref30)</sup> These nonrandomized, differently defined series do not establish a preferred mesh weight. |
| **Vaginal attachment** | Absorbable sutures are a reasonable option. Comparative evidence has not shown worse short-term support and suggests less suture exposure than permanent sutures; it does **not** show that mesh exposure is eliminated.<sup>[[5]](#ref5)[[6]](#ref6)</sup> |
| **Sacral attachment** | Permanent fixation to the anterior longitudinal ligament is commonly used. Evidence about vaginal suture choice cannot be transferred to sacral fixation.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[2]](#ref2)[[6]](#ref6)</sup> |
| **Graft alternatives** | Biologic grafts are less well studied and may be less durable. They are not an established substitute that automatically solves impaired healing or mesh risk; consider the entire procedure and native-tissue alternatives.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[13]](#ref13)</sup> A retrospective series of 12 women, 11 with a history of prior sling or vaginal mesh, used autologous fascia lata for robotic sacrocolpopexy; at a median 14.7-month follow-up (range 5.7–39), no recurrent or persistent apical prolapse was observed, though 3 of 12 (25%) reported recurrent anterior-compartment bulge sensation and there were no intraoperative or postoperative complications.<sup>[[17]](#ref17)</sup> |
| **Peritoneal coverage** | Cover the mesh and manage exposed suture/barbed ends to limit bowel contact. Closure must avoid bowel entrapment and tension on adjacent structures.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[2]](#ref2)</sup> Two delayed small-bowel obstructions from bowel adherent to a barbed suture at the sacral promontory have been reported after peritoneal closure;<sup>[[18]](#ref18)</sup> cinching tissue after each throw and cutting barbed-suture ends flush with the peritoneum has been described to reduce exposed barbs, though this has not been tested against other closure methods.<sup>[[19]](#ref19)</sup> |

The 2023 Pollack systematic review found no detected difference in sacrocolpopexy success in its direct comparisons of permanent and absorbable vaginal sutures (RR 1.00, 95% CI 0.98–1.03). Most included evidence was observational and follow-up was relatively short. Do not interpret pooled single-arm success percentages as a randomized comparison.<sup>[[5]](#ref5)</sup>

In the 150-patient Tagliaferri trial, polyglactin 910 was compared with braided polyester during laparoscopic sacrocervicopexy with supracervical hysterectomy. Both arms used permanent sacral fasteners. At one year, mesh exposures were 0/75 versus 3/75; the difference was not statistically significant and the trial was not powered to establish rare-complication safety. This is not evidence for absorbable fixation at every attachment site.<sup>[[6]](#ref6)</sup>

In a randomized trial of 59 women with post-hysterectomy vaginal prolapse undergoing laparoscopic or robotic sacrocolpopexy, Y-shaped mesh (Restorelle Y, n = 30) and flat mesh (n = 29) did not differ in case time or mesh-placement time, and PFDI-20 improvement at 6 months did not differ by mesh type. No recurrent prolapse on examination, retreatment or mesh erosion was reported, but 6-month follow-up does not address durability or late exposure.<sup>[[33]](#ref33)</sup>

## Open, Laparoscopic and Robotic Approaches

Minimally invasive access can reduce the burden of an abdominal incision and shorten recovery. Open surgery remains an option when clinical circumstances favor it. The operative route should reflect the patient's needs and the surgeon's and team's experience.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[2]](#ref2)</sup>

**Robotic assistance has not been shown to confer universally better clinical outcomes than conventional laparoscopy.** The 2026 Ferrari review found no clear difference in several reported outcomes, but the evidence combines randomized and observational reports, heterogeneous techniques and follow-up. Its reported lower conversion rate with robotics comes from observational cohorts. These findings do not prove equivalence or establish a causal advantage in complex cases.<sup>[[4]](#ref4)</sup>

For open versus conventional laparoscopic access, the three-center UK LAS equivalence trial randomized women with symptomatic post-hysterectomy vault prolapse to abdominal or laparoscopic sacrocolpopexy. At one year, apical support was equivalent (mean POP-Q point C −6.63 cm open versus −6.67 cm laparoscopic), 90% of the open and 80% of the laparoscopic group rated themselves much better, and the laparoscopic group had less blood loss and a shorter hospital stay.<sup>[[34]](#ref34)</sup>

Port layout, robotic platform, retraction and instruments vary. A fixed port diameter, mandatory manipulator or numerical learning-curve cutoff should not be treated as a requirement for every operation. Training and observed competence include dissection, fixation, recognition of injury and management of complications.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[2]](#ref2)</sup>

## Operative Sequence

The following describes core laparoscopic and robotic steps. Sequence, mesh configuration and the extent of dissection vary with vault versus cervical attachment, compartment defects, scars and tissue quality.<sup>[[2]](#ref2)[[13]](#ref13)</sup>

1. **Position and expose.** Use protected lithotomy and the Trendelenburg needed for access, within anesthetic tolerance. Establish abdominal access and retract bowel under direct vision. A vaginal sizer or other retractor can define the vault when useful; it is not required in every configuration. For open access, an assistant-held malleable vaginal retractor to reduce and distend the prolapsed vagina into the pelvis has been described as stabilizing the wall and facilitating anterior and posterior dissection.<sup>[[22]](#ref22)</sup>
2. **Identify sacral landmarks.** Identify the right ureter, iliac vessels and middle sacral vessels. Expose the anterior longitudinal ligament sufficiently for controlled fixation while limiting presacral dissection. Avoid the L5–S1 disc and deep bites into underlying structures. Identifying the aortic bifurcation, inferior vena cava and middle sacral vessels, with meticulous hemostasis around the presacral venous plexus, has been described as part of this exposure; because the robotic surgeon lacks direct haptic feedback, a bedside assistant can help confirm the promontory. If adequate exposure would require mobilizing major vessels, strong consideration should be given to abandoning sacrocolpopexy for an alternative prolapse repair.<sup>[[21]](#ref21)</sup>
3. **Develop the vaginal planes.** Separate bladder anteriorly and rectum posteriorly from the vagina. Tailor distal dissection to the defect and planned attachment; protect the vaginal wall and adjacent organs. Routine deep levator attachment or dissection to a fixed bladder landmark is not a universal requirement. An EEA sizer or vaginal stent, elevated anteriorly or posteriorly by an assistant, has been described to help delineate the vesicovaginal and rectovaginal planes; reported vaginotomy rates in selected minimally invasive series range widely (0.4%–24%), and risk is described as greater at a thin post-hysterectomy cuff closure. If vaginotomy occurs, reinforcing it with a second imbricating layer and avoiding mesh placement directly over the defect has been described.<sup>[[19]](#ref19)</sup>
4. **Attach the mesh.** Spread the vaginal portions smoothly and secure them to supportive tissue without excessive tightening. The number and distribution of sutures depend on the mesh and anatomy. Inspect for vaginal epithelial penetration and correct misplaced sutures. A digital vaginal and rectal examination at the end of the case, alongside cystoscopy, has been described to identify a suture perforating either organ; the clinical significance of vaginal penetration by a delayed-absorbable or barbed suture is reported as unknown in one series with no observed increase in mesh exposure, but a perforating suture should not be assumed harmless.<sup>[[27]](#ref27)</sup>
5. **Set sacral attachment and support.** Secure the mesh stem to the identified ligament with controlled fixation. Assess vaginal length, axis and support while avoiding excessive traction, constriction or distortion. No single ischial-spine target substitutes for assessment of the reconstruction. Leaving approximately two fingerbreadths of space between the mesh and rectum has been described for open surgery to avoid rectal compression; for laparoscopic or robotic surgery, where that spacing cannot be reproduced directly, observing the mesh attachment while the vagina is elevated cephalad with an EEA sizer to its maximum length, without actively stretching it, has been described instead.<sup>[[20]](#ref20)</sup>
6. **Cover and inspect.** Close the peritoneal opening over the mesh when feasible, account for exposed suture ends, and inspect hemostasis and bowel relationships. Reassess any recognized organ injury and its implications for the planned reconstruction. For open access in thin patients, a blunt subperitoneal tunnel (rather than extending the peritoneal incision to the vaginal apex) has been described to simplify retroperitonealization and mesh curvature while avoiding ureteral kinking, though without head-to-head evidence that it is safer.<sup>[[22]](#ref22)</sup> For a recognized bladder injury, two-layer absorbable repair with catheter drainage has been described; whether to continue vaginal mesh attachment is left to the surgeon, but placing mesh adjacent to or near the cystotomy is discouraged because of concern for later bladder-mesh erosion or fistula.<sup>[[24]](#ref24)</sup> For a recognized bowel injury, a Delphi-process, limited-evidence recommendation supports proceeding with sacrocolpopexy after an uncomplicated, intraoperative bladder or small-bowel injury;<sup>[[23]](#ref23)</sup> separately, enterotomy with fecal or enteric soilage, or a large-bowel injury, has been described as precluding mesh placement, favoring repair of the injury and either concluding the case or converting to a non-mesh vaginal or laparoscopic suspension; native-tissue support such as rectus fascia has been suggested as an alternative to consider in that setting.<sup>[[23]](#ref23)[[24]](#ref24)</sup> These are chapter-specific recommendations for a decision that should ultimately reflect the individual injury, contamination and reconstructive goals, not an automatic rule for every recognized bowel injury.
7. **Check urinary integrity.** Perform cystoscopic assessment of the bladder and ureteral efflux, and investigate an abnormal finding before completion. Document the implant, fixation and any concomitant procedures.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[2]](#ref2)[[13]](#ref13)</sup>

**Sacral fixation deserves particular care:** spondylodiscitis is a recognized, uncommon complication, rather than a theoretical concern restricted to one fixation device. Persistent severe back pain, especially with systemic symptoms, warrants assessment after surgery.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[2]](#ref2)[[13]](#ref13)</sup>

**Uncontrolled presacral bleeding** is a distinct intraoperative emergency. Cautious packing has been described first, with the caveats that bleeding may recur when packing is removed and excess pressure can tear the venous network; sutures, clips, cautery and bone wax are described hemostatic options, and stainless-steel or titanium thumbtacks at a retracted vein or sacral foramen are described as a rare, life-threatening bailout.<sup>[[25]](#ref25)</sup> Direct pressure with a blunt instrument and a gauze or cottonoid sponge for at least 5 minutes has also been described, escalating to a topical hemostatic matrix (such as a gelatin-thrombin matrix or an absorbable hemostat) at the bleeding site with continued gauze pressure if bleeding persists.<sup>[[26]](#ref26)</sup> For laparoscopic or robotic cases, whether to convert to open surgery is described as depending on the vessel injured, bleeding control and visualization. Treat these as attributed specialist teaching for a rare emergency, not a routine technique or a comparative outcome finding.<sup>[[25]](#ref25)[[26]](#ref26)</sup>

## Efficacy: Read the Endpoint Before the Percentage

### ASPIRe: post-hysterectomy vault prolapse

The ASPIRe trial randomized 376 women with symptomatic prolapse after total hysterectomy to sacrocolpopexy, vaginal native-tissue repair or transvaginal mesh repair; 360 underwent surgery. Its primary endpoint was the first occurrence of retreatment, prolapse beyond the hymen or bothersome vaginal bulge.<sup>[[3]](#ref3)</sup>

| Three-year modelled composite failure | Sacrocolpopexy | Native-tissue repair | Transvaginal mesh |
|---|---|---|---|
| Adjusted estimate | **28%** | **43%** | **29%** |

Sacrocolpopexy had lower failure than native-tissue repair (adjusted HR 0.57; 99% CI 0.33–0.98; P=.008). The transvaginal-mesh arm met its prespecified noninferiority comparison with sacrocolpopexy, but did not demonstrate superiority over native-tissue repair at the trial's adjusted significance threshold. Noninferiority is not equivalence; the Uphold LITE transvaginal device is no longer marketed in the United States.<sup>[[3]](#ref3)</sup>

Composite failure was much more common than retreatment: 5/120 sacrocolpopexy participants received additional prolapse treatment, and vaginal mesh exposure occurred in 4/120 (3%) (versus 6/115 [5%] with transvaginal mesh). Some failures were examination findings alone. Prior prolapse mesh or grafts and several higher-risk conditions were excluded, limiting generalization.<sup>[[3]](#ref3)</sup>

### Primary uterovaginal prolapse

When hysterectomy is chosen for primary uterovaginal prolapse, the [September 2026 U-POP review](/docs/clinical-conditions/03c-pelvic-support/apical-prolapse#primary-uterovaginal-prolapse-september-2026-review) adds evidence favoring sacrocolpopexy durability. Its largely observational, study-arm comparisons do not establish a universal operative mandate.

### Longer-term outcomes

In E-CARE, the extension of an older open abdominal sacrocolpopexy trial, estimated seven-year anatomic failure was 27% with Burch and 22% without Burch; composite failure was 48% and 34%, respectively. These estimates use different definitions from ASPIRe and have substantial uncertainty and attrition: 215 entered the extension and 126 completed seven-year follow-up. They do not mean that this proportion required another operation.<sup>[[8]](#ref8)</sup>

Against a vaginal alternative, a randomized trial allocated 95 women with vault prolapse to abdominal sacral colpopexy (n = 47) or vaginal sacrospinous colpopexy (n = 48). At a mean of two years (range 6–60 months), subjective success was 94% versus 91% (P = .19) and objective success 76% versus 69% (P = .48); the abdominal route had longer operating time, slower return to daily activities and greater cost.<sup>[[35]](#ref35)</sup>

Counsel with separate outcomes: relief of bulge, anatomic support, urinary and bowel and sexual function, retreatment, and mesh-related harm. Follow-up duration and ascertainment materially change the percentages.<sup>[[3]](#ref3)[[7]](#ref7)[[8]](#ref8)</sup>

## Complications and Follow-up

Early concerns include hemorrhage, bladder, ureter or bowel injury, ileus or bowel obstruction, infection, thromboembolism and voiding dysfunction. Later concerns include recurrent prolapse, new urinary or bowel symptoms, pain or dyspareunia, suture exposure and mesh complications. Avoid applying one historical pooled rate across all contemporary patients and materials.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[2]](#ref2)[[8]](#ref8)</sup>

**Mesh exposure can present years after repair.** In the 2024 e-PACT II follow-up of minimally invasive total hysterectomy and sacrocolpopexy, cumulative mesh exposure was 18/182 (9.9%) at mean 5.3 years, incorporating earlier exposure events. Only 82 of the original 200 participated at this follow-up, and 56 underwent examination. The reported 95% success applies to these participants, not all original patients. This study neither compares total with supracervical hysterectomy nor proves zero risk with a particular suture.<sup>[[7]](#ref7)</sup>

E-CARE estimated mesh erosion at 10.5% by seven years (95% CI 6.8–16.1), using older and varied operative materials. This is a separate population and method, not a direct comparison with e-PACT II.<sup>[[8]](#ref8)</sup>

Review healing and bladder emptying, then assess symptoms and support over time. New bleeding, persistent discharge, pain, dyspareunia or recurrent urinary symptoms should prompt examination and appropriate evaluation. Mesh exposure, organ erosion, infection and pain without visible exposure are different problems and may require different management.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[7]](#ref7)[[8]](#ref8)</sup>

## Concomitant Hysterectomy and Other Repairs

Choose among total hysterectomy, supracervical hysterectomy and uterine preservation according to uterine or cervical pathology, screening needs, anatomy and patient preference. Older series and the 2024 Shahid review associate concomitant total hysterectomy with higher mesh exposure, and many surgeons therefore favor supracervical hysterectomy or a staged approach. A 2024 Premier database cohort of 17,111 minimally invasive cases found similar two-year exposure with supracervical and total hysterectomy (0.7% versus 0.62%), although it relied on administrative coding. Retaining the cervix does not eliminate mesh risk. Discuss the tradeoffs for the actual mesh and operation being planned.<sup>[[2]](#ref2)[[7]](#ref7)[[13]](#ref13)[[14]](#ref14)</sup>

See [Supracervical Hysterectomy at Sacrocolpopexy](/docs/surgical-techniques/04g-prolapse-repair/supracervical-hysterectomy) and [Sacrohysteropexy](/docs/surgical-techniques/04g-prolapse-repair/sacrohysteropexy). Additional vaginal repair or a combined colorectal procedure should address a demonstrated defect or symptom and be planned with the relevant specialist; it is not a routine package for every sacrocolpopexy.<sup>[[2]](#ref2)</sup>

## Concomitant Anti-Incontinence Procedures

Discuss existing symptomatic SUI separately from occult leakage on prolapse reduction and prophylaxis in a woman without preoperative SUI symptoms. Combined and staged procedures have different tradeoffs.<sup>[[9]](#ref9)[[11]](#ref11)</sup>

In CARE, 322 women without preoperative SUI symptoms were randomized during open abdominal sacrocolpopexy. After the journal's 2016 coding correction, the three-month composite SUI endpoint was 33.6% with Burch versus 57.4% without Burch. This includes symptoms, stress testing or treatment and is not a universal risk of bothersome leakage.<sup>[[9]](#ref9)[[10]](#ref10)</sup>

The 2026 Cochrane review describes uncertainty and inconsistency in prophylactic Burch evidence for stress-continent women. Its more favorable concomitant-sling findings concern different populations and procedures. Do not assume the same benefit for every laparoscopic or robotic reconstruction. See [Burch's evidence discussion](/docs/surgical-techniques/04f-incontinence-procedures/procedures/burch-colposuspension#concomitant-prolapse-surgery).<sup>[[11]](#ref11)</sup>

## Postoperative Recovery

Encourage early mobilization and give a recovery plan based on the operation, complications and the patient's function. Routine prolonged home rest is not supported by the available recovery evidence.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[12]](#ref12)</sup>

Same-day discharge is feasible for appropriately selected patients rather than a universal target. In a retrospective cohort of 272 women after robotic sacrocolpopexy, 80 (29.4%) went home the same day; there was no difference in unplanned provider visits, emergency-department visits or readmission between the same-day and later-discharge groups.<sup>[[31]](#ref31)</sup> In a separate prospective same-day-discharge protocol after minimally invasive sacrocolpopexy, 37 of 47 eligible women (78.7%) achieved same-day discharge, with no increase in adverse events or postoperative health-care utilization and high patient satisfaction compared with routine overnight care.<sup>[[32]](#ref32)</sup> Patient selection (age, comorbidity, case start time, a caretaker at home) shaped eligibility in both series and should guide any same-day-discharge plan.

The 2023 O'Shea randomized trial assigned 123 women to standard restrictions or expedited activity after minimally invasive apical prolapse surgery; 107 completed the primary analysis, including 48 who underwent robotic sacrocolpopexy. Expedited instructions produced noninferior anatomic and symptom outcomes at three months. Both groups avoided sexual activity until the six-week visit.<sup>[[12]](#ref12)</sup>

The earlier double-masked ReCOUP trial randomized women having prolapse surgery (not limited to sacrocolpopexy; 61% of the randomized women had mesh sacrocolpopexy, per the AUGS summary) to liberal activity or to 3 months of restricted activity, with no heavy lifting or high-impact activity. In its planned 1-year secondary analysis (77 of 95 women with both functional and anatomic assessment), anatomic failure was 8.8% with liberal and 7.0% with restricted instructions, and anatomic and functional outcomes did not differ significantly. The sample was small.<sup>[[15]](#ref15)[[16]](#ref16)</sup>

The 2025 AUGS consensus statement recommends that surgeons allow patients to resume activity liberally after urogynecologic surgery. It found no evidence to justify different activity advice after mesh versus native-tissue prolapse repair, while noting that the trials were not powered to compare them.<sup>[[16]](#ref16)</sup>

This supports a more flexible return to ordinary physical activity after uncomplicated surgery. It does not establish two-year safety from that report, immediate unrestricted heavy manual work, or early vaginal intercourse. The study selected women with reasonable baseline mobility, and measured postoperative activity was similar despite different instructions.<sup>[[12]](#ref12)</sup>
