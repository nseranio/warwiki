# Pectopexy

**Pectopexy** (pectocolpopexy or pectocervicopexy) is a minimally invasive, mesh-augmented apical suspension in which the vaginal apex or cervix is supported from the bilateral iliopectineal (Cooper) ligaments, rather than the sacral promontory. It is an alternative operative concept, not an established substitute for every sacrocolpopexy or native-tissue repair.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[2]](#ref2)</sup>

For the sacral fixation alternative, see [Sacrocolpopexy](/docs/surgical-techniques/04g-prolapse-repair/sacrocolpopexy). For a uterus-preserving sacral option, see [Sacrohysteropexy](/docs/surgical-techniques/04g-prolapse-repair/sacrohysteropexy). For the broader decision framework, see [Prolapse Repair](/docs/surgical-techniques/04g-prolapse-repair).

## What the operation changes

Pectopexy changes the apical anchor and therefore avoids **sacral-promontory dissection**. It does not remove the need for individualized assessment of vascular, ureteral, bowel, mesh, wound, and anesthesia risks. Whether avoiding the promontory produces a meaningful overall safety, recovery, or functional advantage for a particular patient remains uncertain.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[2]](#ref2)</sup>

The published operation varies by uterine status, vaginal or cervical attachment, mesh configuration, fixation material, concomitant anterior/posterior repair, and platform. Those variations should not be treated as interchangeable or as a standardized recipe.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[2]](#ref2)</sup>

## Selection and informed decision-making

Pectopexy may be considered by an appropriately trained pelvic-floor surgeon when an abdominal mesh apical suspension is being discussed and the anatomy, prior operations, goals, and alternatives make a non-sacral anchor reasonable. It is not possible to infer from the available evidence that obesity, previous surgery, a particular POP-Q stage, or a preference for uterine preservation alone establishes a pectopexy indication or superiority.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[2]](#ref2)</sup>

Counseling should explicitly compare:

- conservative management and the relevant vaginal native-tissue options;
- sacrocolpopexy or sacrohysteropexy when appropriate;
- the distinct abdominal mesh exposure and reoperation considerations; and
- the limited long-term, patient-centered, and comparative evidence for pectopexy.

The current scoping review identified 30 studies (only four randomized) and 85 different reported outcomes; symptom-recurrence outcomes and patient-reported measures were inconsistently reported. Most studies came from tertiary settings, and intervention details varied. This is not a reliable foundation for broad claims of comparative superiority or durable equivalence.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup>

## Evidence boundaries

| Question | What can be said | What cannot be concluded |
|---|---|---|
| Feasibility | Published series and comparative studies show that pectopexy can be performed and can improve anatomic and symptom measures in selected populations. | That it is safer, faster, or more effective for all patients than sacrocolpopexy, USLS, or SSLF. |
| Comparative outcomes | Small trials and cohorts report differing operative and short-term outcomes. A 2023 retrospective single-center comparison included different uterine-status groups and concomitant procedures. | That observed between-group differences are caused solely by the anchor or mesh choice, or establish a preferred operation. |
| Technique | Cadaveric testing compared one continuous with one interrupted fixation method in a small, material-specific model. | That a particular number of sutures, mesh, port layout, implant, tension, or fixation pattern is clinically validated for all pectopexies. |
| Durability and function | Longer follow-up and patient-centered outcome data remain limited and heterogeneous. | Long-term equivalence, superiority, sexual-function benefit, or a lower bowel/urinary complication rate. |

The broader apical-prolapse evidence synthesis groups pectopexy with other lateral-suspension/round-ligament procedures in some comparisons. Those pooled results do not represent a single standardized pectopexy operation, and their sparse, imprecise estimates should not be converted into exact operative-time, recurrence, or safety promises.<sup>[[3]](#ref3)</sup>

## Operative safety

This article is not a step-by-step operative protocol. Exposure, dissection planes, implant selection, fixation method, mesh handling, tensioning, cystoscopy, thromboprophylaxis, antibiotic prophylaxis, and postoperative surveillance should follow current institutional protocols, applicable device instructions for use, and supervised procedural training.

Cadaveric biomechanics can describe the tested construct but do not determine clinical durability, mesh safety, or a universally adequate fixation method. The available experiment used eight fresh cadavers and specific mesh/suture materials; its findings should not be used to prescribe a clinical fixation rule.<sup>[[4]](#ref4)</sup>

## Follow-up

Follow-up should be driven by symptoms, examination when indicated, mesh-related symptoms, urinary and bowel function, sexual function, and the patient’s recovery goals. New or recurrent bulge symptoms, bleeding/discharge, pelvic pain, voiding or defecatory difficulty, dyspareunia, or a suspected mesh complication warrant assessment rather than assumption that the apical repair has failed or succeeded.

## Key principles

- Pectopexy is a bilateral iliopectineal-ligament anchor strategy for mesh-augmented apical suspension, distinct from sacral fixation.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[2]](#ref2)</sup>
- Avoiding promontory dissection does not establish an overall reduction in operative risk.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[2]](#ref2)</sup>
- The literature does not validate a universal mesh, port configuration, suture number, fixation pattern, learning-curve threshold, or patient-selection rule.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[4]](#ref4)</sup>
- Evidence remains heterogeneous and sparse for durable, patient-centered comparative outcomes; shared decision-making should keep that uncertainty visible.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[3]](#ref3)</sup>
