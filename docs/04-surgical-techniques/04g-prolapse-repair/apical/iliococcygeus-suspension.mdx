# Iliococcygeus Fascia Suspension

**Iliococcygeus fascia suspension (ICG fixation, "prespinous fixation")** is a transvaginal native-tissue apical operation in which the **vaginal vault (or cervix) is sutured bilaterally to the fascia overlying the iliococcygeus muscle** to restore apical support. The iliococcygeus fascia is targeted **anterior (ventral) and medial to the ischial spine**, distinguishing it from sacrospinous-ligament fixation, which engages tissue at or posterior to the spine. The prespinous location keeps suture placement away from the pudendal neurovascular bundle.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[2]](#ref2)[[3]](#ref3)[[4]](#ref4)</sup>

For the more commonly performed sacrospinous variant, see [Sacrospinous Ligament Fixation (SSLF)](/docs/surgical-techniques/04g-prolapse-repair/sacrospinous-ligament-fixation). For the uterosacral alternative, see [Uterosacral Ligament Suspension (USLS)](/docs/surgical-techniques/04g-prolapse-repair/uterosacral-ligament-suspension). For the broader prolapse decision framework, see [Prolapse Repair](/docs/surgical-techniques/04g-prolapse-repair).

---

## History and Definition

First described by **Inmon (1963)**, popularized by **Meeks et al. (1994)** and **Shull et al. (1993)**. **Peters and Christenson (1995)** described a related technique using "coccygeus fascia" (the fascia overlying the coccygeus-sacrospinous complex).<sup>[[2]](#ref2)[[3]](#ref3)</sup>

Despite being technically simple and low-morbidity, the procedure is **not commonly performed** compared with sacrospinous or uterosacral ligament suspension.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup>

---

## Relevant Anatomy

The **iliococcygeus** is the most posterior and lateral component of the levator ani complex (alongside pubococcygeus and puborectalis):<sup>[[5]](#ref5)[[6]](#ref6)</sup>

- Origin: **arcus tendineus levatoris ani** (a fascial thickening on the obturator-internus fascia)
- Insertion: **coccyx** and **anococcygeal raphe**
- Geometry: forms a horizontal **levator plate** supporting the pelvic viscera
- The fascia overlying the iliococcygeus is the suture target — located **1–2 cm anterior and medial to the ischial spine** ("prespinous" area), and **further from the pudendal neurovascular bundle** than the sacrospinous ligament<sup>[[2]](#ref2)[[7]](#ref7)</sup>

---

## Indications

- **Posthysterectomy vaginal vault prolapse (POP-Q stage ≥ 2)** — primary and best-studied indication.<sup>[[8]](#ref8)[[9]](#ref9)</sup>
- **Mild uterine prolapse** as a hysteropexy — outcomes acceptable; **less favorable for advanced uterine prolapse**.<sup>[[10]](#ref10)</sup>
- Patients in whom an abdominal approach or synthetic mesh is not desired or contraindicated.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup>
- Both primary and recurrent prolapse.<sup>[[3]](#ref3)</sup>

### Relative contraindications

- **Severe levator-ani defects or atrophy** — fascia may be too thin to hold sutures.<sup>[[12]](#ref12)</sup>
- **Stage IV vault prolapse** — strongest independent predictor of failure.<sup>[[8]](#ref8)[[9]](#ref9)</sup>

---

## Surgical Technique

The operation is performed in dorsal lithotomy under regional or general anesthesia.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[3]](#ref3)</sup>

1. **Vaginal incision.** Midline posterior vaginal-wall incision extended to the apex; identify and address any enterocele.
2. **Pararectal-space dissection.** Enter the rectovaginal space and develop the pararectal space bilaterally by sharp and blunt dissection, sweeping the rectum medially. Carry dissection laterally and superiorly until the **iliococcygeus muscle** is exposed and palpable.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup>
3. **Fascia identification.** Identify the iliococcygeus fascia in the **prespinous area — 1–2 cm anterior and medial to the ischial spine**, using the spine as a palpable landmark.<sup>[[2]](#ref2)[[3]](#ref3)</sup>
4. **Bilateral suture placement.** Place **two delayed-absorbable or permanent sutures** on each side (4 total) through the iliococcygeus fascia in **figure-of-eight** or mattress fashion, incorporating a generous bite of fascia and underlying muscle.<sup>[[3]](#ref3)</sup>
5. **Vaginal-vault fixation.** Pass the sutures through the **full thickness of the vaginal vault** (or cervix in hysteropexy) and tie, suspending the vault bilaterally to the iliococcygeus fascia.
6. **Concomitant compartment repair.** Anterior colporrhaphy, posterior colporrhaphy, and / or perineorrhaphy as needed; adequate repair of all fascial defects and the perineal body is considered critical to durability.<sup>[[3]](#ref3)</sup>
7. **Vaginal closure.**

### Technical points

- **Bilateral fixation** restores a more **midline, physiological vaginal axis** vs the unilateral posterior deflection of sacrospinous fixation.<sup>[[2]](#ref2)</sup>
- Adequate **pararectal-space exposure** is the key technical step.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup>
- Suture placement remains **away from the pudendal neurovascular bundle**.<sup>[[2]](#ref2)</sup>

## Outcomes — Long-Term Efficacy

| Series | Follow-up | n | Subjective cure | Objective cure |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Serati 2021 (10-year follow-up)**<sup>[[8]](#ref8)</sup> | Median 120 mo | 39 | **82%** | **74.4%** |
| **Serati 2015 (5-year follow-up)**<sup>[[9]](#ref9)</sup> | 5 yr | — | **88.6%** | **84.1%** |

Across both series, **only preoperative stage IV vault descensus** independently predicted failure (OR 7.66–8.78).<sup>[[8]](#ref8)[[9]](#ref9)</sup>

### Meeks 1994 (founding series)<sup>[[3]](#ref3)</sup>

- 110 patients; success in 96.4%; only 4 recurrences — **all anterior compartment**.
- All recurrences underscore the dominant late failure mode of any posterior-axis suspension.

---

## Comparative Outcomes

### vs Sacrospinous Ligament Fixation (Maher 2001 matched case-control, 36 pairs)<sup>[[4]](#ref4)</sup>

| Outcome | ICG fixation | Sacrospinous fixation | p |
|---|---|---|---|
| Subjective success | 91% | 94% | 0.73 |
| Objective success | 53% | 67% | 0.36 |
| Patient satisfaction (VAS) | 78/100 | 91/100 | **0.01** |
| Recovery time (days) | 54 | 39 | **0.04** |
| Postoperative cystocele | No difference | No difference | NS |
| Buttock pain | No difference | No difference | NS |
| Hemorrhage requiring transfusion | No difference | No difference | NS |

**Conclusion**: equally effective; sacrospinous fixation should not be discarded in favor of ICG.<sup>[[4]](#ref4)</sup>

### vs Coccygeus Fascial Suspension vs Sacrospinous (Peters 1995)<sup>[[2]](#ref2)</sup>

Projected 2-yr cure: **96%** coccygeus-fascial suspension vs **80%** sacrospinous fixation.

### vs Abdominal Sacrocolpopexy (Milani 2014, n = 36 vs 41)<sup>[[11]](#ref11)</sup>

- **Operative time**: significantly shorter (78 vs 140 min, p < 0.001).
- Comparable outcomes; ICG offers a faster, mesh-free vaginal alternative.

### As hysteropexy (Suh & Jeon 2018, n = 144)<sup>[[10]](#ref10)</sup>

| Subgroup | 4-yr success |
|---|---|
| ICG **with hysterectomy**, mild prolapse (stage II, point C ≤ 0) | **100%** |
| ICG with hysterectomy, advanced prolapse | 91.2% |
| ICG **as hysteropexy**, mild prolapse | 75.3% |
| ICG as hysteropexy, **advanced prolapse** | **48.0%** |

**Independent failure predictors**: advanced prolapse (stage > II, point C > 0) and **uterus-saving technique**. Acceptable as hysteropexy **only for mild uterine prolapse**.

## Complications

The procedure has a notably **low complication profile**:<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[3]](#ref3)[[4]](#ref4)</sup>

| Complication | Rate / note |
|---|---|
| Hemorrhage | 3/110 (2.7%) had EBL > 750 mL in Meeks; 2 required transfusion<sup>[[3]](#ref3)</sup> |
| Bladder injury | Rare (1/110 in Meeks)<sup>[[3]](#ref3)</sup> |
| Bowel injury | Rare (1/110 in Meeks)<sup>[[3]](#ref3)</sup> |
| Buttock / gluteal pain | Similar to sacrospinous fixation in comparative data; **theoretically lower** because suture placement is anterior to the spine and away from the pudendal nerve<sup>[[4]](#ref4)</sup> |
| Postoperative urinary retention | Mean catheterization 6.1 days (Meeks)<sup>[[3]](#ref3)</sup> |
| **Anterior compartment recurrence** | The **dominant late failure mode** — 4 of 4 recurrences in Meeks<sup>[[3]](#ref3)</sup> |
| Mesh-related complications | **None** (native tissue) |

---

## Risk Factors for Failure

- **Preoperative stage IV vault prolapse** — strongest predictor (OR 7.66–8.78).<sup>[[8]](#ref8)[[9]](#ref9)</sup>
- **Advanced prolapse** (stage > II, point C > 0).<sup>[[10]](#ref10)</sup>
- **Uterus-preserving technique** (hysteropexy), particularly for advanced prolapse.<sup>[[10]](#ref10)</sup>

---

## Advantages and Disadvantages

| Advantage | Disadvantage |
|---|---|
| Technically simpler than SSLF — no specialized suture-passing devices required<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup> | Less commonly performed; surgeon familiarity limited<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup> |
| Bilateral fixation → more physiological midline vaginal axis<sup>[[2]](#ref2)</sup> | Anterior compartment recurrence is the dominant late failure<sup>[[3]](#ref3)</sup> |
| Prespinous suture placement → **lower theoretical pudendal NV-bundle risk**<sup>[[2]](#ref2)</sup> | Lower success as hysteropexy, particularly for advanced prolapse<sup>[[10]](#ref10)</sup> |
| Native tissue — no mesh<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup> | Limited high-quality comparative data — no large RCTs vs SSLF / USLS |
| Shorter operative time than abdominal sacrocolpopexy<sup>[[11]](#ref11)</sup> | Dependent on **levator-ani muscle quality** — limited in significant levator defects / atrophy<sup>[[12]](#ref12)</sup> |
| Comparable apical efficacy to sacrospinous fixation<sup>[[4]](#ref4)</sup> | Patient satisfaction and recovery may be slightly inferior to SSLF<sup>[[4]](#ref4)</sup> |

---

## Guideline Position

**ACOG PB 214** (2019) lists iliococcygeus ligament fixation as one of the transvaginal apical suspension procedures available for POP repair, alongside sacrospinous and uterosacral ligament suspension. ACOG/AUGS guidelines focus primarily on sacrospinous and uterosacral suspension as the two best-studied native-tissue vaginal apical operations (Level A evidence for equivalence). ICG is not separately rated, reflecting its more limited comparative evidence base.<sup>[[13]](#ref13)[[14]](#ref14)</sup>

---

## Key Principles

- ICG fixation is a **prespinous, bilateral, native-tissue apical operation** — sutures into iliococcygeus fascia 1–2 cm anterior and medial to the ischial spine.<sup>[[2]](#ref2)[[3]](#ref3)</sup>
- The prespinous position keeps suture placement **away from the pudendal neurovascular bundle**.<sup>[[2]](#ref2)[[7]](#ref7)</sup>
- **Best-studied indication is post-hysterectomy vault prolapse** — 5- and 10-year cure 74–88%.<sup>[[8]](#ref8)[[9]](#ref9)</sup>
- **Acceptable as hysteropexy only for mild uterine prolapse** — failure rises sharply with advanced prolapse and uterus preservation (Suh 2018: 48% success in advanced hysteropexy).<sup>[[10]](#ref10)</sup>
- **Stage IV vault descensus** is the strongest independent failure predictor.<sup>[[8]](#ref8)[[9]](#ref9)</sup>
- Comparable apical efficacy to **SSLF** (Maher 2001 matched case-control); SSLF gives better patient satisfaction and faster recovery.<sup>[[4]](#ref4)</sup>
- **Dominant late failure mode is anterior compartment recurrence** — assess and address anterior support concurrently.<sup>[[3]](#ref3)</sup>
- Underutilized despite simplicity and safety — surgeon familiarity is the primary barrier.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup>
