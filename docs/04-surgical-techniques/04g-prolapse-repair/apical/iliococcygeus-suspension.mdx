# Iliococcygeus Fascia Suspension

**Iliococcygeus fascia suspension (ICG fixation, "prespinous fixation")** is a transvaginal native-tissue apical operation in which the **vaginal vault is attached to the fascia overlying the iliococcygeus muscle**, usually bilaterally, to restore apical support. The AUGS–IUGA terminology report places the suture target in the **medial parietal iliococcygeus fascia, typically about 1 cm caudal to the ischial spine**; this is distinct from engaging the sacrospinous ligament. The site is chosen to reduce proximity to major neurovascular structures, not to eliminate injury risk.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[3]](#ref3)[[15]](#ref15)</sup>

For the more commonly performed sacrospinous variant, see [Sacrospinous Ligament Fixation (SSLF)](/docs/surgical-techniques/04g-prolapse-repair/sacrospinous-ligament-fixation). For the uterosacral alternative, see [Uterosacral Ligament Suspension (USLS)](/docs/surgical-techniques/04g-prolapse-repair/uterosacral-ligament-suspension). For the broader prolapse decision framework, see [Prolapse Repair](/docs/surgical-techniques/04g-prolapse-repair).

---

## History and Definition

First described by **Inmon (1963)** and subsequently described by **Shull et al. (1993)** and **Meeks et al. (1994)**. **Peters and Christenson (1995)** reported a related **coccygeus-fascial** suspension; its outcomes should not be treated as outcomes of an identical iliococcygeus operation.<sup>[[2]](#ref2)[[3]](#ref3)[[15]](#ref15)</sup>

The procedure is **less commonly performed** than sacrospinous or uterosacral ligament suspension. A published single-case technique video describes it as technically straightforward, but does not establish a general low-morbidity rate.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup>

---

## Relevant Anatomy

The **iliococcygeus** is the most posterior and lateral component of the levator ani complex (alongside pubococcygeus and puborectalis):<sup>[[5]](#ref5)[[6]](#ref6)</sup>

- Origin: **arcus tendineus levatoris ani** (a fascial thickening on the obturator-internus fascia)
- Insertion: **coccyx** and **anococcygeal raphe**
- Geometry: forms a horizontal **levator plate** supporting the pelvic viscera
- The **medial parietal fascia** overlying the iliococcygeus is the suture target, **usually caudal to the ischial spine (about 1 cm in the AUGS–IUGA description)**. Exposure identifies the levator fascia lateral to the rectum; a theoretical neurovascular-distance advantage has not been proved as a lower injury rate.<sup>[[7]](#ref7)[[15]](#ref15)</sup>

---

## Indications

- **Posthysterectomy vaginal vault prolapse (POP-Q stage ≥ 2)** — primary and best-studied indication.<sup>[[8]](#ref8)[[9]](#ref9)</sup>
- **Mild uterine prolapse** as a hysteropexy — outcomes acceptable; **less favorable for advanced uterine prolapse**.<sup>[[10]](#ref10)</sup>
- Consider a native-tissue vaginal option when established alternatives are unsuitable after shared decision-making; the 2026 German guideline limits consideration to cases without an adequate alternative.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[16]](#ref16)</sup>
- Both primary and recurrent prolapse.<sup>[[3]](#ref3)</sup>

### Selection cautions

- **Markedly attenuated levator/fascial tissue** may limit a reliable fixation bite and requires intraoperative judgment. The cited 2026 MRI study describes muscle defects but did **not** test iliococcygeus-suspension outcomes or establish a contraindication.<sup>[[12]](#ref12)[[15]](#ref15)</sup>
- **Stage IV vault prolapse** predicted recurrence in small five- and ten-year follow-up cohorts; it is a selection concern, **not an absolute contraindication** established by those studies.<sup>[[8]](#ref8)[[9]](#ref9)</sup>

---

## Surgical Technique

The operation is performed in dorsal lithotomy under regional or general anesthesia.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[3]](#ref3)</sup>

1. **Vaginal incision.** Midline posterior vaginal-wall incision extended to the apex; identify and address any enterocele.
2. **Pararectal-space dissection.** Enter the rectovaginal space and develop the pararectal space bilaterally by sharp and blunt dissection, sweeping the rectum medially. Carry dissection laterally and superiorly until the **iliococcygeus muscle** is exposed and palpable.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup>
3. **Fascia identification.** Palpate each ischial spine and identify the **medial iliococcygeus parietal fascia caudal to it**, commonly about **1 cm inferior** in the standardized description.<sup>[[15]](#ref15)</sup>
4. **Bilateral suture placement.** Place **several interrupted sutures** in the iliococcygeus fascia on each side, progressing caudally along the fascial line toward the perineum. Delayed-absorbable and nonabsorbable materials, suture count and whether fascia alone or fascia plus muscle is incorporated vary by technique; **two per side is not a universal rule**.<sup>[[3]](#ref3)[[15]](#ref15)</sup>
5. **Vaginal-vault fixation.** Attach the vaginal apex and lateral fornices to the fixation sutures and tie them to elevate the vault. The highest fixation is at the apex; subsequent sutures may be placed progressively more caudally. Hysteropexy is a related variation rather than the best-studied vault operation.<sup>[[15]](#ref15)</sup>
6. **Concomitant compartment repair.** Anterior colporrhaphy, posterior colporrhaphy, and / or perineorrhaphy as needed; adequate repair of all fascial defects and the perineal body is considered critical to durability.<sup>[[3]](#ref3)</sup>
7. **Vaginal closure.**

### Technical points

- **Bilateral fixation** can preserve a more midline vaginal axis than unilateral sacrospinous fixation; a better clinical outcome from that axis has not been established.<sup>[[2]](#ref2)[[15]](#ref15)</sup>
- Adequate **pararectal-space exposure** is the key technical step.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup>
- The **caudal-to-spine fascial target** is intended to reduce neurovascular proximity, but does not replace careful identification and dissection.<sup>[[15]](#ref15)</sup>

## Outcomes — Long-Term Efficacy

| Series | Follow-up | n | Subjective cure | Objective cure |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Serati 2021 (10-year follow-up)**<sup>[[8]](#ref8)</sup> | Median 120 mo | **39 evaluated** | **32/39 (82%)** | **29/39 (74.4%)** |
| **Serati 2015 (5-year follow-up)**<sup>[[9]](#ref9)</sup> | Median 68.8 mo | **44 evaluated** | **39/44 (88.6%)** | **37/44 (84.1%)** |

Both follow-up publications identified **preoperative stage IV vault descensus** as an independent predictor of recurrence in their analyzed cohorts (reported ORs 7.66 and 8.78). These are not two independent randomized comparisons, and subjective and objective success use different definitions.<sup>[[8]](#ref8)[[9]](#ref9)</sup>

### Meeks 1994 (early series)<sup>[[3]](#ref3)</sup>

- **110 retrospective cases**, all with complex pelvic-floor defects and at least three years of follow-up; **4/110 recurrent defects**, all involving the anterior vaginal wall.
- This identifies anterior recurrence **in that series**; it does not establish the dominant late failure mode of every apical suspension.

---

## Comparative Outcomes

### vs Sacrospinous Ligament Fixation (Maher 2001 matched case-control)<sup>[[4]](#ref4)</sup>

The source identified **50 iliococcygeus and 78 sacrospinous operations**, then analyzed **36 matched pairs**. Mean follow-up was **21 versus 19 months**; the matched analysis had only **50% power to detect a 20% success difference**.

| Outcome | ICG fixation | Sacrospinous fixation | p |
|---|---|---|---|
| Subjective success | 91% | 94% | 0.73 |
| Objective success | 53% | 67% | 0.36 |
| Patient satisfaction (VAS) | 78/100 | 91/100 | **0.01** |
| Recovery time (days) | 54 | 39 | **0.04** |
| Postoperative cystocele | Arm rates not in abstract | Arm rates not in abstract | Difference not detected |
| Buttock pain | Arm rates not in abstract | Arm rates not in abstract | Difference not detected |
| Hemorrhage requiring transfusion | Arm rates not in abstract | Arm rates not in abstract | Difference not detected |

**Interpretation:** this underpowered observational comparison detected no success or major-harm difference, but **does not prove equivalence**. Satisfaction and recovery favored sacrospinous fixation in the matched pairs; the authors cautioned against discarding it in favor of iliococcygeus fixation.<sup>[[4]](#ref4)</sup>

### vs Coccygeus Fascial Suspension vs Sacrospinous (Peters 1995)<sup>[[2]](#ref2)</sup>

In a **retrospective, nonrandomized** cohort of 121 records, **81** had coccygeus-fascial suspension and **30** sacrospinous fixation; **10** abdominal sacrocolpopexies were not comparable. Kaplan–Meier projected 2-year cure was **96% versus 80%**, respectively. The **coccygeus-fascial** target is related but not identical to iliococcygeus fascia fixation, so this is not a direct iliococcygeus effect estimate.<sup>[[2]](#ref2)</sup>

### vs Abdominal Sacrocolpopexy (Milani 2014, n = 36 vs 41)<sup>[[11]](#ref11)</sup>

- **Operative time**: significantly shorter (78 vs 140 min, p < 0.001).
- Relapse was **22% after ICG versus 15% after sacrocolpopexy** (p = 0.36) in this small retrospective cohort; the nonsignificant result does not establish equivalent durability. Median blood loss was **higher with ICG (150 vs 100 mL)**. ICG remains a mesh-free vaginal option in appropriately selected patients.<sup>[[11]](#ref11)</sup>

### As hysteropexy (Suh & Jeon 2018, n = 144)<sup>[[10]](#ref10)</sup>

| Subgroup | 4-yr success |
|---|---|
| ICG **with hysterectomy**, mild prolapse (stage II, point C ≤ 0) | **100%** |
| ICG with hysterectomy, advanced prolapse | 91.2% |
| ICG **as hysteropexy**, mild prolapse | 75.3% |
| ICG as hysteropexy, **advanced prolapse** | **48.0%** |

**Independent failure predictors in this 144-woman retrospective cohort** were advanced prolapse (stage > II and point C > 0) and a uterus-preserving technique. The study authors considered hysteropexy **an option for mild uterine prolapse**; the lower estimated advanced-hysteropexy success should inform counseling, not be treated as a universal prohibition.<sup>[[10]](#ref10)</sup>

## Complications

Complications are incompletely characterized in small, heterogeneous series. **Meeks reported postoperative complications in 41/110 patients**; the specific events below should not be mistaken for an overall 1–3% complication rate.<sup>[[3]](#ref3)[[4]](#ref4)</sup>

| Complication | Rate / note |
|---|---|
| Any postoperative complication | **41/110** in the retrospective Meeks series; types and severity require the full report<sup>[[3]](#ref3)</sup> |
| Hemorrhage | 3/110 (2.7%) had EBL > 750 mL in Meeks; 2 required transfusion<sup>[[3]](#ref3)</sup> |
| Bladder injury | 1/110 in Meeks; not a population-rate estimate<sup>[[3]](#ref3)</sup> |
| Bowel injury | 1/110 in Meeks; not a population-rate estimate<sup>[[3]](#ref3)</sup> |
| Buttock / gluteal pain | No statistically detected difference from sacrospinous fixation in the underpowered Maher comparison; a theoretical nerve-distance benefit is **not a demonstrated lower pain rate**<sup>[[4]](#ref4)[[15]](#ref15)</sup> |
| Postoperative catheterization | Mean **6.1 days** in Meeks; **not** a measured urinary-retention rate<sup>[[3]](#ref3)</sup> |
| **Anterior compartment recurrence** | All **4 recurrent defects** in Meeks involved the anterior wall; this does not generalize to every series<sup>[[3]](#ref3)</sup> |
| Mesh-related complications | **None** (native tissue) |

---

## Risk Factors for Failure

- **Preoperative stage IV vault prolapse** — independent predictor in the small Serati follow-up cohorts (reported ORs 7.66 and 8.78).<sup>[[8]](#ref8)[[9]](#ref9)</sup>
- **Advanced prolapse** (stage > II, point C > 0).<sup>[[10]](#ref10)</sup>
- **Uterus-preserving technique** (hysteropexy), particularly for advanced prolapse.<sup>[[10]](#ref10)</sup>

---

## Advantages and Disadvantages

| Advantage | Disadvantage |
|---|---|
| Technically simpler than SSLF — no specialized suture-passing devices required<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup> | Less commonly performed; surgeon familiarity limited<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup> |
| Bilateral fixation may preserve a midline axis; a clinical advantage is unproved<sup>[[2]](#ref2)[[15]](#ref15)</sup> | All four recurrences in Meeks involved the anterior wall; this is not a universal failure pattern<sup>[[3]](#ref3)</sup> |
| Caudal-to-spine fascial placement → **theoretical reduction in neurovascular proximity**, not proven lower clinical injury<sup>[[15]](#ref15)</sup> | Lower observed success as hysteropexy, particularly in advanced prolapse in one cohort<sup>[[10]](#ref10)</sup> |
| Native tissue — no mesh<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup> | Limited high-quality comparative data — no large RCTs vs SSLF / USLS |
| Shorter operative time than abdominal sacrocolpopexy in one retrospective series<sup>[[11]](#ref11)</sup> | Attenuated fascia may complicate fixation; MRI-defined defects have not established a clinical failure threshold<sup>[[12]](#ref12)[[15]](#ref15)</sup> |
| No success difference detected against sacrospinous fixation in one matched-pair study<sup>[[4]](#ref4)</sup> | That study was underpowered; satisfaction and recovery favored SSLF<sup>[[4]](#ref4)</sup> |

## Guideline Position

The **AUGS–IUGA 2020 terminology report** classifies iliococcygeus fixation as a distinct native-tissue vaginal-apex operation and standardizes its fascial target and variations; classification is **not an endorsement of equivalent effectiveness** to sacrospinous or uterosacral fixation.<sup>[[15]](#ref15)</sup> The **current 2026 AWMF S3 prolapse guideline**, recommendation **6.E54** (evidence grade 2b, recommendation grade 0), says iliococcygeus fixation **may be considered when no adequate alternative is available** and notes the absence of randomized trials or systematic long-term data for this procedure.<sup>[[16]](#ref16)</sup> The 2019 ACOG/AUGS prolapse bulletin provides the broader POP framework but is not used here to assign an unverified iliococcygeus-specific grade.<sup>[[13]](#ref13)[[14]](#ref14)</sup>

---

## Key Principles

- ICG fixation is a **transvaginal native-tissue apical operation**; bilateral fixation is common, but unilateral variation exists. The usual suture target is **medial iliococcygeus fascia caudal to the ischial spine**, not a universal anterior-to-spine point.<sup>[[15]](#ref15)</sup>
- The fascial target is selected to **reduce neurovascular proximity**; actual injury reduction versus SSLF is unproved.<sup>[[4]](#ref4)[[15]](#ref15)</sup>
- Post-hysterectomy vault prolapse has five- and ten-year **cohort** follow-up: 88.6%/82% subjective and 84.1%/74.4% objective success among 44/39 evaluated women, respectively.<sup>[[8]](#ref8)[[9]](#ref9)</sup>
- Hysteropexy may be reasonable for **selected mild uterine prolapse**; one retrospective cohort estimated **48%** four-year success for advanced prolapse with uterine preservation.<sup>[[10]](#ref10)</sup>
- **Stage IV vault descensus** predicted recurrence in the small Serati follow-up cohorts, but is not an absolute contraindication.<sup>[[8]](#ref8)[[9]](#ref9)</sup>
- The Maher matched-pair comparison with **SSLF** detected no success difference but was underpowered; satisfaction and recovery favored SSLF.<sup>[[4]](#ref4)</sup>
- **All four recurrent defects in Meeks involved the anterior wall**; evaluate other compartments without treating that series as a universal failure pattern.<sup>[[3]](#ref3)</sup>
- The procedure is relatively uncommon; the **2026 AWMF S3 recommendation** limits consideration to situations without an adequate alternative because randomized comparative and systematic long-term evidence is lacking.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[15]](#ref15)[[16]](#ref16)</sup>
