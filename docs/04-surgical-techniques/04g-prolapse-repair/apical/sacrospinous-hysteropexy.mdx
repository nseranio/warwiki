# Sacrospinous Hysteropexy

**Sacrospinous hysteropexy** is a vaginal, uterus-preserving apical suspension. Permanent sutures attach the cervix to a sacrospinous ligament; it is not the same operation as sacrospinous ligament fixation for a post-hysterectomy vault, or as mesh-augmented hysteropexy.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[2]](#ref2)</sup>

For the vault operation, see [Sacrospinous Ligament Fixation](/docs/surgical-techniques/04g-prolapse-repair/sacrospinous-ligament-fixation). For the alternatives discussed below, see [Manchester-Fothergill Procedure](/docs/surgical-techniques/04g-prolapse-repair/manchester-fothergill) and [Sacrohysteropexy](/docs/surgical-techniques/04g-prolapse-repair/sacrohysteropexy).

---

## Selection and counseling

Choice of uterine preservation starts with symptoms, examination, uterine/cervical assessment, future reproductive plans, and the patient's values—not a universal preference for retaining or removing the uterus. Abnormal bleeding, abnormal cervical screening, or suspected uterine/cervical disease require appropriate assessment before a uterus-preserving operation.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[3]](#ref3)</sup>

The best directly applicable native-tissue randomized evidence is SAVE-U: women had stage 2 or greater uterine prolapse, had completed childbearing, and had no prior pelvic-floor/prolapse surgery. Its results should not be projected unchanged to women outside those criteria or to other sacrospinous techniques.<sup>[[2]](#ref2)</sup>

## Operative concept

In SAVE-U, the surgeon entered the right pararectal space through a posterior vaginal incision, placed two permanent sutures in the right sacrospinous ligament under direct vision at least 2 cm from the ischial spine, passed them through the posterior cervix, then reduced the uterus and closed the vaginal incision. Concomitant anterior/posterior repair or anti-incontinence surgery was permitted when indicated.<sup>[[2]](#ref2)</sup>

That trial protocol is an evidence-based description of one technique, not a substitute for training, individualized anatomy, or an operative manual. Exact route, side, number/material of sutures, and device choice vary among described procedures; avoid treating a device or a fixed distance as independently proven safe for every patient.<sup>[[2]](#ref2)[[4]](#ref4)</sup>

## Outcomes

### SAVE-U: native-tissue hysteropexy versus vaginal hysterectomy plus USLS

The five-year SAVE-U follow-up reported apical failure with bothersome bulge symptoms or repeat apical-prolapse surgery in 1/102 (1%) after hysteropexy and 8/102 (7.8%) after vaginal hysterectomy plus uterosacral suspension (difference −6.7 percentage points, 95% CI −12.8 to −0.7). The trial's composite success definition was no prolapse beyond the hymen, no bothersome bulge symptoms, and no repeat surgery or pessary for recurrence; it was 87% versus 76%, respectively. Time-to-event analyses did not show a between-group difference. Attrition, the non-blinded intervention, and the specific comparator matter when interpreting these findings.<sup>[[2]](#ref2)</sup>

### Manchester comparison

The SAM trial compared native-tissue sacrospinous hysteropexy with Manchester repair in a different population: uterine descent with POP-Q point D no lower than −1 cm. At two years, its composite success favored Manchester (87.3% versus 77.0%); the prespecified noninferiority criterion for sacrospinous hysteropexy was not met. This is a procedure-specific result, not proof that every sacrospinous suspension is inferior to every uterus-preserving repair.<sup>[[5]](#ref5)</sup>

### Keep mesh evidence separate

SUPeR tested **polypropylene Uphold LITE mesh** sacrospinous hysteropexy against vaginal hysterectomy with bilateral USLS in postmenopausal women with prolapse beyond the hymen. At five years, the model-based composite-failure estimate favored the mesh-hysteropexy arm (37% versus 54%; adjusted hazard ratio 0.58, 95% CI 0.36–0.94). It does not establish the durability, adverse effects, or comparative benefit of native-tissue sacrospinous hysteropexy. The study's supplemental package was not recoverable in this review.<sup>[[6]](#ref6)</sup>

## Risks and follow-up

Potential complications include bleeding, postoperative buttock/pelvic pain, nerve-related symptoms, urinary or bowel symptoms, recurrent prolapse, and a later need for treatment. Their frequency depends on the approach, concomitant repairs, outcome definition, follow-up duration, and whether mesh was used; broad pooled rates should not be used as individual predictions.<sup>[[2]](#ref2)[[4]](#ref4)</sup>

Continue routine cervical screening when appropriate and reassess new bleeding or recurrent bulge symptoms. Counsel about the possibility of recurrence in any compartment and about the limits of pregnancy evidence after uterine suspension.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[3]](#ref3)</sup>

## Key principles

- Distinguish native-tissue sacrospinous hysteropexy, mesh-augmented hysteropexy, and vault SSLF; their evidence is not interchangeable.
- SAVE-U supports a native-tissue option against one vaginal-hysterectomy/USLS comparator, with trial-specific eligibility and outcome definitions.<sup>[[2]](#ref2)</sup>
- SAM favored Manchester at two years for its selected population; it does not answer every hysteropexy decision.<sup>[[5]](#ref5)</sup>
- Use the operative protocol as a training reference, not as a universal safe-zone or device rule.<sup>[[2]](#ref2)[[4]](#ref4)</sup>
