# Sacrospinous Hysteropexy

**Sacrospinous hysteropexy** is a vaginal, uterus-preserving apical suspension. Permanent sutures attach the cervix to a sacrospinous ligament; it is not the same operation as sacrospinous ligament fixation for a post-hysterectomy vault, or as mesh-augmented hysteropexy.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[10]](#ref10)</sup>

For the vault operation, see [Sacrospinous Ligament Fixation](/docs/surgical-techniques/04g-prolapse-repair/sacrospinous-ligament-fixation). For the alternatives discussed below, see [Manchester-Fothergill Procedure](/docs/surgical-techniques/04g-prolapse-repair/manchester-fothergill) and [Sacrohysteropexy](/docs/surgical-techniques/04g-prolapse-repair/sacrohysteropexy).

---

## Selection and counseling

Choice of uterine preservation starts with symptoms, examination, uterine/cervical assessment, future reproductive plans, and the patient's values—not a universal preference for retaining or removing the uterus. Abnormal bleeding, abnormal cervical screening, or suspected uterine/cervical disease require appropriate assessment before a uterus-preserving operation.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[17]](#ref17)</sup>

If the cervix is elongated and childbearing is complete, consider concomitant cervical amputation (trachelectomy) at the time of uterus-preserving surgery. AUGS grades this a recommendation (grade C) and defines elongation in its table as cervical length above 4 cm; elongation is associated with higher apical recurrence after preservation. The [Manchester-Fothergill procedure](/docs/surgical-techniques/04g-prolapse-repair/manchester-fothergill) is a distinct uterus-preserving operation that incorporates cervical amputation (see the SAM comparison below).<sup>[[18]](#ref18)</sup>

The best directly applicable native-tissue randomized evidence is SAVE-U: women had stage 2 or greater uterine prolapse, had completed childbearing, and had no prior pelvic-floor/prolapse surgery. Its results should not be projected unchanged to women outside those criteria or to other sacrospinous techniques.<sup>[[10]](#ref10)</sup>


Conservative options include pelvic-floor therapy and a pessary; surgery is considered for bothersome symptoms in light of the patient's goals, not the POP-Q stage alone. Desire to retain the uterus does not necessarily mean a desire for future pregnancy. SAVE-U participants had completed childbearing, so its results do not establish pregnancy safety.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[10]](#ref10)</sup>

## Surgical Technique

The operation is performed in dorsal lithotomy under regional or general anesthesia. A posterior approach was used in SAVE-U; anterior and bilateral variants are also described. The following is an orientation to the posterior operation, with trial-specific details identified rather than treated as universal requirements.<sup>[[5]](#ref5)[[6]](#ref6)[[9]](#ref9)[[10]](#ref10)</sup>

1. **Vaginal incision.** Posterior vaginal-wall incision to expose the intended cervical/pararectal dissection.<sup>[[5]](#ref5)[[10]](#ref10)</sup>
2. **Pararectal-space dissection.** Vagina separated from rectovaginal fascia by sharp and blunt dissection until the pararectal space is entered.<sup>[[5]](#ref5)</sup>
3. **Sacrospinous-ligament identification.** The sacrospinous ligament (running from ischial spine to sacrum) is palpated and exposed; overlying connective tissue cleared to allow accurate suture placement.<sup>[[5]](#ref5)</sup>
4. **Suture placement.** SAVE-U used **two permanent sutures** in the right sacrospinous ligament under direct vision, at least 2 cm from the ischial spine. Pudendal structures lie near the spine, while other sacral nerve branches may cross the ligament; a distance alone does not establish a universal safe zone. Described suture-passing instruments include Capio, Deschamps carriers and Miya hooks; device-specific handling follows its instructions and the chosen approach.<sup>[[7]](#ref7)[[8]](#ref8)[[10]](#ref10)[[12]](#ref12)</sup>
5. **Cervical fixation.** Sutures passed through the posterior aspect of the cervix and tied — uterus suspended to the sacrospinous ligament. Right-sided fixation was used in SAVE-U; bilateral techniques (including anterior bilateral) have also been described.<sup>[[3]](#ref3)[[9]](#ref9)</sup>
6. **Concomitant compartment repair.** Anterior and / or posterior colporrhaphy as needed; vaginal incision closed.

---

Kapoor’s earlier review describes the range of hysteropexy outcomes and study methods. Interpretation remains technique- and endpoint-specific.<sup>[[2]](#ref2)</sup>

Concomitant continence surgery is a separate decision based on symptoms and assessment, rather than an automatic addition to apical suspension; the Cochrane POP/SUI review addresses that accompanying question.<sup>[[4]](#ref4)</sup>

## Outcomes

### SAVE-U: native-tissue hysteropexy versus vaginal hysterectomy plus USLS

The five-year SAVE-U follow-up reported apical failure with bothersome bulge symptoms or repeat apical-prolapse surgery in 1/102 (1%) after hysteropexy and 8/102 (7.8%) after vaginal hysterectomy plus uterosacral suspension (difference −6.7 percentage points, 95% CI −12.8 to −0.7). The trial's composite success definition was no prolapse beyond the hymen, no bothersome bulge symptoms, and no repeat surgery or pessary for recurrence; it was 87% versus 76%, respectively. Time-to-event analyses did not show a between-group difference. Attrition, the non-blinded intervention, and the specific comparator matter when interpreting these findings.<sup>[[10]](#ref10)</sup>

### Manchester comparison

The SAM trial compared native-tissue sacrospinous hysteropexy with Manchester repair in a different population: uterine descent with POP-Q point D no lower than −1 cm. At two years, its composite success favored Manchester (87.3% versus 77.0%); the prespecified noninferiority criterion for sacrospinous hysteropexy was not met. This is a procedure-specific result, not proof that every sacrospinous suspension is inferior to every uterus-preserving repair.<sup>[[3]](#ref3)</sup>

### Keep mesh evidence separate

SUPeR tested **polypropylene Uphold LITE mesh** sacrospinous hysteropexy against vaginal hysterectomy with bilateral USLS in postmenopausal women with prolapse beyond the hymen. The report tabulated cumulative first-failure proportions of 29/79 (37%) versus 42/78 (54%) at the five-year visit; these are not reoperation rates. The interval-censored primary analysis favored mesh hysteropexy (adjusted hazard ratio 0.58, 95% CI 0.36–0.94), but proportional hazards were not met, so that ratio summarizes a changing effect over follow-up. It does not establish the durability, adverse effects, or comparative benefit of native-tissue sacrospinous hysteropexy. The specific mesh product is no longer marketed in the United States for transvaginal POP repair.<sup>[[11]](#ref11)</sup>

## Complications

### Buttock / gluteal pain

Pain around the buttock or posterior thigh can follow sacrospinous dissection and suture placement. The sacral branches crossing the coccygeus–sacrospinous complex and pudendal structures are relevant anatomy; attributing every pain episode to one nerve is unreliable.<sup>[[7]](#ref7)[[12]](#ref12)</sup>

Pain studies include vault SSLF and device-specific series rather than only native-tissue hysteropexy. Their early pain frequencies depend on the operation and assessment time. Persistent, severe or neuropathic symptoms merit assessment for suture-related nerve irritation or entrapment; see the [SSLF page](/docs/surgical-techniques/04g-prolapse-repair/sacrospinous-ligament-fixation) for the shared anatomy and pain literature.<sup>[[5]](#ref5)[[13]](#ref13)[[14]](#ref14)</sup>

### Other complications and operative considerations

| Issue | Clinical context |
| --- | --- |
| Bleeding / hematoma | The pudendal and adjacent pelvic vessels make exposure, visualization and hemostasis important; a fixed suture distance does not eliminate vascular risk. |
| Nerve symptoms | New radiating pain, altered sensation or weakness require assessment; symptoms and anatomy guide management. |
| Recurrent prolapse | May occur in the anterior, posterior or apical compartment; distinguish examination recurrence, bothersome bulge and retreatment. |
| Urinary or bowel symptoms | Can persist or arise after combined repairs and are not captured by an apical success rate alone. |
| Mesh exposure | Applies to mesh-augmented variants such as SUPeR, not to a mesh-free native-tissue operation. |

These risks are described in the clinical and technical literature; their rates vary with the precise procedure and follow-up.<sup>[[5]](#ref5)[[8]](#ref8)[[10]](#ref10)[[11]](#ref11)</sup>

## Factors associated with failure

A secondary analysis of SUPeR explored baseline features including BMI and symptom duration in relation to composite failure. These are prognostic associations in a trial of **mesh hysteropexy versus hysterectomy/USLS**, not a validated selection rule or individual risk calculator for native-tissue hysteropexy.<sup>[[15]](#ref15)</sup>

## Advantages and trade-offs

| Domain | Practical consideration |
| --- | --- |
| Uterine preservation | Meets a preference to retain the uterus; does not itself prove future fertility or obstetric safety. |
| Operative route | Vaginal access avoids abdominal mesh dissection. The operation and concomitant repairs determine the total surgical burden. |
| Implant | Native-tissue sacrospinous hysteropexy uses sutures without a mesh implant; mesh-augmented variants are separate procedures. |
| Durability | Trial results depend on the comparator, eligibility, outcome definition and duration of follow-up. |
| Ongoing care | Continue appropriate cervical screening and investigate abnormal bleeding; routine endometrial surveillance is not implied for every asymptomatic patient. |

These trade-offs are best discussed alongside the specific SAVE-U and SAM results above.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[3]](#ref3)[[10]](#ref10)[[17]](#ref17)</sup>

---

## Guideline context and follow-up

ACOG/AUGS guidance includes uterine-preserving surgery among the options for appropriately assessed uterine prolapse. The 2025 AUGS Clinical Practice Statement goes further: in the absence of uterine or cervical pathology, uterine-preserving surgery should be offered (grade B, strong recommendation), after an individualized benefit-risk discussion. This does not make native-tissue hysteropexy, vaginal hysterectomy/USLS, vault SSLF and mesh hysteropexy interchangeable operations or establish universal equivalence.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[16]](#ref16)[[18]](#ref18)</sup>

Continue appropriate cervical screening; investigate abnormal bleeding and reassess recurrent bulge, urinary, bowel or pain symptoms. Later hysterectomy after uterine preservation is uncommon (below 5% in the studies AUGS reviewed) and most often performed for recurrent prolapse.<sup>[[18]](#ref18)</sup> Counseling should include recurrence in any compartment, possible later treatment, and limited pregnancy evidence.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[17]](#ref17)[[18]](#ref18)</sup>
