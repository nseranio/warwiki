# Cervicosacropexy and Vaginosacropexy (CESA/VASA)

**Cervicosacropexy (CESA)** and **vaginosacropexy (VASA)** are bilateral apical suspension techniques performed through open or laparoscopic access, described by Jäger, in which the paired uterosacral ligaments are replaced by two narrow polyvinylidene fluoride (PVDF) mesh strips running from the cervix (CESA) or vaginal vault (VASA) to the prevertebral fascia at the level of S1.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[2]](#ref2)</sup> They were developed as a surgical alternative for women with apical prolapse and coexisting mixed or urgency urinary incontinence, on the theory that lax uterosacral ligaments contribute to both conditions.<sup>[[2]](#ref2)</sup> For the general framework and the more established apical options, see [Prolapse Repair](/docs/surgical-techniques/04g-prolapse-repair) and [Sacrocolpopexy](/docs/surgical-techniques/04g-prolapse-repair/sacrocolpopexy).

---

## Technique

When a uterus is present, CESA generally includes subtotal (supracervical) hysterectomy with the bladder left attached to the cervical stump; a stump left by prior subtotal hysterectomy can also be used. VASA fixes the mesh to the vaginal vault when no cervix remains.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[3]](#ref3)[[4]](#ref4)</sup> In Jäger's described DynaMesh constructs, two PVDF arms replace the course of the uterosacral ligaments; each arm measures 8.8 cm in CESA and 9.3 cm in VASA.<sup>[[2]](#ref2)</sup> The central mesh segment is secured to the cervix or vaginal vault with nonabsorbable sutures. In the published open technique, each arm is fixed to the prevertebral fascia at S1 with nonabsorbable sutures; in the described laparoscopic technique, each arm is fixed there with three titanium tacks.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[3]](#ref3)[[4]](#ref4)</sup> In Rexhepi et al.'s laparoscopic technique, bilateral 1.5-cm peritoneal windows were opened at S1 on either side of the rectosigmoid, with the ureters and iliac vessels lateral and the hypogastric nerve spared.<sup>[[3]](#ref3)</sup> The arms were then tunneled beneath the uterosacral peritoneal folds, as also described by Ludwig et al.<sup>[[3]](#ref3)[[4]](#ref4)</sup> Ludwig et al. describe peritoneal closure over the central segment so the whole construct is covered.<sup>[[4]](#ref4)</sup> In a 120-patient laparoscopic series, baseline apical prolapse was stage I in 63/120 (53%) and stages II–IV in 57/120 (47%); 116/120 (97%) had apical POP-Q stage 0 at four months.<sup>[[3]](#ref3)</sup> The first 4 patients had early apical relapse after fast-absorbing sutures were used to fix the mesh to the cervical stump; they underwent laparoscopic refixation with nonabsorbable sutures, which were then used for subsequent patients.<sup>[[3]](#ref3)</sup>

Unlike sacrocolpopexy, which attaches mesh to both the anterior and posterior vaginal walls, the CESA/VASA construct as described has no routine anterior or posterior vaginal mesh attachment and is not designed to reinforce those compartments directly.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup> In a single-center retrospective CESA comparison, baseline apical prolapse was stage I in 56/145 (39%) completers.<sup>[[4]](#ref4)</sup> At one year, 75/75 open and 70/70 laparoscopic completers had apical POP-Q stage 0 after one participant underwent reoperation for inadequate cervical fixation within five months.<sup>[[4]](#ref4)</sup> The laparoscopic group had shorter operating time, hospital stay, and time to return to usual activity.<sup>[[4]](#ref4)</sup> Thirty-two of 177 operated women lacked one-year follow-up.<sup>[[4]](#ref4)</sup>

## Evidence for Urinary Incontinence

CESA/VASA has been studied specifically as a treatment for urgency and mixed urinary incontinence. The early-stopped randomized URGE 1 trial assigned women with these symptoms and POP-Q stage 0–I to CESA/VASA (n = 55) or solifenacin 10 mg/day (n = 41).<sup>[[6]](#ref6)</sup> At 16 weeks, 23/55 (42%) versus 4/41 (10%) met the study-defined continence endpoint in the intention-to-treat comparison; 13 assigned to surgery did not undergo it, and 10 assigned to medication stopped it within four weeks.<sup>[[6]](#ref6)</sup> In Rexhepi et al.'s uncontrolled cohort (83 laCESA, 37 laVASA), 78/120 (65%) met the study's four-month continence classification: 60/94 with baseline mixed incontinence and 18/26 with urgency incontinence.<sup>[[3]](#ref3)</sup> Four women classified as continent still reported leakage about once weekly or less often. Mean hospital stay was three days. The series also recorded one bladder injury, one bowel perforation requiring revision three days later, and four early apical failures requiring refixation.<sup>[[3]](#ref3)</sup>

A 2022 systematic review did not meta-analyze the heterogeneous studies. It reported descriptive cure proportions of 47.5% for mixed urinary incontinence, 73.8% for urgency urinary incontinence, and 97% to 100% for apical prolapse at a mean follow-up of 9.7 ± 7.3 months; 51.8% of cases had concomitant or subsequent sling surgery, so the continence results cannot be assigned to CESA/VASA alone.<sup>[[2]](#ref2)</sup> Additional incontinence surgery was performed in 38.9% (216/555) of women who had preoperative urinary incontinence, and concomitant or subsequent prolapse surgery in 4.4% (13/299). The review's authors rated the included studies as having moderate-to-high risk of bias and low certainty of evidence overall, concluding that CESA/VASA may relieve urgency and mixed urinary incontinence and appears to correct apical prolapse in the short term, but called for trials with better-defined patient selection and validated outcome measures before the technique can be compared with established options.<sup>[[2]](#ref2)</sup>

## Limitations

NICE HealthTech guidance HTG539 recommends that CESA/VASA using mesh for pelvic organ prolapse be performed only in the context of research because evidence on safety and efficacy is inadequate in quantity and quality.<sup>[[5]](#ref5)</sup> The published comparative evidence is mainly short-term observational series; direct randomized comparisons with established apical suspensions are lacking.<sup>[[2]](#ref2)</sup> The PVDF mesh used in CESA/VASA, fixed with sacral sutures in Jäger's original open technique or tacks in the described laparoscopic approach, differs from the macroporous polypropylene mesh used in standard sacrocolpopexy; separate attention to its own exposure and erosion profile is warranted before adoption outside the centers that have published this early experience.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[3]](#ref3)[[4]](#ref4)</sup> Several of the published outcome series on this technique come from groups whose surgeons disclose a financial or consulting relationship with the mesh manufacturer.<sup>[[3]](#ref3)[[4]](#ref4)</sup>
