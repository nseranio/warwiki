# Sacrohysteropexy

**Sacrohysteropexy** is a **uterine-sparing reconstructive operation** that suspends the uterus (via the cervix) to the **sacral promontory** using a synthetic mesh or biologic graft, restoring apical support while preserving the uterus. It can be performed open, laparoscopically, or with robotic assistance — minimally invasive approaches now predominate.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[2]](#ref2)[[3]](#ref3)</sup>

For the post-hysterectomy mesh equivalent, see [Sacrocolpopexy](/docs/surgical-techniques/04g-prolapse-repair/sacrocolpopexy). For the supracervical hysterectomy variant, see [Supracervical Hysterectomy at Sacrocolpopexy](/docs/surgical-techniques/04g-prolapse-repair/supracervical-hysterectomy). For native-tissue uterus-preserving operations, see [Sacrospinous Hysteropexy](/docs/surgical-techniques/04g-prolapse-repair/sacrospinous-hysteropexy) and [Manchester-Fothergill Procedure](/docs/surgical-techniques/04g-prolapse-repair/manchester-fothergill).

---

## Definition and Rationale

Sacrohysteropexy attaches the cervix or uterine isthmus to the **anterior longitudinal ligament of the sacrum** using a synthetic or biologic graft, recreating the physiologic vaginal axis along the levator plate.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[4]](#ref4)</sup>

Rationale for uterine preservation:

- Shorter operative time and less blood loss than hysterectomy-based mesh repairs.<sup>[[3]](#ref3)[[5]](#ref5)</sup>
- **Lower mesh exposure** vs sacrocolpopexy after total hysterectomy.<sup>[[3]](#ref3)[[6]](#ref6)</sup>
- Preserves fertility and natural menopause timing.<sup>[[7]](#ref7)</sup>
- Patient preference — ~half of patients with prolapse prefer to keep their uterus.<sup>[[5]](#ref5)</sup>
- No significant difference in short-term prolapse outcomes vs hysterectomy.<sup>[[3]](#ref3)</sup>

---

## Indications and Patient Selection

ACOG and AUGS endorse hysteropexy as a **viable alternative to hysterectomy** in women with uterine prolapse.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[8]](#ref8)</sup>

| Criterion | Detail |
|---|---|
| Stage | **Symptomatic POP-Q stage ≥ 2** with apical descent<sup>[[9]](#ref9)[[10]](#ref10)</sup> |
| Uterine preservation | Patient desires uterine preservation (fertility, personal preference, cultural reasons)<sup>[[7]](#ref7)</sup> |
| Childbearing | Standard indication after childbearing complete; selected cases in fertility-desiring patients<sup>[[11]](#ref11)</sup> |
| Uterine status | **No uterine pathology** — normal cervical cytology, no abnormal bleeding, no premalignant or malignant disease<sup>[[7]](#ref7)[[12]](#ref12)</sup> |

### Contraindications

- Abnormal uterine bleeding requiring evaluation.<sup>[[7]](#ref7)[[12]](#ref12)</sup>
- Uterine or cervical premalignancy / malignancy.<sup>[[12]](#ref12)</sup>
- Significant uterine pathology (fibroids requiring treatment, endometrial hyperplasia).<sup>[[7]](#ref7)</sup>
- Cervical elongation without true apical descent — **may be better served by Manchester procedure**.<sup>[[12]](#ref12)</sup>

## Surgical Technique — Laparoscopic / Robotic

The operation is analogous to sacrocolpopexy with uterine preservation.<sup>[[2]](#ref2)[[13]](#ref13)[[14]](#ref14)</sup>

1. **Port placement and exposure.** Standard 4-port laparoscopic or robotic configuration; steep Trendelenburg; sigmoid colon retracted to expose the sacral promontory.<sup>[[2]](#ref2)[[14]](#ref14)</sup>
2. **Sacral promontory dissection.** Incise peritoneum over the promontory; identify and expose the **anterior longitudinal ligament (ALL) of S1**. Critical structures to preserve: **right common iliac vein, middle sacral vessels, left common iliac vein, right ureter** crossing the pelvic brim. **Presacral venous plexus bleeding** is the most feared complication — control with pressure, bone wax, or thumbtacks.<sup>[[2]](#ref2)[[14]](#ref14)</sup>
3. **Peritoneal tunnel.** Create a peritoneal tunnel from the promontory to the cervix along the right pelvic sidewall, lateral to the rectum, to house the mesh and allow subsequent peritonization.<sup>[[2]](#ref2)[[14]](#ref14)</sup>
4. **Cervical / uterine dissection.**
   - **Standard (wrap) technique**: incise anterior and posterior peritoneum overlying cervical stroma; create tunnels through the **avascular plane of the broad ligament lateral to the uterine vessels** to preserve uterine blood supply; dissect vesicovaginal and rectovaginal spaces to expose cervical stroma.<sup>[[13]](#ref13)[[14]](#ref14)</sup>
   - **Simplified posterior-only technique** (Jan 2018): only the posterior aspect of the cervix is dissected — eliminates risk of bladder perforation and anterior mesh erosion.<sup>[[15]](#ref15)</sup>
5. **Mesh selection and preparation.** See table below.
6. **Mesh attachment to the cervix.**
   - **Y-shaped mesh** (standard): anterior arm sutured to anterior cervical stroma / vaginal wall; posterior arm to posterior cervical stroma — interrupted non-absorbable or delayed-absorbable sutures; arms wrap around cervix and meet at the sacral arm.<sup>[[13]](#ref13)</sup>
   - **Flat strip** (simplified): single posterior strip with interrupted sutures, extending directly to the promontory.<sup>[[15]](#ref15)</sup>
   - **Anterior + posterior extension**: in multicompartment prolapse, extend anterior arm along vesicovaginal septum and posterior arm along rectovaginal septum (full sacrocolpopexy-style).<sup>[[17]](#ref17)</sup>
7. **Sacral fixation.** Secure the sacral end of the mesh to the ALL at S1 with **2–4 interrupted non-absorbable sutures** or tackers; place **tension-free** — uterus elevated to anatomic position without excess tension.<sup>[[13]](#ref13)[[4]](#ref4)</sup>
8. **Peritonization.** Close peritoneum over the mesh with a running absorbable suture to prevent bowel adhesion and SBO; tunneling technique reduces peritoneal defect size.<sup>[[2]](#ref2)[[15]](#ref15)</sup>

### Mesh selection

| Mesh | Configuration | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| **Type-1 polypropylene** (macroporous, monofilament) | Y-shaped or flat strip | Most commonly used; diamond weave allows stretch<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[2]](#ref2)</sup> |
| **PVDF (polyvinylidene fluoride)** | Flat strip, square weave | No longitudinal give — wrap not required<sup>[[3]](#ref3)</sup> |
| **SERATEX SlimSling** | Narrow strip | Designed for isolated apical prolapse<sup>[[16]](#ref16)</sup> |
| **Biologic graft** | Flat strip | Alternative when synthetic mesh is declined<sup>[[5]](#ref5)</sup> |

### Concomitant procedures

- **Cervical shortening / amputation** — performed in 75% of patients in one series for cervical elongation.<sup>[[16]](#ref16)</sup>
- **Anterior colporrhaphy** — for concurrent cystocele.<sup>[[16]](#ref16)</sup>
- **Posterior colporrhaphy** — for concurrent rectocele.
- **Midurethral sling** — for concurrent or occult SUI (~30% of cases).<sup>[[16]](#ref16)</sup>

### Operative metrics

- **Mean operative time**: ~112 min; laparoscopic sacrohysteropexy is ~60 min longer than vaginal hysteropexy.<sup>[[9]](#ref9)[[16]](#ref16)[[19]](#ref19)</sup>
- **EBL**: minimal; significantly less than hysterectomy-based mesh approaches.<sup>[[3]](#ref3)</sup>
- **Hospital stay**: 1–2 days.<sup>[[10]](#ref10)</sup>

## Outcomes and Efficacy

| Endpoint | Result |
|---|---|
| Objective apical cure | **90–100%**<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[2]](#ref2)[[3]](#ref3)</sup> |
| Composite surgical failure at 12 mo | 1.6–16.4%<sup>[[4]](#ref4)[[5]](#ref5)</sup> |
| Reoperation for apical prolapse | 3.7–6.1% long-term<sup>[[6]](#ref6)[[7]](#ref7)</sup> |
| Reoperation for any POP | 13.6%<sup>[[7]](#ref7)</sup> |
| Mesh exposure requiring removal | **0.4%**<sup>[[7]](#ref7)</sup> |
| Patient satisfaction ("much / very much better") | 79.6–95%<sup>[[6]](#ref6)[[7]](#ref7)[[8]](#ref8)</sup> |
| Mean POP-Q point C improvement | **−7.4 to −7.6 cm**<sup>[[3]](#ref3)[[9]](#ref9)</sup> |

## Comparative Outcomes

### vs Vaginal Hysterectomy with Apical Suspension

7-year RCT follow-up (Izett-Kay 2022):<sup>[[20]](#ref20)</sup>

- Trend toward **lower apical reoperation** (6.1% vs 17.2%, p = 0.17).
- Significantly **higher apical suspension** (point C −5 vs −4.25, p = 0.02).
- **Longer total vaginal length** (9 cm vs 6 cm, p < 0.05).

The Brennand 2025 prospective cohort (n = 321) found uterine-preserving surgery was associated with **lower composite recurrence** (adjusted RR 0.47, 95% CI 0.32–0.69) and fewer procedural complications (adjusted RR 0.19).<sup>[[5]](#ref5)</sup>

### vs Sacrospinous Hysteropexy — LAVA trial

**Non-inferior** for surgical failure at 12 months (1.6% sacrohysteropexy vs 3.3% sacrospinous hysteropexy). However, more bothersome OAB and fecal incontinence after laparoscopic sacrohysteropexy; **dyspareunia less frequent**.<sup>[[9]](#ref9)</sup>

### vs Sacrocolpopexy with Subtotal Hysterectomy

A propensity-matched study found comparable objective cure rates, but subjective success (PGI-I) was higher after sacrocolpopexy with subtotal hysterectomy (97.8% vs 81.8%, p = 0.034).<sup>[[24]](#ref24)</sup>

### Cochrane 2023

**Little to no difference** between vaginal and sacral hysteropexy in awareness of prolapse, repeat surgery, recurrent prolapse, bladder / bowel injuries, or dyspareunia. Vaginal hysteropexy operative time may be ~60 min shorter.<sup>[[19]](#ref19)</sup>

## Complications

| Complication | Rate / note |
|---|---|
| Intraoperative complications | 2.7% overall (bladder injury, conversion to laparotomy)<sup>[[22]](#ref22)</sup> |
| **Mesh exposure requiring removal** | **0.4%** — significantly lower than after sacrocolpopexy with total hysterectomy<sup>[[21]](#ref21)[[6]](#ref6)</sup> |
| Chronic pain service use | 1.8%<sup>[[21]](#ref21)</sup> |
| Small bowel obstruction | Rare; adequate peritonization is critical<sup>[[22]](#ref22)</sup> |
| Presacral hemorrhage | Rare; related to promontory dissection<sup>[[14]](#ref14)</sup> |
| **De novo SUI requiring surgery** | 14.5% in one long-term series<sup>[[17]](#ref17)</sup> |
| De novo OAB | More frequent after laparoscopic sacrohysteropexy than vaginal approaches<sup>[[9]](#ref9)</sup> |
| Newly diagnosed systemic autoimmune disorders | 5.8% in one large cross-sectional study; causality uncertain<sup>[[21]](#ref21)</sup> |

---

## Long-Term Durability

A 270-patient series with follow-up up to **14.5 years** showed surgical success gradually decreasing with time but remaining durable in most women. PFDI-20 and PFIQ-7 scores remained significantly improved across follow-up. Anal incontinence and constipation rates decreased significantly after surgery.<sup>[[17]](#ref17)</sup>

---

## Fertility and Pregnancy

A 2025 systematic review of pregnancy after laparoscopic sacrohysteropexy:<sup>[[11]](#ref11)</sup>

- All 26 reported patients delivered by **cesarean section** at mean 38 weeks.
- Only **4%** developed recurrent uterine prolapse postpartum.
- 8% developed de novo anterior compartment prolapse.
- Procedure does not appear to negatively impact fetal growth.

A meta-analysis found that sacrohysteropexy was associated with **higher obstetric adverse outcomes** vs native-tissue procedures (p = 0.04) — **native-tissue hysteropexy (sacrospinous or uterosacral) may be safer for women planning pregnancy**.<sup>[[25]](#ref25)</sup>

A simplified PVDF posterior-only technique may allow **vaginal delivery** by not restricting cervical dilation.<sup>[[15]](#ref15)</sup>

---

## Mesh-Free Alternative — Suture Hysteropexy

Laparoscopic / robotic **suture-based hysteropexy** (uterosacral ligament plication) offers a mesh-free option. A retrospective comparison found similar composite success rates between suture hysteropexy and mesh sacrohysteropexy (84.5% vs 81.7%), although mesh provided better uterine elevation (point C change −7.38 vs −6.99 cm, p < 0.05).<sup>[[26]](#ref26)</sup>

---

## Counseling Points

- Sacrohysteropexy preserves the uterus but **requires ongoing gynecologic surveillance** (cervical screening, evaluation of abnormal bleeding).<sup>[[7]](#ref7)[[1]](#ref1)</sup>
- Risk of unanticipated uterine pathology is low in appropriately screened patients.<sup>[[7]](#ref7)</sup>
- **Mesh exposure rate is very low (0.4%)**; counsel about mesh-related risks.<sup>[[21]](#ref21)</sup>
- Future pregnancy is possible but delivery is typically cesarean section; women planning pregnancy may be better served by native-tissue hysteropexy (sacrospinous or uterosacral).<sup>[[11]](#ref11)[[25]](#ref25)</sup>
- Offers **longer vaginal length and stronger apical support** than vaginal hysterectomy with suspension.<sup>[[20]](#ref20)</sup>
- Sexual function is preserved or improved; lower dyspareunia rates than some vaginal approaches.<sup>[[9]](#ref9)</sup>

---

## Key Principles

- Sacrohysteropexy is a **uterine-preserving mesh-augmented apical operation** — the abdominal-mesh analogue of sacrocolpopexy.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[2]](#ref2)</sup>
- **Lower mesh exposure** (~0.4%) than sacrocolpopexy after total hysterectomy.<sup>[[21]](#ref21)[[6]](#ref6)</sup>
- **Preserve the broad ligament's avascular plane** lateral to the uterine vessels to avoid compromising uterine blood supply.<sup>[[13]](#ref13)</sup>
- **LAVA RCT non-inferiority** vs sacrospinous hysteropexy at 12 months; better dyspareunia profile but worse OAB / fecal incontinence.<sup>[[9]](#ref9)</sup>
- **Longer vaginal length and superior apical support** vs VH+USLS at 7 years (Izett-Kay 2022).<sup>[[20]](#ref20)</sup>
- **Adequate peritonization** is critical to prevent SBO.<sup>[[2]](#ref2)[[15]](#ref15)</sup>
- For **pregnancy-desiring patients**, native-tissue hysteropexy is generally safer than mesh-augmented sacrohysteropexy.<sup>[[25]](#ref25)</sup>
- **Suture-only hysteropexy** is a viable mesh-free alternative with comparable composite success.<sup>[[26]](#ref26)</sup>
