# Mesh & Graft-Augmented Prolapse Repair

This page concerns **vaginally placed synthetic mesh and graft augmentation** for pelvic organ prolapse (POP). It is distinct from abdominal mesh used for [sacrocolpopexy](/docs/surgical-techniques/04g-prolapse-repair/sacrocolpopexy) and from mesh slings for stress urinary incontinence.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup>

## Current U.S. regulatory context

There are currently **no FDA-approved surgical-mesh products marketed in the United States for transvaginal POP repair**. On April 16, 2019, the FDA ordered the remaining manufacturers to stop selling and distributing these devices after finding that the submitted applications did not establish reasonable assurance of safety and effectiveness. This does not mean that all mesh procedures, existing implants, or SUI slings are the same category.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[2]](#ref2)</sup>

People with a prior transvaginal POP mesh implant do not need removal solely because it was placed. Routine and symptom-directed follow-up is appropriate; persistent bleeding/discharge, pelvic or groin pain, dyspareunia, or other concerning symptoms warrant assessment.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup>

## Evidence boundaries

| Question | Supportable conclusion | Important limit |
|---|---|---|
| Synthetic vaginal mesh | Compared with native-tissue repair, it can improve some anatomic outcomes, particularly in anterior-compartment studies, while adding mesh-specific adverse outcomes and further surgery for mesh exposure. | It does not establish a favorable benefit-risk profile for routine primary vaginal POP surgery in the current U.S. context. |
| Biologic grafts | Trials do not show a consistent, durable patient-centered advantage over native-tissue repair. | Different grafts, compartments, and historical studies should not be treated as interchangeable. |
| FDA 522 studies | The completed 36-month manufacturer studies reported similar effectiveness and safety outcomes to native-tissue repair. | The FDA maintains that the devices have potential additional risks and no favorable benefit-risk profile; the products are not commercially available for this use. |
| Mesh complications | Exposure/erosion, pain, dyspareunia, urinary or bowel symptoms, infection, and repeat intervention require individualized assessment. | A single rate, risk factor, or removal strategy is not a universal patient prediction or treatment rule. |

The 2024 Cochrane review is the core comparative source for transvaginal mesh, grafts, and native tissue. Its conclusions should be applied by compartment, implant, outcome definition, and follow-up duration—not as a blanket claim about every mesh or graft operation.<sup>[[3]](#ref3)</sup>

## Practical decision frame

For a new U.S. POP repair, counseling generally centers on conservative care, native-tissue vaginal repair, and abdominal/minimally invasive sacrocolpopexy when appropriate; it is not a routine choice among currently marketed transvaginal POP mesh kits. Past mesh surgery, recurrence, tissue quality, coexisting symptoms, patient goals, local expertise, and regulatory availability all matter.

For a patient with a mesh complication, define the symptom and anatomic problem before deciding on observation, local treatment, partial or complete excision, or referral. Complex pain, urinary-tract involvement, fistula, infection, or prior unsuccessful excision should be evaluated by clinicians experienced in mesh-complication care. See [Mesh Complications & Removal](/docs/surgical-techniques/04g-prolapse-repair/mesh-complications) for the dedicated management overview.

## What this page does not prescribe

It does not prescribe a graft material, mesh type, patient-risk threshold, route, cystoscopy policy, estrogen regimen, removal extent, or surveillance interval. Such choices require current product-specific information, patient assessment, local protocols, and specialist judgment.
