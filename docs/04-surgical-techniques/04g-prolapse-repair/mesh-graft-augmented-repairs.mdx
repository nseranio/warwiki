# Mesh & Graft-Augmented Prolapse Repair

**Mesh and graft-augmented vaginal repairs** reinforce a transvaginal repair of pelvic organ prolapse (POP) with synthetic mesh or a biologic graft. Transvaginal synthetic mesh improves anterior-compartment anatomic outcomes but adds mesh-specific morbidity. Biologic grafts have not shown a meaningful advantage over native-tissue repair. Since April 2019, no FDA-approved transvaginal mesh products for POP repair remain available in the United States.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[2]](#ref2)[[3]](#ref3)</sup>

[Sacrocolpopexy](/docs/surgical-techniques/04g-prolapse-repair/sacrocolpopexy), in which mesh is placed abdominally, is a separate operation, and midurethral slings for SUI were not removed from the US market by the 2019 FDA POP-mesh order.<sup>[[23]](#ref23)</sup>

---

## Current U.S. context

The FDA states that no FDA-approved mesh products are currently marketed in the United States for transvaginal POP repair. Its April 16, 2019 stop-sale order concerns this category, not abdominal sacrocolpopexy mesh or SUI mesh slings. A prior implant does not itself require removal: people without complications can continue routine care, while new bleeding, discharge, pain or dyspareunia warrants assessment.<sup>[[23]](#ref23)</sup>

The technical and trial material below remains useful for understanding previous implants, historical operations, studies and complication care; it does not imply that a transvaginal POP mesh kit is available for routine new use in the United States.

---

## Material Types

| Material | Examples | Current role |
|---|---|---|
| **Synthetic nonabsorbable mesh** | Type I macroporous monofilament polypropylene | Most studied material; improves anterior anatomic cure but adds exposure, pain, bladder injury, de novo SUI, and repeat-surgery risk.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[3]](#ref3)</sup> |
| **Synthetic absorbable mesh** | Polyglactin / Vicryl-type materials | Does not show durable benefit over native-tissue repair.<sup>[[3]](#ref3)</sup> |
| **Autologous biologic graft** | Rectus fascia | Avoids permanent synthetic material but adds donor-site morbidity and lacks strong POP benefit data. |
| **Allograft / homologous graft** | Cadaveric fascia lata | Studied historically; no consistent durable advantage over native repair.<sup>[[2]](#ref2)[[3]](#ref3)</sup> |
| **Xenograft** | Porcine dermis, porcine small intestinal submucosa | No meaningful benefit over native tissue in contemporary reviews; posterior-compartment outcomes may be worse in some trials.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[3]](#ref3)</sup> |

## Efficacy by Compartment

| Compartment | Synthetic mesh | Biologic graft | Practical conclusion |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Anterior** | Improves anatomic cure and reduces bulge recurrence compared with native-tissue repair, but subjective outcomes are often similar.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[2]](#ref2)[[4]](#ref4)[[5]](#ref5)</sup> | Minimal benefit; similar prolapse awareness and reoperation rates compared with native tissue.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[3]](#ref3)</sup> | Anatomic advantage must be weighed against mesh-specific morbidity. In the US, no approved TVM POP product remains available. |
| **Posterior** | Does not improve outcomes.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[3]](#ref3)</sup> | Does not improve outcomes and may worsen anatomic results in some trials.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[3]](#ref3)</sup> | Do not routinely use mesh or biologic grafts for posterior colporrhaphy. |
| **Apical** | Native-tissue vaginal repairs have similar prolapse awareness and reoperation compared with TVM in some analyses, while TVM adds mesh complications.<sup>[[6]](#ref6)</sup> ASPIRe met its prespecified TVM-versus-SCP noninferiority comparison for post-hysterectomy vault prolapse, without establishing equivalence or TVM superiority over native tissue.<sup>[[7]](#ref7)</sup> | No clear durable role. | [Sacrocolpopexy](/docs/surgical-techniques/04g-prolapse-repair/sacrocolpopexy) is usually the mesh-based durability benchmark; historical vaginal mesh evidence must be interpreted alongside the product and local regulatory context. |

## Trial Anchors

### PROSPECT

The PROSPECT trials randomized 1,352 women (1,348 analyzed) undergoing primary transvaginal anterior or posterior compartment prolapse surgery to standard repair alone or standard repair augmented with synthetic mesh or a biologic graft. Augmentation did not improve prolapse symptom scores or prolapse-related quality of life at 1 or 2 years. Among women who received synthetic mesh, 51 of 434 (12%) had a mesh complication within 2 years.<sup>[[5]](#ref5)</sup>

### FDA 522 study

A prospective, nonrandomized FDA-mandated 522 study comparing transvaginal mesh with native tissue repair reported 36-month composite success of 89.3% for mesh versus 80.2% for native tissue, demonstrating noninferiority but not superiority on the primary endpoint. These study endpoints did not establish a favorable FDA benefit-risk determination or restore U.S. marketing availability.<sup>[[8]](#ref8)[[24]](#ref24)</sup>

### PROSPERE

The PROSPERE multicenter trial comparing laparoscopic sacrocolpopexy with transvaginal mesh for primary cystocele showed similar success rates at 4 years, but grade III or higher complications were more common after TVM (8.7% versus 2%).<sup>[[9]](#ref9)</sup>

### ASPIRe: post-hysterectomy vault prolapse

ASPIRe randomized 376 women, of whom 360 underwent study surgery, to native-tissue repair, sacrocolpopexy or transvaginal mesh. Modeled 36-month composite failure was 43%, 28% and 29%, respectively. Sacrocolpopexy was superior to native tissue; the transvaginal-mesh comparison with native tissue did not meet the trial's adjusted superiority threshold. TVM met a prespecified noninferiority margin versus sacrocolpopexy, which is not equivalence. Most failures were anatomical rather than repeat operations.<sup>[[7]](#ref7)</sup>

This trial concerned post-hysterectomy vault prolapse, not uterine preservation, and used the now-unmarketed Uphold LITE vaginal product. The [prolapse principles page](/docs/surgical-techniques/04g-prolapse-repair/prolapse-repair-principles#post-hysterectomy-vault-prolapse-aspire) contains the corrected endpoint and analysis details.

## Mesh-Specific Complications

For the canonical complications and removal reference (IUGA / ICS classification, complete-vs-partial removal evidence, surgical-approach algorithm, ACOG Committee Opinion 694 management framework), see [Mesh Complications & Removal](/docs/surgical-techniques/04g-prolapse-repair/mesh-complications). The summary below is the indication-and-augmentation-context view.

The 2024 Cochrane review included 51 trials. Its estimates combine different products, compartments and follow-up periods; they summarize studies and do not predict outcomes for one implant or patient.<sup>[[3]](#ref3)</sup>

| Complication | Signal |
|---|---|
| **Vaginal mesh exposure** | 11.8% overall; 6.1% required surgical intervention.<sup>[[3]](#ref3)</sup> |
| **Repeat surgery** | Total repeat surgery for prolapse, SUI, or mesh exposure is higher with mesh.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup> |
| **De novo SUI** | Increased after mesh-augmented repair.<sup>[[3]](#ref3)</sup> |
| **Bladder injury** | Higher with mesh placement.<sup>[[3]](#ref3)</sup> |
| **Dyspareunia / pain** | Pain and dyspareunia are reported complications; rates depend on baseline symptoms, sexual activity, implant and follow-up.<sup>[[3]](#ref3)[[10]](#ref10)</sup> |
| **Erosion range by compartment burden** | Reported erosion rates range from 1.4-19% after anterior wall placement and 3-36% when mesh spans multiple compartments.<sup>[[2]](#ref2)</sup> |

A retrospective study examined associations between mesh erosion and patient or procedure features. These associations should not be treated as a universal causal risk score; smoking, tissue health and the precise operation belong in individualized counseling.<sup>[[11]](#ref11)</sup> Mesh complications are not purely technical; the host response to polypropylene is shaped by the vaginal hormonal and microbial environment, implant porosity, surface area, and chronic immune activation.<sup>[[12]](#ref12)</sup>

## U.S. regulatory timeline

| Year | Event |
| --- | --- |
| **2008** | FDA public-health notification about complications of urogynecologic mesh. |
| **2011** | Additional FDA safety communication about serious transvaginal POP mesh complications. |
| **2016** | Transvaginal POP mesh reclassified as Class III devices. |
| **2019** | FDA ordered the remaining transvaginal POP mesh manufacturers to stop sales and distribution. |

In an NSQIP analysis, transvaginal mesh use for apical prolapse repair fell from 35.0% to 11.0% between 2008 and 2017, with a decrease of 4.4% per quarter in the year after the 2011 safety communication.<sup>[[13]](#ref13)</sup> These events and subsequent 522-study assessments are summarized by the FDA. Regulatory availability outside the United States is jurisdiction- and product-specific.<sup>[[23]](#ref23)[[24]](#ref24)</sup>

## Technique and selection context

Native-tissue vaginal repair and abdominal or minimally invasive sacrocolpopexy are the main reconstructive alternatives discussed for new U.S. cases, alongside conservative management. Historical recommendations for selected vaginal mesh use should not be presented as current availability of a kit. Synthetic or biologic augmentation has not established a routine advantage for posterior vaginal wall repair.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[3]](#ref3)[[14]](#ref14)[[23]](#ref23)</sup>

When reviewing a previous augmented repair, record the material, operative route, compartment, fixation pattern and any concomitant surgery. Manufacturer documentation and the original operative report identify the device and its placement. Whether a particular augmentation improves outcomes enough to justify its harms is a separate question that only comparative studies answer.

## Mesh and Graft Complication Management

ACOG / AUGS guidance on mesh and graft complications emphasizes symptom-driven management and specialist referral for complex pain, erosion, urinary tract involvement, or failed prior excision.<sup>[[15]](#ref15)</sup>

| Presentation | Management frame |
|---|---|
| **Asymptomatic small exposure** | Expectant management is acceptable for monofilament macroporous mesh.<sup>[[15]](#ref15)</sup> |
| **Small symptomatic exposure** | Trial topical vaginal estrogen, local care, and observation may be reasonable before excision.<sup>[[15]](#ref15)</sup> |
| **Bleeding, discharge, partner pain, or recurrent exposure** | Office trimming may be insufficient; consider operative partial excision with counseling about recurrence and pain persistence.<sup>[[15]](#ref15)[[16]](#ref16)</sup> |
| **Pain / dyspareunia without visible exposure** | The most difficult phenotype; mesh removal may not resolve pain. In one series, complete pain resolution occurred in only about half after operative management.<sup>[[17]](#ref17)</sup> |
| **Urinary tract erosion, fistula, abscess, severe pain, or complex prior excision** | Refer to FPMRS / reconstructive specialist; evaluate with cystoscopy, imaging, and multidisciplinary pain care when indicated.<sup>[[15]](#ref15)</sup> |

For an asymptomatic person, the presence of an implant alone is not an indication for removal. For symptoms, match treatment to the identified problem and explain that removal may not resolve pain and may introduce recurrent prolapse or incontinence.<sup>[[15]](#ref15)</sup>

## Sacrocolpopexy vs. Transvaginal Mesh

Sacrocolpopexy and transvaginal mesh use different routes, dissection and fixation. Their comparative benefit-risk findings should be interpreted for the actual operations studied. A systematic review and meta-analysis found lower mesh complication rates, lower de novo dyspareunia, and similar anatomic / subjective success with sacrocolpopexy compared with TVM, on very low-quality evidence.<sup>[[18]](#ref18)</sup> The VIGI-MESH registry similarly found fewer serious complications after laparoscopic sacrocolpopexy than TVM at 1 year, with low recurrence reoperation rates.<sup>[[19]](#ref19)</sup>

Long-term data are more mixed. A Finnish nationwide cohort with mean 7.4-year follow-up found similar prolapse reoperation rates across native tissue, transvaginal mesh, and abdominal mesh, with differences in recurrence site; observational comparisons can be confounded by procedure selection.<sup>[[20]](#ref20)</sup>

---

## Long-Term Outcomes

Mesh complications do not disappear after the early postoperative window. A New York State observational cohort of 54,194 women found an association between mesh POP repair and higher 5-year reintervention risk than native tissue repair (8.8% versus 6.3%; HR 1.40).<sup>[[21]](#ref21)</sup> Long-term follow-up cohorts confirm that recurrence, reoperation, and mesh complications remain surveillance issues years after the index procedure.<sup>[[20]](#ref20)</sup>

---

## Future Directions

Biologic and biomimetic graft materials aim to reproduce the mechanical advantages of synthetic mesh while improving tissue integration and reducing chronic host response. For now, the clinical translation gap remains large: preclinical promise has not yet produced a durable, low-morbidity vaginal graft that clearly outperforms native tissue repair.<sup>[[22]](#ref22)</sup> The 2024 Cochrane review concludes that transvaginal mesh has limited utility in primary surgery and that any ongoing use should occur with ethical oversight and outcomes reported to regulatory bodies.<sup>[[3]](#ref3)</sup>
