# Perineorrhaphy

Perineorrhaphy reconstructs the perineal body, commonly by reapproximating bulbospongiosus and transverse perineal muscle or connective-tissue components to support the distal vagina and reduce the genital hiatus. The structures and suture sequence vary by surgeon and defect.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[17]](#ref17)</sup> Despite frequent use alongside pelvic reconstructive surgery, it remains "commonly performed yet poorly understood"; indications and technique are heterogeneous, and its independent effect on prolapse recurrence is uncertain.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup>

---

## Anatomy of the Perineal Body

The perineal body is a pyramidal fibromuscular structure between the vagina and rectum, serving as a central tendon for the pelvic floor.<sup>[[4]](#ref4)[[5]](#ref5)</sup> In a histologic study of postmenopausal specimens, median tissue fractions were collagen 29%, adipose cells 27%, smooth muscle 11%, elastin 7%, and skeletal muscle 3%; these are sample measurements, not fixed proportions in every patient.<sup>[[6]](#ref6)</sup>

| Level | Contacts |
|---|---|
| **Superficial** | External anal sphincter, bulbospongiosus, superficial transverse perineal |
| **Deep** | Puboperinealis, puboanalis |

DeLancey's perineal complex triad hypothesis proposes that the perineal body, perineal membrane, and levator ani muscles, linked by vaginal support tissues, work together in urogenital hiatus closure. Disruption of the midline perineal connection may permit a wider hiatus and downward rotation of the perineal membrane; this is an anatomic model, not proof that surgical narrowing prevents recurrence.<sup>[[7]](#ref7)</sup> The perineal body is a Level III support structure; obstetric injury and other defects can coexist with posterior compartment prolapse.<sup>[[8]](#ref8)[[9]](#ref9)</sup>

---

## Indications

A common reason to consider it is an enlarged genital hiatus (GH) with perineal-body deficiency, often alongside pelvic organ prolapse repair. Symptoms and the actual defect matter; GH size alone does not establish benefit.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[10]](#ref10)</sup>

A 2026 multicenter observational analysis found that surgeons more often selected perineoplasty for patients with:<sup>[[10]](#ref10)</sup>

- Larger GH (5.2 versus 4.5 cm)
- Obesity (OR 2.3)
- Higher parity (30-fold higher odds of receiving perineoplasty for ≥ 4 children versus 1 child; a surgical-selection association, not a treatment-effect estimate)

Surgeons cited lack of evidence and fear of dyspareunia as the primary reasons *not* to perform it.<sup>[[10]](#ref10)</sup>

| Specific indication | Threshold / context |
|---|---|
| Perineal body deficiency after obstetric trauma | Visible perineal scar with confirmed anatomic defect<sup>[[11]](#ref11)</sup> |
| **Enlarged genital hiatus** | One retrospective combined-repair series used ≥ 3.5 cm or wide-introitus symptoms; this is not a validated universal operative cutoff<sup>[[12]](#ref12)</sup> |
| Wide introitus with vaginal-laxity complaints | <sup>[[12]](#ref12)</sup> |
| Concomitant with [posterior colporrhaphy](/docs/surgical-techniques/04g-prolapse-repair/posterior-colporrhaphy) | Often considered when a distal perineal defect also needs repair; posterior colporrhaphy and perineorrhaphy are distinct components<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[15]](#ref15)</sup> |
| Concomitant with apical suspension | <sup>[[15]](#ref15)</sup> |

## Surgical Technique

There is significant heterogeneity in how perineorrhaphy is performed; no standardized technique exists.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup> In a survey of 183 responding surgeons at a 2014 Society of Gynecologic Surgeons meeting, 81% reported incorporating structures both proximal and distal to the hymen in their repairs.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup>

1. **Incision.** A described approach uses a diamond-shaped or triangular incision on the posterior vaginal wall at the introitus, extending onto the perineal skin; it may continue from the distal end of a posterior colporrhaphy incision<sup>[[17]](#ref17)</sup>
2. **Dissection.** Dissect the vaginal epithelium and perineal skin off the perineal muscles bilaterally to expose attenuated or separated musculature.
3. **Muscle reapproximation:**
   - **Bulbocavernosus (bulbospongiosus)** identified bilaterally and reapproximated in the midline with interrupted absorbable sutures
   - **Transverse perineal muscle or connective-tissue components** reapproximated according to the identified defect; the depth and order vary<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[17]](#ref17)</sup>
   - Sutures through the separated perineal tissues rebuild the perineal body without relying on epithelial narrowing alone<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[17]](#ref17)</sup>
4. **Rectovaginal support.** When a distal rectovaginal-septal defect is present, its attachment to the reconstructed perineal body may be restored; surgeons vary in whether and how they include this step<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[16]](#ref16)</sup>
5. **Excess tissue excision.** Trim redundant vaginal mucosa and perineal skin.
6. **Closure.** Vaginal epithelium is closed; management of perineal skin varies by technique and tissue condition rather than being a universal separate suture layer<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[17]](#ref17)</sup>

### Technical Considerations

- Suture material and caliber vary. The Kanter survey reported use of monofilament and polyfilament materials but did not establish that permanent sutures improve outcomes<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup>
- Avoid excessive narrowing of the introitus, which can cause introital stenosis and dyspareunia.
- Perineal-body repair is distinct from levator ani plication. Levator plication is a separate choice with potential effects on introital caliber and sexual pain; discuss it explicitly if considered<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[23]](#ref23)</sup>

## Outcomes

### Genital Hiatus Reduction

| Study | n | Result |
|---|---|---|
| **Mothes 2023**<sup>[[12]](#ref12)</sup> | 121 | In a retrospective cohort receiving combined native-tissue POP repair and perineorrhaphy, mean GH was **29.5% smaller** postoperatively (44 → 31 mm, p &lt; 0.001); the contribution of perineorrhaphy alone is not isolated |
| **Bonglack 2022**<sup>[[17]](#ref17)</sup> | 56 (29 with perineorrhaphy) | In a surgeon-selected prospective cohort, median resting GH at 3 mo was **0.5 cm smaller** than at the end of surgery in the perineorrhaphy group (p &lt; 0.01); baseline-to-3-mo GH change was **−1.5 vs −0.5 cm** with vs without perineorrhaphy (p &lt; 0.01), a difference not seen in the sacrocolpopexy subgroup (n = 24). Baseline Valsalva GH was larger in the perineorrhaphy group (4.5 vs 3.5 cm), and apical procedures differed between groups |

### Symptom Improvement After Perineal Reconstruction

- **Rotstein 2023** (131 operated; 119 completed symptom follow-up): standardized reconstruction for symptomatic deficient perineum after vaginal delivery, without concomitant POP or incontinence surgery; mean total symptom-score reduction −9.1 points (from 18.4 / 33, p &lt; 0.001). This uncontrolled cohort does not measure prolapse-recurrence prevention.<sup>[[11]](#ref11)</sup>

### Impact on Prolapse Recurrence — The Critical Question

The evidence on whether perineorrhaphy prevents prolapse recurrence is inconclusive:

- **Sutkin 2020** (OPTIMAL secondary analysis, n = 374): surgeon-selected concomitant posterior repair at SSLF or USLS was not associated with improved composite surgical success (66.7% versus 62.0%; aOR 1.07, 95% CI 0.56–2.07, p = 0.83). Posterior repair was not standardized and may or may not have included perineorrhaphy; this analysis cannot estimate the independent effect of perineorrhaphy.<sup>[[15]](#ref15)</sup>
- **Kikuchi 2021** narrative review: while enlarged GH is strongly associated with prolapse, there is "insufficient evidence to support surgical reduction of the GH as prophylaxis against future prolapse."<sup>[[18]](#ref18)</sup>
- **Shakhaliev 2024**: published protocol for a planned 310-patient RCT comparing mesh-augmented sacrospinal fixation with versus without perineoplasty alongside posterior repair. The [trial registry](https://clinicaltrials.gov/study/NCT05422209) lists status unknown after its July 2024 update and posts no results; the protocol is not efficacy evidence.<sup>[[19]](#ref19)</sup>

## Genital Hiatus Size and Prolapse Risk — The Rationale for Perineorrhaphy

The association between GH size and prolapse is reported in the following studies:

| Study | Finding |
|---|---|
| **Blomquist 2018**<sup>[[20]](#ref20)</sup> | In a postpartum longitudinal cohort, relative to GH ≤ 2.5 cm and stratified by delivery mode: GH 3 cm → HR **3.0** (95% CI 1.7–5.3) for incident POP; GH ≥ 3.5 cm → HR **9.0** (95% CI 5.5–14.8). This is risk prediction, not proof that surgical GH reduction changes risk |
| **Siff 2020** OPTIMAL ancillary<sup>[[13]](#ref13)</sup> | Larger immediate postoperative **resting** GH was associated with anatomic failure (aOR 1.6, 95% CI 1.1–2.3) and surgical failure (aOR 1.5, 95% CI 1.0–2.1) at 2 yr in adjusted models; ≥ 3.5 cm was an exploratory cutoff with weak discrimination, not a proven repair target |
| **DeLancey 2025**<sup>[[8]](#ref8)</sup> | 3D MRI case-control study: **resting urogenital hiatus area** was 68% larger in women with anterior-predominant prolapse versus controls. MRI area is not the POP-Q GH length or an intervention result |
| **Casas-Puig 2023**<sup>[[21]](#ref21)</sup> | After MIS sacrocolpopexy, only patients with 6-mo GH &gt; 3 cm underwent retreatment (composite recurrence NS by GH size) |

## Complications

### Postoperative Urinary Retention

**Cervantes 2026** (retrospective n = 715): patients selected for perineoplasty during native-tissue vaginal POP surgery more often failed the postoperative voiding trial and required a discharge catheter (30.2% versus 19.1%, p &lt; 0.01). This association does not establish perineoplasty as the independent cause of retention.<sup>[[22]](#ref22)</sup>

### Dyspareunia

- Reported de novo dyspareunia after prolapse surgery varies by operation and concomitant repair; posterior repair may carry greater risk, but these rates do not isolate perineorrhaphy<sup>[[23]](#ref23)</sup>
- Excessive narrowing or scarring can contribute to pain with intercourse
- Surgeons frequently cite fear of dyspareunia as a reason to omit perineoplasty<sup>[[10]](#ref10)</sup>
- Mothes 2023 found similar overall QOL improvement in sexually active and inactive subgroups after combined POP repair; that comparison cannot establish whether perineorrhaphy itself changes dyspareunia or sexual function.<sup>[[12]](#ref12)</sup>

### Other

- Wound infection or dehiscence: frequency depends on definition, setting and concomitant procedures
- Hematoma is a recognized operative risk.
- Perineal pain often improves; persistent pain warrants assessment.
- Vaginal stenosis can follow excessive tissue excision.

## Perineorrhaphy in Obstetric Perineal Repair

Reconstruction of the perineal body is also part of obstetric laceration repair when a second-degree or deeper tear disrupts it. That immediate postpartum repair is a different population and operation from elective POP perineorrhaphy. Obstetric guidance describes reapproximation of the bulbospongiosus and transverse perineal muscles after vaginal-mucosal closure. For episiotomy or second-degree tear repair, continuous suturing reduces short-term pain compared with interrupted suturing; when a suitable wound permits it, leaving the skin unsutured can reduce pain at 3 months. These findings should not be transferred to elective prolapse surgery.<sup>[[16]](#ref16)[[24]](#ref24)</sup>

## Summary of Current Evidence and Practice Patterns

| Aspect | Current evidence |
|---|---|
| **Common selection factors** | Enlarged GH, perineal-body defect and symptoms; ≥ 3.5 cm was one study's threshold, not a universal indication<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[10]](#ref10)[[12]](#ref12)</sup> |
| **GH reduction** | Observed after combined repair; one retrospective cohort reported 29.5% mean reduction and a small prospective cohort found a greater 3-month change with perineorrhaphy, without isolating long-term recurrence benefit<sup>[[12]](#ref12)[[17]](#ref17)</sup> |
| **Patient-reported symptoms** | Improved in selected uncontrolled cohorts; Mothes' 87% "cure" and 90% goal achievement are cohort responses, not comparative efficacy estimates<sup>[[11]](#ref11)[[12]](#ref12)</sup> |
| **Prolapse recurrence prevention** | Not demonstrated; the evidence is insufficient<sup>[[15]](#ref15)[[18]](#ref18)</sup> |
| **Standardized technique** | None; practice is heterogeneous<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup> |
| **RCT evidence** | A specific planned trial has a published protocol, but its registry status is unknown and no results are posted<sup>[[19]](#ref19)</sup> |
| **Safety signal** | Discharge with catheter after failed voiding trial: 30.2% vs 19.1% in one retrospective concomitant-surgery cohort<sup>[[22]](#ref22)</sup> |
| **Treatment decision** | No single GH cutoff or observational risk estimate establishes that perineorrhaphy prevents recurrent POP; match repair to the actual defect and symptoms<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[15]](#ref15)[[18]](#ref18)</sup> |

## Key Takeaways

Perineorrhaphy is widely performed, with variable indications and technique. Perineal-body anatomy provides a rationale, and enlarged GH predicts incident POP in observational cohorts; neither finding proves that surgically reducing GH prevents recurrence.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[7]](#ref7)[[20]](#ref20)</sup> Selected cohorts report a smaller postoperative GH and improved symptoms after repairs that include perineorrhaphy, but the operation's independent, long-term benefit remains uncertain.<sup>[[11]](#ref11)[[12]](#ref12)[[17]](#ref17)</sup> The OPTIMAL secondary analysis concerns adjuvant posterior repair, not a randomized perineorrhaphy comparison.<sup>[[15]](#ref15)</sup> Decisions should consider the actual perineal defect, symptoms, GH, planned concomitant surgery and patient preferences, while discussing potential urinary retention and dyspareunia without treating observational associations as causal estimates.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[10]](#ref10)[[22]](#ref22)</sup>
