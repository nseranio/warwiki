# Paravaginal Repair

**Paravaginal repair** is a surgical procedure that reattaches the detached lateral vaginal wall (pubocervical fascia) to its normal insertion on the arcus tendineus fascia pelvis (ATFP, "white line") on the pelvic sidewall, correcting anterior vaginal wall prolapse caused by lateral support defects.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[2]](#ref2)[[3]](#ref3)</sup> It can be performed via transvaginal, open abdominal, laparoscopic, or robotic approaches.

This page is the canonical [04g Prolapse Repair](/docs/surgical-techniques/04g-prolapse-repair) entry for paravaginal repair across all approaches, including the lattice / fascial-patch variant. For the central-defect repair, see [Anterior Colporrhaphy](/docs/surgical-techniques/04g-prolapse-repair/anterior-colporrhaphy).

---

## Historical Background and Anatomic Rationale

The concept of the paravaginal defect was popularized by George White (1909) and refined by A. Cullen Richardson (1976, 1981), who proposed that some cystourethroceles reflect discrete breaks in the endopelvic fascia rather than generalized tissue attenuation.<sup>[[3]](#ref3)[[4]](#ref4)</sup> In Richardson's historical series of 233 procedures for stress urinary incontinence associated with cystourethrocele, a paravaginal break was the most frequently encountered defect; the authors reported &gt; 95% functionally satisfactory results at 2–8 yr follow-up. This is not a contemporary comparative prolapse-success estimate.<sup>[[4]](#ref4)</sup>

Reported paravaginal-defect prevalence depends on the population and diagnostic method; two clinical-versus-operative comparison studies found defects at surgery in up to 47% of women with anterior prolapse, not a universal 60–80% rate.<sup>[[6]](#ref6)</sup> The concept is grounded in DeLancey's Level II vaginal support: the lateral attachment of the mid-vagina to the ATFP and underlying levator ani muscles. Apical and muscular support also matter, so a lateral detachment is not a complete explanation of every cystocele.<sup>[[6]](#ref6)[[7]](#ref7)</sup>

---

## Anatomy of the Paravaginal Defect

Support of the anterior vaginal wall involves the levator ani, ATFP, pubocervical fascia, and uterosacral and cardinal ligaments.<sup>[[6]](#ref6)</sup> A paravaginal defect describes loss of lateral vaginal support near the ATFP. In a 30-case/30-control stress-MRI study, apical location, paravaginal location and hiatus size formed a highly correlated "collinear triad" explaining up to 83% of variation in cystocele size within that model; this is not a population prevalence or proof that any one component alone causes prolapse.<sup>[[7]](#ref7)</sup>

*Conceptual lateral-support schematic in axial view. It is not a complete model of anterior prolapse. **(1)** A lateral attachment defect allows sulcal descent; **(2)** the illustrated sutures reattach lateral vaginal support toward the **arcus tendineus fascia pelvis** ("white line"). Suture number and concomitant midline/apical repair vary by technique and patient. Contrast [anterior colporrhaphy](/docs/surgical-techniques/04g-prolapse-repair/anterior-colporrhaphy). (Original WARWIKI schematic)*

A classification system describes paravaginal defects as:<sup>[[8]](#ref8)</sup>

| Defect type | Description |
|---|---|
| **Fascial defects** | Partial or complete tears in the paravaginal visceral pelvic fascia |
| **Muscular defects** | Levator ani tears of varying location |
| **Combined fascial + muscular** | Both lesions in the same patient |

All of these can produce the same clinical finding: descent of the lateral vaginal sulcus.<sup>[[8]](#ref8)</sup>

## Diagnosis

Diagnosing paravaginal defects remains challenging:<sup>[[6]](#ref6)[[9]](#ref9)</sup>

| Modality | Notes |
|---|---|
| **Physical examination** | Commonly used; operative-comparison studies report inconsistent sensitivity and specificity. Loss of lateral sulci or reduction with lateral support can suggest a defect, but neither proves a discrete fascial tear. |
| **MRI** | Displaced lateral vaginal walls; loss of normal vaginal "H" configuration; "saddlebags" (Level II) and "mustache" (Level III) signs. Missing-H may reflect pubococcygeus defects rather than true fascial paravaginal defects.<sup>[[10]](#ref10)[[11]](#ref11)</sup> |
| **Ultrasound** | Translabial and other approaches can depict support relationships, but criteria and specificity for a discrete fascial defect remain unsettled. Older transabdominal bladder-base signs alone are unreliable.<sup>[[6]](#ref6)[[9]](#ref9)</sup> |
| **Intraoperative assessment** | Direct inspection of the lateral attachment is possible when the retropubic space is exposed; it is an anatomic reference in operative-comparison studies, not a routine diagnostic test for every patient.<sup>[[6]](#ref6)[[12]](#ref12)</sup> |

## Surgical Technique

### Transvaginal Approach<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[2]](#ref2)[[13]](#ref13)</sup>

1. Midline anterior vaginal wall incision
2. Sharp dissection of the vagina from bladder fascia continues laterally until the pelvic sidewall is identified
3. The retropubic space is entered under the inferior pubic ramus
4. The ATFP (white line) is identified on the pelvic sidewall
5. Permanent or delayed-absorbable sutures are placed from the lateral vaginal wall or pubocervical fascia to the ATFP on each side (1–6 Gore-Tex CV-0 sutures per side in the Young series). When the white line is detached from the sidewall or does not seem durable, the obturator-internus fascia is a described alternative anchor; one illustrated technique places three to six held sutures along the white line from near the ischial spine toward the urethrovesical junction, then matches them with sutures through the lateral vaginal muscularis and endopelvic tissue, creating a three-point closure while preserving tissue for the vaginal closure and avoiding a suture bridge.<sup>[[30]](#ref30)</sup>
6. Tying these sutures elevates the lateral vaginal sulci
7. A midline plication ([standard anterior colporrhaphy](/docs/surgical-techniques/04g-prolapse-repair/anterior-colporrhaphy)) is typically performed concurrently
8. Excess vaginal skin is trimmed and the vaginal wall is closed

### Open Abdominal Approach<sup>[[5]](#ref5)[[14]](#ref14)</sup>

1. Pfannenstiel or low midline incision
2. The retropubic space (space of Retzius) is entered
3. The ATFP is identified bilaterally
4. Sutures are placed from the lateral vaginal wall to the ATFP. One described open technique takes a full-thickness vaginal bite, excluding epithelium, into the ATFP about 3–4 cm below the obturator fossa for the first suture, then places two to four further sutures at roughly 1-cm intervals toward the pubic bone, beginning at the ischial spine.<sup>[[31]](#ref31)</sup>
5. Often combined with [Burch colposuspension](/docs/surgical-techniques/04f-incontinence-procedures/procedures/burch-colposuspension) or [sacrocolpopexy](/docs/surgical-techniques/04g-prolapse-repair/sacrocolpopexy). When both are planned, one author's preference is to complete the paravaginal repair first, because exposure of the lateral defects decreases once the Burch sutures are tied.<sup>[[31]](#ref31)</sup>

For bleeding encountered while placing a retropubic paravaginal suture, one described bailout is a figure-of-eight stitch placed in the vagina, with its attached needle then passed into the pelvic sidewall to obtain hemostasis; this is an author-described maneuver without linked comparative outcome evidence.<sup>[[32]](#ref32)</sup>

### Laparoscopic / Robotic Approach<sup>[[5]](#ref5)[[15]](#ref15)[[16]](#ref16)[[17]](#ref17)</sup>

1. Transperitoneal access to the space of Retzius
2. Identification of the ATFP and paravaginal defects under magnified visualization
3. Suture placement from lateral vagina to ATFP (an average of 6 sutures for the paravaginal repair in Miklos and Kohli's series)
4. Often combined with sacrocolpopexy, Burch colposuspension, or both
5. In Miklos and Kohli's retropubic laparoscopic technique, cystoscopy checks lower urinary tract integrity after suturing.<sup>[[16]](#ref16)</sup>

One described unit variant anchors laterally to Cooper's ligament rather than the ATFP: after filling the bladder with 200–300 mL saline to define its dome and entering the Retzius space 2–4 cm cephalad to it, a double bite through the pubocervical fibromuscular tissue at the urethrovesical junction is anchored to the ipsilateral Cooper's ligament, usually with two to four sutures per side placed alternately to preserve symmetry, limiting tension so about 2 cm remains between fascia and ligament. Excessive tension is described as a possible contributor to postoperative voiding difficulty. The same unit routinely places bilateral ureteric catheters before peritoneal entry for visualization and injury recognition, while explicitly stating this step is their own practice rather than literature-supported. Attribute this construct and its measurements to this unit's described technique, not a validated or superior configuration; it is distinct from a Burch colposuspension.<sup>[[33]](#ref33)[[34]](#ref34)</sup>

### Variations

| Variation | Description | Reference |
|---|---|---|
| **Trans-obturator native-tissue paravaginal repair** | Avoids complex retropubic dissection | <sup>[[18]](#ref18)</sup> |
| **Modified cross-stitch paravaginal repair (Huang 2022)** | Reverse bridge repair + cross-stitching of bilateral sutures; reported success was 93/98 (94.9%) at 12 mo, 61/71 (91.0%) at 24 mo, and 16/21 (76.2%) at 48 mo. The later percentages reflect substantial follow-up attrition. | <sup>[[19]](#ref19)</sup> |
| **Ischial periosteum / obturator membrane fixation (Scotti 1998)** | Anchor distal to ATFP for greater suspension | <sup>[[20]](#ref20)</sup> |
| **Paravaginal lattice technique (Rude 2021)** | See the dedicated subsection below. Hybrid native-tissue repair intertwining midline colporrhaphy sutures with lateral ATFP / obturator-fascia sutures into a lattice scaffold | <sup>[[29]](#ref29)</sup> |
| **Mesh-augmented transvaginal paravaginal repair with distal urethral sling (Rodríguez 2005)** | Every patient first had a distal urethral polypropylene sling. After central-defect repair, a 5 × 5 cm soft polypropylene mesh was fixed proximally to the sacrouterine/cardinal ligament complex, distally to the bladder neck and laterally to the infralevator obturator fascia. In 98 women with grade III–IV cystocele (mean age 65 years; 94% also had rectocele repair, 45% enterocele repair and 26% vaginal hysterectomy), postoperative anterior-wall POP-Q stage was 0–I in 85%, II in 13% and III in 2%; no patient had urinary retention, 3 had de novo SUI and 2 de novo urgency incontinence. These are early results, and the concomitant sling and other repairs prevent attributing outcomes to the paravaginal step alone. | <sup>[[36]](#ref36)</sup> |

## Paravaginal Lattice Technique (Rude 2021)

The **paravaginal lattice technique** is a native-tissue technique described by Rude et al. (2021) in the period after transvaginal mesh withdrawal. It combines midline anterior colporrhaphy with lateral paravaginal sutures intertwined into a lattice-work configuration and is designed to address central and lateral defects through a single transvaginal incision without mesh.<sup>[[29]](#ref29)</sup>

### Concept

The technique attempts to create a suture-based scaffold that mimics the broad support previously provided by mesh, using native-tissue fixation points rather than synthetic material. The distinguishing step is intertwining the midline plication sutures with the lateral ATFP or obturator-fascia sutures so the two sets of sutures form a unified support structure rather than independent repairs.

### Step-by-Step

1. Standard midline anterior vaginal wall incision and dissection
2. **Lateral sutures.** 2-0 PDS placed at the level of the obturator fascia or ATFP: one distally and one proximally on each side (4 lateral sutures total)
3. **Midline sutures.** Standard midline anterior colporrhaphy with 4 separate 2-0 PDS sutures through the pubocervical fascia
4. **Lattice formation.** Midline colporrhaphy sutures are intertwined with the lateral obturator or ATFP sutures and tied, creating a cross-hatch pattern
5. The lattice configuration elevates the central repair laterally while anchoring the construct to the pelvic sidewall

### Outcomes (Rude 2021 prospective cohort, n = 109, mean follow-up 12 mo)<sup>[[29]](#ref29)</sup>

| Outcome | Result |
|---|---|
| Anatomic recurrence (POP-Q Ba ≥ −1) | 11% (12 / 109) |
| Mean time to recurrence | 13.9 mo |
| Intraoperative complications | 0% |
| **Transient urinary retention** | 19% (all self-resolved with conservative management) |
| De novo SUI | 4% |
| Recurrence association with concomitant apical / posterior repair | None |

### Comparison with Adjacent Techniques

| Feature | Lattice (Rude 2021) | Standard AC | Standard paravaginal | Modified cross-stitch (Huang 2022) |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Defects addressed** | Central + lateral | Central only | Lateral only | Lateral with cross-stitch reinforcement |
| **Suture material** | 2-0 PDS (absorbable) | Variable | Permanent or delayed-absorbable | Not specified |
| **Lateral fixation** | Obturator fascia / ATFP | None | ATFP | ATFP with cross-stitching |
| **Outcome evidence** | Single-surgeon cohort, 109 patients; 11% met POP-Q Ba ≥ −1 recurrence, mean follow-up 12 mo; 19% transient retention | Native-tissue evidence spans different populations and definitions; see [anterior colporrhaphy](/docs/surgical-techniques/04g-prolapse-repair/anterior-colporrhaphy) | Isolated and combined repairs are both reported; central recurrence may remain after lateral-only repair | Retrospective series with shrinking denominators: 93/98 at 12 mo, 61/71 at 24 mo, 16/21 at 48 mo |

### Caveats

- **Single-surgeon, single-center prospective cohort.** Generalizability is uncertain
- **Short follow-up (mean 12 mo).** Native-tissue repairs characteristically decline over time; long-term durability is unknown
- **Absorbable PDS.** Loses tensile strength by approximately 6 mo, raising the question of whether the lattice scaffold persists long enough for tissue remodeling
- 19% transient urinary retention occurred in this cohort; a causal comparison with standard anterior colporrhaphy cannot be made from different series
- No randomized comparison with AC, paravaginal, or mesh-augmented repair

The Rude lattice technique has been described in one short-term prospective series. Its 11% POP-Q Ba recurrence and 19% transient retention should not be compared directly with mesh, standard anterior colporrhaphy, or other paravaginal cohorts that used different patients, endpoints, and follow-up intervals. Long-term durability and comparative benefit remain unproven.

| Approach | Success rate (prolapse) | SUI cure rate | Key advantages | Key disadvantages |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Transvaginal** | 76–98% across short-term series; 16/21 (76.2%) evaluable at 4 yr in Huang's modified-technique series | 72% no-pad/no-leakage cure at mean 17 mo among 25 women without a sling (other prolapse defects could be repaired concurrently); 84% counted cure or improvement to at most one pad/day<sup>[[26]](#ref26)</sup> | Single incision; no abdominal entry | Technically demanding; hemorrhage risk; limited visualization<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[2]](#ref2)[[3]](#ref3)[[19]](#ref19)</sup> |

## Paravaginal Repair vs. Anterior Colporrhaphy

The two procedures address different anatomic defects: lateral (paravaginal) versus central (midline plication).<sup>[[3]](#ref3)</sup>

| Trial | n | Outcome |
|---|---|---|
| **Weber & Walters 1997** review<sup>[[3]](#ref3)</sup> | — | Reported failure 0–20% for AC and 3–14% for paravaginal repair in case series; no controlled comparison available at the time |
| **Menefee 2011 RCT**<sup>[[23]](#ref23)</sup> | 99 randomized; 78 completed ≥2 yr | 2-yr anatomic failure: AC 58% / porcine-graft paravaginal 46% / polypropylene-mesh paravaginal 18% (p = 0.002 mesh vs AC). Composite (bulge symptoms and anatomic failure) was 13% / 12% / 4%, without a significant between-group difference; mesh erosion was 14%. The polypropylene transvaginal POP mesh arm is historical, not a currently marketed US option. |
| **Minassian 2014 RCT**<sup>[[14]](#ref14)</sup> | 70 | 2-yr objective failure: vaginal AC + absorbable polyglactin mesh 32% vs abdominal paravaginal repair 40% (p = 0.56); satisfaction 88% vs 73% (p = 0.11). This does not compare unaugmented AC with paravaginal repair, and concurrent hysterectomy differed (71% vs 42%). |
| **Cai 2025 RCT**<sup>[[24]](#ref24)</sup> | 102 | In women aged 40–70 with symptomatic SUI and stage 2–3 anterior prolapse, combined paravaginal + pubocervical fascia plication had higher 12-mo patient-reported improvement (94.1% vs 78.4%), objective SUI cure (90.2% vs 74.5%), and anatomic success (92.2% vs 80.4%) than standard AC. The selected combined SUI/POP population limits generalization. |

## Vaginal vs. Abdominal Approach

A prospective comparative study of 45 patients found no significant difference in subjective and objective outcomes between abdominal and vaginal paravaginal repair at 12 months. The laparoscopic arm was abandoned after 5 patients due to inferior outcomes.<sup>[[5]](#ref5)</sup> In the 2016 Cochrane review, one RCT found no clear difference in POP-Q point Ba between vaginal and abdominal repair (n = 50); total vaginal length was 3.20 cm longer after vaginal repair (n = 68; 95% CI 2.58–3.82).<sup>[[25]](#ref25)</sup>

## Effect on Stress Urinary Incontinence

Paravaginal repair has a secondary continence effect by restoring bladder-neck support, but it is inferior to Burch colposuspension as a primary anti-incontinence procedure:<sup>[[21]](#ref21)[[26]](#ref26)</sup>

| Trial | Finding |
|---|---|
| **Colombo 1996 RCT** (n = 36)<sup>[[21]](#ref21)</sup> | Burch 100% vs paravaginal 61% objective cure (p = 0.004); negative cotton-swab 100% vs 33% (p = 0.01). Authors concluded paravaginal repair is not recommended for SUI. |
| **Bruce 1999** (n = 52)<sup>[[26]](#ref26)</sup> | SUI cure 72% with paravaginal alone; 85% when combined with rectus muscle sling for ISD |
| **Scotti 1998** (n = 40)<sup>[[20]](#ref20)</sup> | 94.4% SUI cure with paravaginal repair using fixation to ischial periosteum and obturator membrane |

## Complications

Complications vary by approach and concomitant surgery; the event counts below belong to their named series and should not be treated as a comparative risk estimate against anterior colporrhaphy.<sup>[[12]](#ref12)[[13]](#ref13)[[17]](#ref17)</sup>

| Complication | Rate |
|---|---|
| **Hemorrhage** (most significant risk; predominantly with vaginal approach) | Young et al. — 3 major intraoperative hemorrhagic events in 100 vaginal paravaginal repairs; one converted to AC<sup>[[13]](#ref13)</sup> |
| Transfusion — vaginal approach | 16% in the same 100-woman Young et al. series, which included concomitant procedures; the authors suggested their low transfusion threshold may partly explain the rate<sup>[[13]](#ref13)</sup> |
| Bladder injury / cystotomy | 4/171 (2.3%) cystotomies in a mixed laparoscopic Burch/paravaginal cohort (130 combined, 23 Burch alone, 18 paravaginal alone); this is not a paravaginal-only risk estimate<sup>[[16]](#ref16)</sup> |
| Voiding dysfunction (transient retention) | Common; prolonged retention beyond 7 days requiring catheterization was the most common minor complication (10.4%) in the same 212-woman laparoscopic cohort below<sup>[[17]](#ref17)</sup> |
| Lower-extremity neuropathy | 2% (long-term) in one series<sup>[[13]](#ref13)</sup> |
| **Recurrent midline (central) cystocele** after isolated lateral repair | 22% in one series<sup>[[13]](#ref13)</sup> |
| Recurrent prolapse including grade III enterocele after vaginal paravaginal repair | 5/100 in Young et al.<sup>[[13]](#ref13)</sup> |
| Major complications — laparoscopic approach | 4.2% (9 / 212)<sup>[[17]](#ref17)</sup> |

## Recurrence and Risk Factors

Recurrence and objective failure vary by approach, definition and follow-up duration:

| Time horizon | Recurrence |
|---|---|
| Short-term (1 yr) | 2–5% lateral defect recurrence<sup>[[12]](#ref12)[[13]](#ref13)</sup> |
| At 2 yr | Minassian's abdominal paravaginal RCT arm had 40% objective stage-≥II failure; Huang's modified-technique series reported 61/71 (91.0%) success among those evaluated at 24 mo. These are not equivalent endpoints.<sup>[[14]](#ref14)[[19]](#ref19)</sup> |
| At 4 yr | Huang reported 16/21 (76.2%) success among the much smaller subset evaluated at 48 mo; this is not a 24% cumulative recurrence estimate for all 98 patients.<sup>[[19]](#ref19)</sup> |

**Long-term laparoscopic follow-up.** In a separate prospective cohort of 223 women who underwent laparoscopic paravaginal repair with treatment of associated conditions, median follow-up was 5.2 years (range 1–12; 63% followed at least 5 years). By that follow-up, 79% had developed prolapse of at least POP-Q stage 2 in one or more compartments and 58% had become symptomatic again; 48% underwent further prolapse surgery of any kind, 30% eventually had a further cystocele repair, and 24% had anterior prolapse recurrence beyond the hymen. The authors concluded that cystoceles are difficult to repair successfully over the long term. This is a single-center cohort without a comparison arm, and its recurrence definitions differ from the other series on this page.<sup>[[35]](#ref35)</sup>

**MRI-associated recurrence findings.** In Arenholt's prospective cohort, 46 women completed 6-month follow-up, 20 after paravaginal repair alone and 26 with concomitant surgery. The 39% recurrence rate was for the full cohort, not for the subgroup with pubococcygeus defects. Recurrence was more frequent among women with major pubococcygeus defects and a missing vaginal H configuration; these findings do not establish that the MRI sign diagnoses a fascial paravaginal tear.<sup>[[11]](#ref11)</sup>

Concomitant apical support should also be assessed; anterior descent often reflects more than one support defect.<sup>[[6]](#ref6)[[7]](#ref7)</sup>

## Concomitant Procedures

Paravaginal repair is frequently combined with other procedures:<sup>[[13]](#ref13)[[15]](#ref15)[[16]](#ref16)</sup>

- **[Sacrocolpopexy](/docs/surgical-techniques/04g-prolapse-repair/sacrocolpopexy).** In an uncontrolled series of 43 women, robotic paravaginal repair with sacrocolpopexy improved POP-Q Ba (from 2.1 to −2.0) and C (from 0.3 to −4.3); PGI-I scores of 1–3 were reported by 81.4% at 12 mo<sup>[[15]](#ref15)</sup>
- **[Burch colposuspension](/docs/surgical-techniques/04f-incontinence-procedures/procedures/burch-colposuspension).** Commonly combined when SUI coexists<sup>[[16]](#ref16)</sup>
- **Posterior colporrhaphy and enterocele repair.** Multicompartment prolapse is common
- **Hysterectomy.** When uterine prolapse coexists

## Current Role and Guideline Positioning

Paravaginal repair occupies a niche role in the surgical management of anterior vaginal wall prolapse:<sup>[[27]](#ref27)[[28]](#ref28)</sup>

- **Rationale.** The operation addresses the specific anatomic defect (lateral detachment) rather than midline plication
- Reliable preoperative diagnosis of isolated paravaginal defects remains difficult, limiting targeted application<sup>[[6]](#ref6)[[9]](#ref9)</sup>
- Most surgeons perform standard anterior colporrhaphy (which addresses central defects) as the primary transvaginal native-tissue repair, sometimes incorporating lateral sutures as an "ultralateral" modification
- Paravaginal repair may be performed abdominally or robotically with sacrocolpopexy when lateral defects are identified; vaginal native-tissue approaches are also described. In a 160-woman randomized trial of modified paravaginal repair plus sacrospinous suspension versus laparoscopic sacrocolpopexy, 12-month composite success (no bothersome bulge, no prolapse beyond the hymen, and no additional prolapse treatment) was 73/75 versus 75/76 among completers; neuromuscular pain occurred in 13 versus 3 women (p = 0.008). These findings apply to the combined operation.<sup>[[37]](#ref37)</sup>
- ACOG and AUGS describe paravaginal defects as lateral detachments whose diagnosis by examination is unreliable; when a paravaginal defect is suspected there is usually loss of apical support, and apical procedures may address most anterior defects, including paravaginal ones<sup>[[27]](#ref27)</sup>

## Summary — Paravaginal Repair vs. Anterior Colporrhaphy

| Feature | Paravaginal repair | Anterior colporrhaphy |
|---|---|---|
| **Defect addressed** | Lateral (Level II) | Central (midline) |
| **Anatomic goal** | Reattach vagina to ATFP | Plicate midline fascia |
| **Outcome interpretation** | Isolated and combined approaches differ; use the source-specific cohorts and endpoints above | Native-tissue, graft-augmented, and concomitant repairs must be distinguished; see [anterior colporrhaphy](/docs/surgical-techniques/04g-prolapse-repair/anterior-colporrhaphy) |
| **SUI effect** | Not a substitute for a dedicated continence operation; selected historical SUI cohorts are described above | Neither a standard anterior repair nor a Kelly plication is an established stand-alone SUI treatment |
| **Key complication** | Hemorrhage; central recurrence | Recurrence; de novo SUI |
| **Diagnostic challenge** | Difficult to confirm preoperatively | Not defect-specific |
