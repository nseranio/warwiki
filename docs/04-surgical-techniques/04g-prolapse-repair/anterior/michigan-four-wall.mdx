# Michigan Four-Wall Sacrospinous Ligament Suspension

The **Michigan Four-Wall Sacrospinous Ligament Suspension** is a University of Michigan modification of sacrospinous ligament fixation (SSLF) developed by John O.L. DeLancey and colleagues. Through an apical incision, it attaches the anterior, posterior and two lateral walls of the vaginal apex directly to the sacrospinous ligament; a standard posterior-incision SSLF usually attaches the upper posterior vaginal wall. It was described for post-hysterectomy vaginal vault prolapse. Other compartment repairs may be added when indicated, but they are not the defining four-wall maneuver.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[2]](#ref2)[[3]](#ref3)</sup>

For the underlying procedure, see [Sacrospinous Ligament Fixation](/docs/surgical-techniques/04g-prolapse-repair/sacrospinous-ligament-fixation). For comparative apical-suspension framing, see [Sacrocolpopexy](/docs/surgical-techniques/04g-prolapse-repair/sacrocolpopexy) and [Uterosacral Ligament Suspension](/docs/surgical-techniques/04g-prolapse-repair/uterosacral-ligament-suspension).

---

## Conceptual Foundation

The Michigan modification treats apical support by selecting attachment points on each wall of the vaginal apex rather than assuming the hysterectomy scar is the best new apex. Coexisting anterior or posterior defects may need separate repair. The four-wall name does not mean that every patient undergoes complete anterior, posterior and paravaginal repairs or that this approach has demonstrated lower recurrence than standard SSLF.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[2]](#ref2)[[3]](#ref3)</sup>

---

## Surgical Technique

The published Michigan description uses the following sequence; it is technique reporting, not a universal operative prescription:<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[2]](#ref2)[[3]](#ref3)</sup>

1. **Apical incision.** Identify and excise redundant vaginal apex when present, selecting where each wall can reach the ligament without excess tension. The new suspension point is often not the hysterectomy scar:
   - Apex was at the hysterectomy scar in only 9% of Kearney & DeLancey cases
   - Posterior to the scar in 76%
   - Anterior to it in 14%<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup>
2. **Pararectal dissection.** Approach the sacrospinous ligament through the apical incision. In the Larson report, a Deschamps carrier and looped delayed-absorbable PDS created four ligament suture strands; this describes that series, not a fixed suture rule.<sup>[[2]](#ref2)</sup>
3. **Repair other defects as indicated.** Perform [anterior colporrhaphy](/docs/surgical-techniques/04g-prolapse-repair/anterior-colporrhaphy), [paravaginal repair](/docs/surgical-techniques/04g-prolapse-repair/paravaginal-repair), posterior repair or perineorrhaphy when separately indicated; none is automatically part of the four-wall fixation.<sup>[[2]](#ref2)[[3]](#ref3)</sup>
4. **Four-wall apical attachment and closure.** Bring the ligament sutures through the apical incision and tie them to elevate the newly configured vaginal apex, incorporating the four apical walls.<sup>[[2]](#ref2)</sup>

In the 76-patient Kearney & DeLancey series, mean redundant vaginal length excised was 4.6 ± 2.5 cm, but seven women had no excision; mean measured vaginal length changed from 9.7 to 9.4 cm. These descriptive findings do not require excision in every case.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup>

## Outcomes

### Long-Term Patient Satisfaction (Larson 2013)<sup>[[2]](#ref2)</sup>

The study identified 453 operated patients, 392 eligible for contact and 242/392 (62%) satisfaction responders at a mean 8.0 ± 1.7 years. Anatomy was available for 162/242 responders from their most recent clinic or study examination (mean 4.3 years), not a uniform eight-year examination:

| Outcome | Result |
|---|---|
| **Satisfied with surgical outcome** | **90% of the 242 responders**, not 90% of all 453 operated patients |
| Highly satisfied ("completely" or "very") | 76% |
| Moderately satisfied | 14% |
| **Vaginal support at or above the hymen** (most recent exam available for 162/242 responders) | **86% at mixed follow-up**; in the separate 70-person study examination at mean 8 years, **57/70 (81%)** had this degree of support |
| Patients with prior failed prolapse surgery in cohort | 57% |
| Patients with preop prolapse ≥ 4 cm beyond hymen | 56% |

Factors associated with being highly satisfied:

- Lower postoperative PFDI-20 scores
- **Preoperative Baden-Walker grade 3 or 4** prolapse (paradoxically, more severe preop prolapse was more likely to be highly satisfied than grade 2)
- Postoperative support at the hymen (grade 2) did not negatively affect satisfaction compared with grade 0–1 support

### Suspension-Point Selection (Kearney & DeLancey 2003, n = 76)<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup>

- New vaginal apex was posterior to the hysterectomy scar in 76% of cases
- Mean vaginal length preserved (9.7 to 9.4 cm, a 0.3 cm difference)
- Excess vagina excised in 91% of patients without compromising vaginal length

## Comparison with Standard SSLF

| Feature | Michigan Four-Wall SSLF | Standard SSLF |
|---|---|---|
| **Apical walls attached** | Anterior, posterior and two lateral walls of the vaginal apex | Upper posterior vaginal wall in the conventional description |
| **Apex selection** | Four apical-wall attachment points may differ from the hysterectomy scar | Posterior vaginal-wall attachment in the conventional description |
| **Excess vagina excision** | When redundant; mean 4.6 cm in one 76-patient series, with none in seven women<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup> | Not the defining maneuver |
| **Other compartment repair** | Added when indicated, not intrinsic to four-wall fixation | Also added when indicated |
| **Comparative effectiveness** | No direct matched or randomized comparison with standard SSLF | Historical series use different populations, endpoints and follow-up; their percentages cannot rank the techniques |

---

## Advantages

- **Four-wall apical support.** Directly attaches all walls of the apex; separate compartment repairs can be combined when indicated. Lower recurrence than standard SSLF has not been shown.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[2]](#ref2)</sup>
- **Individualized apex selection.** The surgeon identifies the optimal suspension point rather than assuming the hysterectomy scar is the correct apex<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup>
- **Excess-vagina management.** Selective excision in the original series did not substantially shorten mean measured vaginal length; individual outcomes vary.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup>
- **High reported long-term satisfaction.** 90% among 242 responders at mean 8 years, with potential nonresponse bias.<sup>[[2]](#ref2)</sup>
- **Native-tissue, no mesh.** Avoids implant-specific mesh events, though not the ordinary risks of prolapse surgery.<sup>[[2]](#ref2)</sup>
- **Anterior assessment.** An anterior repair may be combined when indicated; the four-wall attachment alone is not proven to prevent anterior recurrence relative to standard SSLF.<sup>[[2]](#ref2)[[4]](#ref4)</sup>

---

## Limitations and Considerations

- **No direct comparative RCT identified in the cited sources** for Michigan four-wall versus standard SSLF, USLS or sacrocolpopexy; the 90% figure is from responder follow-up, not a head-to-head effect.<sup>[[2]](#ref2)</sup>
- **Single-center experience.** The published data come primarily from the University of Michigan<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[2]](#ref2)</sup>
- **Incomplete or anatomically mixed follow-up.** Larson et al. had postoperative anatomic data for 162/242 responders, mostly from a prior clinic visit; only 70 returned for an approximately eight-year study examination.<sup>[[2]](#ref2)</sup>
- **Different-source reoperation context.** A 2026 Danish observational cohort reported more reoperation after its SSLF group than sacrocolpopexy, but did not isolate Michigan four-wall and cannot provide its comparative effect; the prior [SSLF review](/docs/surgical-techniques/04g-prolapse-repair/sacrospinous-ligament-fixation) discusses broader options.<sup>[[5]](#ref5)</sup>
- **Posterior vaginal axis.** Axis change after sacrospinous suspension is a technical consideration, but this page's cited studies do not establish that the Michigan modification causes anterior recurrence.<sup>[[4]](#ref4)[[6]](#ref6)</sup>

---

## Context Within Apical Suspension Options

The OPTIMAL trial (Jelovsek 2018, JAMA) compared standard SSLF with [uterosacral ligament suspension](/docs/surgical-techniques/04g-prolapse-repair/uterosacral-ligament-suspension) and found no significant difference at 2 or 5 years, with approximately two-thirds meeting failure criteria by 5 years using strict composite definitions.<sup>[[7]](#ref7)</sup> Sustained symptom improvement was observed despite anatomic deterioration, suggesting that surgical counseling should convey higher long-term failure rates while acknowledging sustained QOL improvement.

The ASPIRe trial (Menefee 2024) compared a mixed native-tissue arm (mostly conventional SSLF), transvaginal mesh and [sacrocolpopexy](/docs/surgical-techniques/04g-prolapse-repair/sacrocolpopexy) after total hysterectomy. Modeled 36-month composite failure was 43%, 29% and 28%, respectively; sacrocolpopexy was superior to native-tissue repair at its prespecified threshold (aHR 0.57, 99% CI 0.33–0.98), while the mesh-versus-native comparison did not meet that threshold. This trial did not test Michigan four-wall fixation or provide a universal 60-month failure rate for it.<sup>[[8]](#ref8)</sup>

---

## Summary

Michigan four-wall fixation is a specific apical attachment modification, not automatic repair of all vaginal compartments. The original cohorts show that the new apex often differs from the hysterectomy scar and report 90% satisfaction among 242/392 eligible responders at a mean eight years; only 70 had an examination at that interval. Broader SSLF and ASPIRe comparisons inform counseling but cannot establish this modification's superiority or its individual long-term recurrence risk.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[2]](#ref2)[[5]](#ref5)[[8]](#ref8)</sup>
