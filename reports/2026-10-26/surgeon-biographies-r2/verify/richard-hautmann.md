## Biography

Richard Hautmann turned to medicine after a weightlifting injury ended his plans as a competitive athlete and led to a long hospital stay.[^1] His subsequent research included measurements of calcium and oxalate in renal tissue.[^1] While preparing a 1985 lecture on urinary diversion, he concluded that early successes had often been reported while adverse long-term outcomes received less attention.[^1]

At Ulm, Hautmann and colleagues developed an ileal neobladder designed as a compliant, low-pressure reservoir.[^1] He credits a team of 22 associates with its development. Kurt Miller and Jürgen Gschwend helped extend the operation to women, and Klaus Kleinschmidt proposed a freely refluxing ureteral connection.[^1] The group studied metabolic effects and maintained a database of more than 1,500 neobladders for long-term follow-up.[^1]

Surgeons visited Ulm to observe the operation, and annual seminars broadcast live surgery from its operating rooms.[^1]
