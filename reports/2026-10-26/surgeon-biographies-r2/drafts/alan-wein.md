## Biography

Alan Wein entered urology when its broad mix of surgery and medical care appealed to him.[^11] As lower urinary tract medicine became more specialized, he worked on language clinicians could use to describe a patient's symptoms and functional problem.[^11] With Paul Abrams, he proposed “overactive bladder” in place of terms such as “unstable bladder,” which they thought misleading.[^11]

The change began when they agreed to lead a symposium only if its proposed title could be changed.[^11] Wein says the International Continence Society later adopted the new term.[^11] He also organized lower urinary tract dysfunction around failure to store or failure to empty, using those functions to guide diagnostic testing and treatment.[^11]

After decades at the University of Pennsylvania, Wein continued seeing patients and mentoring at the University of Miami.[^11] His account ties the terminology to an enduring aim: make complex physiology easier to describe and apply in clinical decisions.[^11]

The storage-and-emptying framework came from his observation that older terms largely described neurologic disorders.[^11] He reduced lower urinary tract function to filling at low pressure and emptying through an open outlet with a sustained bladder contraction.[^11] In that account, naming the impaired function points the clinician toward the relevant studies and treatment.[^11] His work on terms and classification thus grew from the same habit of simplifying complex physiology without losing its clinical use.[^11]
