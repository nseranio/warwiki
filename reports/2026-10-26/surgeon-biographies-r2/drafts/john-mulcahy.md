## Biography

John Mulcahy's interest in urologic prosthetics grew through patients whose implants developed complications.[^2] At Indiana University, an infected penile implant prompted him to try removing the device, washing the operative spaces, and replacing it in the same operation, following an approach Brantley Scott had described.[^2] His first patient did well, and Mulcahy repeated the method in subsequent infections.[^2]

Early results drew mixed responses because some surgeons were reluctant to put a new device into a recently infected field.[^2] As referrals grew, Mulcahy built a series of more than 100 salvage procedures and reported an 84% success rate in his own account.[^2] He says the approach later became widely used by implant surgeons.[^2]

Teaching carried the work beyond his own practice.[^2] He returned to an AUA course on penile implant complications for 25 years, adding updates as his experience and the field developed.[^2] His account of the salvage operation shows how a disputed operative choice became a technique taught through case series, referrals, and courses.[^2]

Mulcahy's path to that work was broader than prosthetics.[^2] At a rehabilitation hospital in Kentucky, he treated patients with spinal cord injury and spina bifida, using intermittent catheterization and medication to manage continence.[^2] Visits to Hardy Hendron and Brantley Scott exposed him to reconstructive techniques and implant surgery.[^2] By the time complex prosthetic infections reached him at Indiana, he had combined operative teaching, device experience, and a willingness to test a new salvage method.[^2]
