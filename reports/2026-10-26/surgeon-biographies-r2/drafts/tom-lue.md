## Biography

Tom Lue arrived at UCSF expecting a short research fellowship before private practice.[^3] In a bladder neuroprosthesis laboratory, he noticed that stimulation of some pelvic nerve branches produced penile erection without the bladder and bowel responses seen with broader sacral stimulation.[^3] The observation redirected his work toward penile nerves, blood flow, and erectile function.[^3]

Experiments in dogs and monkeys helped him and his colleagues describe the roles of cavernous smooth muscle relaxation, arterial inflow, and venous compression in erection.[^3] After seeing Giles Brindley's demonstration of injection-induced erection, Lue and Richard Schmidt explored papaverine as an intracavernous agent.[^3] Lue's own account includes an early prolonged erection and a complication from the attempt to reverse it, events that preceded wider clinical use of injection therapy.[^3]

The physiology work led to clinical studies of penile blood flow and to operations for Peyronie's disease and priapism.[^3] Later he helped establish a molecular urology laboratory at UCSF, bringing basic scientists and visiting fellows into work that grew from those early operative questions.[^3]

The direction of Lue's research changed because the bladder-pacemaker project appeared likely to take years, while he had expected only a year in the laboratory.[^3] He chose a more focused study of penile physiology and found a mentor in Emil Tanagho, who came to the lab to see each new result.[^3] By the end of the fellowship, Tanagho had offered him a clinical instructor position and Lue had abandoned the plan for private practice.[^3] That turning point kept experimental work and clinical surgery together in his career.[^3]
