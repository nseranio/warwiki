BIOGRAPHY:

## Biography

Richard Hautmann turned to medicine after a weightlifting injury ended his plans as a competitive athlete and led to a long hospital stay.[^1] His later research included measurements of calcium and oxalate in renal tissue, but urinary diversion became the main surgical problem of his career.[^1] While preparing a 1985 lecture, he concluded that reports of diversion often emphasized early success while leaving adverse long-term outcomes less visible.[^1]

At Ulm he and his colleagues developed an ileal neobladder designed as a compliant, low-pressure reservoir after cystectomy.[^1] He credits the operation's development to a large surgical team and describes later changes proposed by colleagues, including a simpler ureteral connection.[^1] The group kept a long-term database and studied metabolic effects as well as surgical function.[^1]

The technique spread through visitors to Ulm and annual seminars that broadcast live operations.[^1] Hautmann's account places teaching and long follow-up beside the initial design: a new reservoir had to work after the early postoperative period, and other surgeons had to learn how to build it.[^1]

Hautmann also describes an iterative operation rather than a single finished invention.[^1] His colleagues extended the neobladder to women, changed the ureteral connection, and studied the reservoir's capacity to absorb and excrete substances.[^1] Those questions mattered because the substitute bladder was metabolically active as well as mechanically different from a native bladder.[^1] He credits named associates for these changes and for maintaining the database that supported later publications.[^1]
