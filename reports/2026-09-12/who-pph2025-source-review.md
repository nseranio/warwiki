# WHO 2025 postpartum haemorrhage: treatment and source-control review

Reviewed 2026-09-12 by the independent workflow reviewer. This is a bounded primary-guideline and evidence-annex review for the obstetric branch of the vascular damage-control article. No clinical page was edited. The full pregnancy/prevention guideline and every underlying study were **not** independently reviewed.

## Implications for the obstetric branch

1. **Recognize and treat while identifying the cause.** Recommendation 22 uses objectively measured blood loss of at least 300 mL with an abnormal haemodynamic sign, or at least 500 mL, whichever occurs first within 24 hours of birth, with particular vigilance in the first two hours. The signs listed are pulse above 100/min, shock index above 1, systolic pressure below 100 mmHg or diastolic pressure below 60 mmHg. This is a threshold for initial treatment/escalation, not an automatic operation or transfusion threshold. Diagnostic evidence is predominantly from facility-based vaginal births; the caesarean evidence and interpretation under anaesthesia are more limited.

2. **Use the first-response interventions promptly and together.** Recommendation 29 combines uterine massage, an oxytocic, TXA, intravenous fluids, examination of the genital tract and escalation; the remarks aim to initiate available components within 15 minutes of diagnosis. Examination addresses tone, trauma, retained tissue and coagulopathy. Do not turn this into serial waiting for each medication to fail before looking for or treating the bleeding source. The formal bundle recommendation is supported principally by vaginal-birth evidence; this does not make its individual components inapplicable after caesarean birth.

3. **Keep TXA timing anchored to birth.** Recommendation 27 gives 1 g intravenously over 10 minutes as soon as possible and within three hours of birth. A second 1 g is given if bleeding continues after 30 minutes or restarts within 24 hours after completion of the first dose. It applies to PPH regardless of whether the source is traumatic; source confirmation is not required before TXA. Starting the initial dose beyond three hours is unsupported by this recommendation. Avoid use where a clear antifibrinolytic contraindication exists. The different high-dose French research regimen in the annex is not the recommended bedside regimen.

4. **Medical treatment is source-specific.** Recommendation 24 retains IV oxytocin as first-line uterotonic treatment even when prophylaxis was already given; avoid rapid undiluted IV administration. Recommendation 25 provides alternatives when unavailable or ineffective, selected with their contraindications. It does not support routine adjunct misoprostol added to oxytocin or routine carbetocin treatment. Isotonic crystalloids are preferred to colloids, with assessment for overload. Retained placenta with bleeding requires definitive management; additional uterotonics must not become a reason to delay removal. These are companion-page details rather than an invitation to duplicate a complete obstetric drug protocol in the vascular article.

5. **Compression and tamponade have defined limits.** Recommendations 33–35 describe bimanual uterine compression, external aortic compression and the non-pneumatic anti-shock garment as temporizing interventions. The first two concern uterine atony after vaginal birth. They do not establish a safe prolonged occlusion duration. Recommendation 36 supports uterine balloon tamponade for refractory atony after vaginal birth only when all prerequisites are present: immediate access to surgery and blood, a routinely implemented first-line protocol including TXA, reasonable exclusion of trauma/retained tissue, trained personnel and adequate monitoring. Do not quote an uncontrolled device-success percentage as proven comparative effectiveness.

6. **Distinguish intrauterine packing from pelvic damage-control packing.** Updated Recommendation 37 advises against plain or haemostatic-agent-impregnated intrauterine gauze for PPH. The concerns include concealed bleeding, trauma, infection and delayed escalation. Its narrow exceptional temporizing discussion concerns settings where other measures have failed and no better intervention is available, with urgent transfer. This recommendation does **not** evaluate or prohibit retroperitoneal or pelvic packing used for a different surgical bleeding source.

7. **Do not let embolization delay surgical source control.** Recommendation 38 supports uterine artery embolization for refractory uterine atony when resources and timely expertise are available; it explicitly calls for parallel surgical preparation. Recommendation 39 discusses compression sutures, uterine/utero-ovarian/hypogastric vessel ligation, and subtotal or total hysterectomy if life-threatening bleeding persists. It also recognizes provider skill in selection and sequence. A safe editorial inference is to avoid presenting embolization, every suture and every ligation as mandatory sequential hurdles before a life-saving operation. WHO supplies no detailed internal-iliac ligation landmark, ureter-dissection step or branch-preservation rule here; those require another operative source.

8. **Represent cell salvage as WHO's specific recommendation.** New Recommendation 40 restricts cell salvage for treatment of PPH to rigorous research. The remarks discuss uncertain important clinical benefits, costs, trial exclusions of women refusing donor blood and monitoring for autotransfusion events/fetomaternal haemorrhage, including anti-D assessment. Do not call routine use a WHO endorsement, or claim universal agreement across all specialty guidance.

9. **Transfuse for the clinical situation, with repeated assessment.** Recommendation 41 acknowledges no demonstrated superior PPH transfusion protocol. A normal initial haemoglobin can be misleading during active haemorrhage; an often-used haemoglobin goal above 70 g/L is not a requirement to wait for that value in shock. WHO gives a fibrinogen target of at least 2 g/L and prioritizes fibrinogen replacement when coagulopathy is detected or strongly suspected. It discusses laboratory-directed plasma, with an empirical example of one plasma unit to two red-cell units during massive loss when results are delayed, not proof of an optimal universal ratio. Platelets are considered with microvascular bleeding and a count below 50 × 10^9/L. PCC and recombinant factor VIIa effectiveness remains uncertain. These passages do not endorse routine trauma-style 1:1:1 transfusion or REBOA for all obstetric bleeding.

## Evidence strength and denominators that should not be inflated

The annex preserves assessments from several earlier WHO recommendations alongside updates. Its 2025 publication date is not evidence that every underlying source was newly reassessed through 2026.

- **First-response bundle:** E-MOTIVE was a hospital-cluster implementation package incorporating objective measurement, training, supplies and feedback, rather than a drug-only comparison. The principal composite table has 794/48,678 versus 2,139/50,044 vaginal births; it is dominated by severe PPH, with no independently established mortality or laparotomy benefit. The approximately 210,000 overall births, baseline-plus-implementation denominators and approximately 99,000 implementation-period outcome population must not be interchanged.
- **Balloon:** the frequently cited pooled 85.9% success comes from selected device recipients in predominantly uncontrolled evidence. The annex explicitly notes that this outcome cannot be measured in the same way in a no-device arm. Improvised devices, resource setting, timing and concurrent care confound comparisons. Critical randomized comparisons remain very uncertain.
- **Packing:** the seven-study review's 814 total accounts for overlapping German reports using the largest cohort. The sole 204-person trial involved caesarean placenta praevia and excluded suspected deeply invasive placentation. Packing had more anaemia and fever in low-certainty evidence; neutral or imprecise critical outcomes do not establish equivalence.
- **Embolization and surgery:** the preserved embolization evidence is older selected case-series experience; compression-suture and vessel-ligation evidence is also predominantly uncontrolled and very low certainty. Reported high success ranges should not become a general guarantee or comparative ranking.
- **Cell salvage:** six trials involved 3,486 caesarean participants with differing risk, not uniformly women with active severe PPH. The donor-transfusion analysis used 3,115 women in three trials, RR 0.45 (95% CI 0.15–1.33), low certainty. Absence of observed rare events is not proof of safety, and the major SALVO contribution has different randomized and analyzed denominators.
- **Transfusion products:** thresholds in stable medical/surgical populations are indirect for active PPH. Three small fibrinogen trials differ in dosing and eligibility; their results do not prove routine empirical concentrate benefits every bleeding patient. The 180-person PPH cryoprecipitate pilot is distinct from trauma CRYOSTAT-2 and must not be merged with it.

## Internal source discrepancies retained as cautions

The following were found in the source, not introduced on the website. They do not invalidate the clear recommendation text, but should not be copied into a new outcome table without original-study adjudication:

- Annex printed page 748 cell-salvage table has a 116-person major-surgery estimate while its comment says no study measured the outcome; the narrative's procedure is aortic-balloon placement, limiting a generic surgical-morbidity interpretation.
- Annex printed page 782 cryoprecipitate table prints an upper absolute confidence bound as “105 fewer” despite a positive upper risk-ratio bound of 106.26. This is visually confirmed, not an extraction error. Printed page 783 has only 81–82 respondents for fatigue outcomes, unlike the larger denominator in the surrounding narrative. The cryoprecipitate narrative also contains a fibrinogen-concentrate label inconsistency.
- The bundle table labels its composite evidence high certainty despite a serious-imprecision entry; some narrative certainty labels differ from the detailed surgical-outcome rows. Preserve outcome-specific uncertainty rather than describing every component as high-certainty benefit.
- Some rare-event summary rows round absolute effects to zero or substitute illustrative baseline risks. These are not observed zero-event proof of equivalence or safety. The anti-shock-garment narrative's country/denominator attribution and the TXA narrative's account of trial outcome history need original-study checking before reuse; neither is necessary for the proposed concise source-control branch.
