BIOGRAPHY:

## Biography

Jack McAninch arrived in urology when genitourinary trauma and reconstruction had few dedicated training programs.[^2] During residency at Letterman Army Hospital, he encountered the complex pelvic wounds of servicemen returning from Vietnam.[^2] The need to decide what repair was possible in an unpredictable operation became a recurring theme of his later teaching, especially when residents and fellows found injuries different from those anticipated before surgery.[^2] Before medicine, he had expected to study animal husbandry; his interest in basic science led him from a West Texas ranch to medical school.[^2]

At San Francisco General Hospital, McAninch built a clinical record of trauma cases and later a reconstruction database.[^2] Those records gave his team a way to study the results of operations across many injuries rather than relying only on memorable cases.[^2] By 2014 the trauma database contained 4,000 cases, and the reconstruction database begun in the 1980s remained a research tool.[^2] He continued to work at the county hospital after retirement, advising trainees and conducting outcomes research.[^2]

McAninch placed particular weight on teaching judgment in the operating room.[^2] He described trauma surgery as requiring decisions when the actual injury differs from what the surgeon expected before incision.[^2] From 1989 onward he trained a fellow each year in male genitourinary trauma and reconstruction, and former fellows went on to lead programs in other cities.[^2] At a 2014 gathering of those fellows, 23 of the 25 he had trained returned to San Francisco, reflecting the durable training network that grew around his clinical service.[^2]
