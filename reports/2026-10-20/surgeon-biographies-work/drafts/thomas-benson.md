BIOGRAPHY:

## Biography

J. Thomas Benson built a urogynecology program around two problems that received too little attention: access to sustained care and the mechanisms of pelvic floor dysfunction.[^3] At Methodist Hospital in Indianapolis, he began a resident-run office in 1983 that let patients remain with the same physician through residency and used patient feedback to improve care.[^3] He opened a urogynecology fellowship in 1988 as the field was beginning to organize formal training, and his program received board accreditation in 1996.[^3]

Benson questioned whether prolapse and incontinence could be explained solely by connective-tissue defects.[^3] He investigated pelvic nerves and muscle function, moving from pudendal nerve latency tests to reflex studies, evoked potentials, and pelvic needle electromyography.[^3] He argued for a central role of urethral sphincter function in stress incontinence and of levator ani dysfunction in prolapse, positions his obituary says were initially unpopular.[^3] To deepen that work, he completed a clinical neurophysiology fellowship at the Mayo Clinic in 1995, when he was 61.[^3]

His willingness to test a preferred operation changed another part of his practice.[^3] In a prospective trial comparing vaginal with abdominal surgery for prolapse, the outcomes favored the abdominal approach, surprising a surgeon who had strongly valued vaginal repair.[^3] He reported the finding and defended the study in professional debates despite his disappointment.[^3] Colleagues also remembered his insistence that clinicians ask about bowel evacuation and fecal incontinence, topics often left out of pelvic floor consultations at the time.[^3] The fellowship he had built trained 18 surgeons, a result Hale says Benson took particular pride in.[^3]
