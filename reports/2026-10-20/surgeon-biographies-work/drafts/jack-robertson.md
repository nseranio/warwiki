BIOGRAPHY:

## Biography

Jack Robertson encountered female urinary disorders at a time when clinicians often examined women with instruments designed for men.[^1] His concern was practical: the available tools and the separation of gynecology from urology made it harder to investigate the whole pelvic problem.[^1] In 1968 he persuaded instrument maker Karl Storz to make a narrow female urethroscope that permitted carbon dioxide insufflation, giving gynecologists a dedicated instrument for examination.[^1]

Robertson also saw that few gynecologists or urologists had been taught to manage female genitourinary disorders.[^1] He and his wife Janice organized weekend seminars and took them around the world, while he arranged workshops for both surgical and obstetric colleges.[^1] These courses carried practical techniques beyond the institutions where he worked and brought clinicians from several specialties into the same room.[^1]

In 1976 Robertson and Axel Ingelman-Sundberg gathered colleagues at the FIGO meeting in Mexico City to establish the International Urogynecological Association.[^1] He then helped form the Gynecologic Urology Society, which became the American Urogynecologic Society.[^1] Harold Drutz, who trained with Robertson, recalled teaching that drew on difficult clinical experience and treated the female pelvis as a connected problem rather than a set of specialty territories.[^1] Drutz also remembered Janice Robertson's work as an artist and course organizer; the couple's partnership helped sustain the teaching program.[^1] Robertson later taught gynecology interns at several California medical schools and travelled regularly to Nevada to lecture.[^1] Drutz joined him at Los Angeles County–USC Medical Center in 1975 and remembered lessons grounded in practical experience with complex repairs.[^1]
