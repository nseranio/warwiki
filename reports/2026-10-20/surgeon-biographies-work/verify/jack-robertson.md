## Problems

## Corrected BIOGRAPHY

## Biography

Jack Robertson encountered female urinary disorders at a time when clinicians often examined women with instruments designed for men.[^1] He later argued for care of the whole pelvis across specialties.[^1] In 1968 he persuaded instrument maker Karl Storz to make a narrow female urethroscope that permitted carbon dioxide insufflation, giving gynecologists a dedicated instrument for examination.[^1]

Robertson also saw that few gynecologists or urologists had been taught to manage female genitourinary disorders.[^1] He and his wife Janice organized weekend seminars that he taught around the world, while he arranged workshops for the American College of Surgeons and the American College of Obstetricians and Gynecologists.[^1] These courses carried his teaching beyond the institutions where he worked.[^1]

In 1976 Robertson and Axel Ingelman-Sundberg gathered colleagues at the FIGO meeting in Mexico City to establish the International Urogynecological Association.[^1] He then helped form the Gynecologic Urology Society, which became the American Urogynecologic Society.[^1] Harold Drutz, who trained with Robertson, recalled teaching that drew on difficult clinical experience and treated the female pelvis as a connected problem rather than a set of specialty territories.[^1] Drutz also remembered Janice Robertson's work as an artist and course organizer; the couple's partnership helped sustain the teaching program.[^1] Robertson later taught gynecology interns at several California medical schools and travelled regularly to Nevada to lecture.[^1] Drutz joined him at Los Angeles County–USC Medical Center in 1975 and remembered lessons grounded in practical experience with complex repairs.[^1]
