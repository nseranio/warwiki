## 2. Section text

### Cost and Value

Decision models of preoperative urodynamics for stress incontinence found no gain in cure and added cost per cure. In a decision-analytic model of women with prolapse and stress incontinence symptoms (costs from the Federal Register, published 2000), basic office evaluation and urodynamics both reached a 96% cure rate after initial and secondary treatment, and urodynamics cost $328,601 per additional cure of incontinence compared with basic office evaluation; basic office evaluation was more cost-effective when the prevalence of pure detrusor instability was below 8% or the cost of urodynamic testing was above $103.<sup>[[74]](#ref74)</sup> A decision analysis of women with stress incontinence symptoms who were candidates for primary surgery (Federal Register costs, published 2002) also found a 96% cure rate with both strategies and mean costs of care of $5,042 for basic office assessment and $5,046 for urodynamics, or $3,847 per additional cure with urodynamics; basic office assessment was less costly when the prevalence of genuine stress incontinence was 80% or higher.<sup>[[75]](#ref75)</sup>

A secondary analysis of the ValUE trial (630 women, US Medicare allowable fees for 2014) priced urodynamics at $314 to $343 for the complex cystometrogram plus $16 for complex uroflowmetry, varying by site, and projected savings of 13 to 33 million US dollars a year if women with uncomplicated stress incontinence and a confirmatory office evaluation were not tested.<sup>[[5]](#ref5)[[76]](#ref76)</sup> In the UK INVESTIGATE-I pilot trial (218 women with stress or stress-predominant mixed incontinence in the economic analysis, published 2018), invasive urodynamic testing cost £138 less per woman at 6 months than clinical assessment with non-invasive tests (p = 0.071), which the authors attributed to less surgery after testing; QALYs did not differ (difference 0.004 by SF-12, p = 0.425; −0.004 by EQ-5D-3L, p = 0.725), and the probability of cost-effectiveness remained uncertain.<sup>[[77]](#ref77)</sup>

For refractory overactive bladder, economic analyses of the FUTURE trial (1,099 women) found urodynamics more costly with little difference in QALYs.<sup>[[28]](#ref28)</sup> From the NHS perspective, the within-trial analysis estimated incremental costs of £463 and 0.011 QALYs with urodynamics plus comprehensive clinical assessment, an ICER of £42,643 per QALY gained with urodynamics versus comprehensive clinical assessment alone, and a 34% probability of cost-effectiveness at a threshold of £20,000 per QALY.<sup>[[78]](#ref78)</sup> A lifetime model built on the trial data (published 2025) found urodynamics £1,380 more costly with 0.002 fewer QALYs, with a 23.4% probability of cost-effectiveness at £20,000 per QALY; in the subgroup with mixed incontinence the ICER was £26,462 per QALY gained with urodynamics versus assessment alone, with probabilities of cost-effectiveness of 45.3% at £20,000 and 53.8% at £30,000 per QALY.<sup>[[79]](#ref79)</sup> An earlier UK NHS decision tree from the Bladder Ultrasound Study (5-year horizon, women failing first-line treatment for overactive bladder or urgency-predominant mixed incontinence, published 2016) estimated that treatment on clinical history alone cost £491,100 per woman successfully treated and £60,200 per QALY compared with urodynamics in all women, and that restricting urodynamics to women with a history of mixed incontinence was the optimal strategy, at £19,500 per woman successfully treated and £12,700 per QALY compared with urodynamics in all women.<sup>[[80]](#ref80)</sup>
