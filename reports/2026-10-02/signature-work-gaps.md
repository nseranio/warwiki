### `docs/03-clinical-conditions/03c-pelvic-support/pelvic-organ-prolapse.mdx`

- **Deborah Myers: Posterior prolapse and masked stress incontinence** (1998; Obstetrics &amp; Gynecology; doi:10.1016/s0029-7844(98)00049-0). Section: Core assessment.
  Proposed: Severe posterior wall prolapse can also mask stress leakage: in a prospective study of 90 women, retracting a grade 3 posterior defect changed urethral-pressure measures and revealed potential stress incontinence in four women.
- **Ingrid Nygaard: National prevalence estimate for symptomatic pelvic floor disorders** (2008; JAMA; doi:10.1001/jama.300.11.1311). Section: Epidemiology.
  Proposed: In a nationally representative US sample, 2.9% of adult women reported symptoms of pelvic organ prolapse, defined as seeing or feeling a vaginal bulge.
- **Victoria Handa: Mothers' Outcomes After Delivery cohort** (2011; Obstetrics &amp; Gynecology; doi:10.1097/aog.0b013e3182267f2f). Section: Interpreting risk factors.
  Proposed: In the MOAD cohort, pelvic-floor-disorder findings 5–10 years after childbirth differed by delivery mode; this observational association does not determine an individual delivery decision.
- **Victoria Handa: Longitudinal natural history of prolapse** (2004; American Journal of Obstetrics and Gynecology; doi:10.1016/j.ajog.2003.07.017). Section: Epidemiology.
  Proposed: Repeated examinations of menopausal women documented both progression and remission of prolapse, so one examination does not establish inevitable worsening.
- **Anthony Visco: CARE trial reduction-urodynamics analysis** (2008; International Urogynecology Journal; doi:10.1007/s00192-007-0498-2). Section: Concomitant Anti-Incontinence Surgery.
  Proposed: A CARE trial analysis tested whether prolapse-reduction urodynamics identified occult stress leakage before sacrocolpopexy in women without reported stress incontinence.
- **Jennifer M. Wu: Prevalence of symptomatic pelvic floor disorders** (2014; Obstetrics &amp; Gynecology; doi:10.1097/aog.0000000000000057). Section: Epidemiology.
  Proposed: A U.S. population analysis estimated symptomatic urinary incontinence, fecal incontinence and prolapse together, distinguishing reported pelvic-floor symptoms from examination-defined prolapse.

### `docs/04-surgical-techniques/04f-incontinence-procedures/procedures/artificial-urinary-sphincter.mdx`

- **Timothy B. Boone: Nocturnal artificial urinary sphincter deactivation** (2001; Urology; doi:10.1016/s0090-4295(01)00963-3). Section: System Operation.
  Proposed: In a retrospective two-center comparison, atrophy-related recurrent incontinence occurred in 10% of men instructed to deactivate the AUS overnight and 21% of men keeping it active; the difference was not statistically significant.
- **John J. Mulcahy: Double-cuff artificial urinary sphincter** (1993; Journal of Urology; doi:10.1016/s0022-5347(17)36057-3). Section: 3. Tandem Cuff Placement.
  Proposed: An early double-cuff AMS 800 series described adding a second urethral cuff for severe persistent incontinence.
- **Jay Simhan: Radiation-associated erosion with a 3.5 cm AUS cuff** (2015; Journal of Urology; doi:10.1016/j.juro.2014.07.115). Section: Risk factors for earlier revision.
  Proposed: In a 3.5-cm cuff series, erosion occurred predominantly among previously irradiated patients; this association should be considered alongside urethral tissue quality when selecting a cuff.
- **O. Lenaine Westney: Single-incision artificial urinary sphincter placement** (2018; International braz j urol; doi:10.1590/s1677-5538.ibju.2017.0097). Section: Approaches.
  Proposed: A described single-perineal-incision approach places the cuff, pressure-regulating balloon and pump through one incision, with a defined subdartos pocket for the pump.
- **Steven K. Wilson: Single-incision transscrotal artificial urinary sphincter placement** (2003; Journal of Urology; doi:10.1016/s0022-5347(05)64082-7). Section: Approaches.
  Proposed: The upper transverse scrotal approach was described as a single-incision route for placement of all artificial urinary sphincter components.

### `docs/02-evaluation/ancillary-tests/urodynamics.mdx`

- **Jerry G. Blaivas: Blaivas–Groutz female bladder outlet obstruction nomogram** (2000; Neurourology and Urodynamics; doi:10.1002/1520-6777(2000)19:5<553::aid-nau2>3.0.co;2-b). Section: Pressure-Flow Indices.
  Proposed: The Blaivas–Groutz nomogram classifies female outlet obstruction using pressure-flow measurements; it should not be treated as a universally accepted diagnostic threshold.
- **Paul Abrams: Abrams–Griffiths pressure-flow assessment** (1979; British Journal of Urology; doi:10.1111/j.1464-410x.1979.tb02846.x). Section: 3. Pressure-Flow Study (Voiding Phase).
  Proposed: Abrams and Griffiths used pressure-flow measurements to assess prostatic obstruction, forming the basis of the later obstruction nomogram.
- **Edward J. McGuire: Abdominal leak-point pressure assessment of urethral function** (1993; Journal of Urology; doi:10.1016/s0022-5347(17)35806-8). Section: Urethral Function Tests.
  Proposed: In McGuire's study of 125 women with stress incontinence, the abdominal pressure required to produce leakage was unrelated to maximum urethral pressure, showing that the two measures describe different aspects of urethral function.
- **Gary Lemack: Preoperative urodynamics and voiding dysfunction after incontinence surgery** (2008; Journal of Urology; doi:10.1016/j.juro.2008.07.027). Section: Predictive Value.
  Proposed: In a secondary analysis of the SISTEr trial, the tested preoperative urodynamic measures did not predict prolonged catheter use or surgical takedown after Burch colposuspension or pubovaginal sling in the trial's selected population.

- **Keith F. Rourke: Prospective patient-reported urethroplasty outcomes** (2020; Urology; doi:10.1016/j.urology.2020.02.034). Section: Patient-Reported Outcomes.
  Proposed: A prospective urethroplasty cohort assessed voiding, urinary quality of life, sexual function, penile appearance and pain alongside cystoscopic patency.
- **Wesley Verla: Stricture-fecta outcome criteria** (2026; European Urology Open Science; doi:10.1016/j.euros.2025.11.008). Section: Outcomes & Efficacy.
  Proposed: The proposed stricture-fecta outcome combines freedom from retreatment, preserved continence and sexual function, and patient satisfaction rather than treating anatomic patency alone as success.

### `docs/03-clinical-conditions/03d-nlutd/nlutd-spinal-cord-injury.mdx`

- **Anne P. Cameron: National study of bladder management after spinal cord injury** (2010; Journal of Urology; doi:10.1016/j.juro.2010.03.008). Section: Emptying strategies — order of preference.
  Proposed: National spinal cord injury data from 1972–2005 showed that bladder-management methods changed over time and that intermittent catheterization was not sustained by every patient who initially used it.
- **Jeremy B. Myers: Spinal cord injury bladder-management comparative study** (2020; PCORI Final Research Reports; doi:10.25302/06.2020.cer.140921348). Section: Emptying strategies — order of preference.
  Proposed: A multicenter observational cohort found that patient-reported bladder function and satisfaction differed across catheterization, voiding and surgical strategies after spinal cord injury; treatment selection and disability level limit causal comparisons.
- **John T. Stoffel: Neurogenic Bladder Research Group** (2020; ; doi:10.25302/06.2020.cer.140921348). Section: Emptying strategies — order of preference.
  Proposed: In a 1,479-person observational spinal cord injury cohort, patient-reported bladder function varied by management strategy and injury level; these associations do not establish a causal ranking of treatments.
