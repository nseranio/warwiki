# Surgicel — Oxidized Regenerated Cellulose

**Surgicel** (oxidized regenerated cellulose, ORC) is a sterile, absorbable, knitted plant-cellulose fabric labeled as an adjunct for capillary, venous, and small-arterial bleeding when ligation or conventional control is impractical or ineffective.<sup>[[2]](#ref2)</sup> It acts through an acidic blood-cellulose clot and mechanical tamponade.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup>

---

## Mechanism

- Cellulose fibers are chemically oxidized to introduce carboxylic acid groups, producing a low-pH, locally acidic microenvironment on hydration
- The acidic pH drives formation of a gel-like, brown blood-cellulose coagulum as the matrix absorbs blood; the same low pH inactivates thrombin (see Limitations)<sup>[[2]](#ref2)</sup>
- Mechanical tamponade contributes a secondary hemostatic effect
- The low pH is bactericidal in vitro, including against MRSA and VRE; this does not substitute for systemic prophylaxis or treatment of established infection<sup>[[2]](#ref2)[[3]](#ref3)</sup>
- The label states no fixed absorption time — absorption depends on amount used, degree of blood saturation, and tissue bed, and can be prevented in chemically cauterized tissue. Seven to fourteen days is the commonly cited teaching figure, not a labeled value<sup>[[2]](#ref2)</sup>

Surgicel is a passive matrix: it contains no thrombin or other active coagulation factor, so hemostasis depends on the patient's native clotting capacity. It is not indicated for large-artery bleeding, non-hemorrhagic serous oozing, or use as an adhesion barrier.<sup>[[2]](#ref2)</sup>

## Product Forms

| Form | Texture | Best for |
|---|---|---|
| **Surgicel Original** | Knitted fabric (pad) | Sheet placement on a bleeding surface |
| **Surgicel Fibrillar** | Fluffy, cotton-like | Packing into crevices or irregular cavities |
| **Surgicel SNoW** | Structured non-woven fabric | Endoscopic and laparoscopic use (manufacturer states it may handle more easily than other forms in this setting) |
| **Surgicel Nu-Knit** | Knitted fabric, denser | Sheet placement; cuttable to size for endoscopic use |
| **Surgicel Powder** | Fine powder, applied via a 5 mm-or-larger trocar applicator | Broad diffuse oozing; apply dry in the minimum amount and remove excess by irrigation/aspiration once hemostasis is achieved — dry powder can scatter, migrate, and plug the urethra, ureter, or a catheter |

Original, Fibrillar, Nu-Knit, and SNoW are labeled as cuttable for endoscopic use.<sup>[[2]](#ref2)</sup> Handling and removal instructions differ by form; consult the package insert for the form in hand.

---

## GU Applications

- **Partial nephrectomy renal bed** — placed after sliding-clip renorrhaphy for diffuse capillary oozing; kept out of the collecting system, where fragments can obstruct the ureter or a catheter (the label reports a case of ureteral blockage after kidney resection)
- **Pelvic sidewall** — residual oozing after lymphadenectomy
- **Bladder-neck dissection** — diffuse oozing control after radical prostatectomy or bladder-neck reconstruction
- **Urethroplasty donor bed** — bed hemostasis at graft harvest sites (buccal mucosa, penile skin)
- **Retropubic space** — after closure of large pelvic dissections
- **Neurovascular-bundle coverage during RARP** — placing a thin strip over the bundle to achieve hemostasis without cautery is described by some surgeons as an operative technique; it is not a labeled indication and is not supported by comparative data in the cited sources
- **Scrotal or perineal wounds** — do not close it into an infected or contaminated wound; it is not routine packing after Fournier debridement<sup>[[2]](#ref2)</sup>

## Technique Pearls

- Pre-cut the sheet to the size of the bleeding area; oversizing increases residual foreign-body volume
- Apply dry — hemostatic effect is greater dry, so do not moisten with water or saline, and do not pre-treat the site with silver nitrate or other escharotics, which can prevent absorption<sup>[[2]](#ref2)</sup>
- Hold firmly in place until bleeding stops (commonly a few minutes); avoid wiping or rinsing it off afterward
- Use the minimum amount and remove excess before closure — retained bulk can encapsulate and mimic a mass or recurrence on later imaging; in urologic surgery, avoid amounts sufficient to plug the urethra, ureter, or a catheter<sup>[[2]](#ref2)</sup>
- Do not wrap it tightly around a vessel — stenosis has been reported<sup>[[2]](#ref2)</sup>
- It does not substitute for a suture or clip; control an identified arterial source mechanically first<sup>[[2]](#ref2)</sup>

---

## Limitations and Cautions

- **Thrombin incompatibility** — do not combine with topical thrombin; the low pH inactivates thrombin, and the manufacturer states hemostasis is not enhanced by the combination. Do not impregnate with antibiotics or other agents<sup>[[2]](#ref2)</sup>
- **Not for packing or wadding** unless it will be removed once hemostasis is achieved; not for implantation in bone defects<sup>[[2]](#ref2)</sup>
- **Contaminated or infected fields** — closing it into a contaminated wound can cause complications; in-vitro bactericidal activity does not replace drainage or antimicrobial therapy<sup>[[2]](#ref2)[[3]](#ref3)</sup>
- **Urinary tract** — fragments can plug the urethra, ureter, or a catheter; the label reports difficulty voiding after prostatectomy and one blocked ureter after kidney resection, both requiring catheterization. Keep it out of the collecting system. (The stone-nidus warning appears in the gelatin and Arista labels, not in Surgicel's.)<sup>[[2]](#ref2)</sup>
- **Foreign-body reaction and imaging mimicry** — retained material can encapsulate and mimic tumor recurrence or an abscess on subsequent imaging, reported in case series and case reports; document placement in the operative note<sup>[[2]](#ref2)[[4]](#ref4)</sup>
- **Coagulopathy** — as a passive matrix dependent on native clotting, an active thrombin-containing agent is an alternative in heparinized or coagulopathic patients; do not add thrombin to Surgicel
- **Removal near confined or swelling-sensitive structures** — the IFU requires removal near bony foramina, the spinal cord, optic nerve or chiasm, and tubular structures that swelling could constrict. Dislodgement and migration have been reported<sup>[[2]](#ref2)</sup>

## Surgicel vs. Alternatives

| Agent | Mechanism | Preferred over Surgicel when |
|---|---|---|
| **Surgicel** | Passive matrix; acid-pH-driven coagulum | Broad or sheet-like raw surfaces, no coagulopathy |
| **[Gelfoam](/docs/foundations/pharmacology/intraoperative-adjuncts/hemostatic-agents/gelfoam)** | Passive matrix, neutral pH | Bone bleeding; combinable with thrombin |
| **[Arista AH](/docs/foundations/pharmacology/intraoperative-adjuncts/hemostatic-agents/arista)** | Passive; absorbs water and concentrates clotting factors | Diffuse oozing needing rapid absorption (excess still requires removal) |
| **[FloSeal](/docs/foundations/pharmacology/intraoperative-adjuncts/hemostatic-agents/floseal)** | Active — contains thrombin | Coagulopathic patient; high-volume oozing |
| **[Topical thrombin](/docs/foundations/pharmacology/intraoperative-adjuncts/hemostatic-agents/topical-thrombin)** | Active — pure thrombin | Combined with Gelfoam for targeted activity |
