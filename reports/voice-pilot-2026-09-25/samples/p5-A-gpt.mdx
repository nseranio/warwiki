# Parkinson's Disease

**Parkinson disease (PD)** and atypical parkinsonism syndromes — chiefly **multiple system atrophy (MSA)** — are a high-volume, non-reconstructive NLUTD population. The dominant phenotype is storage-phase OAB with preserved sphincter coordination; management is pharmacologic and behavioral. The urologist's role is diagnostic (PD vs. MSA vs. coexisting BPH) and risk-avoidance: anticholinergic burden in a cognitively vulnerable population, functional incontinence in a mobility-impaired one.

See [Neurogenic Lower Urinary Tract Dysfunction](/docs/clinical-conditions/03d-nlutd/neurogenic-bladder) for the general framework. For the procedural therapies referenced below, see [Intradetrusor OnabotulinumtoxinA](/docs/surgical-techniques/04f-incontinence-procedures/procedures/intradetrusor-botox), [Sacral Neuromodulation](/docs/surgical-techniques/04f-incontinence-procedures/procedures/sacral-neuromodulation), and [Percutaneous Tibial Nerve Stimulation](/docs/surgical-techniques/04f-incontinence-procedures/procedures/percutaneous-tibial-nerve-stimulation); for refractory MSA, the [Ileal Conduit](/docs/surgical-techniques/04c-urinary-diversion/ileal-conduit) is the salvage option.

## Epidemiology

- US PD prevalence exceeds 1 million; incidence rises with age.
- Urinary symptoms are common: published estimates range 27–80% depending on age, disease stage, and symptom definition; roughly half of patients report LUTS at some point.<sup>[[3]](#ref3)</sup><sup>[[4]](#ref4)</sup>
- Urgency and nocturia are the most common symptoms; OAB/detrusor overactivity is the dominant phenotype.<sup>[[3]](#ref3)</sup><sup>[[4]](#ref4)</sup>
- MSA is far less common than PD but presents with an earlier and more severe urinary and autonomic phenotype (see PD vs. MSA table below).

---

## Pathophysiology

- **PD** is a suprapontine disease. Loss of basal ganglia dopaminergic input disinhibits the pontine micturition center, producing detrusor overactivity with usually preserved coordination — true detrusor-sphincter dyssynergia (DSD) is atypical and should prompt reassessment of the diagnosis. Storage pressures remain low; upper-tract deterioration is rare.<sup>[[2]](#ref2)</sup>
- **MSA** involves both suprapontine and sacral pathways. The pattern is early, severe OAB followed by retention, DSD-like voiding patterns, and sphincter denervation. Urinary retention with elevated post-void residual (PVR) is common and, per the data below, the single best urodynamic discriminator from PD.
- **Coexisting BPH:** most patients with PD are older men, so combined PD-related OAB and BPH outlet obstruction is common.

## Evaluation

**Initial:**
- History — symptom onset relative to motor onset (urinary symptoms preceding motor disease is an MSA red flag), fall risk, medication list, cognitive status, caregiver availability.
- Urinalysis, PVR, voiding diary.

**Adjunctive (as indicated):**
- Urodynamics before prostate surgery in men with PD, to distinguish outlet obstruction from PD-related OAB (changes postoperative expectations), and whenever MSA is suspected.
- Renal ultrasound and eGFR in patients with elevated PVR or an MSA phenotype.

### PD vs. MSA — the urologic clue

| Feature | PD | MSA |
|---|---|---|
| Onset of urinary symptoms | Late, follows motor symptoms | **Early** — often precedes motor symptoms |
| OAB | Yes | Yes, often severe |
| PVR | Usually low | **Elevated — early retention** |
| Orthostatic hypotension | Modest | **Severe** |
| Response to dopaminergic therapy | Good (motor symptoms) | Poor |
| EMG of external anal sphincter | Usually normal | Denervation pattern in many (less discriminating than PVR in one large series)<sup>[[5]](#ref5)</sup> |

Early urinary retention combined with severe orthostatic hypotension and poor dopamine response should raise suspicion for MSA and change counseling on prognosis and procedure selection. A PVR above approximately 150 mL on free flow, with impaired detrusor contractility, is the urodynamic finding that best separates MSA from PD (odds ratio ~8.7 in a retrospective series of 273 patients); it supports but does not replace neurologic assessment.<sup>[[4]](#ref4)</sup><sup>[[5]](#ref5)</sup>

## Management

### Behavioral and pharmacologic first line

- Timed voiding, bowel regimen, fluid-intake management.
- Pelvic-floor physical therapy — evidence in PD is limited to small trials; benefit, when present, is modest.
- **β3-adrenergic agonists (mirabegron, vibegron)** are commonly preferred to limit anticholinergic burden. The AUA/SUFU guideline permits either drug class, or combination therapy, and does not mandate a beta-3-first sequence.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup> Disease-specific evidence favors mirabegron over an assumed class effect: the PaDoMi RCT (n=117) improved OAB symptoms over 4–8 weeks with a mean PVR rise of ~64 mL and one case of retention; a Medicare cohort found fewer acute-care encounters with beta-3 agonists than with antimuscarinics (HR 1.23 for antimuscarinics).<sup>[[8]](#ref8)</sup><sup>[[9]](#ref9)</sup> Vibegron has limited PD-specific data.<sup>[[4]](#ref4)</sup> Check baseline blood pressure and PVR — mirabegron can raise blood pressure in patients who frequently already have orthostatic hypotension — and review the full antihypertensive and dopaminergic regimen before starting.
- **Antimuscarinics** require weighing benefit against cognition, constipation, retention risk, and cumulative anticholinergic burden; lower CNS penetration of an individual agent does not establish absence of cognitive risk. Avoid oxybutynin IR in cognitively impaired patients. The 2023 AGS Beers Criteria advise avoiding strongly anticholinergic drugs in patients with dementia or cognitive impairment, which covers most antimuscarinics in PD dementia or Lewy body disease; switch to a beta-3 agonist or non-drug measures if cognition declines on treatment.<sup>[[10]](#ref10)</sup><sup>[[4]](#ref4)</sup> Dopaminergic dose changes can improve or worsen LUTS, so review timing of medication adjustments against symptom changes.<sup>[[3]](#ref3)</sup>
- **Alpha-blockers** are indicated for documented outlet obstruction. Counsel on orthostatic hypotension and fall risk, particularly in MSA and other autonomic failure syndromes; dose at night and check standing blood pressure.
- **Desmopressin** for nocturia is a selective option; monitor serum sodium and avoid in frail or older patients with fall risk.

### Intradetrusor onabotulinumtoxinA

- PD-specific randomized data are limited; efficacy is largely extrapolated from neurogenic detrusor overactivity trials in other conditions (multiple sclerosis, spinal cord injury). Use in PD is off-label; the FDA approval for neurogenic detrusor overactivity applies to defined neurologic conditions that do not include PD.<sup>[[4]](#ref4)</sup>
- Post-injection retention can convert a borderline voider into one requiring clean intermittent catheterization (CIC); many patients with PD lack the hand dexterity for self-catheterization. Discuss this risk explicitly before treatment.

### Prostate/outlet surgery in men with PD

- TURP, HoLEP, simple prostatectomy, and comparable procedures are appropriate when obstruction is documented on urodynamics and MSA has been excluded.<sup>[[3]](#ref3)</sup> The evidence base is small and prone to selection bias: a 2024 systematic review identified only two PD-specific studies, with pooled success around 27% and de novo incontinence around 20%.<sup>[[6]](#ref6)</sup>
- Counsel patients that persistent OAB symptoms are expected post-operatively — outlet surgery does not treat the neurogenic component.
- In MSA, outlet surgery is generally contraindicated: it can worsen incontinence in a patient whose sphincter function is already failing.

### Catheter-based management

- Indwelling urethral catheter — a pragmatic option in late PD or MSA with severe mobility limitation and reliable caregiver support.
- CIC — feasible in early PD with intact hand dexterity; becomes harder to sustain as tremor progresses.
- Suprapubic catheter — preferred over urethral catheterization in men for long-term drainage.

### Sacral neuromodulation and PTNS

- Sacral neuromodulation (SNM) is an option in PD with refractory OAB.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup>
- MRI compatibility matters: PD patients often have coexisting indications for brain MRI (e.g., planned deep brain stimulation, DBS), so device MRI-conditional status should be verified before implant.
- Subthalamic DBS does not impair bladder function and, in meta-analysis, is associated with increased cystometric capacity (mean +124 mL); coordinate device selection and timing with the movement-disorders team.<sup>[[7]](#ref7)</sup>
- SNM is not typically used in MSA, given the more aggressive autonomic phenotype.

### Reconstructive surgery

- Rare in PD; augmentation is almost never indicated because upper-tract risk is low.
- In MSA, urinary diversion is occasionally needed for refractory combined retention and incontinence; prognosis and goals-of-care discussion typically drive the decision more than the urologic findings alone.

## Common Scenarios

### PD with OAB and mixed BPH

A pragmatic sequence, not a guideline-mandated order: (1) beta-3 agonist plus alpha-blocker; (2) urodynamics if the response is equivocal; (3) outlet surgery for documented obstruction, with continued treatment of residual OAB; (4) botulinum toxin only after confirming CIC capacity.

### PD with nocturia

Contributing factors include fluid intake, peripheral edema redistribution, sleep fragmentation, and depression. Address modifiable factors first — leg elevation, compression stockings, evening fluid restriction — before adding desmopressin.

### PD with dementia or functional incontinence

Management centers on caregiver-directed toileting schedules, incontinence containment products, and medication review for anticholinergic burden and diuretic timing. Avoid pharmacologic escalation that risks further cognitive decline or falls.

### MSA with retention

Teach CIC early, while dexterity remains; plan for suprapubic catheter placement as disease progression limits self-catheterization.
