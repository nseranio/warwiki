# β3-Adrenergic Receptor Agonists

Two β3-adrenoceptor agonists are FDA-approved for **overactive bladder (OAB)**: **mirabegron (Myrbetriq)** and **vibegron (Gemtesa)**. Both stimulate β3-adrenoceptors in the detrusor muscle, relaxing the detrusor during storage, increasing functional bladder capacity, and reducing urgency. Efficacy is comparable to antimuscarinics; neither agent carries anticholinergic burden, though this does not establish an absence of long-term cognitive risk.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[2]](#ref2)[[3]](#ref3)</sup>

The 2024 AUA/SUFU OAB guideline recommends offering either a β3-agonist or an antimuscarinic (Strong Recommendation, Grade A) within a menu of options rather than a fixed step sequence, and cites a SUFU white paper advising a β3-agonist trial before an antimuscarinic when drug therapy is chosen and anticholinergic burden or cognitive risk is a concern.<sup>[[4]](#ref4)</sup>

---

## Mechanism of Action

β3-adrenoceptors are highly expressed in the detrusor muscle. Stimulation activates adenylyl cyclase, raising cAMP, and produces:<sup>[[2]](#ref2)[[3]](#ref3)[[5]](#ref5)</sup>

- Direct detrusor smooth muscle relaxation during the storage phase
- Decreased afferent signaling from the bladder
- Improved bladder compliance during filling
- Increased functional bladder capacity
- Suppression of detrusor micro-contractions via effects on detrusor smooth muscle cells and suburothelial interstitial cells

### Secondary mechanism — cholinergic inhibition

β3 stimulation also activates a prejunctional EPAC1/PKC pathway that promotes adenosine release, producing additional inhibition of parasympathetic cholinergic transmission at the neuromuscular junction, distinct from direct smooth muscle relaxation.<sup>[[6]](#ref6)</sup>

## FDA-Approved Agents

### Mirabegron (Myrbetriq)

First-in-class; FDA-approved 2012.<sup>[[7]](#ref7)</sup>

| Property | Detail |
|---|---|
| **Indications** | OAB (adults); neurogenic detrusor overactivity in pediatric patients age ≥3 years |
| **Adult dosing** | Start 25 mg PO daily; can increase to 50 mg after 4–8 weeks |
| **Pediatric dosing** | Weight-based; granule or tablet formulations |
| **Onset of efficacy** | 4–8 weeks; sustained through 52 weeks |
| **Key cautions** | Severe uncontrolled HTN (SBP ≥180 and/or DBP ≥110 mm Hg) — not recommended; ESRD — not recommended; Child-Pugh C hepatic impairment — not recommended; lower dose ceiling with Child-Pugh B or eGFR 15–29 |
| **Drug interactions** | Moderate CYP2D6 inhibitor — caution with metoprolol, desipramine, flecainide, thioridazine, propafenone; digoxin levels rise, so start digoxin at the lowest dose and monitor levels |

### Vibegron (Gemtesa)

FDA-approved 2020 (OAB); 2024 expansion to OAB in men on BPH pharmacotherapy (COURAGE trial).<sup>[[8]](#ref8)[[9]](#ref9)</sup>

| Property | Detail |
|---|---|
| **Indications** | OAB (adults); OAB in adult males on BPH pharmacologic therapy (α-blocker ± 5-ARI) |
| **Dose** | 75 mg PO daily (no titration) |
| **Onset of efficacy** | Within 4 weeks; sustained 52+ weeks |
| **BP effects** | Minimal — ambulatory BP monitoring shows no clinically meaningful changes<sup>[[10]](#ref10)</sup> |
| **Drug interactions** | Fewer than mirabegron; no CYP2D6 inhibition. Digoxin levels rise — check levels before and during coadministration<sup>[[23]](#ref23)</sup> |

## Clinical Efficacy

### Mirabegron — phase 3 data<sup>[[7]](#ref7)[[11]](#ref11)</sup>

| Outcome | Mirabegron 50 mg | Placebo |
|---|---|---|
| Micturition frequency reduction | −1.6 to −1.9 episodes / 24 h | −1.0 to −1.3 |
| Incontinence reduction | −1.4 to −1.6 episodes / 24 h | −1.1 to −1.2 |
| Volume per void | +12 to +24 mL | +7 to +12 mL |

Network meta-analysis: mirabegron 50 mg has comparable overall efficacy to most antimuscarinics, with better tolerability on dry mouth (better than 21/22 active comparators), constipation (9/20), and urinary retention (7/10).<sup>[[12]](#ref12)</sup>

### Vibegron — EMPOWUR trial (1,518 patients)<sup>[[8]](#ref8)[[13]](#ref13)</sup>

| Outcome | Vibegron 75 mg | Placebo |
|---|---|---|
| Micturition frequency reduction | −1.8 episodes / 24 h | −1.3 |
| Incontinence reduction | Significantly greater than placebo (P&lt;0.001) | — |
| Volume per void | Significantly greater increase (P&lt;0.001) | — |
| Durable effect | Sustained through 52 weeks | — |

### Vibegron vs. mirabegron

No head-to-head randomized trial exists; comparisons rely on indirect methods:

- Indirect treatment comparison (Kennelly 2021): vibegron may have slightly greater efficacy for total incontinence episodes at 4 and 52 weeks and volume voided at 12 and 52 weeks<sup>[[14]](#ref14)</sup>
- 2025 network meta-analysis (Huang): vibegron outperformed mirabegron and antimuscarinics in reducing micturition frequency; vibegron 100 mg (above the FDA-approved 75 mg dose) showed the greatest reduction<sup>[[15]](#ref15)</sup>
- Vibegron requires no dose titration and does not inhibit CYP2D6, unlike mirabegron

## Special Populations

### Men with BPH — the COURAGE trial

Vibegron's 2024 FDA expansion for OAB in men on BPH therapy is based on the COURAGE trial (1,105 men on α-blocker ± 5-ARI):<sup>[[9]](#ref9)</sup>

| Outcome at 12 weeks | Vibegron effect |
|---|---|
| Daily micturitions | −0.74 (P&lt;0.001) |
| Urgency episodes | Significantly reduced vs. placebo |
| IPSS-storage subscore | Significantly improved vs. placebo |
| Retention / safety | Comparable to placebo |

This is the first β3-agonist FDA-approved specifically for OAB in men receiving BPH pharmacotherapy — a population in which antimuscarinics carry a retention caution in bladder outlet obstruction.<sup>[[7]](#ref7)</sup> The AUA BPH guideline (2026) states clinicians may offer a β3 agonist alone or with an α-blocker for moderate-to-severe predominant storage LUTS (Conditional Recommendation, Grade C). In the PLUS trial, add-on mirabegron in men taking tamsulosin produced adverse-event rates similar to tamsulosin alone and did not worsen Qmax or detrusor pressure at Qmax in men with obstruction.<sup>[[25]](#ref25)</sup>

### Elderly population

- Single-center retrospective series (n=35 β3-agonist recipients): β3-agonist recipients were older (mean age 77.4 years vs. 69.2 for antimuscarinics)<sup>[[16]](#ref16)</sup>
- Japanese claims cohort, adults ≥65 years: anticholinergic users had higher dementia risk than β3-agonist users (adjusted HR 1.22, 95% CI 1.15–1.30) — observational data, consistent with but not proof of a cognitive advantage for β3-agonists<sup>[[24]](#ref24)</sup>

### Pediatric NDO

Mirabegron is FDA-approved for neurogenic detrusor overactivity in pediatric patients age ≥3 years — weight-based dosing with granule formulation.

### Neurogenic LUT dysfunction

Separate symptoms from urodynamic risk. EAU 2026 supports β3 agonists for neurogenic OAB symptoms but recommends against using them specifically to reduce urodynamically proven NDO. Symptomatic improvement in urgency does not establish renal protection; reassess pressure and compliance when these drive treatment.<sup>[[22]](#ref22)</sup> Mirabegron's pediatric NDO indication does not confer an adult NDO indication; adult use for NDO is off-label. See [NLUTD](/docs/clinical-conditions/03d-nlutd/neurogenic-bladder) for risk-based surveillance.<sup>[[7]](#ref7)</sup>

## Adverse Effects

### Common<sup>[[7]](#ref7)[[8]](#ref8)[[13]](#ref13)[[17]](#ref17)[[23]](#ref23)</sup>

| AE | Mirabegron | Vibegron |
|---|---|---|
| Hypertension | ~7.5–9% (label pooled trials: 7.5% at 50 mg vs 7.6% placebo); see BP effects below | 9.0% vs 8.3% placebo in men on BPH therapy (COURAGE); 1.7% vs 1.7% in EMPOWUR |
| UTI | 2–7% | 2–7% |
| Headache | 2–6% | 2–6% |
| Nasopharyngitis | 2–11% | 2–11% |
| Dry mouth | ≈2–3% (2.8% at 52 weeks vs 8.6% with tolterodine in the label's active-controlled study) | Placebo-level (&lt;2%) |
| Constipation | Lower than antimuscarinics | Lower than antimuscarinics |
| Discontinuation for AE | Low | 1.7–2.4% (lower than tolterodine 3.3%) |

### Serious / unexpected adverse effects (pharmacovigilance)

Post-marketing FDA FAERS data on mirabegron flagged rare signals:<sup>[[18]](#ref18)</sup>

- Cardiac arrhythmias — atrial fibrillation, tachycardia, palpitations
- Neurological events — dementia, TIA, Parkinson's disease reports (signals; causality uncertain)
- Vascular — ANCA-positive vasculitis (very rare)
- Angioedema — lip / tongue swelling

### Urinary retention

Japanese JADER adverse event database analysis identified urinary retention as the most frequently reported AE for both agents:<sup>[[19]](#ref19)</sup>

- Mirabegron crude ROR: 62.1
- Vibegron crude ROR: 250 (spontaneous-report disproportionality signals, not incidence rates; not a valid comparison of retention risk between the two drugs)
- Reports were more frequent with concomitant antimuscarinic use and in men with a history of BPH; about half occurred within 15 days of starting

In the COURAGE trial, retention and overall safety in men on BPH therapy were comparable to placebo (table above); vibegron's approval for this population rests on that trial data, distinct from the JADER signal.

### Cardiovascular safety

Vibegron: dedicated ambulatory BP monitoring studies show no clinically meaningful effect on BP or HR.<sup>[[10]](#ref10)[[20]](#ref20)</sup> EMPOWUR: hypertension incidence 1.7% with vibegron vs. 1.7% placebo.

Mirabegron: the label reports mean BP increases of about 0.5–1 mm Hg over placebo at 50 mg in OAB patients (about 3.5/1.5 mm Hg in healthy volunteers), rising dose-dependently to roughly 10 mm Hg systolic at 200 mg (above the labeled dose). Check BP periodically, especially in hypertensive patients.<sup>[[7]](#ref7)</sup>

## Contraindications and Precautions

### Mirabegron<sup>[[7]](#ref7)</sup>

Not recommended for:

- Severe uncontrolled hypertension (AUA/SUFU guideline defines this as systolic ≥180 and/or diastolic ≥110 mm Hg; vibegron carries no equivalent warning)<sup>[[4]](#ref4)</sup>
- End-stage renal disease
- Child-Pugh C hepatic impairment

Adult maximum is 25 mg/day with eGFR 15–29 or Child-Pugh B impairment. With CYP2D6 substrates — particularly narrow-therapeutic-index drugs such as flecainide, thioridazine, and propafenone — monitor and consider adjusting the substrate drug; this is not a blanket contraindication to every CYP2D6 substrate. Use the lowest digoxin dose when starting mirabegron and monitor digoxin levels. Mirabegron also carries a retention caution in clinically significant bladder outlet obstruction and with concomitant antimuscarinics.<sup>[[7]](#ref7)</sup>

### Vibegron

- No adjustment recommended for eGFR 15–89 mL/min/1.73 m² or mild/moderate hepatic impairment. Not recommended with eGFR below 15 (including dialysis) or severe hepatic impairment.<sup>[[23]](#ref23)</sup>
- Digoxin requires level monitoring before and during coadministration, and after stopping vibegron; adjust digoxin as needed.<sup>[[23]](#ref23)</sup>
- Vibegron does not inhibit CYP2D6.<sup>[[23]](#ref23)</sup>

### General precautions

- BP monitoring, particularly for mirabegron in hypertensive patients
- Baseline PVR consideration in men with BPH, particularly during the first 15 days of treatment
- Concomitant antimuscarinic use increases retention risk

## Combination Therapy

EAU 2026 (non-neurogenic female LUTS) offers a β3 agonist to women with OAB who fail conservative management (Strong) and lists a β3 agonist, alone or with an anticholinergic, as an option when an anticholinergic proves ineffective (Strong). It rates mirabegron and vibegron as effective for OAB/UUI with adverse-event rates similar to placebo, and as effective as antimuscarinics with less dry mouth (level 1a); its panel review found β3 agonists more effective than antimuscarinics for nocturia episodes. It suggests adding mirabegron rather than escalating solifenacin 5 mg (Weak).<sup>[[26]](#ref26)</sup>

Mirabegron 25–50 mg plus solifenacin 5 mg improves efficacy over monotherapy for patients with suboptimal response: SYNERGY II found the combination superior to either drug alone, while BESIDE found it beat solifenacin 5 mg but not 10 mg.<sup>[[12]](#ref12)[[15]](#ref15)[[21]](#ref21)</sup> The AUA/SUFU guideline offers a different-class combination after inadequate single-drug response (Conditional Recommendation, Grade B).<sup>[[4]](#ref4)</sup> The AUA/SUFU panel considers this combination principle generalizable within each drug class, not restricted to the specific agents studied. The trade-off is increased anticholinergic side effects (dry mouth, constipation) compared with β3-agonist monotherapy.

## Clinical Positioning

### 2024 AUA/SUFU framework<sup>[[4]](#ref4)</sup>

β3-agonists are positioned as:

- Offered alongside antimuscarinics as an oral OAB option (Strong Recommendation, Grade A), with side-effect profile and shared decision-making guiding the choice
- Preferred when anticholinergic effects carry more risk — the guideline notes lower rates of dry mouth and constipation and likely less effect on PVR:
  - Elderly
  - Cognitive impairment
  - Dry-mouth intolerance
  - Constipation-prone
  - Narrow-angle glaucoma, impaired gastric emptying, or prior retention (where the guideline urges extreme caution with antimuscarinics)
  - Men with BPH at risk for retention (vibegron FDA-approved for men on BPH pharmacotherapy)
- An alternative when antimuscarinics are contraindicated or not tolerated
- Add-on therapy for patients with inadequate response to antimuscarinics

### Other treatment options

The AUA/SUFU guideline permits progression to minimally invasive therapy through shared decision-making without requiring prior medication trials.<sup>[[4]](#ref4)</sup> Options include:

- Intradetrusor onabotulinumtoxinA (100 U idiopathic OAB; 200 U NLUTD)
- Sacral neuromodulation (InterStim, Axonics)
- Posterior tibial nerve stimulation (PTNS, eCoin, Revi)
