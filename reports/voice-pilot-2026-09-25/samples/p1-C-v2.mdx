# Rectovaginal Fistula

A **rectovaginal fistula (RVF)** is an epithelialized communication between the rectum (or anal canal) and the vagina, allowing passage of flatus and feces per vagina. RVF is managed operatively by colorectal surgery, but the urogynecologist and reconstructive pelvic surgeon are routinely involved. RVF clusters with sphincter injury after obstetric trauma, frequently coexists with VVF or rectourethral fistula in multi-compartment cases, and is the prototypical setting for Martius and gracilis interposition in the female perineum.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[2]](#ref2)</sup> Modern management follows the **ASCRS 2022 Clinical Practice Guidelines**, which dictate an etiology-driven, stepwise approach.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup>

For complex multi-compartment cases, see the [Fistulas section landing](/docs/clinical-conditions/03f-fistulas). For the male equivalent, see [Rectourethral Fistula](/docs/clinical-conditions/03f-fistulas/in-males/rectourethral). For operative selection across all repair routes, see the [Female Fistula Repair database](/docs/surgical-techniques/04h-fistula-repair/female-fistula).

## Epidemiology and Etiology

RVF is uncommon in high-resource settings but a significant problem in low-resource countries due to obstructed labor.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[3]](#ref3)</sup> Etiology distribution differs by setting. Obstetric trauma accounts for up to 88% of cases globally; in high-resource referral series, the mix is more diverse, with iatrogenic and Crohn's-related fistulas making up substantial fractions.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[2]](#ref2)[[6]](#ref6)</sup>

| Setting / cause | Notes |
|---|---|
| **Obstetric trauma** | Dominant cause globally; fourth-degree perineal tear, or unrecognized or failed sphincter repair; in the Australian urogynecology series of 41 repairs, obstetric injury explained only 27% of RVF<sup>[[2]](#ref2)</sup> |
| **Crohn's disease** | Up to 43% in referral-center series; tied to active rectal inflammation<sup>[[6]](#ref6)[[7]](#ref7)</sup> |
| **Iatrogenic — colorectal anastomosis** | RVF complicates ~1.6% of low anterior resection (~2.2% of colorectal anastomoses overall); higher with very low tumors and unsatisfactory anastomosis<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[5]](#ref5)</sup> |
| **Iatrogenic — gynecologic surgery** | Hysterectomy (cuff into rectum), prolapse repair (especially mesh), anorectal surgery |
| **Cryptoglandular** | Perianal abscess eroding into vagina |
| **Pelvic radiation** | Cervical, endometrial, or rectal cancer treatment; median onset ~20 months after radiotherapy<sup>[[8]](#ref8)</sup> |
| **Malignancy** | Direct invasion from rectal, cervical, or vaginal cancer |

## Classification

The single most operative-decision-relevant categorization:<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[9]](#ref9)</sup>

| | Simple | Complex |
|---|---|---|
| **Location** | Low (anal canal to lower vagina) | Mid- or high-vaginal septum |
| **Diameter** | &lt; 2.5 cm | ≥ 2.5 cm |
| **Etiology** | Obstetric or traumatic | Crohn's, radiation, malignancy, anastomotic |
| **Tissue bed** | Healthy | Inflamed, irradiated, scarred |
| **Prior repair** | None | One or more failed |

Anatomic level is also commonly described as low (at or near the dentate line), mid (mid-septum, the most common), or high (vault, post-hysterectomy or anastomotic). Anatomic level dictates the operative approach.

---

## Clinical Presentation

- **Passage of flatus per vagina** is often the earliest symptom.
- **Passage of feces per vagina** is pathognomonic.
- **Foul-smelling vaginal discharge** occurs.
- **Recurrent vaginitis and UTI** occur.
- **Fecal incontinence** is frequent in obstetric RVF because of concurrent sphincter damage. Vaginal fecal leakage should be assessed specifically rather than equated with anal incontinence.<sup>[[2]](#ref2)</sup>
- **Dyspareunia** and significant **psychosocial morbidity** occur.

## Diagnostic Evaluation

The ASCRS guideline states that the workup must define both the fistula and the sphincter complex, since sphincter status drives the choice of repair.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup>

### Bedside

- Inspection of the perineum shows scarring, deformity or an external opening.
- Speculum exam identifies the vaginal opening of the tract.
- Digital rectal exam palpates the tract and assesses resting and squeeze sphincter tone and sphincter integrity.
- Anoscopy or proctoscopy identifies the rectal opening.
- **Examination under anesthesia** is often required to fully define the anatomy.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup>

### Imaging

| Modality | Role | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| **MRI pelvis (with vaginal gel)** | The primary imaging study for RVF | Best characterization of tract, secondary extensions, abscess, sphincter destruction, neoplasm; contrast-enhanced MRI carries the highest accuracy<sup>[[3]](#ref3)[[9]](#ref9)[[10]](#ref10)</sup> |
| **3D endoanal ultrasound (EAUS)** | First-line for sphincter mapping; comparable to MRI for simple tracts | Both techniques can be useful; performance data from anal-fistula studies should not be treated as RVF-specific accuracy<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[10]](#ref10)</sup> |
| **Anorectal manometry** | Quantifies resting and squeeze sphincter pressures | Adjunct to EAUS; informs whether sphincteroplasty is required |
| **Vaginography / contrast enema** | Defines the tract when MRI is equivocal | |
| **Proctoscopy / sigmoidoscopy** | Mandatory when Crohn's is suspected | Elective definitive closure generally follows control of active proctitis; urgent drainage of sepsis must not wait for mucosal healing<sup>[[7]](#ref7)</sup> |

## Management — ASCRS Stepwise Algorithm

### 1. Conservative management (Recommendation 12, Weak / 2C)

Conservative management for obstetric and minimally symptomatic benign RVF consists of sitz baths, perineal hygiene, infection control and stool-bulking fiber for 3–6 months. Spontaneous closure has been reported in 52–66% of obstetric RVF.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup>

### 2. Seton drainage (Recommendation 13, Strong / 1C)

A **draining seton** controls acute inflammation or sepsis around the tract, provides durable symptomatic relief in patients who are not surgical candidates, and improves the success of subsequent definitive repair. Seton placement is particularly important in Crohn's, where setons should be placed before initiation of immunosuppression.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[7]](#ref7)</sup>

### 3. Endorectal advancement flap (ERAF) — procedure of choice for simple RVF (Recommendation 14, Strong / 1C)

ERAF is the guideline's procedure of choice for most simple RVF, with or without sphincteroplasty. Recurrent or complex fistulas move to interposition (below).<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup>

**Technique:**

1. Trans-anal exposure is obtained in the prone-jackknife position.
2. A full- or partial-thickness flap of rectal mucosa, submucosa, with or without internal sphincter, is raised proximal to the fistula opening.
3. The tract is excised or curetted.
4. The rectal opening is closed.
5. The flap is advanced distally to cover the closed defect.
6. The vaginal side is left open to drain.

**Outcomes:**

| Setting | Healing |
|---|---|
| ERAF alone | **41–78%** (etiology-dependent)<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup> |
| ERAF + sphincteroplasty (when sphincter defect present) | **80%**<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup> |
| Repeat ERAF after initial failure | **55–93%**<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup> |

In a low-quality network meta-analysis of obstetric RVF (32 studies, 595 patients), sphincter-repair-type operations (transvaginal or transperineal excision with layered closure, Musset procedure) had higher anatomic success than ERAF, and no randomized trial compares ERAF with sphincteroplasty.<sup>[[18]](#ref18)</sup> Predictors of ERAF failure include sphincter defect on EAUS or manometry, Crohn's disease, prior pelvic radiation and recurrent fistula. Diverting stoma is not consistently shown to improve ERAF outcomes and is decided on a per-patient basis.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup>

### 4. Episioproctotomy (Recommendation 15, Strong / 1C)

Episioproctotomy is a transperineal "convert and repair" technique for low RVF with anterior sphincter defect (classically obstetric or cryptoglandular). The anterior anal sphincter complex and rectovaginal septum are intentionally divided to lay open the fistula, then reconstructed in layers with overlapping sphincteroplasty.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup>

- Healing occurs in 78–100%.
- Diversion is used in approximately 50–72% but is not significantly associated with outcome.

### 5. Tissue interposition flaps (Recommendation 16, Strong / 1C)

For recurrent or complex RVF, ASCRS supports Martius or gracilis interposition, selected by anatomy, tissue availability and expertise. Pastier’s retrospective cohort included 62 patients and 79 flap procedures: immediate success was 35% versus 31%, rising to 69% in both groups at median 23 months after additional procedures. Absence of a significant difference does not prove equivalence or require a universal Martius-before-gracilis sequence.<sup>[[11]](#ref11)</sup> A 2024 systematic review and proportional meta-analysis of Martius flaps reported 91.4% healing for primary RVF and 94.6% for radiation-induced RVF.<sup>[[12]](#ref12)</sup>

| Flap | Healing | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| **[Martius flap](/docs/foundations/surgical-principles/flaps/martius)** (bulbocavernosus / labial fat pad) | 65–94% (primary); 77.5% (recurrent); 91.4% pooled (primary); 94.6% (radiation)<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[11]](#ref11)[[12]](#ref12)</sup> | Shorter LOS than gracilis; stoma not mandatory (27% in Pastier series done without diversion); one interposition option |
| **[Gracilis muscle flap](/docs/foundations/surgical-principles/flaps/gracilis)** | 50–92% (individual series); 64% pooled (20 mostly small series, 384 patients); 69% at 23 months<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[4]](#ref4)[[11]](#ref11)</sup> | Higher morbidity (28–47%): wound infection, thigh numbness, hematoma; sexual function is often impaired, and smoking, Crohn's disease and more than two prior repairs predicted failure while a stoma was associated with better closure<sup>[[4]](#ref4)</sup>; may be primary interposition or salvage depending on defect and prior surgery |

**Smoking** was a negative predictor in the Pastier cohort; this does not exclude other modifiable contributors to healing (p = 0.02).<sup>[[11]](#ref11)</sup>

### 6. Anastomotic-related RVF (Recommendation 17, Strong / 1C)

RVF after low anterior resection or other colorectal anastomosis usually requires a transabdominal approach.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup>

- **Fecal diversion** is the typical first step; healing with diversion alone was approximately 37% in the guideline's summary and 15 of 21 (71%) after ostomy in a Chinese series of 24 post-LAR fistulas.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[5]](#ref5)</sup>
- Persistent fistulas need redo colorectal anastomosis, ERAF, or transperineal interposition.

### 7. Radiation-induced RVF (Recommendation 18, Weak / 2C)

Radiation-induced RVF is the most challenging subset; endarteritis obliterans produces a hostile, progressive bed.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[8]](#ref8)</sup>

- Median time to radiation-induced RVF: 20 months (range 5–240) after radiotherapy.<sup>[[8]](#ref8)</sup>
- **Fecal diversion alone** was the only feasible option in 96% (48/50) of patients in one series; only 12% (6/50) healed; healing was associated with fistula &gt; 7 cm from the anal verge (OR 18) and loop ileostomy (OR 17) on univariate analysis, and only distance from the anal verge remained significant in multivariate analysis.<sup>[[8]](#ref8)</sup>
- **Martius flap** for radiation RVF achieved healing in 11/12 and 13/14 patients in two series, a high success rate.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[12]](#ref12)</sup>
- **Proctectomy with coloanal anastomosis** (sleeve excision or pull-through) achieves healing in 75–100% of cases.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[13]](#ref13)</sup>
- **Delayed coloanal anastomosis (Turnbull–Cutait)** achieved 81% success in the multicenter GRECCAR cohort, but with substantial morbidity (45% overall, 23% major) and durable functional sequelae (42% with severe LARS or permanent stoma).<sup>[[14]](#ref14)</sup>
- In a small retrospective comparison, selected restorative-resection patients had greater symptom improvement, and 30% underwent stoma reversal versus none after colostomy alone; selection and operative fitness limit causal comparison.<sup>[[15]](#ref15)</sup>

### 8. Crohn's-disease-associated RVF

Combined medical–surgical management is mandatory.<sup>[[7]](#ref7)[[16]](#ref16)</sup>

- **Infliximab** has the strongest medical evidence for perianal fistulizing Crohn’s disease. Advanced therapy and any immunomodulator should be selected with gastroenterology; perianal-trial results do not guarantee RVF closure.
- **Antibiotics** are used for infection control or as a selected short adjunctive course; routine long-term dual antibiotics are not definitive fistula therapy.
- Abscesses should be drained and sepsis controlled before immunosuppression; a loose seton should be used when appropriate to maintain tract drainage.
- **Elective definitive repair** is favored after control of active rectal inflammation; urgent drainage or diversion for sepsis does not wait for endoscopic healing.
- **Diverting stoma** is used for refractory disease; long-term success of diversion alone in perianal Crohn's is poor.
- Proctectomy or total proctocolectomy with permanent stoma is performed in the most severe cases.

In one retrospective cohort, inflammatory etiologies (Crohn's, cryptoglandular, anastomotic) had healing of only 7.1% after index surgery and 46.4% at final follow-up, versus 45.9% and 91.7% respectively for traumatic (obstetric or iatrogenic) fistulas (55 women, single center; 67% healed overall).<sup>[[17]](#ref17)</sup>

## Outcomes and Prognostic Factors

| Factor | Reported association (mostly retrospective) |
|---|---|
| **Traumatic etiology** (obstetric / iatrogenic) | Best: 91.7% final healing<sup>[[17]](#ref17)</sup> |
| **Inflammatory etiology** (Crohn's, cryptoglandular, anastomotic) | Worst: 46.4% final healing (p = 0.001)<sup>[[17]](#ref17)</sup> |
| **Major procedure** (gracilis, coloanal anastomosis) for failed local repair | OR 6.4 for success per procedure (79 patients, 286 procedures; 72% overall success)<sup>[[6]](#ref6)</sup> |
| **Diverting stoma** in complex cases | OR 3.5 for success<sup>[[6]](#ref6)</sup> |
| **Early surgery** (within 9 months of diagnosis) | OR 2.3 for success<sup>[[6]](#ref6)</sup> |
| **Repair at a specialized center** | OR 3.2 for success<sup>[[6]](#ref6)</sup> |
| **Smoking** | Negative predictor in one interposition cohort<sup>[[11]](#ref11)</sup> |
| **Sphincter defect**, **prior radiation**, **recurrent fistula** | Predict ERAF failure<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup> |

In the Corte series of 79 patients and 286 procedures, the operative algorithm of early temporary stoma with escalation to a major procedure after failure of local repair delivered the highest cumulative success rates.<sup>[[6]](#ref6)</sup>

## Operative Principles

- **Etiology drives strategy.** Obstetric, Crohn's, radiation and anastomotic RVF have distinct pathways.
- **Sphincter integrity and continence** should be assessed. A clinically important defect may favor concurrent sphincteroplasty or episioproctotomy.
- **Infection and inflammation** should be controlled first, with drainage, diversion and treatment preceding definitive repair.
- **Repair proceeds from the high-pressure side.** The rectal advancement flap covers the rectal opening, directing intraluminal pressure away from the suture line.
- **Vascularized tissue interposition** is an option for recurrent or complex fistulas. Martius, gracilis or another reconstruction should be selected by anatomy and tissue quality.
- **Fecal diversion** is individualized for sepsis, symptoms and reconstruction complexity; comparative evidence does not establish a universal closure benefit.
- **Smoking cessation** is advisable; smoking predicted poorer outcomes in one retrospective interposition cohort.<sup>[[11]](#ref11)</sup>
- **Fibrin glue and fistula plugs** have prohibitively poor success rates in RVF and are not recommended.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup>
