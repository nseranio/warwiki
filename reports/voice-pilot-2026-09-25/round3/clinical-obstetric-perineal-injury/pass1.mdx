# Obstetric Perineal & Vaginal Lacerations

Perineal and vaginal laceration affects up to 90% of women after vaginal delivery and is the single largest source of pelvic-floor morbidity the urogynecologist inherits: persistent pain, dyspareunia, fecal and urinary incontinence, prolapse, and depression all trace back, in part, to birth-related injury.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[6]](#ref6)</sup> For the reconstructive pelvic surgeon the topic spans three connected problems: recognizing and classifying the acute injury, repairing it well in the immediate postpartum window, and managing the downstream sequelae (the enlarged genital hiatus, the avulsed levator, the disrupted anal sphincter) that later present as prolapse, incontinence, and dyspareunia.

---

## Anatomy at Risk

- **Perineal body.** The most common laceration site: a mass of dense connective tissue into which the superficial and deep transverse perineal muscles and the bulbospongiosus (bulbocavernosus) insert. It is the keystone of Level III distal support; its disruption widens the genital hiatus and destabilizes the posterior compartment.<sup>[[2]](#ref2)</sup> See [Perineum anatomy](/docs/foundations/anatomy-physiology/pelvis-support/perineum).
- **Anal sphincter complex.** The **external anal sphincter (EAS)** (striated, voluntary, generates squeeze pressure) overlaps for 1–2 cm with the **internal anal sphincter (IAS)** (smooth muscle, autonomic, contributes up to 80% of resting anal-canal pressure). IAS integrity is the chief determinant of passive continence, which is why OASIS subclassification turns on whether the IAS is involved.<sup>[[2]](#ref2)</sup>
- **Levator ani.** Not lacerated at the perineum but avulsed from its pubic insertion by overstretch; the silent injury that drives later prolapse (below).

---

## Classification

Lacerations are graded by depth; the third-degree tier is subclassified by anal-sphincter involvement (the Sultan classification adopted by ACOG and RCOG; **OASIS** means obstetric anal sphincter injury).<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[2]](#ref2)</sup>

| Degree | Structures involved | Repair |
|---|---|---|
| **First** | Vaginal mucosa or perineal skin only; no muscle | May be left if hemostatic and anatomy undistorted; tissue adhesive acceptable |
| **Second** | Into the perineal muscles (bulbospongiosus, transverse perineal) | Layered suture repair |
| **Third (OASIS)** | Anal sphincter complex — subclassified: | Technique depends on EAS thickness and IAS involvement; see primary repair below |
| &nbsp;&nbsp;**3a** | Less than 50% of EAS thickness torn | |
| &nbsp;&nbsp;**3b** | More than 50% of EAS thickness torn | |
| &nbsp;&nbsp;**3c** | EAS **and** IAS torn | |
| **Fourth** | Anal sphincter complex **and** anorectal epithelium | Sphincter + epithelial repair |

![Depth ladder of perineal laceration grades showing tissue planes and how deep each grade tears](/img/diagrams/oasis-grades.svg)

*Schematic classification, not a literal scale drawing of surgical planes. Grades 3a and 3b describe the proportion of EAS thickness torn; 3c includes IAS injury; fourth degree includes anorectal epithelium. An isolated rectal buttonhole tear with an intact sphincter complex is a separate injury.*<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[2]](#ref2)</sup>

Lacerations occur in 53–79% of vaginal deliveries; most are first- or second-degree.<sup>[[2]](#ref2)</sup> Third- and fourth-degree repair is detailed on the [Anal Sphincteroplasty](/docs/surgical-techniques/04h-fistula-repair/female/anal-sphincteroplasty) and [Episioproctotomy](/docs/surgical-techniques/04h-fistula-repair/female/episioproctotomy) pages.

## Mechanism of Injury

Perineal trauma is fundamentally an overstretch phenomenon rather than compression or neuropathy. As the fetal head descends and crowns, the levator ani and birth-canal tissues must elongate to more than three times their resting length.<sup>[[6]](#ref6)</sup> Biomechanical modeling localizes the peak strain to the superior perineal body and its junction with the urogenital hiatus and anal sphincter: the perineal body reaches a stretch ratio of about 1.95 (greatest near the urogenital hiatus) and the EAS transverse diameter narrows by about 51% at delivery, which identifies the tissues most likely to fail.<sup>[[7]](#ref7)</sup>

**Levator ani avulsion** is the consequential occult injury: visible on imaging in up to 19% of primiparous women, present in 55% of women who later develop prolapse, and carrying an odds ratio of 7.3 for prolapse versus intact support.<sup>[[6]](#ref6)</sup> Avulsion enlarges the urogenital hiatus and antedates prolapse by years, providing the mechanistic link between a single delivery and a urogynecologic referral a decade later.

## Risk Factors

Obstetric anal sphincter injury (OASI) complicates about 3% of vaginal births overall, roughly 6% in primiparas versus 2% in multiparas, and about 10% of women report anal incontinence within a year.<sup>[[10]](#ref10)</sup> Pooled analyses and a large Kaiser cohort identify the dominant predictors:<sup>[[8]](#ref8)[[9]](#ref9)[[17]](#ref17)</sup>

| Risk factor | Effect size (95% CI) | Source |
|---|---|---|
| Instrumental delivery | RR 3.38 (2.21–5.18) | Pergialiotis 2020<sup>[[17]](#ref17)</sup> |
| Vacuum-assisted delivery | aOR 4.23 (3.59–4.98) | Ramm 2018<sup>[[9]](#ref9)</sup> |
| Midline episiotomy | RR 2.88 (1.79–4.65) | Pergialiotis 2020<sup>[[17]](#ref17)</sup> |
| Second stage ≥180 min (vs &lt;60 min) | aOR 3.20 (2.62–3.89) | Ramm 2018<sup>[[9]](#ref9)</sup> |
| Persistent occiput posterior | RR 2.73 (2.08–3.58) | Pergialiotis 2020<sup>[[17]](#ref17)</sup> |
| Nulliparity, occiput transverse or posterior position, prior cesarean, Asian ethnicity, epidural, shoulder dystocia, birthweight over 4,000 g | Independent risk factors; effect sizes vary by cohort | Hu 2023, Ramm 2018, Okeahialam 2024<sup>[[8]](#ref8)[[9]](#ref9)[[10]](#ref10)</sup> |

Mediolateral episiotomy is not itself a risk factor; in forceps delivery, episiotomy is protective (OR 0.56, 0.42–0.74), but not in spontaneous or vacuum delivery.<sup>[[8]](#ref8)[[17]](#ref17)</sup> Midline episiotomy combined with forceps is especially destructive for third- and fourth-degree injury.<sup>[[2]](#ref2)</sup>

## Prevention

Prevention matters to the urogynecologist because every OASIS or levator avulsion averted is a future prolapse or incontinence case prevented.<sup>[[6]](#ref6)</sup>

- **Intrapartum (ACOG Level A):** warm perineal compresses during pushing reduce third-/fourth-degree tears (RR 0.46 for third-/fourth-degree tears; moderate-certainty Cochrane evidence), and restrictive episiotomy is preferred over routine.<sup>[[2]](#ref2)[[11]](#ref11)</sup> Combined warm compress plus perineal massage probably reduces second-degree tears (RR 0.63, one trial); massage alone during the second stage has little effect.<sup>[[11]](#ref11)</sup>
- **When episiotomy is needed:** mediolateral or lateral is preferred over midline (midline is associated with sphincter injury); during operative vaginal birth, mediolateral episiotomy reduces OASIS by about 43% with vacuum and about 68% with forceps in nulliparas.<sup>[[2]](#ref2)[[10]](#ref10)</sup>
- **Manual perineal protection** (hands-on) is associated with lower OASIS rates (NNT ≈ 37) in quality-improvement programs; randomized data for hands-on versus hands-off are limited and show no clear difference.<sup>[[11]](#ref11)</sup> Structured "perineal-bundle" programs have lowered OASIS rates nationally.<sup>[[10]](#ref10)</sup>
- **Antenatal:** digital perineal self-massage from about 34–35 weeks reduces perineal trauma requiring suture (RR 0.91, NNT ≈ 15) and episiotomy (RR 0.84); the benefit is confined to women without prior vaginal birth, and third-/fourth-degree tears are not reduced.<sup>[[2]](#ref2)[[10]](#ref10)[[18]](#ref18)</sup>

## Acute Repair (Immediate Postpartum)

**2026 IUGA update:** identify the injury grade before repair. Partial EAS tears require end-to-end repair; full-thickness tears permit either approach, and IAS injury is repaired separately. See [Anal Sphincteroplasty](/docs/surgical-techniques/04h-fistula-repair/female/anal-sphincteroplasty). Future-birth counseling incorporates symptoms, EAUS and manometry where available; elective cesarean does not eliminate later anorectal symptoms (Grade C).<sup>[[16]](#ref16)</sup> Recurrent OASI risk is about 6% (OR 4.9 versus no prior OASI), and anal incontinence is not clearly lower after elective cesarean than after a second vaginal birth (OR 1.1, 0.9–1.4), so counsel individually: symptomatic women or those with abnormal EAUS or manometry are the usual candidates to be offered cesarean.<sup>[[19]](#ref19)</sup> Overlapping and end-to-end repair give equivalent outcomes for full-thickness tears (Grade A); use 3-0 PDS or 2-0 polyglactin for the sphincter and give intravenous antibiotics within an hour before repair (Grade B).<sup>[[16]](#ref16)</sup>

Every vaginal delivery warrants a systematic perineal, vaginal, and rectal examination to avoid missing a sphincter or buttonhole injury; the commonest cause of a "failed repair" is an under-classified one.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[2]](#ref2)</sup>

**First-degree:** repair only if bleeding or anatomy is distorted; tissue adhesive is an acceptable alternative for a small hemostatic tear.<sup>[[2]](#ref2)</sup> For second-degree tears and episiotomy, adhesive reduces pain and repair time but raises dehiscence, so suture remains standard.<sup>[[5]](#ref5)</sup>

**Second-degree.** A layered, deep-to-superficial repair in three stages:<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[3]](#ref3)</sup>

1. **Vaginal mucosa.** Anchor about 1 cm above the apex, then run a continuous (optionally locked-for-hemostasis) closure of mucosa and underlying rectovaginal fascia down to the hymenal ring.
2. **Perineal muscle.** Reapproximate the bulbospongiosus and transverse perineal muscle ends with one or two running, non-locked sutures, rebuilding the perineal body.
3. **Skin.** A running subcuticular closure back toward the hymen, the final knot buried behind the hymen. A separate skin layer is often unnecessary: leaving the skin apposed but unsutured reduces dyspareunia and perineal pain (at the cost of more gaping wounds at 48 h).<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[3]](#ref3)</sup>

### The "crown stitch"

The **crown stitch** is a teaching term (not a standardized entity in ACOG, Cochrane, or AAFP guidance) for the transition stitch at the hymenal ring and posterior fourchette where the running vaginal closure hands off to the perineal-body repair.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[3]](#ref3)</sup> It takes a bite of each side of the fourchette and the underlying perineal-body muscle to reconstruct the posterior fourchette and restore the introitus-to-perineal-body relationship. Tension determines the result: too tight creates a constricting ridge and dyspareunia, and too loose leaves the fourchette splayed and the introitus patulous. It is best understood as the same anatomic checkpoint a urogynecologist re-creates, deliberately and under tension control, during an elective perineorrhaphy.

### Suture technique and material

- **Continuous beats interrupted.** A Cochrane meta-analysis (16 trials, 8,184 women) found continuous repair associated with less pain to 10 days (RR 0.76), less analgesia use with continuous subcuticular skin repair (RR 0.70), and less suture removal (RR 0.56); a 2025 systematic review of 55 RCTs confirmed superiority for pain, repair time, and healing with no significant difference in dehiscence, and found no pain difference by suture material.<sup>[[4]](#ref4)[[5]](#ref5)</sup>
- **Absorbable synthetic** suture (e.g., polyglactin) is preferred over catgut, with less pain, fewer resuturing events.<sup>[[2]](#ref2)</sup>
- **Lock selectively.** A running-[locked](/docs/foundations/surgical-skills/locking-stitch) vaginal-mucosa closure improves hemostasis and traditionally guards against vaginal shortening, but locking the muscle or skin over-tightens as tissue swells and adds pain, so the contemporary evidence favors continuous non-locking for the overall repair, reserving locking for bleeding mucosa.<sup>[[2]](#ref2)[[4]](#ref4)</sup>

### Postoperative care

For OASI repair, use perioperative intravenous broad-spectrum prophylaxis according to the local protocol, postoperative laxatives to maintain soft formed stools, and bladder drainage until recovery from anesthesia and mobility permit a voiding assessment. Avoid figure-of-eight sutures in the sphincters or anorectal epithelium because of tissue-strangulation risk.<sup>[[16]](#ref16)</sup>

Multimodal acetaminophen plus NSAIDs, osmotic laxatives to prevent constipation, and monitoring for urinary retention. Opiates are avoided (constipation); a new opioid requirement should prompt suspicion of infection or repair breakdown rather than escalation.<sup>[[3]](#ref3)</sup>

## The Urogynecologist's Role

The urogynecologist meets birth injury at two moments: the complex acute repair (OASIS, extended or vaginal-sulcus tears, the referral the delivering team escalates) and the delayed reconstruction of what didn't heal or what attenuated over years. The mental model that separates the two is the contrast between acute obstetric repair and elective perineorrhaphy, which share territory but differ in tissue and goals.

| Feature | Acute obstetric repair | Elective perineorrhaphy |
|---|---|---|
| **Timing / setting** | Immediately postpartum, delivery bed | Months–years later, OR under anesthesia |
| **Goal** | Reapproximate fresh torn layers; hemostasis, healing | **Reduce an enlarged genital hiatus**; rebuild perineal body for Level III support |
| **Tissue** | Fresh edges, edematous, vascular | Chronic, attenuated, scarred; thinned perineal body |
| **Dissection** | Minimal — the laceration provides the planes | Extensive — sharp dissection of epithelium off rectovaginal fascia |
| **Key step** | Layered reapproximation; the fourchette transition ("crown") stitch | **Midline fascial plication** + perineal-body build-up; excision of attenuated "perineal gap" |
| **Suture pattern** | Continuous running (less pain) | Interrupted, heterogeneous between surgeons |
| **Combined with** | Nothing (standalone) | Posterior colporrhaphy ± apical suspension |

An enlarged genital hiatus is the single most influential factor urogynecologists cite when deciding to add perineorrhaphy, yet the procedure remains non-standardized (a 2024 IUGA survey found 98% perform it but with wide variation in indication and technique); ACOG cautions that it should be done without tension on the levator ani (a driver of de novo dyspareunia) and should reattach the perineal muscles to the rectovaginal septum.<sup>[[12]](#ref12)[[13]](#ref13)[[14]](#ref14)[[15]](#ref15)</sup>

**Downstream sequelae the urogynecologist manages:**

- **Prolapse.** Via levator avulsion and an enlarged genital hiatus (see [Posterior Compartment Prolapse](/docs/clinical-conditions/03c-pelvic-support/posterior-compartment), [POP overview](/docs/clinical-conditions/03c-pelvic-support/pelvic-organ-prolapse)); reconstructed with [Perineorrhaphy](/docs/surgical-techniques/04g-prolapse-repair/perineorrhaphy) and posterior repair.
- **Fecal incontinence.** Via occult or repaired-but-failed OASIS (see [Fecal Incontinence](/docs/clinical-conditions/03i-defecatory-disorders/fecal-incontinence)); delayed [Anal Sphincteroplasty](/docs/surgical-techniques/04h-fistula-repair/female/anal-sphincteroplasty) or [Episioproctotomy](/docs/surgical-techniques/04h-fistula-repair/female/episioproctotomy) for a chronically disrupted complex.
- **Dyspareunia or introital narrowing or splaying.** From an over- or under-tightened fourchette, managed by revision perineoplasty.
- **Rectovaginal fistula.** The worst-case fourth-degree breakdown (see [Rectovaginal Fistula](/docs/clinical-conditions/03f-fistulas/in-females/rectovaginal)).
