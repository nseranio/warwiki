# Anticholinergic / Antimuscarinic Agents

**Anticholinergic medications** (antimuscarinics) are a cornerstone of urologic pharmacotherapy, primarily for **overactive bladder (OAB)** and **neurogenic lower urinary tract dysfunction (NLUTD)**.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[2]](#ref2)</sup> They block muscarinic receptors (M2 and M3) in the detrusor muscle, inhibiting involuntary contractions, increasing bladder capacity, and reducing urgency and frequency.

The class has six FDA-approved agents with broadly similar efficacy but meaningfully different tolerability, CNS penetration, and dementia-risk profiles. The 2022 SUFU White Paper on anticholinergics and dementia risk and the 2024 AUA/SUFU OAB guideline together have shifted contemporary practice toward β3-agonist-first positioning for many OAB patients, with anticholinergics reserved for specific indications, combination therapy, or patient-specific factors.

---

## Mechanism of Action

- **M3 receptors** produce direct detrusor smooth muscle contraction via extracellular calcium entry (L-type channels) and Rho kinase activation
- **M2 receptors** (more numerous) facilitate M3-mediated contractions and can reverse β-adrenoceptor-mediated relaxation<sup>[[3]](#ref3)[[4]](#ref4)</sup>
- **Antagonism of M3** leads to inhibition of detrusor contraction, which increases bladder capacity and reduces urgency
- **Off-target effects** at other muscarinic receptor populations produce the class side-effect profile

### Receptor selectivity

- **M3-selective:** darifenacin, solifenacin
- **Non-selective (M1–M5):** oxybutynin, tolterodine, trospium, fesoterodine
- Non-selective agents cause broader side effects (salivary glands, CNS, heart, gut)<sup>[[3]](#ref3)[[5]](#ref5)</sup>

### CNS penetration — the mechanism behind dementia risk

- Oxybutynin is lipophilic and non-selective and has high CNS penetration.
- Solifenacin, darifenacin and tolterodine have lower CNS penetration.
- Trospium is a quaternary amine that crosses the BBB poorly and has minimal CNS penetration.<sup>[[3]](#ref3)[[6]](#ref6)</sup>

Blood-brain-barrier properties inform drug selection, but do not establish a validated ranking of long-term dementia risk or prove any agent risk-free.<sup>[[14]](#ref14)</sup>

## FDA-Approved Agents

Six anticholinergic medications are FDA-approved for OAB in the United States.<sup>[[2]](#ref2)</sup>

| Agent | Receptor selectivity | CNS penetration | Typical dose | Distinguishing feature |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Oxybutynin (IR, ER, TD patch, topical gel)** | Non-selective | **High** | IR: 5 mg TID; ER: 5–30 mg daily; patch delivers **3.9 mg/day**, changed twice weekly<sup>[[24]](#ref24)</sup> | Oldest; most extensive evidence; highest dementia concern; **transdermal** avoids first-pass metabolism and reduces systemic side effects |
| **Tolterodine (IR, ER)** | Non-selective | Moderate | IR: 2 mg BID; ER: 4 mg daily | Workhorse; less dry mouth than IR oxybutynin |
| **Fesoterodine** | Non-selective (prodrug → active 5-HMT) | Moderate | 4 mg or 8 mg daily | Prodrug converted to 5-HMT, tolterodine's active metabolite; flexible dosing |
| **Solifenacin** | **M3-selective** | Moderate | 5 mg or 10 mg daily | Better QoL + efficacy vs. tolterodine in head-to-head; widely used |
| **Darifenacin** | **M3-selective** | Lower | 7.5 mg or 15 mg daily | M3 selectivity in theory reduces CNS side effects |
| **Trospium (IR, ER)** | Non-selective | **Minimal (quaternary amine)** | IR: 20 mg BID; ER: 60 mg daily | Lower BBB penetration; **CNS effects remain possible** and renal dosing matters<sup>[[13]](#ref13)</sup> |

International agents not available in the US include imidafenacin, propiverine, and propantheline.<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[3]](#ref3)</sup>

## Clinical Efficacy

### Vs. Placebo (Cochrane 2023, 104 trials, 47,106 patients)<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup>

| Outcome | Effect vs. placebo |
|---|---|
| Micturition frequency | **–0.85 episodes / 24 hr** (95% CI 0.73–0.98 lower) |
| Urgency episodes | **–0.85 episodes / 24 hr** (95% CI 0.67–1.03 lower) |
| Patient-reported cure/improvement | **RR 1.38** (95% CI 1.15–1.66) |
| Quality of life | MD −4.41 (on −100 to 0 scale) |
| Withdrawal due to AEs | **RR 1.37** vs. placebo |

### Class efficacy

- Benefit over placebo is real but modest: about 0.85 fewer voids and 0.85 fewer urgency episodes per 24 hours in the 2023 Cochrane review<sup>[[1]](#ref1)</sup>
- FDA regulatory trials show a 53–80% reduction in incontinence episodes and a 12–32% reduction in frequency<sup>[[2]](#ref2)</sup>
- Placebo response is substantial (30–47% for incontinence), a durable reminder that behavioral therapy and regression-to-mean matter

### Head-to-head comparisons<sup>[[7]](#ref7)[[8]](#ref8)</sup>

**Solifenacin versus IR tolterodine** (solifenacin superior):

- Quality of life (SMD −0.12)
- Patient-reported cure or improvement (RR 1.25)
- Leakage (WMD −0.30)
- Urgency episodes (WMD −0.43)
- Solifenacin causes less dry mouth than IR tolterodine

Fesoterodine 8 mg is superior to ER tolterodine for all efficacy endpoints, but withdrawal (RR 1.45) and dry mouth (RR 1.80) are higher.

Tolterodine and oxybutynin have similar efficacy; tolterodine has less dry mouth (RR 0.65) and fewer withdrawals for adverse events (RR 0.52).

Extended-release and immediate-release formulations have similar efficacy, and ER formulations cause significantly less dry mouth.

### Dose comparisons

- Tolterodine 1/2/4 mg have similar efficacy, and higher doses give more dry mouth.
- Solifenacin 10 mg is more effective than 5 mg but causes more dry mouth.

## Adverse Effects

### Common anticholinergic burden<sup>[[1]](#ref1)[[2]](#ref2)</sup>

| AE | Rate / relative risk |
|---|---|
| **Dry mouth** | Most common; about 25% (trospium, solifenacin, tolterodine) to 44% (oxybutynin); RR 3.50 vs. placebo<sup>[[14]](#ref14)</sup> |
| **Constipation** | Common; about 8-15%; RR 2.03 vs. placebo<sup>[[14]](#ref14)</sup> |
| **Dry eyes** | Frequent |
| **Blurred vision** | Up to about 11%; RR 1.58 vs. placebo<sup>[[14]](#ref14)</sup> |
| **Urinary retention** | RR 3.52 vs. placebo |
| **Withdrawal due to AEs** | RR 1.37 vs. placebo |

### Cognitive impairment and dementia risk

Dementia risk is the most consequential modern controversy in OAB pharmacotherapy.<sup>[[9]](#ref9)[[10]](#ref10)[[11]](#ref11)</sup>

A French case-control study (4,810 cases, 24,050 controls) found that OAB anticholinergic use was associated with OR 1.23 for dementia, with a clear dose-response relationship:

| Cumulative exposure | OR for dementia |
|---|---|
| 1–90 DDDs | 1.07 |
| 91–365 DDDs | 1.29 |
| **&gt;365 DDDs** | **1.48** |

**Agent-specific signal:**

- Oxybutynin and solifenacin carry a marked increased risk.<sup>[[10]](#ref10)[[11]](#ref11)</sup>
- Trospium has limited brain penetration, which supports consideration when an antimuscarinic is needed.<sup>[[13]](#ref13)</sup> Absence of an association in an individual study does not establish long-term cognitive safety.<sup>[[14]](#ref14)</sup>

A 2025 meta-analysis of eight observational studies found about 20% higher dementia risk with OAB anticholinergics versus no drug therapy (RR 1.2, 95% CI 1.09–1.32) and a higher risk than with mirabegron (RR 1.28); heterogeneity was very high, and confounding by indication remains possible.<sup>[[26]](#ref26)</sup>

### 2022 SUFU White Paper conclusions<sup>[[11]](#ref11)</sup>

- Chronic use (&gt;3 months) is likely associated with increased dementia risk
- Short-term use (&lt;4 weeks) was considered likely safe for most patients; this does not remove individual delirium or retention risk
- Dose–response associations strengthen concern but do not eliminate confounding
- Lower CNS penetration with trospium, darifenacin and fesoterodine is a theoretical advantage and does not prove a safest agent<sup>[[14]](#ref14)</sup>
- Patients should be counseled about the dementia association before long-term initiation
- Alternatives (β3-agonists, botulinum toxin, neuromodulation) should be considered especially in elderly and cognitively-vulnerable patients

### Mechanism

Anticholinergic blockade of acetylcholine in the brain disrupts cholinergic neurotransmission involved in learning and memory. Agents with greater CNS penetration (oxybutynin) pose higher cognitive risk than those with limited CNS penetration (trospium).<sup>[[3]](#ref3)[[12]](#ref12)</sup>

However, one propensity-matched cohort study (782 pairs, adults with normal cognition, MCI or dementia) found no significant association with clinically relevant cognitive decline, though dose and duration were unknown and oxybutynin or tolterodine users trended higher (CDR OR 1.65, 95% CI 0.98–2.77). Residual confounding remains a concern.<sup>[[27]](#ref27)</sup>

## Contraindications and Precautions

### Contraindications<sup>[[2]](#ref2)[[13]](#ref13)</sup>

- **Uncontrolled narrow-angle glaucoma** (the 2024 AUA/SUFU guideline advises extreme caution in any narrow-angle glaucoma and coordination with the treating ophthalmologist)<sup>[[14]](#ref14)</sup>
- **Gastric retention / severe GI obstructive disorders**
- **Urinary retention** (relative to absolute depending on severity)

### Use with caution

| Situation | Consideration |
|---|---|
| **Elevated PVR** | Increased risk of urinary retention; consider an alternative or a cautious trial with repeat PVR. There is no accepted cutoff; most pivotal trials excluded PVR 150-200 mL<sup>[[14]](#ref14)</sup> |
| **Significant BPH** | May worsen obstructive symptoms; use with α-blocker if chosen |
| **Cardiac arrhythmia** | Can exacerbate tachyarrhythmias |
| **GI motility disorders** | Ulcerative colitis, intestinal atony, myasthenia gravis |
| **Renal impairment** | Trospium IR: **20 mg nightly if CrCl &lt;30 mL/min**; ER is **not recommended** at that clearance. Oxybutynin patch (Oxytrol) and gel (Gelnique) labels give no renal or hepatic dose guidance; the gel label states there is no experience in renal or hepatic impairment. Check each other agent’s label separately<sup>[[13]](#ref13)[[25]](#ref25)[[28]](#ref28)[[29]](#ref29)</sup> |
| **Elderly** | Higher risk of cognitive effects, falls, constipation. Trospium IR label: at 75 years or older, the dose may be titrated down to 20 mg once daily according to tolerability<sup>[[13]](#ref13)</sup> |
| **Parkinson's / MS / scleroderma / hypothyroidism** | Conditions affecting motility and cognition |

### AUA/SUFU guidelines<sup>[[14]](#ref14)</sup>

- Clinicians discuss dementia risk with patients taking or about to be prescribed antimuscarinics
- Antimuscarinics are used with extreme caution in narrow-angle glaucoma, impaired gastric emptying, or history of urinary retention
- Clinicians consider a PVR to exclude incomplete emptying, especially with voiding or emptying symptoms, obtain one if there is a history of retention or risk of it, and weigh risk and benefit with the patient
- Efficacy and side effects are assessed at 4-8 weeks, with a PVR considered in men, older patients and those starting an antimuscarinic
- Efficacy and side effects are reassessed after initiation

## Clinical Positioning

### Contemporary paradigm

**2024 AUA/SUFU OAB guideline** positions pharmacotherapy as:<sup>[[14]](#ref14)</sup>

- Clinicians offer behavioral measures and discuss β3-agonists or antimuscarinics according to symptoms, adverse effects and preferences.
- A different-class combination can be considered after an inadequate single-drug response.
- **Botulinum toxin, sacral neuromodulation and PTNS** need not await completion of a fixed medication ladder; the guideline permits their selection without prior drug trials through shared decision-making.

**EAU 2026 (non-neurogenic female LUTS)** offers anticholinergics to women with OAB who fail conservative management (Strong) and advises shared decision-making, extended-release formulations whenever possible, early review of efficacy and adverse effects, and, if a drug fails, dose escalation, a different formulation or a β3 agonist alone or combined (all Strong). Immediate-release products allow flexible or on-demand dosing but cause more adverse events. EAU finds no anticholinergic clearly superior for cure or improvement and reports that most patients stop within three months. For older women it advises caution with long-term use, especially with existing or risk of cognitive dysfunction, and assessment of anticholinergic burden (Strong). Cognitive harm is cumulative with exposure (level 2); oxybutynin may worsen cognition, while darifenacin, fesoterodine, solifenacin and trospium have not been shown to in short-term studies (level 1b).<sup>[[31]](#ref31)</sup>

### When to choose antimuscarinic over β3-agonist

- **Patient has tried β3-agonist** without adequate response
- Combination therapy has a synergistic effect in patients with suboptimal monotherapy
- **Patient preference / cost** (antimuscarinics are generic and cheaper)
- **Specific subpopulations** where antimuscarinic evidence is stronger (NLUTD, pediatric)

### When to avoid anticholinergics

- An elderly patient with cognitive concerns is managed with a β3-agonist or trospium
- Anticholinergics are avoided in active cognitive impairment or dementia where an alternative exists
- In severe BPH with high PVR, anticholinergics worsen retention
- **Narrow-angle glaucoma**

### Adherence

Long-term adherence is suboptimal, with pooled 1-year persistence about 12-25% for antimuscarinics versus 32-38% for mirabegron.<sup>[[14]](#ref14)</sup> Discontinuation is driven by dry mouth, constipation, cognitive side effects and inadequate efficacy.

---

## Combination Therapy

**Antimuscarinic plus β3-agonist** (e.g., solifenacin 5 mg plus mirabegron 25–50 mg) provides superior efficacy versus monotherapy in patients with partial response; SYNERGY and SYNERGY II showed this against each drug alone, and in BESIDE the combination beat solifenacin 5 mg but not 10 mg.<sup>[[15]](#ref15)[[16]](#ref16)</sup> The AUA/SUFU 2024 guideline offers different-class combination after inadequate single-drug response (Conditional Recommendation, Grade B); in SYNERGY II, adverse events were 49% with combination versus 41-44% with monotherapy.<sup>[[14]](#ref14)</sup>

- The combination gives additional frequency reduction over monotherapy
- The combination gives additional urgency or incontinence reduction
- The combination increases anticholinergic side effects versus β3-agonist alone, so the choice is a trade-off

## Special Populations

### Neurogenic Lower Urinary Tract Dysfunction (NLUTD)

Antimuscarinics are first-line pharmacotherapy for bladder storage dysfunction in NLUTD:<sup>[[17]](#ref17)[[18]](#ref18)[[19]](#ref19)</sup>

- Antimuscarinics reliably increase maximum cystometric capacity and voided / catheterized volumes
- Antimuscarinics decrease detrusor pressure
- Antimuscarinics improve urgency and incontinence across SCI, MS and spina bifida
- There is no consistent evidence of superiority among agents
- Antimuscarinics may be combined with β3-agonists or α-blockers
- Intravesical oxybutynin delivers the drug locally with reduced systemic absorption and is an option in CIC-dependent NLUTD patients with systemic intolerance

### Pediatric neurogenic bladder

Oxybutynin is the most-used agent, effective at increasing capacity, lowering storage pressures and abolishing overactive contractions. Mirabegron and (rarely) gabapentin are alternatives in intolerant patients.<sup>[[20]](#ref20)</sup>

### Men with BPH

Antimuscarinics benefit men with predominantly storage symptoms and coexisting BPH, alone or combined with an α-blocker; the AUA/SUFU 2024 guideline supports antimuscarinic or β3 monotherapy, or α-blocker plus either class (Conditional Recommendation, Grade B).<sup>[[14]](#ref14)</sup> Antimuscarinics may raise PVR slightly but have not been associated with significantly more retention in men with BPH-related obstruction, although effects with a PVR above 200 mL are not well studied. Combination with an α-blocker improves OAB symptoms more than α-blocker alone.<sup>[[21]](#ref21)</sup> PVR is checked before initiation and at follow-up. The AUA BPH guideline (2026) likewise allows an antimuscarinic alone or with an α-blocker for moderate-to-severe predominant storage LUTS (Conditional Recommendation, Grade C), and adds behavioral measures when storage symptoms persist on medication.<sup>[[22]](#ref22)[[30]](#ref30)</sup>

## Formulations

| Formulation | Advantages | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| **Extended-release oral** | Less dry mouth than IR; QD dosing | Default for most agents |
| **Immediate-release oral** | Faster onset; cheapest | Higher dry mouth burden |
| **Transdermal patch** (oxybutynin) | Bypasses first-pass metabolism; less dry mouth | Skin reactions in some patients |
| **Topical gel** (oxybutynin) | Application flexibility | Transfer risk to partner / children |
| **Intravesical instillation** (oxybutynin) | Minimal systemic absorption | NLUTD / CIC patients with systemic intolerance |

## Progression to Advanced Therapies

With inadequate response, intolerance or preference for a procedure, clinicians discuss minimally invasive therapies. Prior medication failure is not a universal prerequisite:<sup>[[14]](#ref14)[[23]](#ref23)</sup>

- **Intradetrusor onabotulinumtoxinA** (100 U for idiopathic OAB; 200 U for NLUTD)
- **Sacral neuromodulation** (InterStim, Axonics)
- **Posterior tibial nerve stimulation** (PTNS, eCoin, Revi)
